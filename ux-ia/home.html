Information Architecture

For a mid-level UX designer, written to be handed on: what information architecture is, the shapes it takes, how to build it at four levels of complexity on one fictional product (Perqo: a website, an employee app, a backoffice), where it sits in the product cycle and in the business, how to validate it, and how to deliver it anyway when the research, the content or the stakeholders are missing. Twelve core units in four parts and three deep dives.

What you will be able to do

By the end of the course the reader can read any information-architecture artefact a team hands them and say what it claims and what it leaves out; choose and build the right artefact for a system of a given complexity, from a website's sitemap to a cross-surface interaction map that joins the product map to its decision trees and lifecycles; place that work inside a product development cycle with a named touchpoint for product management, engineering, content, sales, support and clients; and deliver a defensible IA when the research, the content or the stakeholders are missing, with every assumption labelled and a test named for each.

  • Outcome 1 — reads any IA artefact and draws one in the course notation, chooses an organisation system, and judges a structure by the behavioural evidence on scent, mental models, categories and labels (IA1, IA2, IA3, IA4; DD-IA1 reads five real products this way).
  • Outcome 2 — delivers the four Perqo IAs at rising complexity, ending in the interaction map that joins product map, decision trees and lifecycles (IA5, IA6, IA7, IA8).
  • Outcome 3 — runs IA inside a product cycle and keeps it whole: a touchpoint matrix, a validation plan with evidence, an incomplete-inputs playbook with an assumption register, and the measurement, governance and shared vocabulary that follow (IA9, IA10, IA11, IA12; DD-IA2, DD-IA3).

Every unit is sourced to the field's standard works, Nielsen Norman Group research or peer-reviewed studies, labels vendor guidance and practice as such, carries its figures as drawings built from the unit's own content, and ends with a shipped deliverable built on the fictional Perqo.

    Frame

    A course for other designers, on the one skill the field documents least well

    Information architecture is taught in the standard works as a set of systems (organisation, labelling, navigation, search) and shown in practice as a sitemap. The gap is the middle: how the same discipline scales from a brochure site to a backoffice with roles, workflows and three surfaces; how the artefact changes shape as the system does; and what a designer does when the research, the content or the stakeholders that the method assumes are not there. This course is built around that gap, on one fictional product, Perqo, that grows through four levels of complexity, and it is written for a mid-level designer who will be handed it, which makes it the Project's one exception to its single-reader rule (decided 29 Oct 2026).

    Pick

    One fictional product, four levels, one notation, and a whole part on working without the inputs.

    • A single growing example keeps every artefact comparable.
    • Levels, not tools: tools change yearly, structures do not.
    • The missing-inputs case is the job as it is actually done.

    Override if: after IA8 the Perqo example cannot carry a reader's own product; then a second worked example (a content-heavy public site) is added as a deep dive in place of DD-IA3.

    • Reader: a mid-level UX designer, three to six years in, little hands-on research experience. Every brief says so; reader.md is unchanged.
    • Example: Perqo, a fictional flexible-benefits platform: a sales site, an employee app, a managers' backoffice. Every Perqo fact is invented by the unit that needs it and labelled.
    • Figures: three to eight per unit, every one a drawing built from the unit's own content by kit/viz.py; no screenshots, no third-party images. The IA figure kinds are added to the kit before IA1 is written.
    • Where the evidence pushes back: most of the field's guidance is practice, not measurement. The course labels it as such and keeps the measured claims (foraging, breadth versus depth, card-sort reliability, findability failure) separate from the craft advice.
