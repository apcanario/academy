01How a real product's IA is read from outside

What can be read. Two kinds of evidence are open to someone outside a company. The first is its public screens: the pages a visitor sees without signing in. The second is what its makers publish about the structure: design-system documentation, help-centre articles, developer documentation, and posts on the company's own blogs. The course's sourcing rule sets the limit: nothing is asserted about a real company's IA beyond what is visible on its public screens, dated, or documented by the company itself, cited. Two further limits come from how this unit read the pages. Every page was fetched as text on 10 November 2026, so a layout that cannot be read from a page's text is not asserted; and nothing behind a sign-in was seen. Three of the five products (the Monzo app, the Stripe Dashboard and Shopify's admin) sit almost wholly behind a sign-in, so their readings rest on their makers' documentation, and each reading names the screens it could not see.

What it is worth to Perqo. IA12's proxy table gives the answer before any product is read. Competitors' public screens show "what others chose to show, and their words for shared things"; they cannot show "whether it works for anyone; anything behind a sign-in, which is where a backoffice sits"; alone they reach C0 on IA11's ladder (IA12, section 2; IA11, section 6.4). The five products here are not Perqo's competitors, and some of their makers publish tests, but the same ceiling holds for anything this unit carries to Perqo: a test GOV.UK ran on GOV.UK's users is evidence about GOV.UK. For Perqo it is a reasoned default, C0, until Perqo's own test raises it. Every lesson in Deliverable C is held there.

The lens. Each reading applies three filed tools. IA2's five faults (IA2, section 3) are asked of every published artefact: does it state its decision, reader, scope and date; is its notation declared; are its levels mixed; does it show conditions and unhappy paths; do its labels drift from the vocabulary. IA12's triage tree (IA12, Deliverable A) is turned outward: for each product, the published account shows which inputs the makers had (research, stakeholders who answered, trusted content, requirements that held still) and which shortage it shows. And IA12's register fields (IA12, section 3.2) are the form in which Deliverable A records what each published artefact claims, what evidence the makers published, and what it leaves untested.

Why these five. Each was chosen because its makers document its structure in public, so that the reading rests on evidence, and because together they stand for the four levels:

02GOV.UK: a content-rich public site (IA5)

Scheme and structure. The home page at gov.uk, read on 10 November 2026, is split in two. Under "Services and information" it lists 16 topics, from "Benefits" and "Births, deaths, marriages and care" to "Visas and immigration" and "Working, jobs and pensions"; under "Government activity" it lists links by department and document type: "Departments", "News", "Guidance and regulation", "Research and statistics", "Policy papers and consultations", "Transparency", "How government works" and "Get involved" (GOV.UK, home page, gov.uk, and "Services and information", gov.uk, browse, both read 10 November 2026; public screens). The services half is a topic scheme, IA4's ambiguous scheme by subject (IA4, section 1.1); the government half is organised by who publishes and what kind of document it is. Each topic opens a browse page, and GOV.UK's public content API shows how those are built: the Benefits page is a record of type mainstream_browse_page whose eight children are curated, "second_level_ordering": "curated" (GOV.UK content API, Benefits browse page record, gov.uk, content API, read 10 November 2026; publisher's public data). That is IA4's hierarchy, designed top-down (IA4, section 2.1). Beside it runs a second structure, a topic taxonomy. Its principles, published by GDS, say "The taxonomy is hierarchical", "A topic should have between 2 and 12 child topics (or none)", "The maximum number of topics at any level should ideally be around 12", "Content should be tagged to the lowest possible level of the taxonomy" and "Content should not be tagged to both a topic and its parent" (GDS, "GOV.UK Taxonomy principles", published 13 June 2019, gov.uk, taxonomy principles, read 10 November 2026; publisher's documentation). The taxonomy's topic pages are reached from content pages, through sidebar links and breadcrumbs (same page).

Labelling. The labels are plain and specific, and mostly front-loaded with the word that tells them apart, as IA3's A2 asks (IA3, checklist). Two observations, both of 10 November 2026, fall short of the checklist. Seven of the eight children of Benefits begin with the same four words, "Benefits and financial support", followed by "for families", "if you're caring for someone", "if you're disabled or have a health condition", "if you're looking for work", "if you're on a low income", "if you're temporarily unable to work" and "when someone dies"; the eighth is "Manage an existing benefit, payment or claim" (content API, browse/benefits). Read by their first words, the seven are identical, which fails A2. And two labels drift within a page: the topic list reads "Births, deaths, marriages and care" where the navigation menu reads "Births, death, marriages and care", and the home page's first "Government activity" list says "Transparency documents" where its second says "Transparency" (gov.uk and gov.uk/browse, read 10 November 2026). That is IA2's fifth fault and IA3's D2 (one thing, one label) on the one site of the five whose makers publish rules for its vocabulary.

Navigation model. The global routes are the header's "Menu" and "Search GOV.UK", present on both pages read; the home page and gov.uk/browse carry the 16 topics as local routes into the services half; contextual routes are the taxonomy's sidebar links and breadcrumbs on content pages (taxonomy principles, 2019); and the supplemental route is the step by step, a pattern the GOV.UK Design System documents: it "presents an end to end journey in logical steps", is for journeys that "have a specific start and end point", and content pages that are part of one "always have a link at the top that goes to the standalone step by step page" (GOV.UK Design System, "Step by step navigation", design-system.service.gov.uk, step by step, read 10 November 2026; design-system guidance). In 2021 GDS described what the model had become: about 200 mainstream topics, 400 specialist topics and 4,000 taxonomy topics, three different menu bars depending on page type, and "This triple-system, which has substantial duplication, causes confusion for users" (Sam Dub, "Our plans to improve navigation on GOV.UK", GDS blog, 17 May 2021, gds.blog.gov.uk, navigation plans; practice, a maker's account). The same post gives the scale, more than half a million pages and an average of 17.8 million users a week in 2021, and the route most people take: "while most users start from a search engine, around 15% use topic pages to navigate" (Dub 2021).

Search. GOV.UK's site search is "one of the main ways that people find information on GOV.UK"; in 2024 it moved to Google's Vertex AI Search, live from the end of February, and the makers report from an A/B test "a 14% increase in overall clickthrough from the search results page", "an 11% decrease in users having to refine their search" and "an 8% reduction in users leaving site from the search page" (Catriona Fraser, Christian Sutter and Tara Stockford, "A new search engine for GOV.UK", Inside GOV.UK, 23 October 2024, insidegovuk.blog.gov.uk, new search engine; a maker's report of its own test, no intervals given). A later post gives its use, "more than 4 million times a month"; it moved filters and sorting into "a single panel above the search results", because before the change "only a small minority of people selected filters", and it reports autocomplete suggestions used in 55% of searches where they appeared (Catriona Fraser and Monica Crusellas, "Making it quicker and easier to search on GOV.UK", Inside GOV.UK, 12 December 2024, insidegovuk.blog.gov.uk, quicker search; same status). Read against IA3: the site has the supplemental search the Polar Bear book asks about first, and its makers measure it by refinement, IA3's reformulated query (IA3, section 5.2; checklist E2), and by clickthrough and exit from the results page.

Objects. The objects a visitor meets are pages of a few types, and the content API names them: a browse page is a mainstream_browse_page, with links to its children, its top_level_browse_pages, its primary_publishing_organisation and its available_translations (content API, browse/benefits). On this unit's reading of the record, every page is a content item with a document type, an organisation that publishes it, and tags into one or more of the topic systems; the taxonomy's topics are a second kind of object, and the step by step a third. That is IA4's database model underneath a hierarchy: items described by fields, with the groups made from the tags (IA4, section 2.3).

Lifecycle. GOV.UK serves a citizen's task with a start and an end. The step by step is the only pattern read that draws such a lifecycle across pages, and the Design System is explicit that it is not for everything: not when "the user only needs to read guidance and not take an action", and not "inside a transactional service" (Step by step navigation, read 10 November 2026). The services themselves, where a claim is made or a licence applied for, sit behind their own start pages and were not read.

The shaping decision. The decision that most shapes GOV.UK, on its makers' own account, is to drive navigation from tagging. In 2019 a GDS product manager wrote that the goal is "a single sitewide taxonomy that powers front end navigation", that taxonomy tagging was rolled out to all Whitehall publisher app users in July 2018, and that "all content from the point of the rollout has been tagged to the taxonomy"; the older policies and policy areas "have been removed and redirected to equivalent topics" (Si Stephens-Manassiev, "An update on the GOV.UK taxonomy", Inside GOV.UK, 12 February 2019, insidegovuk.blog.gov.uk, taxonomy update; practice, a maker's account). A site of half a million pages from many departments cannot be curated by hand page by page; the tag decides where a page appears.

What it got right, and where it strains. On the evidence its makers published, GOV.UK tests its structural changes before rolling them out: the 2025 change from a grid to a list on the 16 topic pages followed an A/B test in which "the list design helped people find content faster, with less time spent on the page", and a "Most viewed" section, named after user research, was rolled out to all 16 (Jen Richardson, "Rolling out simpler ways to navigate GOV.UK's topic pages", Inside GOV.UK, 31 January 2025, insidegovuk.blog.gov.uk, topic pages; the post gives no figures). Its taxonomy principles are the kind of written rule IA4's R1 to R10 are for Perqo, and on one point they meet: GOV.UK tags content to the lowest possible level and never to both a topic and its parent, and IA4 gives every catalogue item exactly one narrower term, and so one category (taxonomy principles, 2019; IA4, Deliverable B, B.1 and B.4, R2). The strain is the one its makers named: the single taxonomy did not replace the curated browse, so in 2021 there were three topic systems, publishers "need to tag their content to multiple topics, and to multiple topic systems", and in 2019 "We know that we need to do more to make it easier for publishers to tag their content accurately" (Dub 2021; Stephens-Manassiev 2019). In a reply on the topic-pages post, dated 4 February 2025, its author wrote that "the topic taxonomy pages don't form part of our current phase of work" (Richardson 2025). Run through IA12's triage tree, the published account shows research in hand (A/B tests, usability testing) and the first shortage the tree asks about: requirements that did not hold still, a merger of the three systems that GDS wrote in 2021 it was in "the early stages of thinking about", and that no post read reports finished. The screens this reading could not see: the services behind their start pages, the publishing tools, and any page drawn only by script.

Services and information (section, 16 topics) holds Benefits (section), which holds the Benefits and financial support pages (stack, "7 of 8 labels open with the same four words", tone 6) and Manage an existing benefit, payment or claim (section); and 15 other topics (stack, "Births, deaths, marriages and care to Working, jobs and pensions"). 03The Monzo app: a consumer app (IA6)

Scheme and structure. The Monzo app's screens sit behind a sign-in, and none was seen; this reading rests on Monzo's own help pages and blog, read on 10 November 2026. One of them, "Your guide to the home screen", is Monzo's English-language page for Spain (monzo.com/en-es), and the "Save tab" is named only there; the other two help pages are at the UK address, so the structure below joins two markets' pages and may match neither app exactly. On those pages the first screen, Home, is a summary: "Your accounts, including joint, are at the top of the screen"; "Right below your accounts, the Activity section shows your latest and upcoming payments"; "Your Pots have their own dedicated area, which you can view as a list or with images"; Savings & Investments is a separate section below Pots; and a "Save tab" is where to "Find your Savings Pots" (Monzo, "Your guide to the home screen", monzo.com, home screen guide; Monzo, "Finding important features in the new Home screen", monzo.com, finding features; both undated, read 10 November 2026; vendor documentation). The scheme, on this unit's reading, is exact at both levels: by account first, then by time inside Activity. The structure is IA6's partial hub (IA6, section 3.2): Home holds routes into each account and each Pot, and the actions live on the account they act on, a "Card" button on each account and three dots for actions such as adding money, statements and scheduled payments (finding features).

Labelling. The labels the pages name are the customer's words for money: accounts, Activity, Pots, Card, Savings & Investments. "Pots" is a product name, and on this unit's reading the one label a newcomer has to learn, the branded-term risk IA3 names after Farrell (IA3, section 4.2); its own area on Home and its own word in the "Save tab" keep it in one place. The "+" at the top right is an iconic label, IA3's fifth form (IA3, section 4.1), and the pages tell people what it opens: "open a new account, create a new Pot, apply for a loan, or more" (home screen guide). Whether the icon carries text on the screen, IA3's A4, could not be seen.

Navigation model. The pages name a Home, a Save tab and a Help page, and say settings are reached by tapping "your profile photo in the top left corner", where a new option lets the customer edit the Home screen's layout (home screen guide; finding features). The full tab bar is not named on any page read, and this reading does not assert it. What is documented is the model's centre of gravity: almost every route starts on Home, and the customer sets its order. Monzo's post on the redesign says the Home screen summarises "your latest activity across all your Monzo accounts", and that the order and level of detail can be changed, so that people can make the screen look much like the previous version (Max White, "The new and improved Home screen", Monzo blog, 1 November 2023, monzo.com, new Home screen; vendor's account).

Search. One search covers the app: "You can search for everything in one place, with search available on both the Home and Help pages of the Monzo app"; it finds transactions with filters, "individual payees", and lets people "take certain actions from search results, including applying for other Monzo products"; it tolerates typos, so "Searching for amazn will still pull up all your Amazon transactions" (Monzo, "We're changing how search works in the Monzo app", monzo.com, search changes, undated, read 10 November 2026; vendor documentation). The same page lists what was removed: exporting search results as a file, and filtering by location. Search here is a route to objects and to actions, which IA3's E1 would ask about: whether browsing still reaches every task without it (IA3, checklist E1). The pages read do not say.

Objects. The objects the pages name are the account, the card (reached from a Card button on each account, with its number, CVC and a freeze), the payment, the Pot and the payee. Each has its home: payments in Activity, Pots in their area, the card on its account. On this unit's reading the account is the parent object, and the card and the payments nest inside it, Prater's nesting from IA4 (IA4, section 4.1).

Lifecycle. The app serves a customer's month: see what is there, pay, look back. No payment lifecycle is published on the pages read; Activity shows "latest and upcoming payments", so the pages draw two states, upcoming and done, and no more.

The shaping decision. The decision that most shapes the app is to let the customer set the first screen. The makers' account of why: "Our app had outgrown its own design and architecture", and "It was way too hard for customers to get the most out of Monzo because they couldn't discover things or easily see their full financial picture"; early ideas were "overwhelming and confusing", which led to customers controlling "what they see on their Home screen" (Max White, "How we built the new Home screen", Monzo blog, 6 November 2023, monzo.com, how we built it; vendor's account).

What it got right, and where it strains. The post names the core jobs the redesign had to keep in reach: "viewing balances, copying card details and searching for transactions" (White, 6 November 2023). Each of the three sits on Home in the help pages read: the balances at the top, the Card button on each account, the search on Home. That is IA3's B1 met as the makers describe it: the units the system acts on are shown where the person makes the decision they govern (IA3, checklist B1). The evidence the makers report is that they "heard from more than 1,000 Monzo customers" and "run 4 major and 2 minor experiments over the last 7 months", reaching "more than 8 million people" (White, 6 November 2023); no method or result is published, so for an outside reader the claim is a vendor's account of research, and no finding can be checked. The strain shows in the help centre itself: a page exists to tell customers where things are, "Finding important features in the new Home screen", and among them it says Pots moved to their own area under Activity (finding features). A redesign of the first screen moved routes people had learnt, and the makers wrote a page to find them again. The screens this reading could not see: all of them, including the tab bar.

Monzo app, the surface, drawn without a type tag as IA6 draws the Perqo app (IA6, B.3), its note "tab bar not fully named in the pages read", leads to Home (home, "layout set by the customer"), Save tab (section, "Savings Pots") and Help (section). Home holds Accounts (index, "at the top; Card button on each"), Activity (index, "latest and upcoming payments; See all", See all being the link the finding-features page names for the full list of payments), Pots (index, "list or images"), Savings & Investments (section), Profile (section, "top left: Settings; Edit layout"), + (section, "open an account, create a Pot, apply for a loan") and Search (index, "also on Help; transactions, payees"). Every box is tone 2.

04The Stripe Dashboard: a business backoffice (IA7)

Scheme and structure. The Dashboard sits behind a sign-in and was not seen; Stripe documents it page by page. Its sidebar, as the documentation gives it on 10 November 2026, opens with a first section "where you can access and act on information related to your balances, transactions, customers, and products": Home, Balances, Transactions, Customers and Product catalog. Home "provides analytics and charts about your business performance", and its widgets are added or removed under Your overview (Web Dashboard). Then comes a Shortcuts section, "your pinned and most recently visited pages". Then a Products section, Stripe's own products: Connect, Payments, Billing and Reporting, with "More" for Workflows, Tax, Identity, Atlas, Issuing, Financial connections, Capital and Climate. Settings are "broken into three categories: Personal, Account, and Product" (Stripe, "Web Dashboard", docs.stripe.com, Web Dashboard, read 10 November 2026; vendor documentation). The scheme is by object first and by Stripe product second, and the structure is IA4's database model: Transactions are a list with filters and exports, Customers a list "using filters to locate specific customers", and a click on a customer's name opens their details, "including subscriptions, payments, payment methods, invoices, and quotes" (same page). That is IA7's list and detail, one pattern per object type (IA7, section 4.3).

Labelling. Two labels strain against IA3's D2, one thing one label. "Products" names Stripe's products in the second section, while "Product catalog" in the first holds "products and prices for your business", the merchant's products (Web Dashboard). And the label "Transactions" opens a page whose address is dashboard.stripe.com/payments, and the same documentation calls that page "the Payments page" elsewhere (Web Dashboard; Stripe, "Payment status updates", docs.stripe.com, payment status, read 10 November 2026; vendor documentation). The search syntax adds a third layer of names: is:charge is "A payment (identical to payment)" (Stripe, "Perform searches in the Dashboard", docs.stripe.com, Dashboard search, read 10 November 2026; vendor documentation). So one payment is a Transaction in the sidebar, a payment on its page and in search, and a Charge in the API, which the documentation maps and which IA2's fault 5 asks to be written down (IA2, section 3).

Navigation model. The global routes are the sidebar's sections; the search is a supplemental route, read in the next paragraph; Shortcuts are a supplemental route that the person builds, pinned and recent pages; the local routes are each Stripe product's pages; and the contextual routes run from one object to the objects it holds, a customer to their subscriptions, payments and invoices (Web Dashboard). Two further routes are documented: a keyboard, "press the question mark key (?) for a list of available keyboard shortcuts", and, for businesses with several accounts, Organizations, where a list such as payments can be seen "across all of your accounts" (same page). Team members "can have different levels of access" through user roles (same page), so the role layer of IA7, section 3, exists; its matrix was not read.

Search. Search is the Dashboard's strongest supplemental route. It covers "connected accounts", "customers", "invoices", "payouts" and "products" among others, shows top results at once and the full results in groups, each with "View all"; it takes field filters (amount:, email:, status:, last4: and others), range operators, negation with a hyphen, and is: with one of 26 object types, from account to transfer, or with a state such as is:refunded; an object identifier takes the person "directly to the object you're looking for"; and "As search terms are included in the URL, you can bookmark the search or share it with other team members" (Dashboard search, read 10 November 2026). Against IA3's frame it serves Spencer's known-item seeking, with the item's own words or its identifier (IA3, section 5.1).

Objects. The objects are the API's: customer, invoice, payout, product, price, subscription, refund, dispute, PaymentIntent and the rest of the is: list (Dashboard search). The Dashboard's search names the same objects the developer documentation does, so the backoffice and the API share one model. The object a payment rests on, the PaymentIntent, carries the lifecycle below.

Lifecycle. A PaymentIntent moves through statuses that Stripe's documentation lists in a table: Requires payment method, Requires confirmation, Requires action, Processing, Succeeded and Canceled, with requires_capture when funds are authorised and captured separately; "If the payment attempt fails (for example, due to a decline), the PaymentIntent's status returns to requires_payment_method so that the payment can be retried", and cancellation "can't be undone" (Stripe, "How Payment Intents and Setup Intents work", docs.stripe.com, intents, read 10 November 2026; vendor documentation). The Dashboard does not show those statuses. It shows a payment status that "summarises the payment but doesn't include the additional details provided by the PaymentIntent status", and the documentation publishes the mapping: the first three API statuses show as Incomplete (the table adds that under requires_payment_method and requires_action the status can also be Partially paid, Waiting on funding or Failed), processing as Pending, requires_capture as Uncaptured or Partial capture, succeeded as Succeeded, and cancellation as Cancelled, with the caveat "Edge cases such as expired attempts and specific decline codes can affect this mapping. Use the API or Workbench Inspector to get the authoritative state" (Payment status updates, read 10 November 2026). The English (UK) page read writes the API value cancelled; the unlocalised lifecycle page and the English (US) search page write it canceled (Payment status updates; intents; Dashboard search, status:canceled).

The shaping decision. The decision that most shapes the Dashboard, on this unit's reading of Stripe's documentation, is that the backoffice's objects are the API's objects. Search, lists and detail pages are organised around the same nouns the developer uses, and where the Dashboard needs a simpler word for a lifecycle it publishes the mapping instead of inventing a second model. That is Evans's ubiquitous language, which IA7 took for Perqo (IA7, section 6.2), run from the other side: the model's words reach the merchant's screen.

What it got right, and where it strains. The status mapping is the published artefact in this unit that most resembles the course's own: it is IA1's vocabulary mapping, which IA2 draws as a line "Surface: Label" (IA1, section 2.4; IA2, section 5.2) and IA8's J-01, the join from a status attribute to its lifecycle (IA8, D.4), written as a table for the people who use the product. It also states what it leaves out, the edge cases, and where the authority lies, the API: IA2's first fault avoided (IA2, section 3). The strain is in the labels above, and in a mapping that is many to one in its main line, with the exceptions the table lists: three API statuses become one Dashboard word, so a merchant who sees Incomplete cannot tell from the list which of the three it is, and the documentation sends them to the JSON (Payment status updates). The documentation publishes no research on the Dashboard's navigation; through IA12's triage tree, what is in hand is a model that held still, the API, and the structure is drawn from it, the move the tree gives for a content shortage: "structure from the objects and the data model" (IA12, Deliverable A). The screens this reading could not see: the whole Dashboard, the role matrix, and the mobile app, which the documentation says is available "in 14 languages" (Web Dashboard).

The sidebar, Settings and search, as figure 3 draws them. Dashboard, the surface, drawn without a type tag as IA7 draws Perqo Admin (IA7, Deliverable C), leads to Home (home, "analytics; Your overview widgets"), Balances (index), Transactions (index, "address: /payments", tone 6), Customers (index), which holds Customer details (detail, "subscriptions, payments, payment methods, invoices, quotes"), Product catalog (index, "your products and prices", tone 6), Shortcuts (section, "pinned and recently visited pages"), Products (section, "Stripe's products", tone 6), which holds Connect, Payments, Billing and Reporting (each section) and More (stack, "Workflows, Tax, Identity, Atlas, Issuing and others"), Settings (section, "Personal, Account, Product") and Search (index, "is: filters, field filters, object IDs"). Three boxes are tone 6, the labels that strain: the two meanings of product, and Transactions, whose page is /payments; every other box is tone 2.

The lifecycle and its mapping, as figure 4 draws them. The states carry the lifecycle table's names, and the figure writes requires_capture in the same form, as Requires capture. Requires payment method, Requires confirmation and Requires action each carry "Dashboard: Incomplete"; Processing carries "Dashboard: Pending"; Requires capture "Dashboard: Uncaptured or Partial capture"; Succeeded, an end state, "Dashboard: Succeeded"; Canceled, an end state in tone 6, "Dashboard: Cancelled; can't be undone"; the other states are tone 2. A card payment's direct path to Succeeded is not stated in the PaymentIntent column of the page read, so it is not drawn.
