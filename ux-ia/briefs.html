What information architecture is, and where it sits

written

The definitions that hold up, the four systems, the five planes, and the lines between IA and navigation, UI, content strategy and the data model.

Shipsone-page definition sheet for a product team + the Perqo surfaces map

Read the unit →
Opus brief
Unit IA1, What information architecture is, and where it sits. Cover: the definitions of information architecture in the standard works and what each one includes and leaves out (Rosenfeld, Morville and Arango's four systems of organisation, labelling, navigation and search; Covert's sense-making and the difference between information, content and data; Arango's architecture of information environments; Garrett's structure plane between scope and skeleton); the lines a designer has to hold between IA and navigation design, IA and the user interface, IA and content strategy, IA and the engineering data model, with one worked example per line; why IA exists as a discipline (library science and the first web, the Polar Bear book's history, Wurman's coinage), told briefly; what an IA deliverable is for, which decision it supports, and who reads it; a first look at how IA shows up in a system of three surfaces, using Perqo as the example. Figures: the four systems; Garrett's planes with IA located; the Perqo surfaces and who uses each. Deliverable: a one-page definition sheet a product team can be handed (what IA is, what it is not, the four systems in a sentence each, the five questions an IA answers), and the Perqo surfaces map that later units build on.

Shipsthe course notation sheet + the artefact decision table

Opus brief
Unit IA2, The shapes IA takes: the artefact catalogue and the course notation. Cover: the catalogue of IA artefacts and what question each one answers: content inventory and audit; sitemap (structural, not page-by-page); object map and object-relationship map; taxonomy and controlled vocabulary; user flow, task flow and wireflow; decision tree; state diagram; navigation model (global, local, contextual, supplemental); service blueprint; the cross-surface interaction map; for each, who reads it, what fidelity it needs at which stage, and what it must not be mistaken for; the published notations (Garrett's visual vocabulary of 2002; Brown's chapters on site maps and flow charts in Communicating Design) and what they standardise; how to read an artefact someone else made, and the five faults to look for first; how to edit one without breaking what others rely on; the single notation this course uses from here on, derived from Garrett and Brown and reduced to what the course's figure kinds can draw. Figures: one small example of each artefact family in the course notation; the legend itself. Deliverable: the course notation sheet (every symbol, line and label rule, with a one-line meaning each) and the artefact decision table (question asked → artefact → fidelity → reader → unit of this course that builds it).
IA3

How people find and understand things

not written

Information scent and foraging, mental models, categorisation, labelling: the research that decides whether a structure works.

Shipsthe findability and labelling checklist, applied to a Perqo navigation

Opus brief
Write one unit of a self-study course in the same long-form report format as the Brand + AI units of the Learning Project: a long-form report in Markdown. Length is whatever the topic needs (2,000–6,000 words); do not pad and do not cut to a target. Reader: a mid-level UX designer, three to six years in product teams, with a working grasp of UX practice and little hands-on user-research experience; reads in order (each unit assumes the ones before it), reads on a phone, checks the citations, and wants genuine depth rather than a primer. This course is written to be handed to other designers; it is the Learning Project's one exception to its single-reader rule (decided 29 Oct 2026). Running example: Perqo, a fictional flexible-benefits platform in the technology space, invented for this course (any resemblance to a real company is unintended), with three surfaces: perqo.com, the sales and product website; Perqo Admin, the backoffice where a client company's HR and finance managers configure budgets, policies, approvals and reports; and the Perqo app, the employee client app for seeing balances and spending benefits; a merchant side exists where a unit needs it. Every Perqo fact is invented by the unit that needs it, labelled '(Perqo, fictional)' on its first use in the unit, kept consistent with the Perqo facts in the filed units, and never used as evidence for a claim about the world. Tool-agnostic: name no diagramming or research tool as required; describe every technique so it can be done in any tool or on paper. Tone: direct, no flattery, no filler, no motivational language, no 'not X but Y' constructions. Course goal: by the end of the course the reader can read any information-architecture artefact a team hands them and say what it claims and what it leaves out; choose and build the right artefact for a system of a given complexity, from a website's sitemap to a cross-surface interaction map that joins the product map to its decision trees and lifecycles; place that work inside a product development cycle with a named touchpoint for product management, engineering, content, sales, support and clients; and deliver a defensible IA when the research, the content or the stakeholders are missing, with every assumption labelled and a test named for each. Sourcing standard: every claim that carries a number, a named source, a mechanism or a recommendation is sourced where it is made. Acceptable sources are: the field's standard works cited by author, title, edition and year (Rosenfeld, Morville and Arango, Information Architecture: For the Web and Beyond, 4th ed., 2015; Covert, How to Make Sense of Any Mess, 2014; Spencer, A Practical Guide to Information Architecture; Brown, Communicating Design, 2nd ed., 2011; Garrett, The Elements of User Experience, 2nd ed., 2011, and his visual vocabulary of 2002; Arango, Living in Information, 2018; Kalbach, Mapping Experiences, 2nd ed., 2020; Young, Mental Models, 2008; Krug, Don't Make Me Think, Revisited, 2014; Resmini and Rosati, Pervasive Information Architecture, 2011; Hedden, The Accidental Taxonomist; Welchman, Managing Chaos, 2015; Halvorson and Rach, Content Strategy for the Web, 2nd ed., 2012; Cooper and others, About Face, 4th ed., 2014; and others of the same standing, each with a page or chapter where a specific claim is drawn); Nielsen Norman Group articles and reports cited with author, title, date and URL; peer-reviewed HCI and information-science research with the venue and year; platform, standards and vendor documentation labelled as such (a research-tool vendor's guidance on sample sizes is vendor guidance, not a study); a practitioner's article or talk only with a named author and date, labelled as practice rather than evidence. A diagram redrawn or adapted from a source credits that source in its caption. No real company's information architecture is asserted beyond what is visible on its public screens or documented by the company itself. No uncited figures; no invented articles, authors, studies, URLs, names or numbers; a claim that cannot be sourced is said to be unsourced and is left out of the deliverable. A unit that reads as advice without evidence is a failed unit. Links are given as links, so the course bibliography can collect them. Figures: this is a visual subject, and every unit carries figures (three to eight) where structure is the content: a sitemap, an object map, a flow, a decision tree, a matrix, a map of surfaces. A figure is declared in the unit as a fenced viz block of one of the kinds kit/viz.py draws at build time (the kinds and their fields are in its docstring; the IA kinds added for this course are listed there), never as a screenshot, an embedded image or hand-written SVG; its labels and numbers are the unit's own content, and its caption says what it shows and, for a redrawn diagram, the source. Every figure uses the course notation defined in unit IA2 once that unit is filed. Where the unit depends on current facts (a live product's screens, a published figure, a tool's documentation), search before writing and date the facts. End with the unit's deliverable, built out in full, not described.

Unit IA3, How people find and understand things. Cover: information foraging and information scent (Pirolli and Card, 1999, and the Nielsen Norman Group's articles that apply it), with what a strong and a weak scent look like in a label and a link; mental models as Indi Young and Norman use the term, and the gap between a user's model and a system's model; how people categorise (basic-level categories, prototype effects, and the Polar Bear book's organisation challenges: ambiguity, heterogeneity, differences in perspective, internal politics) and what that means for a navigation scheme; labelling: the forms labels take, why vocabulary mismatch happens, and the evidence on consistency and specificity; search versus browse behaviour and what search logs reveal about failed structures; findability (Morville) and Krug's don't-make-me-think argument, with the limits of each. Figures: the foraging loop; a scent comparison of two labels for the same page; the user-model versus system-model gap; a categorisation example from Perqo's benefits. Deliverable: the findability and labelling checklist (every item sourced to the research it rests on), applied item by item to a Perqo app navigation that the unit invents as the as-is (Perqo, fictional), with the verdict and the fix for each failing item.

Unit IA4, Organisation systems: schemes, structures, objects, taxonomies. Cover: organisation schemes (exact: alphabetical, chronological, geographical; ambiguous: topic, task, audience, metaphor, hybrid) and when each is right, with the Polar Bear book as the spine; organisation structures (hierarchy, its breadth-versus-depth trade-off and the evidence on it; polyhierarchy; database and faceted models; hypertext) and how real systems combine them; faceted classification and controlled vocabularies (Hedden; Rosenfeld, Morville and Arango on metadata and thesauri) and the difference between a taxonomy and a folksonomy; object-oriented UX (Sophia Prater's object mapping, cited to her published articles) as a way to find the nouns before the pages, and where it stops; how an organisation system shows up in navigation, filters, search and URLs. Figures: the scheme catalogue as a tree; breadth versus depth; a facet grid; the Perqo object map. Deliverable: the Perqo object map (objects, attributes, relationships, the calls to action on each object, Perqo facts invented and labelled) and a faceted taxonomy for the Perqo benefits catalogue (facets, values, the rules for adding a value), built in full.
