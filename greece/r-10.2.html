Agamemnon and the kingship of the Atreids

01The sceptre: a genealogy of authority

When Agamemnon rises to speak in Iliad 2.100–108, the poet pauses over the object in his hand. Hephaestus made the sceptre and gave it to Zeus; Zeus to Hermes; Hermes to Pelops; Pelops to Atreus "shepherd of the people"; Atreus, dying, to Thyestes "rich in flocks"; Thyestes to Agamemnon, "to rule over many islands and all Argos". The authority is six hand-overs from a god's forge, and the house of Pelops is an orderly succession.

That orderliness is the point. There is no golden lamb, no seduction of Aerope, no banquet of children, no curse of Myrtilus. Homer either does not know or deliberately excludes the Tantalid horrors that Aeschylus makes the engine of the Oresteia; Tantalus is never named in either epic. The Homeric sceptre carries only themistes, the ordinances of Zeus. Nestor states the theology: Zeus gave Agamemnon "the sceptre and the themistes, that you may take counsel for them" (9.98–99); Odysseus repeats it while restoring order, "let there be one lord, one king, to whom the son of Kronos gave sceptre and themistes" (2.204–206).

Yet the sceptre is also a stick. Achilles swears on his and hurls it down (1.234–246); Odysseus takes Agamemnon's from his hand (2.186) and uses it to beat the ranks back to assembly and then to beat Thersites (2.265–268). The emblem of Zeus-given rule is, in the same book, a bludgeon.

Where sources disagree. Pausanias (9.40.11–12) says Chaeronea worshipped a sceptre it called "the spear", claiming it was Homer's, with a priest and daily sacrifices; no other source corroborates this local appropriation. More seriously, every post-Homeric genealogist (Apollodorus, Epitome 2.10–15; the tragedians) has Thyestes and Aegisthus expel the Atreidae, with Agamemnon regaining the throne by force. Whether Homer suppressed the feud or whether it had not yet been invented is uncertain.

02Anax andron and the Mycenaean wanax

Agamemnon's signature formula is anax andron, "lord of men", some forty times in the Iliad. Zeus, Apollo and Poseidon are anax, and metre lends the title to minor figures such as Anchises and Eumelus, but its concentration on Agamemnon is deliberate. It stands above basileus, the ordinary word Homer uses for every chieftain in the camp. Agamemnon is one basileus among many; he alone is habitually anax andron.

The Linear B tablets (thirteenth century BC) give the word's prehistory. The palace ruler at Pylos and Knossos is the wa-na-ka, wanax, whose initial w- Homeric metre still "hears" in hiatus. At Pylos his temenos is three times that of the lawagetas, "leader of the people" (PY Er 312); craftsmen are wa-na-ka-te-ro, "royal"; he appears in offering and initiation records. The qa-si-re-u (basileus) is a minor local official, in one case over a group of bronze-smiths. Homer preserves this hierarchy in fossil form: by the eighth century the wanax was a poetic word and basileus had risen to fill the vacancy. Agamemnon commanding a camp of basileis is the memory of a system, not the description of one.

Where sources disagree. L. R. Palmer read the Pylian wanax as a sacral king and saw Homer's divine anax as a trace of this. T. G. Palaima argues the word is probably non-Indo-European with real cultic associations but no divine kingship; P. Carlier (La royauté en Grèce avant Alexandre, 1984) stresses administration over cult. The tablets are too laconic to settle it.

03The limits of his authority

The Iliad is an experiment on how far a paramount king's power reaches when he has no palace, bureaucracy or standing army behind him, only consent.

Book 1

Agamemnon's power is first shown as refusal: he threatens Chryses (1.26–32) and, when Calchas tells him to return the girl, demands an immediate replacement geras (1.118–120). Achilles calls him "most grasping of men" (1.122) and demoboros basileus, "people-devouring king" (1.231). Nestor's mediation (1.254–284) states the rule the poem will test: Achilles must not "strive against a sceptre-bearing king", but Agamemnon must not take the girl "whom the sons of the Achaeans gave him". Both halves are ignored. Agamemnon can take Briseis because nobody physically stops him; he cannot make Achilles fight.

Book 2: Diapeira and Thersites

Deceived by the Dream, Agamemnon "tests" the army by proposing flight (2.110–141). They bolt for the ships; only Athena, through Odysseus, turns them. The king does not know the mood of his own men. Then Thersites, "the ugliest man who came to Troy" (2.216), repeats Achilles' charges almost verbatim: Agamemnon's huts are full of bronze and women the army won for him; "let us sail home and leave him to digest his prizes" (2.236–238). Odysseus beats him and the army laughs. Order is restored, but the complaint has now been voiced by the best warrior and the worst, and refuted by neither.

Where sources disagree. W. G. Thalmann (1988) reads Thersites as a scapegoat through whom heroic ideology disciplines dissent by laughter; P. W. Rose reads the scene as the demos briefly speaking. The later tradition (Proclus on the Aethiopis; Apollodorus, Epitome 5.1) made him Diomedes' kinsman, killed by Achilles for mocking his grief over Penthesilea, which suggests more than a comic foil.

Book 9: the Embassy

Agamemnon weeps and proposes flight sincerely (9.17–28). Diomedes answers that Zeus "gave you the sceptre to be honoured above all, but not courage, which is the greatest power" (9.37–39). Nestor tells him he "dishonoured a mighty man" (9.109–111); he concedes, "I was blinded, I do not deny it" (9.115–116), lists a king's ransom (9.121–157), and ruins it: "let him yield to me, since I am more kingly (basileuteros) and older" (9.160–161). Odysseus silently omits that clause when he delivers the offer. Achilles refuses anyway. The king can command gifts; he cannot command what gifts are meant to buy.

Book 10: the Night Council

Sleepless, tearing his hair (10.3–16), Agamemnon convenes the chiefs by night and gives one instruction that reads as self-criticism: Diomedes must choose a companion by merit, "not looking to birth, even if he is more kingly" (10.237–239), a glance at Menelaus. The poem's one meritocratic sentence comes from the man whose rank is the problem. A scholion on 10.1 reports that the Doloneia was inserted by Pisistratus, and many analysts treat it as a later addition; its Agamemnon is nonetheless consistent with Books 2, 9 and 14.

Book 19

Achilles renounces his wrath in nine lines (19.56–73). Agamemnon's reply is long and evasive: Zeus, Fate and the Erinys blinded him, as Ate once blinded Zeus at the birth of Heracles (19.86–136). He re-offers the gifts and swears he never touched Briseis (19.258–265). Nowhere does he say, in the first person, that he did wrong. The reconciliation is Achilles' indifference, Odysseus's ceremony and Agamemnon's theology of excuse.

He proposes flight three times (2, 9, 14); on the third Odysseus says he should command "some other, inglorious army" (14.84). He insults Odysseus and Diomedes on review (4.338–400), fights magnificently once (Book 11), and is harsher to suppliants than any Greek (6.55–65; 11.122–147). Priam, from the wall, never saw a man "so kingly" (3.170). Appearance and performance diverge.

Is this a critique of kingship? Oliver Taplin ("Agamemnon's Role in the Iliad", 1990) argues Homer draws a deliberately inadequate king. Dean Hammer (The Iliad as Politics, 2002) and David Elmer (The Poetics of Consent, 2013) read the poem as working out when collective approval, epainos, may be withheld from a leader; Kurt Raaflaub sets it in the emergent eighth-century polis; Moses Finley denied it reflects stable institutions at all. The safe statement: the Iliad never questions that there should be a sceptre, only whether this man deserves to hold it.

04The Catalogue of Ships as a political map

The Catalogue (2.494–759) lists twenty-nine contingents and about 1,186 ships. Agamemnon brings 100 (2.569–580), and the poet infers rank from number: "he was the best, since he led by far the most people". Nestor brings 90, Diomedes and Idomeneus 80, Menelaus 60, Achilles 50, Odysseus 12. Thucydides (1.9) reasoned from this that Agamemnon led through power and fear, not Tyndareus's oaths, backed by inherited Pelopid wealth and the strongest navy.

His territory is Mycenae, Corinth, Cleonae, Sicyon and the Achaean coast to Helice and Aegion: the north-east Peloponnese. Argos and Tiryns belong to Diomedes (2.559–568), contradicting "all Argos" at 2.108 and the Odyssey's "corner of horse-pasturing Argos" (3.263). The seven cities "near sandy Pylos" he offers Achilles (9.149–153) imply a reach into Messenia. The map is a composite of claims, not a survey.

Where sources disagree. Hope Simpson and Lazenby (1970) argued the Catalogue preserves Mycenaean geography, since it names places abandoned after the Bronze Age and omits Dorian centres; Giovannini (1969) and Visser (1997) argued for Archaic composition. The Boeotians coming first, with their sixty-man detail, has suggested a Boeotian origin. No consensus exists.

05Homer's king and the tyrant of tragedy

The Homeric Agamemnon is anxious, generous, quick to despair, brutal in battle, insecure in council. He is never a tyrant, because the concept belongs to the seventh century. Tragedy, performed in a democracy that had expelled its tyrants before an audience that was itself an assembly, recast him. Aeschylus's Agamemnon thanks the gods and promises to purge the city "by cutting or burning" (Ag. 848–850), a man who rules by force; then resists the crimson cloths, "honour me as a man, not a god" (925), and yields in six lines. Sophocles' Ajax (1226–1315) shows him arrogant and legalistic, cowed by Odysseus. Euripides goes furthest: in Hecuba he will not help openly because "the army would say I did it for Cassandra's sake" (854–863); in Iphigenia at Aulis Menelaus accuses him of canvassing humbly for command and growing haughty once he had it (337–375), and he confesses "Hellas compels me" (1271–1275). The king who could not control the army has become the king controlled by it.

06Iphigenia: four moral weights

Homer knows nothing of the sacrifice; Agamemnon offers Achilles one of three living daughters, Chrysothemis, Laodice and Iphianassa (9.144–145). The Cypria, in Proclus's summary, is the earliest source: Agamemnon shoots a deer at Aulis and boasts he surpasses Artemis; she sends contrary winds; Calchas prescribes the sacrifice; Iphigenia is lured by a false marriage to Achilles; Artemis substitutes a deer and makes the girl immortal among the Taurians. The Hesiodic Catalogue of Women (fr. 23a M-W) has "Iphimede" saved to become Artemis Einodia. The offence is a hunter's boast, the punishment divine, the girl does not die.

Aeschylus removes the rescue. The parodos (Ag. 184–247) gives the eagles and the pregnant hare, Artemis's anger half-explained, and quotes Agamemnon: "how can I desert the fleet and fail my allies?" (212–213). The chorus judges: he "put on the yoke of necessity" and "breathed an impious change of mind" (218–221); he "dared to become his daughter's sacrificer" (224–225). Then the gag, the saffron robe falling, the glance that struck each sacrificer "as in a picture" (231–247). Necessity is real and the choice is his; Clytemnestra's "he sacrificed his own child" (1415–1418) is the debt coming due.

Euripides wrote it twice. Iphigenia among the Taurians (c. 414) restores the rescue and tells it from the victim's side; she blames Helen, Calchas and Odysseus more than her father (354–377), who becomes a remote cause. Iphigenia at Aulis (405) inverts this: Agamemnon writes to stop her coming, recants, is caught by Menelaus, weeps, and yields once the army knows. Iphigenia, who begged for life at 1211–1252, volunteers at 1368–1401, "I give my body to Hellas". Moral agency passes from king to girl; the king is left with fear. Aristotle (Poetics 1454a) cited her reversal as inconsistent character.

Where sources disagree. The IA was produced posthumously and is the most interpolated Euripidean text; the messenger speech reporting the deer-substitution (1532–1629) is almost certainly spurious, so whether Euripides let her die is uncertain. Why Artemis is angry in Aeschylus is also unresolved: the hare omen is the surface motive, and some (Lloyd-Jones) see the Cypria's boast behind it.

07Chryseis, Briseis, Cassandra

Three captured women mark Agamemnon's career, each a geras that signals his precedence over his peers. Chryseis, taken at Hypoplacian Thebe (1.366–369), he prefers to Clytemnestra "in form, stature, mind and work" (1.113–115); a god forces her return. Briseis, taken at Lyrnessus by Achilles, who killed her husband (2.689–693), he seizes as compensation and twice swears he never slept with her (9.132–134; 19.258–265). Her one speech, the lament for Patroclus (19.282–300), recalls his promise that Achilles would make her his wedded wife; she alone speaks of the geras economy from inside it.

Cassandra is his prize from the sack. In Homer she is betrothed to Othryoneus (13.365–369), first to see Priam bring Hector's body home (24.699–706), and killed over Agamemnon's body (Od. 11.421–423). Tragedy gives her the voice Briseis lacks: in Aeschylus (Ag. 1035–1330) she sees the murder before it happens; in Euripides' Trojan Women (353–405) she sings her own wedding song and prophesies that her marriage will destroy the house of Atreus. Each woman is the token of the king's status and the instrument of its undoing: Chryseis through Apollo, Briseis through Achilles, Cassandra through Clytemnestra.

08Return, carpet, murder; and the shade in Hades

The Odyssey tells the homecoming three times before Agamemnon does. Zeus makes Aegisthus the paradigm of the warned transgressor (1.35–43). Nestor (3.254–312) says Aegisthus seduced Clytemnestra despite a bard set to guard her, killed Agamemnon, ruled seven years and died at Orestes' hand in the eighth. Proteus (4.512–537) has Agamemnon blown round Malea, landing in joy, ambushed at a banquet with twenty men. In every Homeric version Aegisthus kills and Clytemnestra assists.

Aeschylus reverses this. Clytemnestra kills, in the bath, with sword and entangling robe. Before it, the carpet scene (905–974): she spreads crimson cloths, he protests that such honours belong to gods, she argues him down, and he walks barefoot on the wealth of his house ("there is the sea; who shall drain it dry?", 958). Over the body she claims justice for Iphigenia (1372–1398). Pindar (Pythian 11.22–25) asks what the tradition never settled: was it the daughter's slaughter, or another man's bed?

Where sources disagree. Homer places the murder at Agamemnon's house in Mycenae or "Argos"; Stesichorus and Simonides (scholia on Euripides, Orestes 46) and Pindar (Pyth. 11.31–32) set it at Amyclae near Sparta; Aeschylus moved the Oresteia to Argos, almost certainly for Athens's alliance with Argos in 462/1. The weapon shifts from sword (Aeschylus) to axe (Sophocles, Electra 99; Euripides).

In Hades (Od. 11.385–464) Agamemnon died "like an ox at the manger", and draws the lesson: "never be gentle with your wife; do not tell her everything" (441–443), exempting Penelope; he urges Odysseus to land in secret (455–456). In Book 24 he envies Achilles' death at Troy (24.36–94) and contrasts Penelope's "gracious song" with Clytemnestra's "hateful song for women" (24.192–202). He is the Odyssey's negative template: the king who came home openly, trusted his house and died at dinner. Odysseus does everything he did not.

09Cult, Sparta, and the archaeology that is not his

Pausanias (2.16.6–7) saw at Mycenae the tombs of Atreus, Agamemnon, Eurymedon, Cassandra's twins Teledamus and Pelops, and Electra, inside the walls; Clytemnestra and Aegisthus lay "a little outside, not deemed worthy". About a kilometre away the British School excavated (1950–53) the Agamemnoneion, a shrine with votives from the late eighth century and Hellenistic sherds inscribed to Agamemnon. Hero cult at Mycenae begins as the epics take fixed form.

Sparta claimed him too. Clytemnestra and Helen were Tyndareus's daughters; Euripides (IA 1148–1156) gives Clytemnestra a first husband, Tantalus son of Thyestes, whom Agamemnon killed with her infant. Pausanias (3.19.6) saw a tomb of Agamemnon and a shrine of Cassandra at Amyclae; Sparta fetched Orestes' bones from Tegea (Herodotus 1.67–68); Gelon mocked Spartan pride with "Agamemnon son of Pelops would groan" (Hdt. 7.159); Lycophron (Alexandra 1369) attests a Spartan Zeus Agamemnon. Argos and Sparta fought over the Pelopid inheritance as title to Peloponnesian hegemony, and the lyric poets who moved the murder to Amyclae took Sparta's side.

Then Schliemann. In 1876, following Pausanias literally, he dug inside the Lion Gate and found Grave Circle A: five shaft graves (a sixth by Stamatakis in 1877) with gold masks, diadems, inlaid daggers, and the mask from Grave V. The telegram line "I have gazed upon the face of Agamemnon" is probably apocryphal; the identification was his. It cannot stand. The shaft graves date to c. 1600–1450 BC, three to four centuries before any Trojan War date (Eratosthenes' 1184/3; Troy VIIa's fall c. 1180; Troy VI c. 1300). The Lion Gate and the wall extension that enclosed the old graves belong to c. 1250, the right century but anonymous: no Linear B tablet from Mycenae names a king. The "Treasury of Atreus" and "Tomb of Clytemnestra" are tholoi of c. 1250 and 1220, labelled by modern guides. Pausanias himself saw the Lion Gate (2.16.5); it was never lost. Schliemann found the graves of a dynasty the Mycenaeans venerated enough to wall in; its names are gone.

Where sources disagree. David Traill (Schliemann of Troy, 1995), with William Calder before him, questioned the mask's authenticity on its upturned moustache and Schliemann's record of fabrication at Troy; most specialists (Demakopoulou, Dickinson) accept it as genuine, while agreeing it is not Agamemnon's. The Hittite "king of Ahhiyawa" of the Tawagalawa letter (c. 1250) was treated as a Great King but is unnamed; Forrer's equation of Attarissiya with Atreus is generally rejected.

10What does a king owe to those he commands?

The Iliad states the obligation in the mouth of a lie: the false Dream tells Agamemnon that "a man of counsel, to whom peoples are entrusted and so many cares, ought not sleep all night" (2.24–25). Nestor supplies the content: the sceptre brings themistes, so the king must "speak and listen, and carry out what another says for the good" (9.100–102). Diomedes adds that honour without courage is empty; Achilles, that a king who eats the people's winnings devours them. Assembled from its critics, the poem's answer is that a king owes counsel, restraint, a fair division of time, and the capacity to listen; he holds the sceptre on a consent he cannot compel.

Tragedy answers what the epic poses. Aeschylus takes the one act of command Homer never mentions, a child for a fleet, and makes it the unpaid debt collected in the bath; the cycle ends only when judgment leaves the house for the city's court in the Eumenides. Euripides pushes further: in the IA the king owes the army so much that nothing is left for his daughter, and the army, not Zeus, now holds the sceptre. Homer's Agamemnon could not make Achilles fight; Euripides' cannot make himself stop. Between them lies the Greek discovery that authority is a relation, not a possession, and that a sceptre handed down from Zeus is still a stick in the hand of a man who does not know what he owes.

11Self-test

  1. Trace the sceptre's genealogy in Iliad 2.100–108 and say what is conspicuously absent from it.
    Answer

    Hephaestus to Zeus to Hermes to Pelops to Atreus to Thyestes to Agamemnon. Absent is any trace of the Pelopid feud: Atreus and Thyestes hand on peacefully, with no golden lamb, cannibal banquet or curse.

  2. What is the relationship between Homeric anax and basileus, and how do the Linear B tablets illuminate it?
    Answer

    In Homer anax is the grander title (gods, Agamemnon), basileus the ordinary word for any chieftain. In Linear B the wanax is the palace ruler whose temenos is three times that of the lawagetas, the qa-si-re-u a minor local official. Homer preserves a Mycenaean hierarchy in fossil form.

  3. How many times does Agamemnon propose abandoning the war, and which proposal is insincere?
    Answer

    Three: Books 2, 9 and 14. Only the first, the Diapeira, is a test; the others are sincere and are rebuked by Diomedes and Odysseus respectively.

  4. What clause does Odysseus omit when conveying Agamemnon's offer to Achilles in Book 9, and why does it matter?
    Answer

    "Let him yield to me, since I am more kingly and older" (9.160–161). Agamemnon cannot separate compensation from submission, and Odysseus knows the demand would wreck the embassy.

  5. Which cities does Agamemnon lead in the Catalogue of Ships, and who holds Argos?
    Answer

    Mycenae, Corinth, Cleonae, Sicyon and the Achaean coast to Helice and Aegion, with 100 ships. Argos and Tiryns belong to Diomedes (80 ships), contradicting "all Argos" at 2.108.

  6. How does the Cypria explain Artemis's anger at Aulis, and how does Aeschylus differ?
    Answer

    In the Cypria Agamemnon shoots a deer and boasts he surpasses Artemis; she substitutes a deer and saves Iphigenia. Aeschylus gives the eagles-and-hare omen, leaves the motive obscure, and removes the rescue: the girl dies and the choice is Agamemnon's.

  7. In Homer, who kills Agamemnon, and how does Aeschylus change it?
    Answer

    In every Odyssey account (1.35–43; 3.254–312; 4.512–537; 11.385–464) Aegisthus kills him with Clytemnestra's complicity. Aeschylus has Clytemnestra kill him herself, in the bath, with sword and robe, citing Iphigenia.

  8. What advice does Agamemnon's shade give Odysseus in Odyssey 11, and how does Odysseus act on it?
    Answer

    Not to trust his wife fully and to land in secret (11.441–456). Odysseus lands in secret and does not trust his house as Agamemnon did; Agamemnon is the negative template for the second half of the poem.

  9. Why can the "mask of Agamemnon" not be Agamemnon's?
    Answer

    It comes from the Grave Circle A shaft graves, c. 1600–1450 BC, three to four centuries before any plausible Trojan War date (c. 1300–1180). The Lion Gate (c. 1250) is the right century, but no Linear B tablet from Mycenae names a king.

  10. Name two ways Sparta laid claim to Agamemnon.
    Answer

    Any two of: Clytemnestra and Helen as Tyndareus's daughters; the tomb of Agamemnon and shrine of Cassandra at Amyclae (Pausanias 3.19.6), where Stesichorus, Simonides and Pindar set the murder; Orestes' bones fetched from Tegea (Hdt. 1.67–68); the cult of Zeus Agamemnon.

12Carry forward

  1. Homeric kingship is a relation of consent, not a possession: the sceptre comes from Zeus, but the Iliad shows it cannot compel a peer, and the obligation it carries is counsel, restraint and a fair division of honour.
  2. Each age rewrites Agamemnon to answer its own question: Homer's anxious paramount chief becomes Aeschylus's debtor to his house and Euripides' politician captive to the army, and the Iphigenia story is the hinge on which that rewriting turns.
  3. Mycenae's archaeology confirms a thirteenth-century palace-state ruled by a wanax that venerated older royal graves, but attaches no name; Agamemnon is a figure of cult and poetry, claimed by Argos and Sparta alike, never of record.

The prompt this answers

Give me an in-depth report on Agamemnon and the nature of kingship in the Trojan tradition. Cover: the sceptre of Agamemnon in Iliad 2 and its genealogy from Zeus through Pelops; what anax andron means and how it relates to the Mycenaean wanax of the Linear B tablets; the limits of his authority (the quarrel in Book 1, the Diapeira in Book 2, Thersites, the Embassy, the Night Council, the reconciliation in Book 19) and whether the Iliad is a critique of kingship; the Catalogue of Ships as a political map and the hundred ships of Mycenae; his character: the Iliad's Agamemnon versus the tyrant of Aeschylus and Euripides; the sacrifice of Iphigenia in the Cypria, Aeschylus and both Euripidean plays and how each poet changes the moral weight; Chryseis, Briseis and Cassandra; his return, the purple carpet scene, the murder; Agamemnon in Odyssey 11 and 24 as foil to Odysseus; his cult at Mycenae and the hero shrine, and Clytemnestra's Spartan-Argive connections; the archaeology: Schliemann's mask, Grave Circle A, the Lion Gate, and why none of it is Agamemnon's. End with the question the Iliad poses and tragedy answers: what does a king owe to those he commands? Format: about 2500 words, headed sections, name sources, flag disagreements, end with a 10-question self-test with answers and the three ideas I must carry forward.
