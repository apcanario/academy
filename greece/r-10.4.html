01A thief's grandson: ancestry, name and scar

Homer gives Odysseus a respectable father and a disreputable grandfather. Laertes is a minor Ithacan king who by the Odyssey's end digs his orchard in a patched tunic (Odyssey 24.226–231). Anticleia, who died of longing for her son (11.202–203), is the daughter of Autolycus, who surpassed all men in thievery and the oath, a gift from Hermes (19.394–398). Deceptive swearing is presented as a craft with a divine patron, and it sits in the bloodline.

The scar tells the inheritance. When Eurycleia recognises it, the narrative pauses for some seventy lines (19.392–466). Autolycus named the baby: having been angered by (or having angered) many, he called him Odysseus, punning on odussomai, "to be wrathful, to hate" (19.407–409). Whether the name means the man who gives pain or the man who suffers it is ambiguous, and the poem plays on both (1.62, 5.340). On a visit to Parnassus the youth was gashed by a boar: the scar marks initiation into his mother's family of tricksters, and it is also what breaks his disguise.

Later writers made the inheritance worse. Autolycus stole the cattle of Sisyphus, who had marked their hooves; Sisyphus then lay with Anticleia before her marriage, and Odysseus was his son (Hyginus, Fabulae 201). Tragedy uses this as an insult: "the Sisyphid" (Sophocles, Ajax 189), "son of Sisyphus" (Philoctetes 417, 1311; Euripides, Iphigenia at Aulis 524, Cyclops 104).

Where sources disagree. Homer never hints at Sisyphus; Laertes is the father without qualification. The earliest surviving attestation is Aeschylus (fr. 175 Radt, from the Judgement of the Arms), so the story is at least as old as the early fifth century. W. B. Stanford (The Ulysses Theme, 1954) treated it as part of the post-Homeric hostile tradition, a bad father invented for a bad son once Autolycus' trickery was already in the epic; Timothy Gantz (Early Greek Myth, 1993) notes that nothing in the Cycle as summarised by Proclus has it, so a pre-Homeric folk version cannot be shown. The question stays open because the evidence between Homer and Aeschylus is lost.

02The reluctant volunteer and the death of Palamedes

The Cypria, summarised by Proclus, records that Odysseus feigned madness to avoid the expedition and that the Greeks, on Palamedes' advice, seized the infant Telemachus to expose him. Later handbooks supply the scene: Odysseus yokes ill-matched animals and sows salt; Palamedes puts Telemachus before the plough (Hyginus, Fabulae 95) or draws a sword over him (Apollodorus, Epitome 3.7). The trickster is caught by another trickster, through his family.

His revenge is the first lasting stain on his name. Pausanias (10.31.2) says the Cypria had Odysseus and Diomedes drown Palamedes while he was fishing. The later standard version (Apollodorus, Epitome 3.8; Hyginus, Fabulae 105) has Odysseus forge a letter from Priam, bury gold in Palamedes' tent, and let the army stone him as a traitor. All three tragedians wrote a Palamedes (all lost; Euripides' was staged in 415 with Trojan Women), Gorgias wrote a Defence of Palamedes, and Plato's Socrates looks forward to meeting Palamedes and Ajax in Hades, men killed by unjust judgement (Apology 41b). The pattern of the hostile tradition is set: his victims are the honest and inventive, destroyed through rigged procedure.

Where sources disagree. Homer never mentions Palamedes. Ancient critics said Homer suppressed him out of partiality for Odysseus (Philostratus, Heroicus 25.13); most modern scholars think the inventor-hero is a later elaboration who grew because he made the perfect foil.

03The Iliad's Odysseus

In the Iliad he is not yet a wanderer but an institutional man, often the poem's instrument of order.

The sceptre and Thersites

When Agamemnon's test of the army in Book 2 sends the men running for the ships, Athena dispatches Odysseus. He takes Agamemnon's ancestral sceptre (2.185–187): kings he persuades, commoners he strikes, for "the rule of many is not good; let there be one lord" (2.204). When Thersites repeats Achilles' charge that the war is Agamemnon's private profit, Odysseus beats him with the sceptre and the army laughs (2.211–277). Antiquity read this as royal correction; modern readers see class politics. Odysseus here enforces hierarchy, and democratic Athens remembered who held the stick. Antenor's portrait (3.216–224) adds the orator: he stood stiff, eyes down, like a fool, until words came "like winter snowflakes".

The Embassy (Book 9)

Odysseus repeats Agamemnon's gift-list almost verbatim (9.260–299) but quietly drops the demand that Achilles submit to the "more kingly" man (9.158–161): tactful editing, and the selective truth that defines him. Achilles answers that hateful as the gates of Hades is the man who hides one thing in his heart and says another (9.312–313). The line need not target Odysseus, but ancient readers took it so, and Plato's Hippias Minor rests on the contrast of truthful Achilles and polytropos Odysseus.

Where sources disagree. The embassy is described with dual verbs (9.182–198) though Phoenix, Ajax and Odysseus go. Explanations range from an older two-man version with Phoenix inserted (Page, History and the Homeric Iliad, 1959) to a loose archaic use of the dual (Hainsworth, The Iliad: A Commentary vol. 3, 1993); Nagy (The Best of the Achaeans, 1979) thinks the duals pointedly exclude Odysseus. There is no consensus.

The Doloneia (Book 10)

Odysseus and Diomedes capture the spy Dolon, extract information under an ambiguous promise of safety, and Diomedes kills him as he pleads; they then kill the sleeping Thracian king Rhesus and take his horses. It is the Iliad's only night raid and its only victory by stealth and near bad faith. A scholion on 10.1 reports that Homer composed it separately and Peisistratus inserted it; its style and loose fit persuade many modern scholars it is a later addition, though some defend its deliberate difference. Either way, it is the Iliadic seed of the Cyclic Odysseus: the night operative paired with Diomedes.

The funeral games

In Book 23 he wrestles Telamonian Ajax to a draw, cunning against bulk (23.700–739), and wins the footrace when Athena, answering his prayer, makes the lesser Ajax slip in dung (23.740–783). Both contests foreshadow the judgement of the arms. Small unresolved hints elsewhere complicate him: in Book 8 he does not respond to Diomedes' call to stand (8.92–98, the verb ambiguous between "did not hear" and "did not heed"); in Book 11, isolated, he reasons himself into holding his ground (11.401–410).

04The Cyclic Odysseus: arms, Palladium, horse, child

The lost Epic Cycle, known chiefly through Proclus' summaries, supplies the episodes that later made him suspect.

The Palladium and the horse are the two exploits the Cycle gave him outright. In the Little Iliad he and Diomedes steal the Palladium from Troy by night, in some versions by a sewer or over the wall, with Helen's or Theano's help; the Roman tradition later made Diomedes the thief and Odysseus the one who tried to kill him for the credit (the "Diomedean compulsion" below). The horse is Epeius' craft and Odysseus' command: he chooses the men, keeps them silent in the belly while Helen imitates their wives' voices, and stops Anticlus' mouth with his hands (Odyssey 4.271–289; 8.492–520, where Demodocus sings it at his request).

The arms defined him for the classical world. In the underworld Ajax's shade refuses to speak to him (Odyssey 11.543–567). A scholion on Aristophanes' Knights 1056 preserves a Little Iliad version in which Greek spies overhear Trojan girls debating who was braver, one arguing, with Athena's prompting, for Odysseus. The prize is settled by talk, by the wrong jury, against the man who fought best. A later story explaining the proverb "Diomedean compulsion" (Conon, Narrations 34; Suda s.v.) has Odysseus try to kill Diomedes from behind on the way back with the Palladium, to keep the credit.

Where sources disagree. Proclus' Iliou Persis has Odysseus kill Astyanax; a Little Iliad fragment (via Tzetzes) has Neoptolemus hurl him from the walls; in Euripides' Trojan Women the army decrees the death on Odysseus' motion (721–725). In every version his name attaches to the worst act of the sack, as killer or as the man whose argument made it policy.

05Metis and the man of many turns

The Odyssey's first line names him only polytropos: "much-turned" (driven off course) or "of many turns" (versatile, wily). A scholion on 1.1, deriving from Porphyry, reports Antisthenes' defence: he knows many modes (tropoi) of speech for many kinds of hearer, a mark of wisdom. Plato's Hippias reads it the other way: Homer made Achilles true and simple, Odysseus polytropos and false (Hippias Minor 365b). The only other early use, of Hermes (Homeric Hymn to Hermes 13, 439), points towards cleverness and the grandfather's god. Emily Wilson's "complicated" (2017) refuses to choose.

His epithets polymētis, polymēchanos and polytlas name the cluster. Metis is the intelligence of the hunter, sailor and politician: disguise, delay, trap and turn. Detienne and Vernant (Les ruses de l'intelligence, 1974) showed that Greeks prized it but uneasily, as the weapon of the weaker party and the opposite of straight force and plain speech. Hesiod's Zeus swallows the goddess Metis to keep her power inside himself (Theogony 886–900); her daughter Athena embodies it. Hence Odyssey 13.287–310: having heard his lies, Athena smiles, strokes him, and says she is best among gods for metis as he is among mortals. The poem's patron goddess endorses the trickster as kin. The ambiguity is structural: the intelligence behind the "Nobody" trick is the one that forges the letter that kills Palamedes.

06The Odyssey's lies, the slaughter, and death from the sea

The Cretan tales

On Ithaca he tells five false life stories: to the disguised Athena (13.256–286), to Eumaeus (14.192–359), to Antinous (17.415–444) and to Penelope (19.165–202), three of them Cretan (to Antinous he tells only of Egypt and Cyprus); and to Laertes (24.244–279), as Eperitus of Alybas. He "spoke many lies like the truth" (19.203), echoing Hesiod's Muses (Theogony 27). The tales are disguised autobiography (raider, wanderer, survivor of companions lost through folly); they test hospitality and loyalty; and they remind the audience that Books 9–12 are also his own unverifiable account. The poem never condemns them. The test of a lie here is whom it serves.

The ethics of the slaughter

The killing of the suitors is framed as justice from Zeus' opening speech on Aegisthus (1.32–43): men suffer beyond their share through their own recklessness, after warnings. But the cost shows. Leodes, the seer who disapproved of the suitors, begs and is beheaded (22.310–329), while Phemius and Medon are spared. Telemachus hangs the twelve disloyal slave women, denying them the sword-death his father ordered (22.457–473); Melanthius is mutilated (22.474–477). In Book 24 the suitors' kin take up arms, and only Athena and Zeus' decree of oblivion (24.482–486) prevent civil war. The poem admits the slaughter created a feud only gods could cancel.

Where sources disagree. The scholia on 23.296 report that Aristophanes of Byzantium and Aristarchus called that line the "end" (telos or peras) of the Odyssey; whether they meant its original ending or its fulfilment is disputed, and with it the status of Book 24. On the maids, older translations call them "sluts"; Wilson calls them slaves, and Atwood's The Penelopiad (2005) gives them the voice.

Death from the sea: the Telegony

Teiresias foretells an oar carried inland until mistaken for a winnowing fan, a sacrifice to Poseidon, then a gentle death ex halos in sleek old age (11.119–137). The phrase means "away from the sea" or "out of the sea". Eugammon of Cyrene's Telegony (known from Proclus) took the second sense: Odysseus marries Callidice of Thesprotia, returns, and is killed unknowingly by Telegonus, his son by Circe, with a spear tipped with a stingray's spine. Telegonus then marries Penelope and Telemachus marries Circe. Aeschylus' Psychagogoi apparently had a heron drop the spine on him; Sophocles wrote a lost Odysseus Akanthoplex.

Where sources disagree. Most scholars read Teiresias as promising a peaceful end away from the sea and treat the Telegony's grotesque irony as a later twist. The live question is whether the Odyssey poet knew a Thesprotian sequel: Alfred Heubeck (in Heubeck and Hoekstra, A Commentary on Homer's Odyssey II, 1989, on 11.134–137) holds that the prophecy's vagueness is deliberate and presupposes no sequel, while Malcolm Davies (The Greek Epic Cycle, 1989) allows that Eugammon worked up older Thesprotian material the Odyssey already alludes to (14.315–335).

07The fifth-century prosecution

Pindar announces the turn. In Nemean 7 (20–27) he suspects Odysseus' fame exceeds his sufferings through sweet-voiced Homer, whose lies have majesty; if the crowd could see the truth, Ajax would not have fallen on his sword. In Nemean 8 (23–34) the Danaans favoured Odysseus in "secret votes", and hateful deceit (parphasis) existed even then. For a poet of aristocratic victors, Odysseus is the man talk and ballots unjustly preferred.

Sophocles

In Ajax (probably 440s) Odysseus is the most humane figure on stage: shown his maddened enemy by a gloating Athena, he pities him, seeing that all men are shadows (121–126), and he wins Ajax burial from Agamemnon (1332–1373). In Philoctetes (409) he coaches Neoptolemus to deceive the marooned archer, asking for a brief shameless part of a day (83–85), since in life the tongue, not deeds, rules (96–99). Neoptolemus' nature rebels; only Heracles ex machina resolves the play.

Where sources disagree. Cedric Whitman (Sophocles, 1951) saw the Philoctetes Odysseus as a sophist and political operator, the play's villain; C. M. Bowra (Sophoclean Tragedy, 1944) stressed that he serves the gods' declared will and the common good, so the play indicts his methods, not his aims. The same split runs through Ajax 1332–1373: Bernard Knox ("The Ajax of Sophocles", 1961) reads Odysseus' plea for burial as the play's one act of humane reason, Whitman as calculation that keeps the army whole. The admirable Odysseus of Ajax shows that the fifth century did not speak with one voice.

Euripides

In Hecuba (c. 424) he persuades the army to sacrifice Polyxena. Hecuba recalls that he once entered Troy as a spy, was recognised, and begged her for his life (239–250), and denounces the ungrateful breed who court the mob (254–257); the chorus calls him a sweet-talking people-pleaser (131–133). In Trojan Women (415) Hecuba, allotted to him, laments serving a treacherous man with a double tongue (281–288), and he proposes Astyanax's death. Only the satyr play Cyclops keeps something of the Homeric hero.

Why Athens found him uncomfortable

Three pressures converged. Rhetoric: a democracy run by persuasion, with sophists selling it, saw in him speech detached from truth; Thucydides' Cleon calls the Athenians spectators of speeches (3.38). Voting: the judgement of the arms looked like a ballot, and its victim Ajax was a Salaminian hero who gave his name to the tribe Aiantis. War: Hecuba and Trojan Women were staged during the Peloponnesian War, the latter in the year after Melos, when expediency was beating pity in real assemblies. W. B. Stanford's The Ulysses Theme (1954) is still the classic account, though it perhaps overstates the uniformity.

08Philosophers and Romans

Plato. In the Myth of Er (Republic 10.620c–d) Odysseus' soul draws the last lot, remembers its toils, is cured of ambition, and searches out the neglected life of a private man who minds his own business, saying it would have chosen the same had it drawn first. The man of many turns chooses the life with none.

Cynics and Stoics. Antisthenes' paired speeches of Ajax and Odysseus survive: Odysseus argues he endured disguise, beggary and beating for the common good while Ajax fought only by day for glory. The patient, self-sufficient beggar became a Cynic model. Stoics allegorised him into the sage: Cicero reads the Sirens as the lure of knowledge (De Finibus 5.49); Seneca asks not where Ulysses wandered but how we may stop wandering (Epistles 88.7); Epictetus holds up his wanderings as the model of the man at home anywhere under providence, while refusing him his tears for Penelope (Discourses 3.24.13–20). Porphyry's On the Cave of the Nymphs makes his homecoming the soul's return.

Rome. Claiming Trojan descent, Rome sharpened the hostile figure. Virgil's Sinon episode makes him scelerum inventor, "deviser of crimes" (Aeneid 2.164), and dirus Ulixes (2.261); Laocoon asks "is this how you know Ulysses?" (2.44); in Book 3 he has abandoned Achaemenides to the Cyclops. Seneca's Troades gives him the hunt for Astyanax (the Andromache act, 524–813). Horace offers both faces: the "useful exemplar" of virtue and wisdom who resisted Circe and the Sirens (Epistles 1.2.17–26), and the legacy-hunter of the parody Satires 2.5. Ovid stages the contest of arms (Metamorphoses 13.1–398) and lets eloquence win (13.382–383). This Latin Ulysses, not Homer's, reached medieval Europe.

09The modern voyage: Dante to Kazantzakis

Dante, without Greek, knew him from Virgil, Ovid, Statius and Cicero. In Inferno 26 he burns in a twin flame with Diomedes among the fraudulent counsellors, for the horse, the deception of Achilles at Scyros and the Palladium. Yet the flame tells an invented last voyage: no love of son, father or Penelope could overcome his desire for experience; he urges his old crew past the Pillars of Heracles, "you were not made to live like brutes, but to pursue virtue and knowledge" (26.119–120), and after their "mad flight" (26.125) they sight Purgatory before a whirlwind sinks them. Romantic readers heard heroic aspiration; theological readers stress transgression and the eloquence that killed those it inspired. Dante leaves the tension live.

Tennyson's "Ulysses" (written 1833 after Arthur Hallam's death, published 1842) follows Dante: the old king leaves "an aged wife" and the prudent Telemachus, "to strive, to seek, to find, and not to yield". Victorians heard heroism; many modern readers hear abandonment of duty. Joyce's Ulysses (1922) makes him Leopold Bloom on 16 June 1904; Joyce told Frank Budgen that Ulysses was literature's only "complete" man, son, father, husband, lover, and a soldier who tried to dodge the war. Bloom's metis is decency; there is no slaughter. Kazantzakis' The Odyssey: A Modern Sequel (1938), 33,333 lines, sends him out again to found and lose an ideal city in Africa and die on Antarctic ice, a Nietzschean hero of pure striving. Horkheimer and Adorno (Dialectic of Enlightenment, 1944) read him bound to the mast as the prototype of instrumental reason, surviving by cunning and self-denial.

10Is Odysseus admirable?

Each era's answer reveals the answerer. Homer admires him because homecoming is the highest good in a dangerous world and metis is how the weaker survive; Athena's smile is the verdict. Pindar and the Athenian stage distrust him because they feared that persuasion could defeat merit in a vote and expediency silence pity; the Odysseus of Hecuba is a portrait of a politician. The philosophers keep his endurance and self-knowledge and strip the tricks. Rome hated the Greek deceiver and praised the Stoic wanderer in one generation. Dante condemns his fraud and is transfixed by his curiosity; the nineteenth century made him an explorer, the twentieth an ordinary man or the alienated rational self.

The constant is that Odysseus is a hero of means rather than ends. Achilles and Ajax are judged by the values they embody; Odysseus is judged by what one thinks intelligence is for. Where the purpose is trusted, home, city, the gods' will, his cunning is virtue. Where cleverness is suspected of serving only itself, he becomes the liar, the demagogue and the murderer of Palamedes. To ask whether he is admirable is to ask whether a society trusts its own cleverness.

11Self-test

  1. What does the Odyssey say Autolycus excelled in, and who gave him the gift?
    Answer

    Thievery and the oath (deceptive swearing), a gift from Hermes (Odyssey 19.394–398).

  2. How does the Cypria explain Palamedes' death, and how does it differ from the later standard version?
    Answer

    According to Pausanias (10.31.2), the Cypria had Odysseus and Diomedes drown him while fishing; the later version (Apollodorus, Epitome 3.8; Hyginus) has Odysseus forge a letter from Priam and plant gold, so the army stones Palamedes as a traitor.

  3. What does Odysseus leave out when he repeats Agamemnon's offer to Achilles in Iliad 9?
    Answer

    Agamemnon's demand that Achilles yield and submit to him as the more kingly man (9.158–161).

  4. What ancient claim casts doubt on the authenticity of Iliad 10?
    Answer

    A scholion on 10.1 says Homer composed the Doloneia separately and Peisistratus inserted it; modern arguments from style and loose connection point the same way, though some defend it.

  5. In the Little Iliad version preserved by a scholion on Aristophanes' Knights, who decides the judgement of the arms?
    Answer

    Trojan girls, overheard by Greek spies; one of the girls argues, with Athena's prompting, for Odysseus over Ajax.

  6. How did Antisthenes defend the epithet polytropos?
    Answer

    As versatility in modes of speech, adapting discourse to different hearers, a mark of wisdom rather than deceit (scholion on Odyssey 1.1).

  7. What does Athena say about herself and Odysseus after he lies to her in Odyssey 13?
    Answer

    That she is best among the gods for metis as he is among mortals; she delights in his cunning as kin to her own.

  8. Why do Pindar's and Euripides' hostilities to Odysseus converge on the same two things?
    Answer

    Both fasten on the vote and the tongue. Pindar (Nemean 7.20–27, 8.23–34) makes him the man whose fame exceeded his sufferings because Homer's sweet speech and the Danaans' secret ballots favoured talk over merit, at Ajax's cost; Euripides (Hecuba, Trojan Women) makes him the demagogue whose persuasion wins assemblies and silences pity. For an aristocratic poet and a democratic dramatist alike, the danger is the same: cleverness that works through speech and procedure rather than deeds.

  9. What life does Odysseus' soul choose in the Myth of Er?
    Answer

    Drawing the last lot and cured of ambition, he chooses the neglected life of a private man who minds his own business, saying he would have chosen it even with the first lot (Republic 10.620c–d).

  10. For what sins is Dante's Ulysses punished, and what does his final speech urge?
    Answer

    Fraudulent counsel: the wooden horse, the deception of Achilles at Scyros, and the theft of the Palladium; he urges his men not to live like brutes but to pursue virtue and knowledge (Inferno 26.119–120).

12Carry forward

  1. Odysseus is the hero of metis, a morally neutral intelligence: every era's verdict on him is really a verdict on whether cleverness can be trusted without a fixed loyalty to truth.
  2. The negative Odysseus comes mostly from outside Homer, from the Cycle (Palamedes, the arms, Astyanax) and from fifth-century Athens, where he became the image of the demagogue and sophist in a city ruled by persuasion.
  3. Later Europe inherited a Latin Ulysses (Virgil's deceiver, Horace's sage, Ovid's orator), and Dante's invented last voyage, not Homer's homecoming, became the template for the modern restless Ulysses.

The prompt this answers

Give me an in-depth report on Odysseus as a figure across the whole tradition, assuming I know the Odyssey's plot. Cover: his ancestry (Laertes, Anticleia, the thief-grandfather Autolycus, and the Sisyphus slander) and the scar; the feigned madness and Palamedes' exposure of it, then Palamedes' framing and death as the first stain on his reputation; the Iliad's Odysseus (Book 2 with the sceptre and Thersites, the Embassy in Book 9, the Doloneia in Book 10 and its problems, the funeral games); the Little Iliad and Iliou Persis: the arms, the Palladium, the horse, the killing of Astyanax in some versions; metis as his defining virtue and its ambiguity (polytropos, polymetis, the man of many turns); the Odyssey's handling of his lies, the Cretan tales, and the ethics of the suitors' slaughter; his death from the sea in the Telegony; the fifth-century turn against him: Pindar's suspicion, Sophocles' Ajax and Philoctetes, Euripides' Hecuba and Trojan Women, and why democratic Athens found him uncomfortable; Plato's Odysseus choosing a quiet life in the Myth of Er; the Stoic and Cynic Odysseus as sage; Ulysses in Rome (Horace, Ovid, the Ulysses of Virgil's Book 2); Dante's Ulisse, Tennyson, Joyce, Kazantzakis. End with the question of whether Odysseus is admirable and what each era's answer says about it. Format: about 2500 words, headed sections, name sources, flag disagreements, end with a 10-question self-test with answers and the three ideas I must carry forward.
