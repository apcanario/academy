What "myth" meant to a Greek, and why there was never one version

In Homer mythos means simply "utterance": a hero's mythos is what he says in assembly. Only in the fifth and fourth centuries BC does it shade towards "an unverifiable tale" set against logos, reasoned account. Pindar warns that stories "embroidered with intricate lies" deceive (Olympian 1.28–29); Thucydides disowns to mythodes, "the storylike element" (1.21–22); Plato uses mythos for fables he invents. "Mythology" as a separate domain is partly the product of the Greeks' own critical turn and partly a modern construction.

The structural fact behind everything else is that Greek religion had no scripture, no priestly college with authority over narrative, and no sanctuary whose version outranked the others. Stories lived in performance: epic recited at festivals, choral lyric commissioned by a city or an athlete, tragedy staged in competition, cult aetiology told by guides at a shrine, images painted on pots. Each telling was authorised by its occasion, not by a canon, and poets corrected one another openly. Hesiod's Muses know how "to tell many lies like true things" (Theogony 27–28). Stesichorus' Palinode retracted the story that Helen ever went to Troy and substituted a phantom (quoted at Plato, Phaedrus 243a); Euripides built his Helen (412 BC) on it. Pindar refuses the version in which Tantalus served Pelops to the gods, calling it the invention of envious neighbours (Olympian 1.36–53). Herodotus credits Homer and Hesiod with having "made the theogony for the Greeks" (2.53), but that prestige was never exclusive: Pausanias finds Thebans, Arcadians, Cretans and Argives each claiming the birthplace or tomb of the same god, and reports them side by side. Variation was the normal condition of Greek myth, not a corruption of it.

Where sources disagree. Paul Veyne (Did the Greeks Believe in Their Myths?, 1983) argued that Greeks held several "programmes of truth" at once; Marcel Detienne (The Creation of Mythology, 1981) treated "mythology" as a category Greeks invented when they began to distrust old tales. Robert Fowler and others reply that a working mythos/logos distinction is already visible in Herodotus and Thucydides. How far "myth" is a Greek or a modern category remains open.

The archaic poets: Homer, Hesiod, the Hymns, the Epic Cycle

Homer. The Iliad and Odyssey are conventionally placed around 750–700 BC. They are not primers of mythology; they assume an audience that already knows the stories and allude to much they do not tell: the Judgement of Paris (Iliad 24.25–30), Heracles' labours (8.362–369), the Argonauts (Odyssey 12.69–72), Oedipus (11.271–280). Their settled Olympian family became the default across the Greek world. The text we read descends from the Alexandrian editors, above all Aristarchus of Samothrace (second century BC), whose judgements survive in the scholia.

Where sources disagree. Ancient tradition (Cicero, On the Orator 3.137) credited the Athenian tyrant Pisistratus with a written recension of Homer; many modern scholars treat the "Pisistratean recension" as Hellenistic back-projection. On date and authorship there is no consensus: M. L. West placed the Iliad around 680–640 BC and gave the Odyssey to a different poet; Gregory Nagy's evolutionary model denies that a single moment of composition is recoverable. Any precise date for "Homer" is a convention.

Hesiod. The Theogony (c. 700 BC) is the earliest surviving systematic account of the gods' origins: the castration of Uranus (154–210), Cronus swallowing his children and Zeus's birth on Crete (453–506), Prometheus and the first woman (507–616), the Titanomachy. The Works and Days gives the Ages of Man (109–201) and the fuller Pandora story (42–105). The Catalogue of Women, a genealogical epic in Hesiod's name but probably sixth-century, survives only in papyrus fragments yet fed later mythographers, Apollodorus included. Hesiod is a poet with an agenda, Zeus's just order, and where he and Homer differ (Aphrodite born from sea-foam at Theogony 188–206, daughter of Zeus and Dione at Iliad 5.370) neither is "right".

The Homeric Hymns. Thirty-three hexameter hymns under Homer's name, composed by various poets between the seventh and fifth centuries BC. The four long ones matter most: Demeter (Persephone and the founding of Eleusis), Apollo (Delos and Delphi; Thucydides 3.104 quotes it as Homer's), Hermes (the cattle-theft and the lyre) and Aphrodite (Anchises). They are the earliest full narratives of several gods' cult origins.

The Epic Cycle. Beyond Homer stood lost hexameter epics that told the rest of the Trojan story: the Cypria, Aethiopis, Little Iliad, Sack of Ilion, Returns and Telegony, attributed to names such as Stasinus, Arctinus and Lesches, plus a Theban group (Oedipodea, Thebaid, Epigoni) and a Titanomachy. Their plots are known almost entirely from prose summaries by one Proclus in a Chrestomathy, preserved in Photius and in the Venetus A manuscript of the Iliad, plus fragments collected in West's Greek Epic Fragments (2003). Much that Apollodorus and the vase painters show, Troilus, the wooden horse, Ajax and Cassandra, came from here, not from Homer.

Where sources disagree. Whether this Proclus is the fifth-century AD Neoplatonist or an unknown grammarian of the second century is unresolved. Scholars also divide over whether the Cyclic poems are post-Homeric fillers (Aristotle's and West's view) or preserve traditions older than the Iliad that Homer suppressed, the "neoanalyst" position associated with Wolfgang Kullmann.

Lyric and tragedy: myth reshaped for the occasion

The lyric poets used myth as argument and ornament. Sappho (fr. 16) sets Helen's choice beside her own desire; Stesichorus composed narrative poems of near-epic scale (Geryoneis, Oresteia); Bacchylides 17 gives Theseus' dive to Poseidon's palace. Pindar (518–c. 438 BC) is the richest and most self-conscious reviser: because an epinician praises a victor and his city, he selects, trims and rejects versions on moral grounds (Olympian 1; Olympian 9.35–41). His scholia preserve variants he declined to use.

Tragedy is where fifth-century Athens did most of its mythmaking. Aeschylus (c. 525–456), Sophocles (c. 496–406) and Euripides (c. 480–406) wrote well over two hundred plays; thirty-two survive. Aristotle observed that the poet must keep the received outline (Clytemnestra dies at Orestes' hands) but is free in everything else (Poetics 1453b22–26). The freedom was large. Oedipus dies at Colonus in Sophocles but lingers blind inside Thebes in Euripides' Phoenician Women; Electra is married to a peasant in Euripides and unmarried in Sophocles; Aeschylus' Eumenides (458 BC) moves Orestes' acquittal to the Athenian Areopagus, almost certainly an Athenian innovation. Each play is a dated political and theatrical document first and a "source" for myth second.

Where sources disagree. In Euripides' Medea (431 BC) Medea kills her children. Pausanias (2.3.6–11), citing the Corinthian epic poet Eumelus, reports older versions in which the Corinthians killed them or they died when Medea tried to make them immortal; a scholion on Medea 264 even claims the Corinthians bribed Euripides. Most scholars take the infanticide as Euripides' innovation or his elevation of a minor variant; a minority point to the near-contemporary Medea of Neophron.

The historians: Herodotus and Thucydides

Herodotus (c. 484–c. 425 BC) opens the Histories with a Persian chain of abductions, Io, Europa, Medea, Helen, and declines to adjudicate it (1.1–5). His stance is explicit: "I am obliged to say what is said, but not obliged to believe it" (7.152). He dates Homer and Hesiod four hundred years before his own time (2.53), places Heracles seventeen thousand years before Amasis on Egyptian priestly reckoning (2.43–45), reports an Egyptian account in which Helen never reached Troy (2.113–120), and traces Leonidas' descent to Heracles (7.204). He is a source for how fifth-century Greeks reasoned about myth and for many local legends, never a neutral mythographer.

Thucydides (c. 460–c. 400 BC) wanted none of this, yet his "Archaeology" (1.2–19) treats Minos, Hellen and Agamemnon's expedition as real if exaggerated events, inferring the small scale of the Trojan war from the Catalogue of Ships (1.9–11) and warning that poets "adorn and exaggerate" (1.21). He is the first to treat Homer as evidence to be weighed rather than authority to be cited. Both historians stand where Greek thought began separating what we call myth from history, without drawing the line where a modern reader would.

Compilers and transmitters: Apollodorus, Pausanias, Plutarch, scholia, Ovid, Hyginus

Apollodorus' Library. The one surviving systematic handbook, running from the Theogony through the heroic genealogies to Odysseus' death. Books 1–3 are complete; the end is known from an Epitome found in 1885–87. It is organised by heroic family and routinely names its sources ("Hesiod says… Acusilaus says… Pherecydes…"), which makes it precious for lost archaic material. It lists variants rather than ranking them (three accounts of Cronus' children at 1.1.5–7).

Where sources disagree. Photius and the manuscripts name the author Apollodorus, and the work was long given to Apollodorus of Athens (second century BC). But it cites Castor of Rhodes, active in the first century BC, so it cannot be his. Most scholars date it to the first or second century AD and call the author "Pseudo-Apollodorus". Its material is often ancient; its text is not.

Pausanias. The Description of Greece (ten books, c. AD 150–180) surveys the monuments and cults of the mainland, recording what local guides said at each shrine. He is the principal source for regional variants that Panhellenic poetry ignored, and for lost art: the Chest of Cypselus at Olympia with its labelled scenes (5.17–19), Polygnotus' paintings at Delphi (10.25–31). He admits he began thinking Greek legends foolish and ended convinced the ancients spoke in riddles (8.8.3); he is not a sceptic.

Plutarch (c. AD 45–120) opens his Theseus admitting he has crossed into territory "where there is nothing beyond but prodigies and fables" (Theseus 1). The Lives and the Moralia (Greek Questions, On Isis and Osiris) preserve antiquarian, ritual and local lore, filtered through a moralising Platonist who served as a priest at Delphi.

Scholia and mythographers. Marginal commentaries in manuscripts of Homer, Pindar, Euripides, Apollonius and Lycophron preserve Alexandrian learning; the A-scholia of the Venetus A (tenth century) go back to Aristarchus, the D-scholia carry mythological "stories" explaining Homeric allusions. Beneath the poets ran prose genealogists, Acusilaus, Pherecydes of Athens, Hellanicus (collected in Fowler, Early Greek Mythography, 2000–2013), and later handbooks: Palaephatus' rationalising On Unbelievable Things, Diodorus Siculus books 4–5, Parthenius, Antoninus Liberalis' Metamorphoses, the Catasterisms under Eratosthenes' name. Late, derivative and indispensable, because they often name the archaic author they abridge.

Ovid. The Metamorphoses (c. AD 8) is the most influential single book of classical myth in later Europe, and it is a Latin poem for an Augustan audience by a poet who prized wit and novelty. Echo paired with Narcissus (3.339–510), Arachne (6.1–145), Pygmalion (10.243–297) and Medusa raped by Poseidon in Athena's temple and punished with serpent hair (4.790–803) are Ovidian or recast beyond recognition; Hesiod had said only that Poseidon lay with Medusa in a soft meadow (Theogony 278–279). Ovid drew on lost Hellenistic poets (Nicander, Boios), so he sometimes preserves Greek material, but you cannot tell which without independent Greek evidence.

Hyginus. The Latin Fabulae, some 277 short entries, transmits plots of lost tragedies found nowhere else. It survives through a single printed edition of 1535 made from a manuscript since destroyed, so its text is corrupt. Servius, Lactantius Placidus, Fulgentius and the Vatican Mythographers carried a Romanised, moralised mythology into the Middle Ages.

Where sources disagree. Manuscripts ascribe the Fabulae to C. Julius Hyginus, Augustus' librarian. The Latinity and the summarising of a Greek handbook point instead to a compilation of the second century AD or later; whether any Augustan core survives is uncertain.

Material sources

Vase painting. Tens of thousands of mythical images from the seventh to the fourth centuries BC, many earlier than any surviving text of the same story. The Sophilos dinos (c. 580 BC) and the François Vase by Kleitias (c. 570 BC) show labelled scenes of the wedding of Peleus and Thetis, the ambush of Troilus and the return of Hephaestus, fixing stories otherwise known only from late summaries. Exekias' amphora of Achilles and Ajax dicing (c. 530 BC) depicts an episode attested in no surviving text at all. Painters followed their own conventions; images are witnesses, not illustrations.

Sculpture. Temple sculpture put myth in civic form: the Gigantomachy of the Siphnian Treasury at Delphi (c. 525 BC), the pediments of the Temple of Zeus at Olympia (c. 460 BC), the Parthenon metopes (447–432 BC), the Great Altar of Pergamon (c. 170 BC). The choice of story is evidence of what a city wished to say about itself.

Inscriptions. The Parian Marble (264/3 BC) counts years back through Deucalion's flood to the fall of Troy, placed in 1209/8 BC. The Lindian Chronicle (99 BC) lists dedications at Lindos from Cadmus, Minos, Heracles and Helen onward. Attic deme calendars (Erchia, Thorikos) record offerings to heroes; decrees invoke mythical kinship between cities.

Linear B. The Mycenaean script, deciphered by Michael Ventris in 1952, records palace administration at Knossos (c. 1400–1370 BC), Pylos (c. 1200 BC), Thebes and Mycenae. No narrative, but offerings to di-we (Zeus), e-ra (Hera), po-se-da-o (Poseidon), e-ma-a2 (Hermes), a-re (Ares), pa-ja-wo (Paean) and di-wo-nu-so (Dionysus) show the core Olympian names at least five centuries older than Homer. Apollo and Aphrodite are not securely attested.

Where sources disagree. The Pylos tablets naming Dionysus overturned the older view, drawn from Herodotus 2.49 and Euripides' Bacchae, that he was a late arrival from Thrace or Phrygia. Some still ask whether di-wo-nu-so is the god rather than a personal name, though the Khania tablet pairing him with Zeus in a cult context has strengthened the case.

Archaeology. Schliemann at Hisarlik (from 1870) and Mycenae (1876), then Blegen and Korfmann at Troy, together with Hittite texts naming Wilusa, its king Alaksandu (c. 1280 BC) and a power called Ahhiyawa, show a real political landscape in which a Trojan conflict could have occurred; Troy VI (c. 1300 BC) and VIIa (c. 1180 BC) are the usual candidates. Eighth-century tomb cult at Mycenaean graves shows Greeks of Homer's era venerating a past they already saw as heroic. Archaeology confirms a setting; it does not confirm a story.

How a scholar decides between variants

There is no "original" to recover, so the first move is to replace "which is true?" with "who told this, where, when and to what end?"

  1. Date the attestation, not the story. Timothy Gantz's Early Greek Myth (1993) arranges every episode by its earliest witness, literary or pictorial. A version first found in Apollodorus may be archaic or Hellenistic; without earlier confirmation you cannot say.
  2. Weigh genre. Epinician praises, tragedy needs catastrophe, aetiology explains a ritual, a vase sells. Each pressure bends the story predictably.
  3. Distinguish local from Panhellenic. Pausanias, deme calendars and the local historians in Jacoby's Fragmente der griechischen Historiker show what Homer and Hesiod flattened.
  4. Follow the chain of transmission. When Apollodorus says "Pherecydes says", a fifth-century source is in view; when Hyginus summarises a tragedy, you read Sophocles at third hand.
  5. Use images as independent witnesses. A sixth-century vase can prove a detail "first found" in a Roman author is archaic.
  6. Treat Roman material as Roman until proven otherwise.
  7. Use comparative and structural approaches with care. Indo-European parallels (West, 2007), Near Eastern borrowings (Hittite Kumarbi behind Hesiod's Cronus), the structuralism of Vernant and Detienne and Burkert's ritual readings illuminate patterns; none decides which variant is older.

The main traps

Treating Ovid as Greek. The Metamorphoses is the fount of most popular retellings; it is also Latin, Augustan and deliberately original. Roman names (Jupiter, Minerva, Ulysses) are the surface symptom; the deeper problem is that Ovid's shaping of character and motive is his own.

Treating Apollodorus as early. Because the Library is orderly and complete it is tempting to use it as "the" Greek myth. It is a Roman-period digest, contemporary with Plutarch and Pausanias and eight centuries after Hesiod. Cite it as a lens on the lost sources it names, not as a primary witness.

Treating Homer as history. Schliemann's gold does not make the Iliad a chronicle. The poems see a Bronze Age world through an Iron Age lens, boar's-tusk helmets beside iron tools. Hittite evidence makes a conflict at Troy plausible; it does not supply Achilles. The reverse error, dismissing the epics as pure fiction, ignores the real Mycenaean names in Linear B and the real geography the poets knew.

Smaller traps. Taking a scholion's "the story is" for a consensus rather than one scholar's note; forgetting that a tragedy survives because Byzantine schoolmasters chose it; and assuming that a dictionary entry blending Hesiod, Euripides, Apollodorus and Ovid into one smooth narrative represents anything any Greek ever believed.

Self-test

  1. What did mythos mean in Homer, and when did it begin to acquire its modern sense?
    Answer

    Simply "speech" or "utterance". The contrast with logos, as "unverifiable tale", emerges in the fifth and fourth centuries BC in Pindar, Thucydides and Plato.

  2. Why was there no canonical version of any Greek myth?
    Answer

    No scripture, no priestly authority over narrative, no single dominant sanctuary; myth lived in performance (epic, lyric, tragedy, cult, art), and each telling was authorised by its occasion.

  3. Name two major mythical episodes that Homer alludes to but does not narrate.
    Answer

    Any two of: the Judgement of Paris (Iliad 24), Heracles' labours (Iliad 8), the Argonauts (Odyssey 12), Oedipus (Odyssey 11).

  4. How do we know the plots of the lost Epic Cycle?
    Answer

    Chiefly from prose summaries by Proclus in his Chrestomathy, preserved via Photius and the Venetus A manuscript, plus quoted fragments.

  5. Give an example of an archaic poet openly rejecting a mythical variant.
    Answer

    Pindar, Olympian 1, refusing the story that Tantalus served Pelops to the gods; or Stesichorus' Palinode denying that Helen went to Troy.

  6. Why can the Library not be by Apollodorus of Athens?
    Answer

    It cites Castor of Rhodes, active in the first century BC, after Apollodorus of Athens (second century BC). It is now dated to the first or second century AD.

  7. What makes Pausanias uniquely valuable among the compilers?
    Answer

    He records local cult versions told at the shrines themselves and describes lost monuments such as the Chest of Cypselus, preserving regional variants Panhellenic poetry ignored.

  8. Which Olympian's presence in Linear B overturned an older scholarly consensus, and why?
    Answer

    Dionysus (di-wo-nu-so, Pylos and Khania). Herodotus and Euripides had implied he was a late import from Thrace or Phrygia; the tablets show his name in Greece c. 1200 BC.

  9. What does the Exekias amphora of Achilles and Ajax dicing teach about images as sources?
    Answer

    It shows an episode attested in no surviving text, proving vase painting is an independent witness to myth rather than an illustration of literature.

  10. Which detail of the Medusa story is Ovidian rather than Greek, and what is the Greek evidence?
    Answer

    Her rape by Poseidon in Athena's temple and punishment with snaky hair (Metamorphoses 4.790–803). Hesiod, Theogony 278–279, says only that Poseidon lay with her in a meadow.

Carry forward

  1. Greek myth had no canon: every version belongs to an author, a genre, a place and a date, and the question is never "which is true?" but "who told this, where and why?".
  2. Date the witness, not the story. A late handbook can preserve archaic material and an early vase can prove a detail older than any text, but neither can be assumed without checking.
  3. Keep the Roman layer visible. Ovid and his Latin successors shaped the mythology Europe inherited; anything known only from them is Roman until a Greek source confirms it.
