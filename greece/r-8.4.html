The Homeric psyche and the shadow-world

In Homer the psyche is not a soul in the later sense but the breath-life that leaves the body through mouth or wound and flies to Hades "bewailing its fate" (Iliad 16.856, 22.362). There it is an eidolon, an image keeping the dead person's shape but without strength or, usually, mind. Achilles cannot embrace the ghost of Patroclus; it slips through his arms "like smoke" (Iliad 23.99–101). The living person was a compound of body and several life-forces (thymos, menos, noos); what survives is the least of them.

Odyssey 11, the Nekyia, is the fullest picture. Odysseus does not descend; at the world's edge he digs a pit and lets sheep's blood run into it, and the shades must drink before they can speak. Teiresias alone keeps his wits by a gift of Persephone (11.90–96). Anticleia explains that once the fire has consumed flesh and bone the psyche "flutters away like a dream" (11.218–222). Achilles delivers the famous verdict that he would rather be a hired labourer on earth than king over all the dead (11.488–491). There is no judgement of ordinary lives; Tityus, Tantalus and Sisyphus (11.576–600) are mythical offenders against the gods, and Minos "giving judgements among the dead" settles disputes rather than assigning fates. Elysium is reserved for a few favoured heroes (Odyssey 4.561–569; Hesiod, Works and Days 167–173).

Where sources disagree. Scholars since the Alexandrians have treated the catalogue of heroines (225–332) and the vision of Minos, the great sinners and Heracles (565–627) as interpolated, since they sit oddly with the pit-side framing where shades come to Odysseus. Others defend the book's unity. The point matters because the "sinners" passage is the main evidence for any Homeric posthumous punishment.

Funeral rites from Homer to Solon

The ritual sequence barely changed in a thousand years, from the funerals of Patroclus and Hector (Iliad 23–24) to Lucian.

Prothesis

Women of the family washed, anointed and dressed the body and laid it on a bier inside the house, feet towards the door; an obol for Charon, unknown to Homer, appears from the fifth century. Funerary plaques and loutrophoroi show the standard tableau: women around the bier, hands to heads, men approaching with right arm raised in salute.

Ekphora

Before dawn the body was carried in procession to the cemetery outside the walls. Solon's legislation (Plutarch, Solon 21; Demosthenes 43.62) required the ekphora before sunrise, limited the women attending to close kin over sixty, forbade laceration of the flesh and set lamentation for persons outside the family, and capped grave goods at three garments and a modest offering of food and drink. Similar laws are attested at Delphi (the Labyad inscription), Ioulis on Keos (IG XII 5.593) and Gambreion. Their aim is debated: curbing aristocratic display, or disciplining the ritual power of women.

Cremation and inhumation

Homer knows only cremation; the pyre of Patroclus with its slaughtered horses, dogs and twelve Trojans (Iliad 23.166–177) exaggerates Iron-Age practice. Archaeologically the two rites alternate and coexist at Athens from the Protogeometric period to the Classical cemetery, with no demonstrable doctrinal difference; the choice was family tradition, cost and fashion. Children were almost always inhumed, often in jars.

Grave goods and the lekythos

Geometric graves held weapons and the great Dipylon amphorae as markers; after Solon and under the democracy goods shrink to pottery, strigils and mirrors. The characteristic fifth-century vessel is the white-ground lekythos, an oil flask painted with the tomb visit, the prothesis, Charon's boat or Hermes conducting the psyche. Many have a false inner cup so that a little oil appeared to fill the vase, a telling detail about the economy of grief.

Mourning, women and the politics of lament

Lament was women's work and women's power. The goos was the improvised wail of kin, the threnos a composed song, sometimes by hired professionals. Andromache, Hecuba and Helen each lead a lament over Hector (Iliad 24.723–776) in antiphony with the crowd. Tearing the hair and scratching the cheeks until they bled are the gestures the law tried to forbid. Offerings at the tomb on the third, ninth and thirtieth days and at the annual Genesia were also women's responsibility; the lekythoi show them bringing ribbons and cakes to the stele.

Tragedy stages this power and its danger: Antigone, Electra at Agamemnon's tomb in the Choephoroi, the Trojan women. Modern scholarship (Alexiou, Holst-Warhaft, Loraux) reads the sumptuary laws and the public funeral as a transfer of death from household to state: the funeral oration, delivered by a man over anonymous bones, replaces the women's goos over an individual body.

The Kerameikos and the grave stelai

The Kerameikos, the potters' quarter outside the Dipylon and Sacred Gates, was Athens' principal cemetery from the Submycenaean period. Along the road to the Academy lay the demosion sema, where each year's war dead were laid in common tombs after the funeral described by Thucydides (2.34); the Marathon dead were exceptionally buried on the battlefield.

Archaic Attica produced tall stelai crowned with sphinxes or palmettes and carved with the standing youth or girl, with name, patronymic and sometimes an epigram ("stand and mourn at the tomb of dead Kroisos"). Stelai almost vanish between about 500 and 430 and return as the family-group relief: Hegeso with her maid, Dexileos spearing a fallen enemy (dated by inscription to 394/3), Ampharete with her grandchild. They show the dead and living together, the handshake (dexiosis) disputed as farewell or reunion, and almost nothing of Hades. In 317 Demetrius of Phalerum restricted monuments to a small column or slab, and the series ends.

Where sources disagree. The gap in Attic grave reliefs has been attributed to a sumptuary law "some time after Solon" (Cicero, De legibus 2.64), to a shift of patronage towards public monuments, or to accidents of survival. No Athenian text records such a law; its date and existence are uncertain.

The evolution of afterlife belief

From Homer to the mysteries

Homer's grey democracy of shades was one view among several. The Homeric Hymn to Demeter (480–482) already declares the uninitiated to have "no share of such good when dead"; Eleusis promised a better lot below for ritual purity, not moral conduct. Aristophanes' initiates in the Frogs (440–459) dance in sunlit meadows while the impious lie in mud, a pattern repeated in Plato and the gold leaves.

The Orphic-Bacchic gold tablets, from Hipponion (c. 400 BC) to Thessaly and Crete, instruct the dead: avoid the first spring, drink from the lake of Memory, say "I am the child of Earth and starry Heaven"; one from Pelinna announces "Tell Persephone that Bacchios himself freed you". Here the soul is divine, the body a tomb (soma sema, Plato, Cratylus 400c), and salvation comes by initiation. Transmigration, attributed to Pythagoras (Xenophanes fr. 7) and Empedocles (fr. 115, 117), belongs to the same current.

Pindar's Second Olympian

Pindar, writing in 476 for Theron of Acragas in Pythagorean Sicily, gives the earliest worked-out moral afterlife in mainstream poetry (Olympian 2.56–80). The dead are judged; the wicked suffer "toil not to be looked upon"; the good live without tears where the sun shines by night; those who thrice in each world have kept their souls from injustice travel the road of Zeus to the tower of Cronus and the Isle of the Blessed, with Peleus, Cadmus and Achilles. Fragment 133 adds rebirth as kings and sages for souls who have paid for "ancient grief". Other odes speak in Homeric terms; Pindar tailors eschatology to patron.

Plato's eschatological myths

Plato ends three dialogues with a judgement myth, each time calling it a mythos whose detail he will not defend while its lesson is "worth the risk" (Phaedo 114d). In the Gorgias (523a–527a) Zeus moves judgement to after death and strips the dead of their bodies so scarred souls are seen naked; the incurable hang in Tartarus as examples. In the Phaedo (107d–115a) each soul is led by its daimon to judgement, the true earth and its four rivers are mapped, and the purified philosopher lives bodiless. In the Republic (614b–621d) Er the Pamphylian revives on the pyre and reports the two openings, the thousand-year journey, the spindle of Necessity, the lottery of lives and the river of Forgetting. The choice of lives, with the former Odysseus picking a private man's life, turns eschatology into ethics.

The Epicurean denial

Epicurus cut the knot: the soul is atoms dispersed at death, and "what is dissolved has no sensation, and what has no sensation is nothing to us" (Principal Doctrines 2; Letter to Menoeceus 124–127). Fear of death is the chief disturbance of life. Lucretius' third book reads the Acheron of myth as the torment of the living: Tantalus is the man paralysed by fear, Sisyphus the office-seeker. The epitaph non fui, fui, non sum, non curo is found across the Empire.

Where sources disagree. How widespread were such beliefs? Rohde and Dodds saw a "Puritan" counter-religion of the soul from the sixth century; Burkert and Parker stress that the gold leaves are a few dozen among hundreds of thousands of graves and that most Athenians held vague, inconsistent expectations. Whether "Orphism" was ever a movement or only a label for texts and rites is contested.

Hero cult

A heros in cult is a dead mortal who retains power to help or harm from the grave. Ordinary dead receive nomima, the customary offerings; a hero receives priests, festivals and sacrifice, and his power is local, tied to the bones. The phenomenon appears archaeologically in the eighth century, when offerings begin at Mycenaean tombs (Menidi, Prosymna, Mycenae).

Ritual: enagismata and the heroic feast

The traditional distinction is between thysia to Olympians (throat up, meat shared at a raised altar) and enagismata to heroes and the dead (throat down into a pit or low hearth, blood poured into the earth, carcass burned whole, libations of water, milk, honey and wine). Pausanias 2.10.1 describes Heracles at Sicyon receiving part as hero and part as god; Herodotus 2.44 says the same of Thasos. Banquet reliefs show the hero reclining, cup in hand, with snake, horse and armour.

Where sources disagree. Gunnel Ekroth (2002) concluded that most Archaic and Classical hero sacrifice was ordinary thysia with dining, and enagismata the exception. The sharp Olympian/chthonian dichotomy inherited from Rohde and Nilsson is now widely regarded as overdrawn.

Who became a hero

Founders (oikistai) were buried in the agora of their colonies with annual rites, as Battus at Cyrene (Pindar, Pythian 5.93–95). Lawgivers and poets: Lycurgus had a shrine at Sparta (Herodotus 1.66), Archilochus a heroon on Paros, Sophocles cult as Dexion. Athletes: Cleomedes of Astypalaea vanished in a chest after killing an opponent and Delphi called him "the last of the heroes" (Pausanias 6.9.6–8); the statue of Theagenes of Thasos was tried for murder, drowned, recovered and given healing cult (Pausanias 6.11.2–9). The common factor is extraordinary power, not goodness; the violent and the murdered became heroes as readily as the virtuous.

The war dead

The Athenian war dead received annual games (Lysias 2.80 calls them "immortal honours"), and the Marathon fallen had cult as heroes into the second century AD (Pausanias 1.32.4, who adds that horses and fighting are heard there by night). Pericles' "most conspicuous grave, not that in which they lie" (Thucydides 2.43) substitutes civic memory for chthonian power.

Brasidas at Amphipolis

Thucydides 5.11 is the clearest text on heroisation in historical time. After Brasidas died defending Amphipolis in 422, the citizens buried him inside the city before the agora, "enclosed his tomb, and sacrifice to him as a hero, with games and yearly offerings". They demolished the buildings of Hagnon, the Athenian founder, and transferred his honours to Brasidas as "saviour". Heroisation is a political act: a new founder replaces the old, and the tomb sits inside the walls against the normal exclusion of the dead.

Bones as state relics

Herodotus 1.67–68: Sparta, failing against Tegea, was told by Delphi to fetch the bones of Orestes from "where two winds blow"; Lichas found a seven-cubit coffin in a smith's yard, and after the transfer Sparta prevailed. Size is the point: heroes were larger than men, and fossil or Bronze-Age bones were read accordingly. Cimon brought Theseus' bones from Scyros in 476/5 to the Theseion, an asylum for fugitive slaves (Plutarch, Theseus 36; Cimon 8); Tisamenus went from Helice to Sparta (Pausanias 7.1.8). The hero protects where his bones lie, so possessing them is possessing him.

Hero cult and the heroes of myth

The relation of cult heroes to epic heroes is the central problem. Rohde and Farnell saw faded gods or ancestor worship; Coldstream argued the eighth-century tomb cults were prompted by the spread of epic; Snodgrass and Antonaccio decoupled them, reading tomb cult as land claims by new communities that only later acquired epic names. Homer himself barely knows hero cult (Erechtheus, Iliad 2.550–551, is the sole clear case). By the Classical period the categories interpenetrate: Ajax is a tribal eponym at Athens, Achilles has cult at Troy and on the Black Sea, the Seven against Thebes at Eleusis, and Delphi chose the ten Cleisthenic tribal heroes from a hundred candidates (Aristotle, Constitution of Athens 21.6).

Ghosts and the restless dead

The unburied (ataphoi), the violently killed (biaiothanatoi), the untimely dead (aoroi) and the unmarried were held to wander, dangerous or useful. Patroclus demands burial so he may pass the gates of Hades (Iliad 23.71–74); Elpenor is the first shade Odysseus meets because unburied (Odyssey 11.51–80). Clytaemnestra's ghost rouses the Furies (Aeschylus, Eumenides 94–139); Darius is raised by libation (Persians 619–842); Polydorus opens Euripides' Hecuba. Pausanias 6.6.7–11 tells of the Hero of Temesa, a stoned sailor of Odysseus whose ghost demanded a maiden yearly until the boxer Euthymus expelled him.

Lead curse tablets, pierced with nails and deposited in graves of the untimely dead from the fifth century on, use them as couriers to Hermes and Persephone. Oracles of the dead at Ephyra, Tainaron, Heraclea Pontica and Avernus are attested by Herodotus 5.92, where Periander's wife Melissa complains of cold because her clothes were not burned with her. Psychagogoi ("soul-leaders", the title of an Aeschylus play) and the Attic Anthesteria, which ended with "Out of doors, Keres, the Anthesteria is over", managed the annual return. Plato, Phaedo 81c–d, rationalises ghosts as souls still weighted with body, flitting about tombs.

Where sources disagree. The Anthesteria formula survives in late lexica with the variant "Kares" (Carian slaves) for "Keres" (spirits); whether the festival originally involved the dead is uncertain. Johnston's picture of rising Classical anxiety about the restless dead, against an unanxious Homer, is questioned by those who note how little ghost-lore Athenian prose preserves.

The Roman-period shift to individual immortality

Under the Empire the centre of gravity moves to the salvation of the individual soul. Platonism (Plutarch, On the Delays of Divine Vengeance 563b–568a; Plotinus) makes astral immortality the educated default. Isis promises Lucius worship after death in the Elysian fields (Apuleius, Metamorphoses 11.6); Dionysiac sarcophagi carry the god's triumph; Mithras and Christianity compete. Epitaphs assert "I did not die but went to the gods", place the soul in the ether and the body in earth, and in Boeotia and Thessaly heros becomes an honorific for any dead man. Sarcophagi show Endymion, Alcestis and Persephone, myths of transit rather than judgement; Herodes Atticus heroised Regilla and his pupils, Hadrian deified Antinous.

The old apparatus persisted. Eleusis ran until Alaric; Pausanias describes hero cult as living practice; Lucian's On Mourning satirises the same prothesis, wailing and obol that Solon regulated. But the questions Plato asked in myth became ones a cult or philosophy had to answer plainly, and when Christianity made personal judgement and resurrection the common creed it built on four centuries of Greek expectation that death was a passage of the self, not the dissolution of a compound into breath, bone and shadow.

Where sources disagree. MacMullen and others warn that the "spiritualisation" of Roman epitaphs is a trend among the literate and wealthy; Epicurean formulae persist alongside astral ones, and whether belief or fashion changed cannot be settled from the stones alone.

Self-test

  1. In what state does Homer depict the psyche after death, and how does Teiresias differ from the other shades in Odyssey 11?
    Answer

    It is a witless image (eidolon) without strength or memory, needing to drink sacrificial blood to speak; Teiresias alone keeps his mind intact by Persephone's gift (11.90–96).

  2. Name the two stages of the Greek funeral before burial and say what Solon's laws regulated about each.
    Answer

    Prothesis (laying-out in the house) and ekphora (procession to the grave). Solon restricted the women attending to close kin over sixty, required the ekphora before sunrise, forbade self-laceration and lament for non-kin, and limited grave goods (Plutarch, Solon 21; Demosthenes 43.62).

  3. What is a white-ground lekythos and what tells us about its function?
    Answer

    A fifth-century Attic oil flask painted with funerary scenes (tomb visits, Charon, Hermes) and deposited at graves; many have a false inner cup so a small amount of oil appears to fill it.

  4. What was the demosion sema, and which Athenian dead were the exception to burial there?
    Answer

    The public cemetery along the road from the Kerameikos to the Academy where each year's war dead were buried collectively after the state funeral (Thucydides 2.34). The Marathon dead were buried on the battlefield.

  5. What does Pindar's Second Olympian say happens to souls who have thrice kept themselves from injustice?
    Answer

    They travel the road of Zeus to the tower of Cronus on the Isle of the Blessed, where Peleus, Cadmus and Achilles dwell (Olympian 2.68–80).

  6. In which dialogue does Zeus move judgement to after death so that souls are judged naked, and why?
    Answer

    The Gorgias (523a–524a): living judges were deceived by fine bodies, clothes and witnesses, so Zeus had the dead judged stripped of their bodies.

  7. State the Epicurean argument that death is nothing to us.
    Answer

    The soul is atomic and dispersed at death; what is dissolved has no sensation; what has no sensation is nothing to us (Principal Doctrines 2; Letter to Menoeceus 124–127).

  8. What did the Amphipolitans do after Brasidas died in 422, according to Thucydides 5.11?
    Answer

    Buried him inside the city in front of the agora, enclosed the tomb, sacrificed to him as a hero with games and annual offerings, and transferred to him the founder's honours of the Athenian Hagnon, whose buildings they demolished.

  9. Why did Sparta bring the bones of Orestes from Tegea, and how was his tomb identified?
    Answer

    Delphi told them they would defeat Tegea once they possessed the bones. Lichas identified the spot as a smith's yard ("where two winds blow", the bellows) and found a seven-cubit coffin (Herodotus 1.67–68).

  10. Which categories of dead were believed most likely to become restless ghosts?
    Answer

    The unburied (ataphoi), the violently killed (biaiothanatoi), the untimely dead (aoroi) and the unmarried; curse tablets were deposited in their graves to use them as couriers.

Carry forward

  1. Greek afterlife belief was plural and unsystematic: Homer's witless shades, the initiates' meadow, Pindaric and Platonic judgement and Epicurean dissolution all coexisted, and the same person could hold several at once. Later modules on tragedy, philosophy and early Christianity presuppose this plurality.
  2. Hero cult is worship of a powerful dead person tied to the physical location of the bones; it is a political instrument (founders, bone-transfers, Brasidas) and it is the hinge between the myths of the heroic age and the historical cities that claimed them.
  3. Death ritual was a contest over who controls the dead: the household and its women against the city and its men, visible in Solon's laws, the public funeral and tragedy. Keep this frame when reading Antigone and the funeral oration.
