The empire Darius inherited

In 336 the Achaemenid king ruled from the Aegean to the Indus. Power ran through about twenty-five satrapies, each under a satrap who was usually a Persian noble, often a royal kinsman. Garrison commanders and treasurers reported separately to the court as a check on him. Herodotus' list of twenty tribute districts (3.89–97), totalling 14,560 Euboean talents of silver, is schematic, but the Persepolis Fortification and Treasury tablets show tribute and rations being accounted for with great care. The empire was not living on credit. By the ancient figures, Alexander took about 50,000 talents at Susa (Arrian 3.16.7) and 120,000 at Persepolis (Diodorus 17.71.1).

The Royal Road from Sardis to Susa, with 111 staging posts (Herodotus 5.52–54), was one artery of a larger network. The tablets record travellers drawing rations on sealed authorisations as far as Arachosia and India, and relay couriers carried royal orders day and night (Herodotus 8.98). The "King's Eye" was probably a real office of royal inspection that Greek imagination turned into a secret police. Herodotus has the boy Cyrus appoint one in a game (1.114), Aristophanes mocks one on stage (Acharnians 91–125), and Xenophon expands it into a web of the king's "eyes and ears" (Cyropaedia 8.2.10–12).

Herodotus' 10,000 Immortals (7.83) had their successor in Alexander's day in the thousand melophoroi, the "apple-bearers" with golden apples on their spear-butts, who stood beside the king (Heracleides of Cyme in Athenaeus 12.514b; Arrian 3.11.5). Around them came Bactrian and Saka horsemen, Median and Armenian cavalry, scythed chariots and levies of uneven quality. Persia's chronic weakness was heavy infantry, so it hired Greeks. Arrian puts about 30,000 Greek mercenaries in Darius' centre at Issus (2.8.6).

Bagoas, the succession and the problem of Darius

The empire of 336 had just been restored, not run down. Artaxerxes III Ochus (r. 359/8–338) burned rebel Sidon in 345, retook Egypt in 343/2 after some sixty years of independence (Diodorus 16.40–51), and ordered the satraps of Asia Minor to disband their private mercenary armies. His generals in Egypt were the eunuch Bagoas and Mentor of Rhodes, Memnon's brother.

According to Diodorus (17.5.3–6), Bagoas, by then chiliarch, poisoned Artaxerxes III in 338, crowned his son Arses (Artaxerxes IV), poisoned him in 336, and raised Darius from a collateral branch of the royal house. When Bagoas tried to poison the new king, Darius made him drink the cup himself. Darius therefore came to the throne almost at the same moment Philip II was murdered, with a Macedonian advance force already in Asia.

Where sources disagree. One Babylonian tablet says only that Artaxerxes III "went to his fate", a formula that does not imply murder, so the poisoning of 338 is uncertain. Darius' background is contested as well. Plutarch (On the Fortune or Virtue of Alexander 326F) makes him an obscure royal courier (astandes), and Strabo (15.3.24) says he was not of the royal family; both have Bagoas promote him. Diodorus (17.6.1–2) and Justin (10.3), who calls him Codomannus, credit him with killing a Cadusian champion in single combat. Both versions may be propaganda, one hostile and one legitimising.

The Greek Darius is the one in Arrian's obituary (3.22.2–6): "most cowardly and incompetent" in war, a man who fled twice and was betrayed by his own men. Pierre Briant (Darius dans l'ombre d'Alexandre, 2003; English translation 2015) argues that the Alexander historians built this Darius as a foil, the coward who makes the hero's courage visible. Briant sets against it what Darius did. He raised two complete imperial armies in three years, funded a serious naval war, chose good ground at Gaugamela and kept most of his nobility loyal almost to the end. Ernst Badian ("Darius III", Harvard Studies in Classical Philology 100, 2000) is harder on Darius as a commander. The current view is that Darius cannot be known from these sources, not that he was contemptible.

Memnon and the western strategy

When the western satraps met at Zeleia in 334, Memnon of Rhodes advised against battle. The Macedonian infantry was superior, he argued, while Alexander was short of money and supplies, so the Persians should burn the crops and carry the war to Macedon by sea. Arsites refused, saying he would not allow a single house of his subjects to burn (Arrian 1.12.9–10; Diodorus 17.18.2–3). Greek writers saw Persian pride in this, but it was also a rational choice for a satrap whose standing rested on protecting his own land.

At the Granicus the Persians lined the bank with cavalry and kept the Greek mercenaries in reserve. The nobles fought around Alexander and several satraps died. The mercenaries were then surrounded and massacred, and about 2,000 survivors were sent to Macedonia in chains (Arrian 1.16.2–6). Memnon held Halicarnassus through 334 and then took his own plan to sea. Darius gave him supreme command and funds; Memnon had already sent his family to court as guarantees (Diodorus 17.23.5). In 333 he took Chios and most of Lesbos and besieged Mytilene, but died of illness there (Arrian 2.1.1–3). Diodorus (17.29.4) says his death ended Darius' hopes in the west. Pharnabazus and Autophradates carried on and gave Agis III of Sparta money and ships (Arrian 2.13.4–6), but Darius now chose to fight on land and in person.

The Persian decisions at Issus and Gaugamela

Issus, November 333

The Athenian exile Charidemus advised Darius to send a smaller army under a Greek general and was executed for speaking too freely, a scene that may be a Greek moral fable (Diodorus 17.30; Curtius 3.2.10–19). The deserter Amyntas told Darius to wait on the open plain at Sochoi (Arrian 2.6.3–6). Darius instead crossed the Amanus and came out behind Alexander, cutting his communications. Strategically this was bold. Tactically it was fatal, because the narrow coastal plain at the Pinarus cancelled out his numbers. When the Persian left collapsed, Darius fled.

Where sources disagree. In Arrian (2.11.4; 3.14.3), Darius flees at the first danger in both battles. Diodorus (17.34; 17.60) and Curtius (3.11; 4.15) describe hard fighting around the royal chariot, and at Gaugamela a charioteer struck down and mistaken for the king, which started the panic. Numbers are worthless too. Arrian's million infantry at Gaugamela (3.8.6) is absurd, and Polybius (12.17–22) had already taken apart Callisthenes' account of Issus.

The women in the tent

The camp at Issus yielded Darius' mother Sisygambis, his wife Stateira, two daughters and a small son (Arrian 2.11.9). Alexander left them their royal titles and attendants. In the anecdote Arrian reports without vouching for it, Sisygambis bowed to Hephaestion by mistake and Alexander said he "too is Alexander" (2.12.6–8). Plutarch insists that Alexander never saw Stateira (Alexander 21). She died in captivity, apparently in childbirth, around 332/1 (Plutarch, Alexander 30; Curtius 4.10.18–34), a timing the sources leave unexplored. Keeping the royal household was also a way of taking over part of the king's legitimacy. In 324 Alexander married Darius' daughter Stateira at Susa, and her sister Drypetis married Hephaestion (Arrian 7.4.4–5).

The offers and Parmenion's remark

After Issus, Darius wrote asking for his family and for friendship; Alexander told him to address him as lord of Asia (Arrian 2.14). During the siege of Tyre, Darius offered 10,000 talents, everything west of the Euphrates and a daughter in marriage. Parmenion said he would accept if he were Alexander. Alexander replied that so would he, if he were Parmenion (Arrian 2.25.1–3; Plutarch, Alexander 29.7–8).

Where sources disagree. Diodorus (17.39; 17.54) and Curtius (4.5; 4.11) give three embassies, the last before Gaugamela offering up to 30,000 talents. Plutarch (Alexander 29.7–9) sets the exchange at Tyre in 331, after Egypt; Diodorus (17.54) and Curtius (4.11) move it to the eve of Gaugamela. The escalating series may be a literary device, but the core is plausible: a rational Achaemenid ruler would give up the west to save the Iranian heartland.

Gaugamela and the Babylonian diary

In 331 Darius did what the critics of Issus would have advised. He chose an open plain beyond the Tigris, levelled it for about 200 scythed chariots and brought in the eastern cavalry. Mazaeus, on the Persian right, nearly broke Parmenion, and Persian and Indian horse reached the Macedonian baggage. But the chariots failed, the attempt to outflank Alexander's right opened a gap in the line, and Alexander drove through it toward the king. Darius left, and his army fell apart.

For once there is an independent witness. A Babylonian astronomical diary (Sachs and Hunger, Astronomical Diaries I, year −330) records the lunar eclipse of 20 September, also noted by Arrian (3.7.6) and Plutarch (Alexander 31), and the fear that followed. It dates the battle to 24 Ulūlu, which is 1 October, and says the king's troops deserted him and he fled to the land of the Guti. It calls Alexander "king of the world" and records his message to Babylon:

Into your houses I shall not enter.

The diary confirms the Greek date and the result but says nothing of cowardice. It suggests that the army deserting the king mattered as much as the king's own choice.

Mazaeus withdrew to Babylon, surrendered the city and was made its satrap, the first Persian given a great satrapy by Alexander (Arrian 3.16.4; Curtius 5.1.17–23). Abulites did the same at Susa. The terms were plain: those who came over kept their rank and office.

Persepolis: the palace and the fire

Darius I and Xerxes built Persepolis as a ceremonial centre. The Apadana reliefs show subject peoples bringing gifts in order and harmony. Alexander forced the Persian Gates, entered in early 330, let the lower city be plundered and removed the treasure. Months later he burned the palace.

Arrian (3.18.11–12) presents the fire as policy: revenge for Xerxes' burning of Athens, carried out over Parmenion's objection that Alexander was destroying his own property. Arrian himself thought it unwise. The vulgate (Diodorus 17.72; Plutarch, Alexander 38; Curtius 5.7.2–7) tells of a drunken feast at which the Athenian courtesan Thaïs urged the burning, and torch-carrying revellers followed her up the terrace. Plutarch adds that Alexander quickly regretted it. The Thaïs story probably goes back to Cleitarchus (Athenaeus 13.576d–e).

Where sources disagree. Eugene Borza ("Fire from Heaven: Alexander at Persepolis", Classical Philology 67, 1972) and others read the fire as deliberate. The treasury had been emptied first, and the burning was heaviest in the Apadana, the Hall of a Hundred Columns and the buildings of Xerxes. On this view the fire told the Greeks that the war of revenge was over and told the Iranians that Achaemenid rule had ended. A drinking party could still have been the occasion for a decision already taken. The question is open.

The end of Darius and the eastern resistance

Darius fell back on Ecbatana and then, in summer 330, toward Bactria to raise a third army. Near the Caspian Gates, Bessus, satrap of Bactria and a royal kinsman, together with the chiliarch Nabarzanes and Barsaentes of Arachosia, arrested him. Curtius says he was bound in golden fetters (5.12.20) and that the Greek mercenary captain Patron offered him a bodyguard, which he refused out of trust in his Persians (5.11). When the Macedonian cavalry closed in, in July 330, the conspirators stabbed him and left him in a wagon (Arrian 3.21.10). Plutarch's deathbed message, passed to a Macedonian soldier (Alexander 43), is probably embellishment. Alexander sent the body to Persepolis for royal burial (Arrian 3.22.1); the unfinished tomb south of the terrace is sometimes assigned to Darius, but this is uncertain. By burying the king and pursuing his killer, Alexander made himself Darius' avenger and successor.

Bessus put on the upright tiara and took the name Artaxerxes (Arrian 3.25.3). Among the Aramaic documents from Bactria published by Naveh and Shaked (2012) is one dated to "year 1 of Artaxerxes", which the editors take to be Bessus; if they are right, local administration recognised him as king. In 329 his officers Spitamenes and Dataphernes gave him up. He was mutilated in the Persian manner and executed at Ecbatana (Arrian 4.7.3). Spitamenes then led the Sogdian and Bactrian revolt of 329–328, destroying a Macedonian column at the Polytimetus (Arrian 4.5–6), the hardest fighting of the whole war. The outcome was settled by marriages: Alexander married the Sogdian Roxane, and Spitamenes' daughter Apame married Seleucus (Arrian 7.4.6), so the Seleucid kings descended from the rebel.

Where sources disagree. Arrian (3.30.5) notes that Ptolemy claims to have fetched Bessus himself, while Aristobulus says Spitamenes and Dataphernes handed him over. In Arrian (4.17.7) the Massagetae send Spitamenes' head to Alexander, but Curtius (8.3.1–15) says his wife killed him.

Persian voices and Persian memory

The Babylonian Dynastic Prophecy (Grayson, Babylonian Historical-Literary Texts, 1975) describes past reigns in the form of predictions. One passage appears to show a king, usually identified as Darius III, defeated by the "Hanaeans" (Macedonians), who then re-equips his army and, with divine help, overthrows them.

Where sources disagree. Grayson (1975) and Matthew Neujahr (Journal of Near Eastern Studies 64, 2005) read the reversal as a genuine hope, written down by Babylonians who expected Darius to return. Others, notably R. J. van der Spek (Achaemenid History XIII, 2003), see a text reworked in the age of the Successors and aimed at early Seleucid politics. The tablet is broken at the crucial point, so the question is unresolved.

The Zoroastrian priestly books, written down in Middle Persian in the early Islamic centuries from Sasanian traditions, call him "the accursed Alexander the Roman" (gizistag Aleksandar hrōmāyīg), an epithet otherwise used for the Evil Spirit. In the opening chapter of the Ardā Wirāz Nāmag he kills the priests and burns the Avesta, which had been written in gold on ox hides and kept at Istakhr. The Dēnkard repeats the charge. These texts serve the Sasanian claim to have restored a broken faith, but they may preserve a memory of the Persepolis fire.

The other tradition took him in. Through the Alexander Romance, Alexander became Iskandar. In Ferdowsi's Shāhnāmeh (c. 1010) he is the son of King Darab by a daughter of Filqus (Philip), and so half-brother to Dara. When Dara is murdered by his ministers, Iskandar cradles the dying king, avenges him and marries his daughter Roshanak. In Nizami's Iskandarnāmeh (c. 1200) he becomes a philosopher and sage. Iranian memory made him either destroyer or legitimate heir, never simply a foreign conqueror.

Why the empire fell so fast

The Greek explanation was decadence. Plato (Laws 3.694a–698a) blames heirs raised by women and eunuchs; the last chapter of Xenophon's Cyropaedia (8.8) portrays a Persia gone soft; Isocrates (Panegyricus 150–151) calls Persians servile. Darius' flights fitted the stereotype neatly. The evidence does not. The empire had just retaken Egypt, held huge reserves, kept functioning archives and mobilised Iran twice in three years. Heleen Sancisi-Weerdenburg's question, "decadence in the empire or decadence in the sources?" (Achaemenid History I, 1987), gave rise to a generation of work reading the empire from its own documents.

Better explanations are structural and contingent: a Macedonian army without equal, the loss of Memnon, and an ideology centred on the king, so that his departure from a battlefield could dissolve an army. Above all, Alexander offered the satrapal elite continuity, with the same offices and tribute under a new king. Briant's phrase for him, "the last of the Achaemenids", captures this. Where Persians did refuse, in Persis, Bactria and Sogdiana, the conquest was slow and bloody.

The lesson is that this war is known almost entirely from the winning side. Arrian follows Ptolemy and Aristobulus and the vulgate follows Cleitarchus, all writing for readers who wanted to see a coward lose to a hero. The Persian side survives only in fragments: a diary, a broken prophecy, some Aramaic receipts and the memories of priests and poets centuries later. Wherever the Greek account can be checked, it gets the dates right and the characters wrong.

Self-test

  1. What three sources does the Greek picture of the "King's Eye" mainly rest on, and what was it probably in reality?
    Answer

    Herodotus 1.114 (Cyrus' game), Aristophanes, Acharnians 91–125 (the comic "King's Eye") and Xenophon, Cyropaedia 8.2.10–12 (the king's "eyes and ears"). It was probably a royal inspector reporting directly to the king, enlarged by Greeks into a network of spies.

  2. Which recent achievements of Artaxerxes III undermine the idea that the empire was decadent in 336?
    Answer

    He crushed the Phoenician revolt and burned Sidon (345), reconquered Egypt in 343/2 after some sixty years of independence, (Diodorus 16.40–51), and forced the satraps of Asia Minor to disband their private mercenary armies.

  3. According to Diodorus, how did Darius III come to the throne, and why is the first link in that chain uncertain?
    Answer

    Bagoas poisoned Artaxerxes III and then Arses, raised Darius, and was forced by Darius to drink his own poison (Diodorus 17.5.3–6). A Babylonian tablet says only that Artaxerxes III "went to his fate", which does not necessarily mean murder.

  4. What strategy did Memnon propose at Zeleia, and why did Arsites reject it?
    Answer

    Avoid battle, burn the crops and countryside to starve Alexander's army, and take the war to Macedon by sea. Arsites refused to allow even one house of his subjects to burn (Arrian 1.12.9–10), a choice that reflected both pride and a satrap's duty to protect his own territory.

  5. Why was Darius' march to Issus strategically clever but tactically ruinous?
    Answer

    By crossing the Amanus he came out behind Alexander and cut his communications. But he gave up the open plain at Sochoi that Amyntas had recommended and fought on a narrow coastal plain where his greater numbers and cavalry could not deploy.

  6. How do Arrian and the vulgate differ on Darius' conduct in battle?
    Answer

    Arrian (2.11.4; 3.14.3) has him flee at the first danger. Diodorus (17.34; 17.60) and Curtius describe fierce fighting around the royal chariot and, at Gaugamela, a fallen charioteer mistaken for the king, which set off the rout.

  7. What did Darius offer during the siege of Tyre, and what did Parmenion and Alexander say?
    Answer

    10,000 talents, all land west of the Euphrates and a daughter in marriage. Parmenion said he would accept if he were Alexander; Alexander said so would he, if he were Parmenion (Arrian 2.25.1–3; Plutarch, Alexander 29).

  8. What does the Babylonian astronomical diary add to our knowledge of Gaugamela?
    Answer

    It independently confirms the lunar eclipse of 20 September and dates the battle to 24 Ulūlu (1 October 331). It records that the king's troops deserted him and that he fled to the land of the Guti, calls Alexander "king of the world", and records his promise not to enter Babylonian houses.

  9. Give the two versions of the burning of Persepolis and the archaeological argument for one of them.
    Answer

    Arrian (3.18.11–12) presents it as deliberate revenge for Xerxes' burning of Athens, against Parmenion's advice. The vulgate (Diodorus 17.72; Plutarch, Alexander 38; Curtius 5.7) has the drunken feast and Thaïs. The treasury was emptied first and the burning is concentrated in Xerxes' buildings, the Apadana and the Hundred Columns Hall, which supports a planned act.

  10. How does Iranian tradition treat Alexander in the Zoroastrian books and in the Shāhnāmeh?
    Answer

    The Zoroastrian books (Ardā Wirāz Nāmag, Dēnkard) call him "the accursed Alexander the Roman", who killed priests and burned the Avesta. In Ferdowsi's Shāhnāmeh he is Iskandar, son of Darab and half-brother of Dara, who mourns the murdered king, avenges him and marries his daughter Roshanak, making him a legitimate Iranian successor.

Carry forward

  1. Darius III and the "decadent" Persia of the Greek writers are literary constructs. The empire of 336 had just been restored, was rich and well run, and fought hard. Its fall needs structural explanations (Macedonian military quality, an ideology centred on the king's person, the elite's willingness to switch sides) rather than moral ones.
  2. Alexander conquered by taking over the Achaemenid system: he kept the satrapal offices, reappointed defecting nobles such as Mazaeus, held the royal women, buried and avenged Darius, and married into the dynasty. The Persepolis fire is the exception that needs explaining.
  3. When the only full narrative comes from the victor, the best checks are independent and partial sources (Babylonian diaries, prophecies, Aramaic documents, archaeology) and the defeated side's later memory. These usually confirm the Greek dates and outcomes while undermining the Greek explanations and characters.
