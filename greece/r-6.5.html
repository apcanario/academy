The Sophists

Sophistēs began as a compliment (Herodotus 1.29 uses it of Solon). By the 420s it meant a travelling professional who taught, for a fee, what a citizen needed in assembly and court: argument, speaking, general culture. The Sophists were not a school; they shared a market, not doctrines. Almost nothing they wrote survives whole; we read them through Plato, who made them his foils, and the fragments in Diels and Kranz (DK 80 to 90).

Protagoras can be stated with most confidence. "Man is the measure of all things, of things that are that they are, of things that are not that they are not" (DK 80 B1). "Concerning the gods I am unable to know whether they exist or not" (B4). On every matter there are two opposed logoi (B6a). Plato's Protagoras adds the Great Speech (320c to 328d): justice and shame are given to all humans as the condition of city life, so virtue is teachable as a mother tongue is. He was no revolutionary; he wrote laws for Thurii in 444 (Diogenes Laertius 9.50).

Gorgias came to Athens on an embassy in 427 (Diodorus 12.53) and dazzled it with antithesis. His Encomium of Helen acquits her under every hypothesis and gives the first theory of speech as a drug: logos is "a great potentate, which with the smallest body accomplishes the most divine works" (Helen 8). On Not-Being, known through Sextus, argued that nothing exists, could not be known if it did, and could not be communicated if known. In the Meno (76c) he laughs at the idea he teaches virtue; he sold technique. Prodicus taught fine distinctions between words (Protagoras 337a to c), composed the Choice of Heracles (Xenophon, Memorabilia 2.1.21 to 34), and held that early humans deified sun, rivers, bread and wine, whence Demeter and Dionysus (Sextus 9.18). Hippias compiled the Olympic victor list on which Greek chronology depended, and at Protagoras 337c to d calls "law, the tyrant of men", a force against nature. Antiphon's On Truth (DK 87 B44) says a man best serves himself by honouring law before witnesses and nature when alone; law's demands are adventitious, nature's necessary; Greeks and barbarians are by nature alike. Thrasymachus in Republic 1 calls justice "the advantage of the stronger" (338c) and "another's good" (343c): rulers legislate for themselves and the just obey at their own cost. The historical Thrasymachus was a stylist; his one real fragment (DK 85 B1) defends the ancestral constitution and sounds conservative.

Where sources disagree. Antiphon the Sophist and Antiphon of Rhamnus, the oligarch executed in 411, may be one man or two. Hermogenes separated them on style; Gagarin argues for one, Pendrick for two. If one, the theorist of law's artificiality was also an active anti-democratic politician. Unresolved.

Nomos, physis and fear

The fault line ran between nomos (law, custom) and physis (nature). Herodotus had shown through Darius's Greeks and Callatians (3.38) that customs differ and each people prefers its own; Ionian physics had dissolved the gods into process. If law is agreement and nature everywhere the same, which has authority? Protagoras: nomos makes the city possible and is good. Hippias and Antiphon leaned to nature. Callicles in Plato's Gorgias (482c to 486d) says laws are made by the weak many to tame the strong few. The Sisyphus fragment (DK 88 B25), Critias's or Euripides', has a clever man invent the gods to deter secret wrongdoing. The Dissoi Logoi (DK 90) argues both sides of every moral pair.

Four fears follow. Fees: Anytus in the Meno (91c) wishes no citizen would let a Sophist near his son. Religion: agnosticism in a city whose festivals were its politics. Courts: Aristophanes' Clouds (423) has the Worse Argument beat the Better and a son justify beating his father. Class: the pupils were rich, and the two most associated with Sophistic training were Alcibiades and Critias, traitor and leader of the Thirty. Protagoras himself (Protagoras 316c to 317c) admits the profession attracts envy.

Where sources disagree. Diogenes Laertius (9.54) says Protagoras was tried for impiety and his books burned; Plato's Meno (91e) says he died in high repute. Dover showed the fifth-century intellectual impiety trials rest on late evidence, and most scholars treat this one as fiction modelled on Socrates'. The rehabilitation of the Sophists begins with Hegel and Grote (1850); Kerferd's The Sophistic Movement (1981) is the standard case.

Socrates: sources and method

Socrates (469 to 399) wrote nothing. Aristophanes' Clouds, the only evidence from his lifetime, shows a natural philosopher teaching argument for money; it is comedy. Plato's Socrates says things in the Phaedo and Republic that the Socrates of the Apology disclaims. Xenophon's Memorabilia and Apology show a practical moral adviser hard to imagine being executed. Aristotle, the best external check, says Socrates dealt with ethics not nature, sought universal definitions, and did not separate them from particulars as Plato did (Metaphysics 1.6, 987b1 to 6; 13.4, 1078b17 to 32). Lost are the Socratic writings of Antisthenes and Aeschines, and Polycrates' hostile Accusation (c. 393), which Memorabilia 1.2 answers.

On the method, the elenchus, the witnesses converge. The interlocutor asserts a thesis; Socrates secures agreement to further propositions; these entail the thesis's negation; the interlocutor is left in aporia. Socrates insists he teaches nothing. The Apology gives the origin: Delphi said no one was wiser than Socrates, who set out to refute it by examining politicians, poets and craftsmen and found each thought he knew what he did not (21a to 23b). His wisdom is not to think he knows what he does not (21d); "I know that I know nothing" is a later slogan. The Laches, Charmides and Euthyphro show the method and end without a definition.

Positions that recur across the witnesses: virtue is a kind of knowledge; no one does wrong willingly (Protagoras 345d to e, 358c to d); it is better to suffer injustice than commit it, and never right to return a wrong (Gorgias 469c; Crito 49a to e); the soul outranks body and money (Apology 29d to 30b); a daimonion turns him back from actions (Apology 31c to d; Memorabilia 1.1.2 to 4). The ugly face and bare feet are attested (Symposium 215b, 220b); the shrewish Xanthippe is later embroidery.

Where sources disagree. Vlastos (Socrates: Ironist and Moral Philosopher, 1991) held that Plato's early dialogues preserve the real man's method and doctrines, the metaphysical Socrates being Plato's. Kahn (1996) replied that even the early dialogues are Plato's design; Dorion calls the Socratic problem unanswerable. Vlastos's "problem of the elenchus" also stands: showing beliefs inconsistent does not show which is false.

The trial of 399 and the death

The indictment, which Favorinus claimed to have read in the Metroon (Diogenes Laertius 2.40): "Socrates does wrong by not acknowledging the gods the city acknowledges and by introducing new divinities; he also does wrong by corrupting the young. Penalty: death." The prosecutor was Meletus, an obscure poet; behind him stood Anytus, a tanner and leader of the democratic restoration, and Lycon. Socrates says thirty votes the other way would have acquitted him (Apology 36a), implying roughly 280 to 220 of 500. He proposed free meals in the Prytaneum, then thirty minae guaranteed by Plato and Crito (38b). The jury chose death by a larger margin than it convicted.

The context governs the charges. In 404 the Thirty under Critias killed perhaps 1,500 citizens; Critias and Charmides, Socrates' associates and Plato's relatives, were among the regime's leaders, and Alcibiades had betrayed the city twice. The amnesty of 403 barred prosecution for acts before that year, so Socrates could not be charged with training oligarchs, and the case had to be religious. Aeschines (1.173) later said it plainly: "You put to death Socrates the sophist, because he was shown to have educated Critias." Socrates' refusal to arrest Leon of Salamis under the Thirty (Apology 32c to d) and his denial that he ever had pupils (33a to b) answer that unspoken charge.

Execution waited a month for the sacred ship from Delos (Phaedo 58a to c). The Crito refuses escape because fleeing would wrong the laws he had lived under. The Phaedo narrates the last day: the hemlock at sunset, numbness rising from the feet, the last words "Crito, we owe a cock to Asclepius" (118a). Plato was not there (59b); Xenophon was in Asia. The symptoms match Conium maculatum.

Where sources disagree. Xenophon says Socrates spoke with deliberate arrogance (megalēgoria) because he preferred death to decay; Plato gives a reasoned if provocative defence. Moderns divide between a genuine religious prosecution (Parker), a political purge under religious cover (Stone, 1988), and a scapegoat for 415 to 403 (Waterfield, 2009). The vote shows the city itself divided.

Plato: Academy, dialogue, Forms

Plato (c. 428 to 348/7) was an aristocrat related through his mother to Critias and Charmides. The Seventh Letter, of disputed authenticity but well informed, says he expected a political career and was repelled first by the Thirty, then by the democracy that killed Socrates. After travels, including Syracuse around 388, he taught in the gymnasium in the grove of Academus. The Academy was a circle, not a university: mathematics, dialectic and politics, with Eudoxus, Speusippus, Xenocrates and, from 367, Aristotle. Two more Syracusan visits (367, 361) to shape Dionysius II failed. The inscription about geometry at the door is attested only in the sixth century AD.

Plato never speaks in his own voice. Socrates leads most dialogues but yields in the late ones to an Eleatic visitor, a Locrian and an unnamed Athenian. The Phaedrus (274b to 278b) calls writing a dead image of speech; the Seventh Letter (341c) denies any treatise of Plato's on what matters most. Stylometry since Campbell (1867) fixes a late group (Sophist, Statesman, Philebus, Timaeus, Laws); the early and middle groups are broadly agreed, their internal order is not.

The theory of Forms is what Aristotle credits to Plato as distinct from Socrates. A Form is what a general term names: the Beautiful itself, the Equal itself. Forms are outside space and time, unchanging, grasped by thought; sensible things imperfectly participate in them. The Phaedo argues from recollection (72e to 77a): we judge sticks deficiently equal, which presupposes knowledge of Equality that sense never gave. Republic 5 to 7 add the epistemology: knowledge is of what is, opinion of what becomes, and the Good is to the intelligible what the sun is to the visible, "beyond being" (509b). In the Parmenides (130a to 135c) Plato raises the hardest objections, including the regress Aristotle called the Third Man (132a to b), and leaves them unanswered. Whether the late dialogues abandon, revise or restate the theory is disputed.

The Republic, the Symposium, the late dialogues

The Republic asks what justice is and whether it pays. Book 1 clears Thrasymachus. In Book 2 Glaucon's ring of Gyges (359d to 360b) restates the Sophistic challenge: show justice good for its possessor apart from reputation. Socrates founds a city in speech with three classes, producers, auxiliaries, rulers, and in Book 4 matches them to three parts of the soul, appetitive, spirited and reasoning, distinguished because one thing cannot at once want and refuse the same object (436b to 441c). Justice is each part doing its own work under reason (433a to b, 441d to e). Books 2 and 3 censor poetry that shows gods doing evil and install the noble lie of the metals (414b to c). Book 5 abolishes the guardians' families, admits women to rule, and declares that cities' ills will not end until philosophers rule (473c to d). Book 6 gives the ship of state and the Sun; Book 7 the Cave (514a to 517a), whose returning prisoner would be killed, an allusion to Socrates, then a mathematical curriculum ending in dialectic. Books 8 and 9 trace decline through timocracy, oligarchy, democracy and tyranny. Book 10 returns to poetry: imitation stands at third remove from the Form (597e), feeds the lower soul, and is exiled unless it can defend itself, "an ancient quarrel between philosophy and poetry" (607b).

The Symposium is set after Agathon's tragic victory of 416 and written around 385. Five speakers praise Eros, Aristophanes with his severed halves; Socrates reports Diotima: Eros is a daimōn, child of Resource and Poverty (203b to e), the desire to possess the good for ever, leading from one beautiful body to all, to souls, laws, knowledge, and the sight of the Beautiful itself (210a to 212a). Alcibiades bursts in drunk and praises Socrates instead, recounting his failed seduction and Socrates' courage at Potidaea and Delium (215a to 222b).

The late dialogues change register. The Theaetetus refutes Protagoras on his own terms (171a to c) and fails to define knowledge. The Sophist treats not-being as difference. The Statesman makes weaving the model of rule. The Philebus ranks pleasure and knowledge. The Timaeus has a Demiurge shape a receptacle after the Forms, the Plato the Middle Ages knew. The Laws, unfinished, describes Magnesia, a second-best city under law. Aristotle mentions Plato's "unwritten doctrines" (Physics 209b14 to 15); whether these were a systematic oral metaphysics (Krämer, Gaiser) or little beyond the Philebus (Cherniss) is unresolved.

Aristotle

Aristotle (384 to 322) was born at Stagira, son of a physician to Amyntas III of Macedon. He joined the Academy at seventeen and stayed twenty years. After Plato's death, with Speusippus as head, he left for Assos, then Mytilene, where much of the biology was done, then in 343/2 Macedon as tutor to Alexander; how much he shaped the boy is unknown (Plutarch, Alexander 7 to 8). In 335/4 he returned to teach in the gymnasium of Apollo Lyceius, the Lyceum. After Alexander's death in 323 he was charged with impiety (Diogenes Laertius 5.5) and withdrew to Chalcis, dying in 322; the line about not letting Athens sin twice against philosophy is Aelian's (Varia Historia 3.36) and probably invented. The surviving treatises are lecture material edited by Andronicus of Rhodes in the first century BC; the dialogues antiquity admired are lost.

The break with Plato was philosophical: where friends have introduced the Forms, truth must come before friends (Nicomachean Ethics 1096a11 to 17). Metaphysics 1.9 argues that separated Forms double the world without explaining it and cannot cause change. For Aristotle the primary realities are individual substances, compounds of form and matter; universals exist in particulars. Knowledge begins in perception and rises by induction to first principles (Posterior Analytics 2.19). Hence his work is a set of distinct sciences, not a single ascent to the Good.

The four causes (Physics 2.3, 194b16 to 195a3) are four answers to "why?". Of a statue: the bronze (material), the shape (formal), the sculptor (efficient), the purpose (final). Predecessors had found only the first and third, Plato the second; the final cause, which Aristotle makes central to nature, had been neglected.

The Nicomachean Ethics identifies the human good as eudaimonia via the function argument (1.7, 1097b22 to 1098a20): the human function is rational activity, so the good is rational activity done well, in accordance with virtue, over a whole life. Virtue of character is a disposition to choose, lying in a mean relative to us, determined by reason as the person of practical wisdom would determine it (2.6, 1106b36 to 1107a2). The mean is not moderation: courage is the right amount of fear for this situation, and some acts have no mean (1107a9 to 17). Book 7 rejects Socrates' denial of weakness of will (1145b22 to 27); Book 10 crowns the life of contemplation (1177a12 to 1178a8). The aim is to become good, not to know what goodness is (1103b26 to 29).

The Politics opens with the city as natural and the human as a political animal (1253a2 to 3). Book 1 defends slavery by nature while admitting the controversy (1255a3 ff.); Book 2 attacks the Republic's communism; Book 3 defines the citizen as one who shares in judging and ruling (1275a22) and sets out six constitutions, kingship, aristocracy and polity with their deviations tyranny, oligarchy and democracy (1279a22 to b10); Book 4 praises the middle class (1295b).

The Poetics answers Republic 10 without naming it. Tragedy is "an imitation of an action that is serious, complete, of a certain magnitude, through pity and fear accomplishing the katharsis of such emotions" (1449b24 to 28). Plot is its soul; the best plot moves through reversal and recognition to a fall caused by hamartia, an error not a vice (1453a7 to 17), as in the Oedipus Tyrannus. Katharsis, used once and unexplained, is read as purgation (Bernays), purification, or clarification (Golden, Nussbaum). The book on comedy is lost; the three unities are Castelvetro's (1570), not Aristotle's.

Biology fills more of the corpus than any other subject: History of Animals, Parts of Animals, Generation of Animals, some five hundred kinds. He divided animals into blooded and bloodless, separated dolphins from fish because they breathe and suckle, described the placenta-like attachment of the smooth dogfish embryo (HA 6.10), confirmed in 1842, and the octopus's hectocotylus arm (5.6), disbelieved until the nineteenth century. He also said men have more teeth than women (501b19). Parts of Animals 1.5 (645a) argues that even humble animals reward study because nature works to ends. Darwin told Ogle in 1882 that Linnaeus and Cuvier "were mere schoolboys to old Aristotle".

Where sources disagree. Jaeger (1923) read the corpus developmentally, from Platonist youth to empirical maturity; the thesis depends on dating treatises whose order cannot be fixed, and recent scholarship treats them as concurrent inquiries. The tale that the treatises lay in a cellar at Scepsis (Strabo 13.1.54) is doubted, since Hellenistic authors knew some of them.

The minor Socratics

Cynics. Antisthenes (c. 445 to 365), pupil of Gorgias then Socrates, taught that virtue suffices for happiness; "I would rather go mad than feel pleasure" (Diogenes Laertius 6.3). Diogenes of Sinope (c. 412 to 323) made it a way of life: the storage jar, public shamelessness, the lamp "looking for a human being" (6.41), "defacing the currency" of convention (6.20, 6.71), the first recorded kosmopolitēs (6.63). The Alexander anecdotes (Plutarch, Alexander 14) are legend. Zeno of Citium studied with Diogenes' pupil Crates before founding the Stoa (7.2 to 3), keeping the sufficiency of virtue and dropping the shamelessness.

Cyrenaics. Aristippus of Cyrene (c. 435 to 356) appears in Memorabilia 2.1 and 3.8 as a hedonist Socrates tries to correct; "I possess Laïs, I am not possessed" (Diogenes Laertius 2.75). The doctrine that the end is present bodily pleasure, and that only our own affections (pathē) are knowable, was probably systematised by his grandson. Epicurus despised them and built a hedonism of stable pleasure, but they made pleasure the candidate end every Hellenistic ethics had to address.

Megarians. Euclides of Megara, present at Socrates' death (Phaedo 59c) and host to Plato afterwards (Diogenes Laertius 2.106), fused Socratic ethics with Eleatic monism: the good is one thing under many names. His school turned to logic. Eubulides devised the Liar and the Heap (2.108); Stilpo taught apatheia and taught Zeno (7.2, 7.24); Diodorus Cronus and Philo, whom Sedley assigns to a separate Dialectical school, debated the conditional (Sextus, Outlines 2.110 to 112), and Diodorus's Master Argument (Epictetus, Discourses 2.19.1) forced the Stoics to build a modal theory. Stoic logic grew from this ground.

Where sources disagree. The succession Socrates, Antisthenes, Diogenes, Crates, Zeno is Hellenistic doxography, which wanted lineages. Dudley (1937) argued Antisthenes was no Cynic and that Diogenes barely met him; whether Cyrenaic epistemology is the elder Aristippus's or his grandson's is open. What is secure is that by 300 BC every Hellenistic position had a Socratic pedigree someone claimed.

Self-test

  1. Which three fragments carry most of what we know of Protagoras, and what do they say?
    Answer

    DK 80 B1, man is the measure of all things; B4, agnosticism about the gods; B6a, two opposed arguments on every matter.

  2. What is the nomos/physis distinction, and who took which side?
    Answer

    Law or convention against nature. Protagoras defended nomos as the basis of city life; Hippias, Antiphon and Plato's Callicles treated law as an artificial constraint on nature.

  3. Why is the report of Protagoras's impiety trial doubted?
    Answer

    It rests on Diogenes Laertius 9.54, conflicts with Meno 91e, and belongs among the fifth-century impiety trials Dover showed rest on late evidence; most scholars treat it as fiction modelled on Socrates'.

  4. What does Aristotle say about Socrates that separates him from Plato?
    Answer

    That Socrates dealt with ethics not nature, sought universal definitions, and did not separate universals from particulars (Metaphysics 1.6, 13.4).

  5. What were the charges against Socrates, and why religious rather than political?
    Answer

    Not acknowledging the city's gods, introducing new divinities, corrupting the young. The amnesty of 403 barred prosecution for anything before that year, so his link to Critias and Alcibiades could not be charged directly.

  6. Name the three parts of the soul in the Republic and the argument that distinguishes them.
    Answer

    Appetitive, spirited, reasoning, distinguished because one thing cannot at once want and refuse the same object (436b to 441c).

  7. What is the Third Man argument and where does Plato raise it?
    Answer

    A regress objection to the Forms, which Aristotle named the Third Man; Plato raises it in the Parmenides (132a to b) and leaves it unanswered.

  8. State Aristotle's definition of virtue and say why the mean is not moderation.
    Answer

    A disposition to choose, in a mean relative to us, determined by reason as the practically wise person would (NE 1106b36 to 1107a2). The mean is the right amount for the situation; some acts have no mean.

  9. Give two biological observations of Aristotle confirmed only in modern times, and one error.
    Answer

    The dogfish embryo's placental attachment (1842) and the octopus hectocotylus. Error: men have more teeth than women.

  10. Through which two minor Socratics did Zeno of Citium train, and what did each give Stoicism?
    Answer

    Crates the Cynic, whose ethics of virtue as sufficient Stoicism kept; Stilpo the Megarian, whose school's logic fed Stoic dialectic.

Carry forward

  1. The nomos/physis question posed by the Sophists is the question every later Greek ethics answers: Plato by grounding justice in a nature the soul shares with the cosmos, Aristotle by making the city natural, the Cynics by rejecting nomos, the Stoics by identifying nature with reason and law.
  2. Socrates is reconstructed, not reported. Every claim about him must name its witness, and the four witnesses agree on method and manner far more than on doctrine. The same discipline applies to Pythagoras, Diogenes and every figure known through followers.
  3. Aristotle's break with Plato over separated Forms produced the model of knowledge as distinct sciences with their own principles and final causes central to nature. Transmitted through Andronicus, the Arabic commentators and the medieval universities, it is the framework later modules presuppose and the scientific revolution overturns.
