01Rome: the city that chose to descend from the losers

The Roman claim on Troy rests on one Homeric hinge. In Iliad 20.302–308 Poseidon rescues Aeneas from Achilles because fate has decreed that he and his sons' sons will rule the Trojans. A survivor with a future was a gift to any people looking for a pedigree. Greek writers had already brought Aeneas west: Dionysius of Halicarnassus (Roman Antiquities 1.72) reports that Hellanicus, in the fifth century BC, had Aeneas founding Rome, oddly in company with Odysseus. By the third century BC the Romans had made the story their own. Its political use is visible in the 260s and 240s BC, when Rome treated Ilion in the Troad as kin.

Where sources disagree. When Rome took Aeneas up is argued. Karl Galinsky (Aeneas, Sicily, and Rome, 1969) read the cult at Lavinium and the Etruscan Aeneas figurines as evidence that the legend was at home in Latium by the sixth century; Erich Gruen (Culture and National Identity in Republican Rome, 1992) argued that Rome adopted the Trojan pedigree late and deliberately, as a diplomatic instrument in its third-century dealings with the Greek world. The Lavinium evidence is real but does not show who told the story or why.

The first Latin poets built on this. Livius Andronicus translated the Odyssey (Odusia) into Saturnians for the schoolroom. Naevius, a veteran of the First Punic War, opened his Bellum Punicum with Aeneas leaving the burning city. The fragments survive mostly through Servius and Macrobius, and Macrobius says Virgil took the storm and Venus' complaint to Jupiter from Naevius. Ennius' Annales began with a dream in which Homer's shade told him that his soul had passed into Ennius, so the Roman epic would be Homer reborn. Lucretius (De rerum natura 1.117–126) mentions the dream with some irony. Ennius made Ilia, Aeneas' daughter, the mother of Romulus. Later antiquarians had to stretch a list of Alban kings to fill the four centuries between Troy's fall and 753 BC.

Virgil's Aeneid is a deliberate answer to Homer from the other side. Books 1–6 are an Odyssey of wandering and Books 7–12 an Iliad of war in Italy, but this time the Trojans win the Iliad. Book 2 tells the sack from inside the city. The Greeks become liars and butchers: Sinon's false story, Pyrrhus killing Priam at the altar. Laocoön's line sums up the Roman view of the enemy:

timeo Danaos et dona ferentes (Aeneid 2.49)

The dynastic point is made openly. In Jupiter's prophecy (1.267–288) Ascanius takes the name Iulus, and a Julius "whose name comes from great Iulus" will carry Rome's rule to the stars. Julius Caesar had already dedicated the temple of Venus Genetrix in 46 BC to honour his divine Trojan ancestress. Augustus made Troy ceremonial. The lusus Troiae, a cavalry display by noble boys, gets its origin story in Aeneid 5.545–603, where Virgil says Rome still calls the boys "Trojan". Suetonius (Julius 39.2; Augustus 43.2) records that both Caesar and Augustus staged it. Augustus gave it up after boys were injured and Asinius Pollio complained. Then comes Virgil's sharpest turn. In 12.819–828 Juno gives up her anger only on condition that Troy's name, dress and language disappear. Rome will be Trojan by blood and Latin by name. Troy survives by agreeing to die.

Where sources disagree. Whether the Aeneid celebrates the Augustan settlement or mourns what it cost is the oldest quarrel in Virgil scholarship. Adam Parry's "The Two Voices of Virgil's Aeneid" (1963) and the so-called Harvard school stress the private grief and the killing of Turnus that ends the poem. Readers such as Brooks Otis (Virgil: A Study in Civilized Poetry, 1964) and Francis Cairns (Virgil's Augustan Epic, 1989) stress its imperial teleology. Most current readers hold that both voices are present.

Nero took the Trojan connection to the stage. Suetonius (Nero 38.2) says that while Rome burned in AD 64 he sang The Sack of Ilium in stage costume. Tacitus (Annals 15.39) gives this only as a rumour and notes that Nero was at Antium when the fire started. Cassius Dio (62.18) repeats it. Nero also wrote a Troica, which Juvenal mocks in Satire 8. According to Servius (on Aeneid 5.370) it made Paris strong enough to beat Hector at the funeral games. That makes sense for an emperor who preferred the lyre-player prince to the warrior. Petronius' Eumolpus recites a Troiae Halosis (Satyricon 89), which is very likely a parody aimed at Nero's poem.

02Dares and Dictys: the eyewitnesses who outranked Homer

Two short prose works shaped the Latin Middle Ages more than any epic. The Ephemeris belli Troiani of "Dictys of Crete" claims to be the war diary of a companion of Idomeneus. Its Latin version, by one Lucius Septimius, probably dates to the fourth century AD. A prefatory letter says shepherds found the text in a box in Dictys' tomb at Knossos after an earthquake in Nero's reign, written in Phoenician letters, and that it was translated into Greek for the emperor. The tale of the found manuscript is a forger's convention, but a Greek original did exist. A Tebtunis papyrus (P.Tebt. 2.268) of the early third century preserves part of it, and the Byzantine chronicler John Malalas used it. Dictys writes from the Greek side, rationalises the gods away, and has Achilles lured to his death by his love for the Trojan princess Polyxena.

The De excidio Troiae historia of "Dares the Phrygian" is shorter, flatter and pro-Trojan. It probably dates to the fifth or sixth century. A fake letter from Cornelius Nepos to Sallust says Nepos found it in Athens. Its author borrows the name of a minor Trojan priest of Hephaestus (Iliad 5.9). Dares gives brisk portraits of the main figures (chapters 12–13), which medieval poets copied almost word for word, and closes with an exact casualty count. Awkwardly for Rome, Dares has Aeneas and Antenor betray the city.

The Middle Ages trusted these texts over Homer for several reasons:

  • Eyewitness over poet. Medieval historiography ranked testimony above song. Isidore of Seville (Etymologies 1.42.1) names Dares as the first pagan historian, after Moses among the Hebrews, and claims he wrote on palm leaves. Homer was a later poet who, as the Nepos letter puts it, had been thought mad for showing gods fighting men.
  • No Greek. The Latin West could not read Homer. Its Homer was the Ilias Latina, a first-century abridgement of about 1,070 hexameters.
  • No gods. A war without divine meddling suited Christian readers better.
  • The right side. Dares favoured Troy, which mattered to nations that believed they were descended from it.
Where sources disagree. The papyrus proves the Greek Dictys. No Greek Dares has ever been found; Aelian (Varia Historia 11.2) says a Phrygian Iliad by Dares still existed in his day, and whether one did is uncertain. Dates for both Latin texts are estimates. The Greek Dictys is fixed only at one end: the Tebtunis papyrus (P.Tebt. 2.268) is of the early third century AD, so the Greek text is older than that; whether it was written under Nero, as its own preface claims by dating the "discovery" of the tin box to Nero's reign, or in the second century, is open.

03Medieval Troy: romance, genealogy and the invention of Cressida

Benoît de Sainte-Maure's Roman de Troie (c. 1160–1170) runs to about 30,000 octosyllabic lines. It belongs with the Roman de Thèbes and the Eneas among the "romances of antiquity" written in the Plantagenet orbit. Its praise of a "riche dame de riche rei" is usually taken to mean Eleanor of Aquitaine, though this cannot be proved. Benoît follows Dares, then Dictys, and turns the war into chivalric romance. His great addition is the story of Troilus and Briseida. She is sent to the Greek camp in an exchange of prisoners, swears to stay faithful, and gives herself to Diomedes. A minor figure in Dares became the West's emblem of female inconstancy.

In 1287 Guido delle Colonne, a judge at Messina, produced the Historia destructionis Troiae. It is a Latin prose version of Benoît that never names Benoît, cites Dares and Dictys as its authorities, and scolds Homer for lying. Because it was in Latin and looked like history, Guido's text became the standard account and was translated across Europe. Boccaccio's Il Filostrato (c. 1335–1340) turned Briseida into Criseida and invented Pandaro as go-between. From Boccaccio came Chaucer's Troilus and Criseyde (c. 1385). Chaucer credits an invented authority, "Lollius". His narrator sympathises with Criseyde and is reluctant to condemn her. The poem ends by lifting Troilus' soul to the eighth sphere, where he laughs at those who mourn him. Chaucer calls it a tragedy:

Go, litel bok, go, litel myn tragedye (Troilus 5.1786)

Robert Henryson's Testament of Cresseid answered Chaucer by striking her with leprosy. John Lydgate's Troy Book (1412–1420), written for Henry, Prince of Wales, put Guido into some 30,000 English lines. William Caxton's Recuyell of the Historyes of Troye (Bruges, c. 1473–1474), a translation of Raoul Lefèvre, was the first book printed in English. Troy and English print began together.

Behind the romance sat genealogy. A Trojan ancestor made a people Rome's cousin rather than its subject.

04Early modern Troy: Shakespeare's anti-epic and Homer recovered

Shakespeare's Troilus and Cressida (c. 1601–1602) drew on Chaucer, Caxton and Lydgate, and perhaps also on Chapman's 1598 Homer. It treats the whole tradition with contempt. The Prologue enters "armed" and starts in the middle of things, as epic should. The play then takes epic apart. Hector concedes that Helen ought to go back (2.2) and then argues for keeping her. Ulysses' great speech on "degree" (1.3) is followed by his cynical handling of Achilles. Helen chatters about love songs (3.1). Cressida's betrayal (5.2) is watched in layers: Troilus watches her, Ulysses watches Troilus, and Thersites watches them all and gives his verdict: "still wars and lechery". Achilles does not defeat Hector. His Myrmidons kill Hector while he is unarmed (5.8), a detail that comes from the medieval Achilles of Benoît, Guido and Lydgate, who kills Hector unguarded and sends the Myrmidons against Troilus, never from Homer, nor from Dares, whose Achilles kills Hector in open battle (ch. 24). The play ends with Pandarus leaving the audience his venereal diseases.

Where sources disagree. The play's genre was unsettled from the start. The 1609 quarto exists in two states: one says it was acted at the Globe, and the other carries an epistle claiming it was never staged and calling it a comedy. The Folio printed it late, unpaginated, between the histories and the tragedies. F. S. Boas classed it as a "problem play" in Shakspere and His Predecessors (1896). Peter Alexander ("Troilus and Cressida, 1609", The Library, 1928) suggested it was written for an Inns of Court audience. That remains uncertain.

Homer himself came back slowly. Petrarch owned a Greek Homer he could not read. Leontius Pilatus made a crude Latin prose version for him and Boccaccio in the 1360s. The editio princeps appeared in Florence in 1488 under Demetrios Chalkokondyles. George Chapman published Seaven Bookes of the Iliades in 1598, the full Iliad in fourteeners in 1611, and the Odyssey in 1614–1615. He read Homer as Neoplatonic allegory and claimed Homer's spirit inspired him. Two centuries later Keats's sonnet "On First Looking into Chapman's Homer" (1816) recorded the shock of that rough energy. Alexander Pope's Iliad (1715–1720), in heroic couplets, earned enough by subscription to make him independent. His Odyssey (1725–1726) was partly farmed out to William Broome and Elijah Fenton. Richard Bentley's reputed remark, "a pretty poem, Mr Pope, but you must not call it Homer", is anecdote, but it names the problem: Homer made polite.

The deeper change concerned authority. Once Homer could be read, Dares was exposed as a late forgery, and the oldest text replaced the supposed eyewitness. Then the oldest text was itself questioned. Robert Wood's Essay on the Original Genius of Homer (1769) read the poems against the landscape of the Troad. F. A. Wolf's Prolegomena ad Homerum (1795) argued that "Homer" was a product of oral tradition and later editing. The question of whether there was a Homer reopened the question of whether there was a Troy.

05Schliemann and the Victorian Troy

Victorian Britain took Homer as moral education. Gladstone wrote Studies on Homer and the Homeric Age (1858) out of office, between his two terms at the Exchequer (1855 and 1859). For Gladstone, Homer was close to a second revelation, a record of a moral order older than Greece; that is why the poems could carry the weight of a gentleman's education. Matthew Arnold's On Translating Homer (1861), answering F. W. Newman's ballad-metre version, demanded rapidity, plainness and nobility. Tennyson's "Ulysses" (1842) made the returned hero a restless imperial explorer. The habit ran deep in the public schools. In 1915, across the Dardanelles from Hisarlik, Patrick Shaw-Stewart wrote in his copy of Housman the lines "Stand in the trench, Achilles, / Flame-capped" (printed in Ronald Knox, Patrick Shaw-Stewart, 1920). Rupert Brooke, bound for Gallipoli, was buried on Skyros, the island where Achilles had been hidden.

The site itself was disputed. Late eighteenth-century travellers favoured Pınarbaşı. Charles Maclaren argued for Hisarlik in 1822, and Frank Calvert, who owned part of the mound, dug trial trenches there in the 1860s. Heinrich Schliemann used Calvert's ground and excavated from 1871. His great north–south trench destroyed much of the upper layers he was looking for. In 1873 he announced "Priam's Treasure" and photographed his wife Sophia wearing the gold. The hoard came from Troy II, about a thousand years before any plausible Trojan War. He smuggled it out of the Ottoman Empire. It went to Berlin, vanished in 1945, and in 1993 was acknowledged to be in the Pushkin Museum, Moscow.

His successors refined the picture. Wilhelm Dörpfeld identified the walls of Troy VI. Carl Blegen in the 1930s favoured Troy VIIa, whose fall he put at about 1250 BC; Penelope Mountjoy's pottery dating has since moved it to about 1190–1180 BC. From 1988 Manfred Korfmann traced a large lower town. Hittite texts name a western Anatolian state called Wilusa, widely equated with (W)ilios, and a treaty between Muwatalli II and its king Alaksandu (c. 1280 BC), a name that resembles Alexandros, Paris's other name.

Where sources disagree. Schliemann's treasure (Traill and Calder against Easton) is argued in module 10.10. The Kolb–Korfmann quarrel over the lower town is in module 10.10, the Wilusa equation in module 10.3. Whether any of this confirms a single war behind Homer is uncertain.

06The twentieth century: Troy against war

After 1914 Troy became the West's way of thinking about its own wars. Jean Giraudoux's La guerre de Troie n'aura pas lieu (1935) shows Hector, back from war, trying to send Helen home and keep the Gates of War closed. In the central scene with Ulysses, the two men almost succeed. Then the warmonger poet Demokos lies with his dying breath, and the war happens anyway. Staged between Hitler's rise and Munich, its irony arrived on schedule. Christopher Fry translated it as Tiger at the Gates (1955).

Simone Weil wrote "L'Iliade ou le poème de la force" in 1939–1940 and published it in Cahiers du Sud (1940–1941) under the anagram Émile Novis. Her claim is that the real subject of the Iliad is force, which "turns anybody who is subjected to it into a thing" (Mary McCarthy's 1945 translation). In her reading Homer gives the same bitter pity to both sides, and only the Gospels match it.

Where sources disagree. Rachel Bespaloff's De l'Iliade (1943), written in the same years, reads the poem as tragic but also as a celebration of beauty and heroic life. Set against Weil, Bespaloff is the case that Weil underplays how fully the Iliad honours kleos, the glory a warrior wins by killing; the two essays were published together in English (War and the Iliad, 2005) because they are the two sides.

Christa Wolf's Kassandra (1983) is spoken by Cassandra at Mycenae in the hour before her death. It reimagines Troy as a militarised state with a security apparatus, under its official Eumelos, that silences its own prophet. Achilles is simply "the brute", and real hope lies in a community of women in the caves of Mount Ida. Published with Wolf's Frankfurt poetics lectures, during the NATO missile debate and under East German censorship, it reads as a feminist critique of a whole civilisation's investment in war.

Christopher Logue's War Music began in 1959 with a BBC commission. He knew no Greek, worked from cribs, and called his versions "accounts". He was still writing when he died in 2011, leaving the work unfinished. Its cinematic cutting, anachronistic similes and violent speed made the Iliad modern without the polish of Pope. Derek Walcott's Omeros (1990) moves Troy to St Lucia. The fishermen Achille and Hector fight over Helen, a waitress. Philoctete suffers a wound that will not heal. "Omeros" is Homer's name in modern Greek. Here the Trojan story belongs to the descendants of the enslaved, though the narrator keeps questioning whether a Caribbean life needs a Homeric frame at all.

07The women-centred retellings: what they correct and what they flatten

Margaret Atwood's The Penelopiad (2005) has Penelope speak from Hades, with a chorus of the twelve maids whom Telemachus hangs in Odyssey 22. Pat Barker's The Silence of the Girls (2018) gives the Iliad to Briseis, and its opening lines mock the epic praise of Achilles ("Great Achilles. Brilliant Achilles"). Its sequels continue past the sack. Madeline Miller's The Song of Achilles (2011) is narrated by Patroclus as a love story, and Circe (2018) moves to the edge of the Odyssey, correcting the goddess into a person and flattening Odysseus into a guest who passes through. Natalie Haynes's A Thousand Ships (2019) gives the war to the Muse Calliope and dozens of women; it corrects the silence of Euripides' survivors into speech and flattens the men into a chorus of causes. Emily Wilson's Odyssey (2017) and Iliad (2023) are line-for-line translations, not retellings. Her opening "Tell me about a complicated man" and her decision to call the hanged women "slaves" rather than "maids" changed how the poem is read in English. Caroline Alexander's Iliad (2015) was the first complete English version by a woman.

What they correct is real. Enslaved women, rape as a spoil of war, and the fate of a captured city were the subject of the tradition, and the epic voice rarely lingered on them. What they claim to restore is older than they sometimes admit. The Iliad gives Briseis her own lament over Patroclus (19.282–300). It ends with three women's laments, by Andromache, Hecuba and Helen (24.723–776). Euripides' Trojan Women (415 BC) is a women-centred Troy staged two thousand four hundred years before Barker. The flattening is also real. Gods tend to vanish or shrink. Achilles becomes either a romantic lead or a thug. A modern therapeutic idiom replaces formulaic speech. A sharp line between victim and perpetrator crowds out the Iliad's hardest scene, in Book 24, where Priam and Achilles weep together for different dead.

Where sources disagree. The objection to Wilson is that "complicated" narrows polytropos, "of many turns", and that some of her plain diction loses the formulaic grandeur. Wilson's own answer, in her translator's note, is that older translations brought their own Victorian or Augustan values into the text unremarked. The disagreement is less about Greek than about whether any translation can be neutral.

The BBC series drew racist abuse for casting black actors as Achilles, Patroclus, Zeus and Aeneas. Critics cited Homer's xanthos ("fair", "tawny") for Achilles' hair. Classicists replied that modern racial categories do not map onto Homeric colour terms. The row, more than the series, showed how much ownership some modern viewers still claim over Troy.

09Why this war remains the West's master story

Several features explain its persistence. First, it has two sides, and either can be claimed. The Iliad grants the enemy full humanity, so Rome, the Franks, the Britons, Wolf's Cassandra and Walcott's fishermen could all take the losing side, the side of survivors and founders. Second, it is complete. A cause, a quarrel, the death of the best man, the fall of a city, captive women and homecomings that go wrong supply nearly every plot later literature needs, and Aeschylus, Euripides and Virgil all took their material from it. Third, its centre is almost empty. A war fought over one woman, or an insult, or a ghost, invites each age to name the real motive: honour, desire, trade, empire, oil, patriarchy. Fourth, it is probably real but cannot be proved. Hisarlik and Wilusa show that something was there without ever confirming Homer. Finally, the story is always a contest over who has the right to tell it: Dares against Homer, Guido against Benoît, Schliemann against the sceptics, Briseis against Achilles. Each age re-fights the Trojan War over the right to narrate it.

10Self-test

  1. Which Homeric passage gives the Romans their opening, and what does it say?
    Answer

    Iliad 20.302–308: Poseidon saves Aeneas from Achilles because fate has decreed that Aeneas and his descendants will rule the Trojans.

  2. What condition does Juno set in Aeneid 12 before she gives up her anger, and why does it matter for Rome's Trojan identity?
    Answer

    Troy's name, language and dress must disappear and the new people must be called Latin. Rome is Trojan by descent but Latin by name, so Troy survives only by ceasing to exist.

  3. What was the lusus Troiae, and who records its staging?
    Answer

    A cavalry display by aristocratic boys, given an origin story in Aeneid 5.545–603. Suetonius (Julius 39.2; Augustus 43.2) records that Caesar and Augustus staged it, and that Augustus dropped it after injuries.

  4. How does Tacitus' account of Nero singing during the fire of 64 differ from Suetonius'?
    Answer

    Suetonius (Nero 38.2) states it as fact. Tacitus (Annals 15.39) reports it as a rumour and notes that Nero was at Antium when the fire began.

  5. What evidence shows that Dictys Cretensis had a Greek original?
    Answer

    A Tebtunis papyrus (P.Tebt. 2.268) of the early third century AD preserves part of the Greek text, and John Malalas used it. No comparable evidence exists for Dares.

  6. Give four reasons medieval readers preferred Dares and Dictys to Homer.
    Answer

    They claimed to be eyewitness prose history rather than later poetry (Isidore, Etymologies 1.42.1, calls Dares the first pagan historian); the Latin West could not read Greek; they removed the gods; and Dares took the Trojan side favoured by peoples claiming Trojan descent.

  7. Who first told the Troilus–Briseida story, and how did the woman's name reach Chaucer's form?
    Answer

    Benoît de Sainte-Maure, in the Roman de Troie (c. 1160–1170). Guido delle Colonne carried it into Latin prose. Boccaccio's Filostrato renamed her Criseida and added Pandaro, and Chaucer's Troilus and Criseyde followed Boccaccio.

  8. In Shakespeare's Troilus and Cressida, how does Hector die, and why is that significant?
    Answer

    Achilles' Myrmidons kill him while he is unarmed. The treacherous Achilles comes from the medieval Benoît–Guido–Lydgate tradition, not Homer or Dares, and the scene demolishes epic heroism.

  9. What did Schliemann's "Priam's Treasure" turn out to be, and where is it now?
    Answer

    A hoard from Troy II, roughly a thousand years earlier than any plausible Trojan War. It vanished from Berlin in 1945 and has been acknowledged since 1993 to be in the Pushkin Museum, Moscow.

  10. What women-centred version of Troy existed long before the modern retellings?
    Answer

    The Iliad itself, with Briseis' lament (19.282–300) and the three women's laments that close the poem (24.723–776), and above all Euripides' Trojan Women (415 BC).

11Carry forward

  1. Troy was a claim of descent before it was a story. From Rome to the Franks and Britons, the losing side was the prize because it supplied founders, not subjects.
  2. Each age fights over who has authority to tell the war: eyewitness forgery, then the oldest poem, then the excavated site, then the silenced voice. The way a culture tells Troy shows what it counts as truth.
  3. Homer and Euripides already contain most of the counter-voices later retellings claim to add. Reception usually amplifies what the old texts left at the margins rather than inventing it.

The prompt this answers

Give me an in-depth report on the reception of the Trojan War from Rome to the present. Cover: the Roman Troy (Naevius, Ennius, Virgil's Aeneid as a pro-Trojan answer to Homer, the Julian claim through Iulus, Augustus and the Troy Game, Nero's Troica); the late antique prose forgeries Dares Phrygius and Dictys Cretensis and why the Middle Ages trusted them over Homer; the medieval Troy: Benoît de Sainte-Maure's Roman de Troie, Guido delle Colonne, Chaucer's Troilus and Criseyde, Lydgate, the Trojan ancestry claimed by Franks, Britons (Brutus of Troy) and others; Shakespeare's Troilus and Cressida as anti-epic; the Renaissance recovery of Homer (Chapman, Pope); Schliemann and the Victorian Troy; Troy in the twentieth century (Giraudoux's La guerre de Troie n'aura pas lieu, Simone Weil's The Iliad or the Poem of Force, Christa Wolf's Cassandra, Logue's War Music, Walcott's Omeros); the recent women-centred retellings (Atwood's Penelopiad, Barker's Silence of the Girls, Miller, Haynes, Wilson's translations) and what they correct and what they flatten; Troy on screen (the 1956 Helen of Troy, Petersen's Troy, the BBC Troy: Fall of a City) and the historical liberties each takes. End with why this one war has stayed the West's master story. Format: about 2500 words, headed sections, name sources, flag disagreements, end with a 10-question self-test with answers and the three ideas I must carry forward.
