What the Iliad is

The Iliad is a poem of roughly 15,700 hexameter lines, divided since Hellenistic times into 24 books, telling about fifty days in the tenth year of the siege of Troy. Its subject is announced in the first word, mēnis, the wrath of Achilles: how it began, what it cost, and how it ended not in victory but in a shared meal with the father of the man he killed. Aristotle (Poetics 23) singled out this restraint as the mark of Homer's superiority over the poets of the Epic Cycle, who tried to narrate the whole war.

9Agamemnon, in despair, is persuaded by Nestor to send Odysseus, Phoenix and Ajax to Achilles with lavish gifts. 10The Doloneia: Diomedes and Odysseus go out by night, capture and kill the Trojan spy Dolon, then slaughter the Thracian king Rhesus and steal his horses. Already in antiquity suspected of being a later insertion.
11Agamemnon's aristeia, until he, Diomedes, Odysseus and the healer Machaon are all wounded. Achilles sends Patroclus to Nestor, who urges him to fight in Achilles' armour if Achilles will not.
12The Trojans assault the Greek wall on foot. Sarpedon delivers his speech on why princes must fight in front, and Hector smashes a gate with a boulder.
13Poseidon secretly aids the Greeks behind Zeus's back; Idomeneus has his aristeia; the fighting is a grinding stalemate at the ships.
14Hera seduces Zeus on Ida with Aphrodite's charm and the help of Sleep, freeing Poseidon to rally the Greeks. Ajax fells Hector with a stone.
15Zeus wakes, recalls Poseidon, has Apollo revive Hector and lays out the whole plan to come: Patroclus will die, Hector will die, Troy will fall. The Trojans reach the ships and Ajax alone holds them off with a pike.
16Patroclus begs to fight; Achilles lends his armour with orders to drive the Trojans from the ships and no further. Patroclus kills Sarpedon, ignores his orders, and is struck by Apollo, speared by Euphorbus and killed by Hector, to whom he prophesies death.
17Day-long struggle over Patroclus' corpse. Hector puts on Achilles' armour; Menelaus and Ajax finally carry the body off while Antilochus runs to tell Achilles.
18Achilles learns of the death and his cry reaches Thetis in the sea; he accepts that killing Hector means his own death. Unarmed, wreathed in Athena's fire, he routs the Trojans by his shout alone. Hephaestus forges the new armour and the Shield.
19Public reconciliation: Agamemnon blames Atē and returns Briseis and the gifts; Achilles refuses food. His horse Xanthus speaks, foretelling his death.
20Zeus releases the gods into battle. Achilles fights Aeneas, who is saved by Poseidon, and would kill Hector but for Apollo's mist.
21Slaughter at the river: Achilles kills the suppliant Lycaon, telling him that even Patroclus had to die. The river Scamander rises against him and is burned back by Hephaestus; the gods brawl; Apollo decoys Achilles while the Trojans flee inside.
22Hector alone stays outside despite his parents' pleas, runs three laps round the walls, and turns when Athena, disguised as his brother Deiphobus, deceives him. Achilles kills him, refuses his dying plea for burial and drags the body behind his chariot while Priam, Hecuba and Andromache lament.
23Patroclus' ghost asks for burial. Achilles sacrifices twelve Trojan youths on the pyre and then presides, with sudden courtesy, over funeral games.
24Achilles drags Hector's corpse for twelve days until the gods intervene. Hermes escorts Priam by night to the Greek camp; the old king kisses the hands that killed his son, and the two weep together. Achilles returns the body, grants a truce, and the poem ends with Hector's funeral.

Structure

The poem narrates about 51 days but spends almost all its length on four. Nine days of plague and twelve of the gods' absence pass in Book 1; the first day of battle fills Books 2–7; the second, Book 8; the night of the Embassy and the Doloneia, Books 9–10; the third and longest day, Books 11–18; the fourth, Books 19–22. Then come the funeral of Patroclus, twelve days of outrage to Hector's corpse, and the eleven-day truce. A ten-year war is made present through four days that contain it.

Where sources disagree. Handbooks speak of "three great days of battle" (Books 2–7, 11–18, 19–22), treating the short second day in Book 8 as a bridge; others count four. The crowded first day, with two duels and a broken truce, led Analyst critics since Lachmann to see it as a stitching-together of once separate episodes.

Cedric Whitman (Homer and the Heroic Tradition, 1958) argued that the poem is built as a ring, like the concentric bands of Geometric vase painting. Book 1 and Book 24 mirror each other: an old father (Chryses, Priam) comes to ransom a child from an Achaean who at first refuses; twelve-day intervals frame each; Thetis travels between Achilles and Zeus in both; the quarrel of gods in 1 answers the council of gods in 24. Books 2 and 23 both catalogue the Greek host, at arms and at play. Hector's duel with Ajax (7) answers his duel with Achilles (22). Not every pairing persuades, but the outer frame is undeniable.

Within the ring the plot turns on a few load-bearing scenes. The Embassy (Book 9) is where the heroic exchange of honour for service is tested and found wanting; after it Achilles' isolation is chosen, not imposed. The death of Patroclus (Book 16) is the hinge: everything before it is the consequence of the wrath against Agamemnon, everything after it the consequence of grief for Patroclus, and the wrath is transferred from Greek to Trojan. Zeus himself summarises the chain at 15.59–71. The Shield (18.478–608) halts the narrative to show the whole world of cities, harvest, dance and lawsuit that the battle has excluded; Achilles carries a cosmos into a fight he knows to be his last. The duel (Book 22) is the long-delayed climax, staged so that the reader sees it from the Trojan wall. The ransom (Book 24) replays Book 1 redeemed: a supplicating father is received rather than driven off.

The characters and what they carry

CharacterWhat the poem makes of them
AchillesThe absolute hero and the problem of absolutes. His choice of short life and lasting fame (9.410–416) is made consciously; his grief exposes the hollowness of the honour system he was raised in. He alone articulates the shared condition of mortals to an enemy (21.106–113, 24.525–533).
AgamemnonAuthority without the stature to justify it. He commands by rank, not merit, and treats honour as property to be confiscated. His admission that Zeus sent Atē upon him (19.86–138) is an apology that still refuses blame.
HectorDefender of a city, husband and son, driven by shame before his people (aidōs, 6.441–446; 22.99–110). He is not Achilles' equal and knows it; his tragedy is overreach after one day's success, against Polydamas' counsel (18.249–313).
PriamKingship in old age, fatherhood reduced to supplication. In Book 24 he does what no hero does: walks unarmed into the enemy camp and asks for pity.
HelenCause and conscience. She weaves the war into cloth (3.125–128), reproaches herself without pause, and is the last to speak over Hector (24.761–775). The poem never says plainly whether she went willingly.
AndromacheWhat war costs those who do not fight. Her plea in Book 6, premonition in 22 and lament in 24 trace a widow's story in three scenes; her forecast of Astyanax's fate (24.734–738) points outside the poem.
DiomedesThe model younger warrior: devastating in Book 5, yet he knows how far a mortal may go against a god (6.129–141), and speaks sense in Book 9 when Achilles has refused.
AjaxDefence, endurance, plain speech. He never has an aristeia of slaughter; his glory is holding the line (15.674–746) and his short speech to Achilles (9.624–642) is the one that moves him.
OdysseusPersuasion, craft and loyalty to the collective. He saves the army in Book 2, leads the Embassy, and quietly omits Agamemnon's demand that Achilles submit (9.158–161).
NestorThe authority of memory. His counsel sets both the Embassy and the fatal plan for Patroclus in motion (11.794–803).
PatroclusGentleness (enēeiē, 17.670) inside a warrior; Achilles' surrogate in armour and in death, and the one person he values above his own honour.
ParisDesire and irresponsibility, the archer who fights from a distance, yet not simply a coward: Hector concedes his courage (6.521–523).
Where sources disagree. The nature of Achilles' bond with Patroclus is nowhere stated. Aeschylus in the lost Myrmidons and Phaedrus in Plato's Symposium (180a) took it as erotic; Xenophon (Symposium 8.31) denied it. Modern readers divide the same way, and the text supports either reading without settling it.

The gods, fate and Zeus

The gods are a quarrelling aristocratic household whose interventions are constant and partisan. Athena and Hera hate Troy; Apollo, Aphrodite and Ares defend it; Zeus loves Hector and Sarpedon but has decided the war's outcome. Divine action takes three forms: direct combat (5.855–861; the theomachy of Book 21), rescue in mist or disguise, and the inward prompting of an impulse the human experiences as his own.

Fate (moira, aisa) sits beside this and never quite coincides with it. Zeus weighs destinies in golden scales (8.69–74; 22.209–213), which suggests he reads fate rather than decrees it. When Sarpedon is about to die, Zeus wonders aloud whether to save him; Hera does not say he cannot but that the other gods will not approve, and he desists (16.431–461). The same happens with Hector (22.167–185). The phrase "beyond fate" (hyper moron, 20.30, 21.517) names a possibility that must be prevented, so fate can in principle be broken.

Where sources disagree. One line of scholarship, following Wilamowitz and the Dios boulē ("plan of Zeus") of 1.5, treats fate as simply the will of Zeus seen from below. Another holds that moira is an older, impersonal order that Zeus administers but does not author, as in Hesiod's Moirai. A third, influential since the oral-poetry turn, reads fate as the tradition itself: what "must" happen is what the audience already knows happened. The Sarpedon scene is the crux and supports all three.

For the humans the result is double motivation: Patroclus dies because he disobeyed and because Apollo struck him. The poem does not treat these as rival explanations; a god's intervention doubles the weight of the human act.

Themes

Mēnis

The first word. Elsewhere in Homer the noun is reserved for gods; Leonard Muellner (The Anger of Achilles, 1996) argued it names a sanction against violated order, not personal temper. It is what Agamemnon provokes by overturning the rules of distribution, and what Achilles must formally renounce (19.67–68, 19.74–75) before the poem can proceed to its second movement.

Timē and kleos

Timē is honour made visible in prizes, seats and shares of meat; kleos is fame in song, the only immortality a hero can have. Achilles accepts the bargain of kleos aphthiton (9.413) but in the same speech rejects the exchange of goods for honour: no gifts can equal a life, because a life once breathed out cannot be recovered (9.401–409). Sarpedon's speech (12.310–328) gives the system's positive case.

Mortality

Glaucus compares generations to leaves (6.146–149); Apollo tells Poseidon that mortals are not worth a god's quarrel (21.462–466); Achilles tells Lycaon that Patroclus died, and so will he (21.106–113). The gods' immunity is the foil that makes human courage legible.

Pity

Eleos and the related aidōs are what Achilles loses after Book 16. He kills suppliants, mutilates Hector, sacrifices prisoners; Apollo accuses him of having destroyed pity itself (24.44). Priam's appeal, "pity me, remembering your own father" (24.503–504), restores it, and the two men's shared weeping is the resolution the wrath plot never offered.

The heroic code and its breakdown

The code says that honour is quantifiable, that a king's distribution is just, and that death is worth fame. Book 1 breaks the second premise, Book 9 the first, and Books 18–22 show a hero fighting for grief rather than honour: a force beyond the code's limits, compared to fire, a lion, a star. Book 24 does not restore the code; it finds something outside it.

Oral style and the Parry–Lord theory

The Iliad repeats itself at every level. About a third of its lines recur in whole or in part; heroes bear fixed epithets; arming, sacrifice and launching a ship follow set sequences, the "type-scenes" catalogued by Walter Arend (1933). Milman Parry, in his Paris theses of 1928 and his Harvard papers of 1930–32, showed that the epithet system is both extensive and economical: for a given hero in a given metrical slot there is almost always exactly one epithet, which is why Achilles is "swift-footed" when sitting still. No poet with a pen would build such a system; it is the toolkit of singers composing in performance.

Parry and Albert Lord tested this against living oral epic in Yugoslavia in 1933–35, recording singers who produced poems of thousands of lines without writing. Lord's The Singer of Tales (1960) generalised the results: oral poets do not memorise texts but recompose them from formulae and themes, and every performance is a new version.

Where sources disagree. How the oral poem became the written one is unresolved. Lord proposed a "dictated text", one performance taken down by a scribe. Gregory Nagy proposes instead a long "evolutionary" crystallisation through repeated performance, with no decisive moment of writing before the Athenian recension. Martin West (The Making of the Iliad, 2011) argues for a single literate poet composing over decades, expanding his own text. Critics also note that the Yugoslav songs are nowhere near the Iliad in scale, which some take as proof that writing was involved and others as proof only that Homer was better.

The Homeric Question and the current view

Ancient scholars already argued about the text. The chōrizontes ("separators") assigned the two epics to different poets; Zenodotus and Aristarchus at Alexandria athetised lines they thought spurious, and Aristarchus' judgements survive in the scholia.

The modern Question opens with Friedrich August Wolf's Prolegomena ad Homerum (1795), which argued that the poems were transmitted orally and shaped by later editors. Nineteenth-century Analysts (Lachmann, Kirchhoff, Wilamowitz) tried to dissect an original core from accretions, pointing to inconsistencies such as the dual verb forms in Book 9 (9.182–198), which seem to assume two ambassadors rather than three. Unitarians, culminating in Wolfgang Schadewaldt's Iliasstudien (1938), replied that the design of the whole is too coherent for a committee. Parry's work changed the terms: inconsistencies are what oral composition produces, and coherence is what a great singer produces.

Neoanalysis (Kakridis, Kullmann) added that the poet drew on specific earlier stories: the death of Patroclus and the fight over his body appear modelled on the death of Antilochus and Achilles' own death in the tradition later written down in the Aethiopis, which explains why the Iliad keeps forecasting Achilles' death in scenes it does not need.

On date and place, most scholars put the poem in Ionia or the eastern Aegean, in the second half of the eighth century BC, with the Odyssey somewhat later. The language is Ionic with an Aeolic layer, and the tradition preserves Bronze Age fossils: a boar's-tusk helmet (10.261–265), a Catalogue of Ships naming places abandoned after the Mycenaean collapse. The Greek alphabet was in use for hexameters by about 740–730 (the Dipylon jug, Nestor's cup). Hittite texts name a city Wilusa, generally equated with (W)Ilios, and Hisarlık shows a city (Troy VI/VIIa) destroyed around 1300 and again around 1180.

Where sources disagree. Richard Janko's statistical study of the language (1982) supports a date around 750–725. West and Walter Burkert argue for the seventh century, Burkert because the reference to Egyptian Thebes (9.381–384) would suit the period after 663. Nagy declines to assign a date at all. Whether the Trojan War happened is a separate question, argued between Korfmann and Kolb and treated in module 10.10. The evidence shows that Troy existed and was destroyed, not by whom.

What the poem leaves out

The Iliad assumes a listener who knows the story and declines to tell most of it. The causes of the war are glanced at only: the Judgement of Paris in one passage (24.25–30), Helen's abduction only through her own regret. There is no Iphigenia at Aulis (Agamemnon's daughters are three others, 9.144–145), no Palamedes, and Achilles has no invulnerable body: Asteropaeus draws his blood (21.166–167) and there is no heel.

At the other end the omissions are more deliberate still. The death of Achilles is foretold by Thetis (18.95–96), by Xanthus (19.416–417) and by the dying Hector (22.358–360), who names Paris and Apollo, but the poem ends with him alive. The Wooden Horse appears only in the Odyssey (4.271–289, 8.492–520) and the Cyclic Little Iliad and Sack of Ilion, known through Proclus' summaries. The fall of Troy is certain, stated by Zeus (15.69–71), by Hector (6.447–449) and by Agamemnon (4.164–165), but it is never shown. Priam's vision of his own body eaten by dogs at his door (22.66–76) lets the audience supply the ending the poet withholds.

The effect is that the Iliad is about the war without being the story of the war. By stopping at Hector's funeral the poet makes the burial of an enemy, not the capture of a city, the act the whole poem has been moving towards. This is what Aristotle meant by praising Homer for taking one part and using the rest as episodes: the omissions are the design.

Self-test

  1. What is the first word of the Iliad, and why does its normal usage matter?
    Answer

    Mēnis, wrath. In Homer the word is otherwise applied to gods, so giving it to Achilles marks his anger as something more than a human temper, a sanction against violated order (Muellner).

  2. Roughly how many days does the poem cover, and how many of them are days of battle?
    Answer

    About 51 days; four days of fighting (Books 2–7, 8, 11–18, 19–22), of which three are usually called the great battle days.

  3. Name three correspondences between Book 1 and Book 24 that support the ring-composition reading.
    Answer

    An old father (Chryses, Priam) comes to ransom a child; a twelve-day interval frames each book; Thetis travels between Achilles and Zeus in both; a divine quarrel in 1 is answered by a divine council in 24.

  4. Why is the death of Patroclus called the hinge of the poem?
    Answer

    Before it, the plot is driven by Achilles' wrath at Agamemnon; after it, by grief and the transferred wrath against Hector. Zeus states the whole causal chain in 15.59–71.

  5. What does Achilles reject in Book 9, and what does he accept?
    Answer

    He rejects the exchange of gifts for honour, because no goods equal a life (9.401–409), but he accepts and states the choice of a short life with imperishable fame (9.410–416).

  6. What does the Sarpedon episode in Book 16 show about Zeus and fate?
    Answer

    Zeus considers saving Sarpedon against his fate and desists because Hera warns of the other gods' disapproval, not because it is impossible, so fate is a norm Zeus upholds rather than an absolute limit.

  7. What did Milman Parry demonstrate about Homeric epithets, and why does it imply oral composition?
    Answer

    That the epithet system is extensive and economical: for each hero and metrical slot there is usually one epithet. Such a system serves a singer composing in performance, not a writer choosing words for sense.

  8. Which book was already suspected in antiquity of being a later addition, and what is its name?
    Answer

    Book 10, the Doloneia, the night raid of Diomedes and Odysseus.

  9. Give the mainstream date and place of the poem's composition, and one argument for a later date.
    Answer

    Ionia or the eastern Aegean, second half of the eighth century BC. Burkert argued for after 663 because 9.381–384 praises the wealth of Egyptian Thebes, which would suit that period.

  10. Name three major Trojan War events the Iliad does not narrate, and one passage where each is foreshadowed.
    Answer

    The death of Achilles (Hector's prophecy, 22.358–360); the Wooden Horse (not in the Iliad at all; Odyssey 8.492–520); the fall of Troy (Zeus, 15.69–71; Hector, 6.447–449).

Carry forward

  1. The Iliad is built on omission and compression: four days stand for ten years, and the audience supplies the fall of Troy the poet refuses to show. Later Greek literature, from the Epic Cycle to tragedy, works inside the space Homer left.
  2. Honour (timē) and fame (kleos) are an economy of exchange, and Achilles is the first figure to find that economy inadequate to a human life. Every later Greek hero, from Ajax to Antigone, is measured against that refusal.
  3. The poem is the end product of a long oral tradition, fixed in writing around the late eighth or seventh century; its formulae, type-scenes and inconsistencies are evidence of how it was made, and the Homeric Question is really the question of how an oral art became a text.
