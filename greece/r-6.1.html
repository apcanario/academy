The empire that came west: Cyrus, Cambyses, Darius

Herodotus opens with Cyrus (Book 1) because his whole work asks how a people the Greeks had scarcely heard of in 560 BC came to stand in Attica in 490. Cyrus overthrew the Median king Astyages (c. 550), defeated Croesus of Lydia and took Sardis (c. 546; Herodotus 1.84–86), and with Lydia inherited the Greek cities of the Ionian coast, which Croesus had made tributary. Babylon fell in 539 (1.188–191).

Cambyses (530–522) added Egypt (Book 3). Herodotus' portrait of him as a madman who stabbed the Apis bull is probably Egyptian priestly polemic; Egyptian evidence shows a conventional king.

Darius (522–486) came to the throne through the murder of a man who was either a usurping Magus or, as the more sceptical reading of his own Behistun inscription suggests, the real brother of Cambyses. Herodotus 3.70–88 tells of the seven conspirators; Behistun gives Darius' version. He organised satrapies with fixed tribute (3.89–97) and pushed west: a Scythian expedition across the Danube (Book 4), then Thrace and Macedon. By 500 the Ionian Greeks were the empire's westernmost subjects.

Where sources disagree. Herodotus (3.80–82) has the conspirators debate monarchy, oligarchy and democracy, and insists the debate happened though "some Greeks do not believe" it. Most scholars treat it as fifth-century Greek material, not Persian tradition.

The Ionian Revolt and the burning of Sardis, 499–494

The revolt began not from a surge of liberty but from the private trouble of Aristagoras, tyrant of Miletus, who had promised Artaphernes, satrap at Sardis, an easy conquest of Naxos and failed (Herodotus 5.30–35). Facing ruin, he raised Ionia, abolished the tyrannies, and went begging for help. Sparta refused (5.49–51). Athens sent twenty ships, Eretria five.

In 498 the Ionians and Athenians marched up from Ephesus and took Sardis. A soldier set fire to a reed-roofed house, the flames spread, and the temple of Cybebe burned with the city (5.100–102). The Greeks were beaten near Ephesus and the Athenians went home. Herodotus' set-piece is Darius hearing the news, shooting an arrow into the sky, praying to be allowed to punish the Athenians, and ordering a servant to say to him three times at every dinner, "Master, remember the Athenians" (5.105).

The revolt was ground down; the decisive battle was at sea off Lade in 494, where the Samian contingent deserted (6.7–17); Miletus was stormed, its men killed, its women and children deported to the Persian Gulf (6.18–20). Herodotus calls the Athenian ships the "beginning of evils" for Greeks and barbarians (5.97).

Where sources disagree. Herodotus treats the revolt as Aristagoras' opportunism; modern historians, following hints at 5.28 and the fact that the Ionians abolished tyranny everywhere, see economic pressure from Persian control of Black Sea trade and resentment of satrap-backed tyrants. Herodotus' contempt for Ionians (1.143) probably colours his reading.

The first expedition and Marathon, 490

Darius' first attempt, under his son-in-law Mardonius in 492, took Thasos and Macedon but lost its fleet in a storm off Mount Athos (6.43–45). In 490 Datis and Artaphernes sailed straight across the Aegean instead, with Hippias, the aged exiled tyrant of Athens, aboard. They burned Eretria (6.101) and Hippias guided them to the plain of Marathon (6.102).

The Athenians marched out under the polemarch Callimachus and ten generals including Miltiades. Before leaving they sent the runner Philippides to Sparta; he arrived the next day (6.105–106). The Spartans said they would come, but not before the full moon ended the Carneia festival. The Plataeans came in full force. The generals were split five to five; Miltiades won Callimachus' casting vote (6.109) and attacked on his own day of command.

Herodotus' battle is brief (6.111–117). The Athenians thinned their centre to match the Persian line and stacked the wings. They advanced "at a run" across roughly eight stades. The centre gave way to the Persians and Sacae; the wings won, turned inward and routed the enemy to the ships, where Callimachus died. Herodotus counts 6,400 Persian dead and 192 Athenian. The fleet rounded Sunium to attempt Athens, found the army already back, and went home. Two thousand Spartans arrived after the full moon, viewed the dead and praised the Athenians (6.120).

Miltiades' triumph lasted a year. He failed at Paros, was prosecuted by Xanthippus, fined fifty talents, and died of a wound (6.132–136).

Where sources disagree. The run from Marathon to Athens with the news and the runner's dying word is not in Herodotus. It first appears in Plutarch (On the Glory of Athens 347c), who names the runner Thersippus or Eucles, and in Lucian (On a Slip of the Tongue 3), who calls him Philippides and so fuses two stories. The modern marathon rests on Lucian. Herodotus' "run" is the whole phalanx charging. The Persian cavalry's absence from his battle is unexplained.

The interwar decade: Themistocles, Laurion, ostracism

Darius died in 486; Xerxes spent his first years on an Egyptian revolt, and Athens used the pause. Themistocles, archon in 493/2, had already begun fortifying Piraeus in preference to the open beach at Phalerum (Thucydides 1.93). In 483/2 a rich new vein of silver was struck at Maroneia in the Laurion mines. Themistocles persuaded the assembly to build two hundred warships with the surplus rather than distribute ten drachmas a head, ostensibly for the war with Aegina (7.144); Herodotus calls it the decision that saved Greece. Aristotle (Constitution of the Athenians 22.7) gives a different mechanism: a hundred talents lent to a hundred rich citizens, each to build a trireme. The figures do not reconcile; the policy is not in doubt.

Ostracism, the device Cleisthenes created and Athens first used in 487, removed the opposition. Each year the assembly could vote to hold one; the man named on most potsherds left for ten years, keeping his property. The first victim, Hipparchus son of Charmus, was a Pisistratid kinsman; Megacles the Alcmaeonid followed in 486, Xanthippus in 484, and Aristides, Themistocles' chief rival, in 482 (Aristotle, Constitution 22). Friends of tyranny and Persia went first, then opponents of the sea policy. Plutarch (Aristides 7) has an illiterate voter ask Aristides to write "Aristides" on his sherd. A deposit of 190 ostraka naming Themistocles, found on the Acropolis north slope, was written by about fourteen hands: ballots prepared for distribution. Ostracism was organised politics.

The exiles were recalled before Xerxes arrived.

Xerxes' invasion: canal, bridges, Thermopylae, Artemisium, 480

Book 7 opens with a council in which Mardonius urges war, Artabanus warns against it, and a dream twice compels Xerxes (7.8–18). Herodotus is explicit that the gods wanted him to go. A canal was cut through the isthmus behind Mount Athos (7.22–24); its line has been confirmed by coring. Two bridges of ships were thrown across the Hellespont at Abydos after a first pair broke in a storm and Xerxes had the sea whipped and the engineers beheaded (7.33–36). His army totals, 1,700,000 infantry and over five million souls (7.60, 7.184–186), are impossible; modern estimates run from 80,000 to 250,000. His 1,207 triremes are at least arguable.

The Greek response was a league sworn at the Isthmus in 481 under Spartan command; Argos, Crete and Syracuse stayed out, and Thessaly and most of Boeotia went over once a first position at Tempe was abandoned (7.173). The chosen line was Thermopylae on land and Artemisium at sea, mutually dependent.

Leonidas held the pass with about 7,000 men, including 300 Spartans chosen from those with living sons, 700 Thespians and 400 Thebans (7.202–205). For two days the Persians, Immortals included, failed to break the hoplites in the narrows. Then Ephialtes of Malis showed them the Anopaea path over the mountain; the Phocians guarding it fled (7.213–218). Leonidas dismissed most of the allies and died with the Spartans and Thespians; the Thebans surrendered. Herodotus records Dieneces' "we shall fight in the shade" (7.226), the epitaphs (7.228), and Aristodemus, the survivor who redeemed himself at Plataea (7.229–231).

At Artemisium the Greek fleet of 271 ships under the Spartan Eurybiades fought three indecisive days against a Persian fleet already mauled by storms (8.1–18); Themistocles used a Euboean bribe of thirty talents to keep Eurybiades and the Corinthians in place (8.4–5). On news of Thermopylae the fleet withdrew to Salamis.

Where sources disagree. Herodotus (7.220) says Leonidas dismissed the allies deliberately to win glory for Sparta and fulfil a Delphic oracle, but admits another version in which the allies simply left. Diodorus (11.9–10) adds a night attack on the Persian camp. Whether Leonidas was a sacrifice, a rearguard, or a man awaiting reinforcement remains open.

The evacuation of Athens and Salamis

Delphi, consulted twice, finally promised that "the wooden wall alone shall not fall" and said Salamis would destroy "the children of women" (7.141). Themistocles argued that the wooden wall meant the ships and that the children destroyed would be Persian (7.143). The city was evacuated to Salamis, Aegina and Troezen (8.41); a few held the Acropolis behind a wooden barricade until the Persians scaled the north face, killed them and burned the temples (8.51–53). The "Decree of Themistocles" found at Troezen in 1959 purports to record the order; it is a fourth-century text and its authenticity is disputed.

The Greek fleet, 378 ships (8.48), lay in the strait between Salamis and Attica. The Peloponnesians wanted to retreat to the Isthmus. Themistocles threatened to take the Athenians to Italy (8.62), then sent his slave Sicinnus by night to tell Xerxes the Greeks were about to flee (8.75). Aristides arrived from Aegina to report the exits closed (8.79). In the narrows Persian numbers became a liability; the Phoenicians broke first; Artemisia of Halicarnassus, Herodotus' compatriot, escaped by ramming a Persian ally (8.87–88); Xerxes watched from the shore.

Aeschylus' Persians, produced in 472, is the only surviving eyewitness account; its messenger speech agrees with Herodotus on the night message and dawn attack but counts 310 Greek ships. Xerxes withdrew to Asia, leaving Mardonius and the army in Thessaly (8.113–120).

Plataea, Mycale and the Serpent Column, 479

Over winter Mardonius sent Alexander of Macedon to detach Athens with an offer of autonomy. The Athenian reply, as Herodotus writes it, is a founding text: as long as the sun holds its course they will not come to terms with Xerxes, and they will not betray "the Greek thing", shared blood, shared tongue, shared sanctuaries and sacrifices, shared ways of life (8.143–144). Mardonius burned Athens a second time.

Sparta, after dawdling behind its Isthmus wall, marched under the regent Pausanias with 5,000 Spartiates; Herodotus' allied hoplite total is 38,700 (9.28–30). The armies faced each other across the Asopus for more than ten days. Persian horse fouled the Greek spring and cut supply lines; a night withdrawal went wrong and left the Spartans and Tegeans isolated. Mardonius attacked them, and the decisive fight was Spartan hoplites against Persian infantry behind a wall of wicker shields. Mardonius fell (9.63); the Persians broke and were slaughtered in their stockade. Pausanias refused the suggestion to impale Mardonius' corpse in revenge for Leonidas, saying such deeds were fit for barbarians (9.78–79).

On the same day, Herodotus claims, the Greek fleet under Leotychidas landed at Mycale in Ionia and destroyed the beached Persian fleet (9.100–101). The Ionians changed sides again. The Histories end with the Athenians besieging Sestos on the Hellespont in winter 479/8 (9.114–121).

From a tenth of the Plataea spoils the Greeks dedicated a gold tripod at Delphi, set on a bronze column of three intertwined serpents (9.81). Pausanias had it inscribed with a couplet naming himself; the Spartans erased it and the allies replaced it with the names of the thirty-one cities who fought, Lacedaemonians first, Athenians second (Thucydides 1.132; the names survive). The gold was melted by the Phocians in the Third Sacred War (Pausanias 10.13.9). Constantine moved the bronze column to his hippodrome in Istanbul, where it still stands, headless: the one monument of the war that can be seen and read today.

Where sources disagree. Simonides' Plataea elegy (P.Oxy. 3965, published 1992) gives Pausanias and the Spartans full Homeric credit; Herodotus is cool towards Pausanias and generous to Athens; Plutarch (Aristides 19) says he belittles the allies. The Spartan and Athenian shares of Plataea were contested within a generation.

Herodotus: method, sources, biases, trust

Herodotus of Halicarnassus was born around 484, a subject of Persia, and lived into the 420s. He wrote fifty years after the events, from oral tradition. His sources, where he names them, are the traditions of cities and great families (the long defence of the Alcmaeonids against the charge of signalling with a shield at Marathon, 6.121–131, shows a family source at work), Delphi, Persian logioi (1.1) and monuments he saw himself. His rule is at 7.152: he must report what is said, not believe all of it.

His biases are visible. He is pro-Athenian: 7.139, where he says Athens saved Greece and braces for the unpopularity of saying so, is the spine of the work. He follows Athenian slander of Corinth (8.94, which he notes other Greeks deny), is ambivalent about Themistocles, is hostile to Ionians and Thebans, inflates Persian numbers, and loves dreams and oracles. He also names Persian commanders correctly, knows Persian customs, and respects Persian courage as later Greek writers do not.

Why did the Greeks win? He gives several answers at once. Freedom made men fight: Demaratus tells Xerxes the Spartans are free but have a master, law, which they fear more than Persians fear the king (7.104), and Herodotus himself says Athens grew strong once its tyrants fell because free men work for themselves (5.78). Divine jealousy punishes excess: Artabanus warns that the god strikes down the tall (7.10). Themistocles says publicly that gods and heroes, not men, did the deed (8.109). Modern explanations add hoplite armour against lighter Persian infantry, Persian dependence on a fleet to feed an oversized army, and the Greek choice of narrow ground at every battle.

How far to trust him? Thucydides (1.20–22) implies he wrote to please; Plutarch wrote On the Malice of Herodotus. Detlev Fehling (1971) argued that Herodotus invented his source citations; Pritchett and most scholars rejected this, and archaeology has vindicated details such as the Athos canal and the Serpent Column names. The reasonable position: his sequence and the shape of the war are reliable; speeches, numbers, motives and Persian court scenes are literary; where he reports a dispute, the dispute is real.

Where sources disagree. For 480–479 the alternatives to Herodotus are thin. Aeschylus is a play; Simonides is fragmentary; Diodorus Book 11 depends on the fourth-century Ephorus and contradicts Herodotus on numbers; Ctesias, a Greek doctor at the Persian court around 400, survives only in epitome and is trusted by no one. Herodotus is the mainstream because he is the only full one.

The war and Greek identity; the Athenian claim

Before 480 the word barbaros meant a speaker of babble, no more. After it, the Persian became the barbarian: the despot's slave, whipped into battle (7.223), luxurious and servile, against the free hoplite who fights for his own. Aeschylus' Persians first builds the contrast fully; Edith Hall's Inventing the Barbarian (1989) traces how tragedy fixed it within a generation. Herodotus is subtler than his successors, but the polarity runs through Books 7 to 9, and 8.144 is the earliest surviving definition of Hellenic identity by blood, language, religion and custom.

The war gave the Greeks shared monuments: the Marathon mound, the Thermopylae epitaphs, the Serpent Column, the Stoa Poikile with Marathon painted beside the Trojan War, the burnt Acropolis temples left unrepaired for a generation. Plataea founded a cult of Zeus the Liberator and the Eleutheria festival. The "Oath of Plataea", preserved in Lycurgus (Against Leocrates 80–81), Diodorus 11.29 and a fourth-century stele from Acharnae, was already called a forgery by Theopompus.

Athens turned the war into title. The argument is laid out by the Athenian envoys at Sparta in Thucydides (1.73–75): we fought alone at Marathon, we gave the fleet, the general and the resolve at Salamis, and so we deserved the empire we took. The Delian League of 478/7, formed after Pausanias disgraced himself and the Ionians asked Athens to lead (Thucydides 1.95–96), was formally a war alliance against Persia; it became the Athenian empire, and every funeral oration recited Marathon and Salamis as its warrant. Herodotus, writing while that empire fought Sparta, knew what his 7.139 would be used for and said it anyway. The Persian Wars are the point at which the Greeks began to describe themselves to themselves, and the Athenians were the loudest voice in the description.

Self-test

  1. What private failure of Aristagoras of Miletus triggered the Ionian Revolt, according to Herodotus?
    Answer

    His failed expedition against Naxos, promised to the satrap Artaphernes and funded by Persia; facing ruin, he raised Ionia in revolt (Herodotus 5.30–35).

  2. What did Darius order a servant to say to him at dinner after Sardis was burned?
    Answer

    "Master, remember the Athenians", three times at every meal (Herodotus 5.105).

  3. Why were the Spartans absent from Marathon, and when did they arrive?
    Answer

    They were observing the Carneia festival and would not march before the full moon; 2,000 arrived after the battle, having marched from Sparta in three days (Herodotus 6.106, 6.120).

  4. Does Herodotus report a runner carrying news from Marathon to Athens?
    Answer

    No. He reports Philippides running to Sparta before the battle. The run to Athens appears first in Plutarch and Lucian; Herodotus' "run" is the phalanx charging.

  5. How did Themistocles get the Athenians to build a fleet, in Herodotus' version?
    Answer

    He persuaded them to spend the Laurion silver surplus on 200 ships instead of a ten-drachma distribution, nominally for the war with Aegina (Herodotus 7.144). Aristotle gives a different mechanism of 100 talents lent to 100 rich men.

  6. Who was ostracised in 482, and why does that matter?
    Answer

    Aristides, Themistocles' main rival; his removal cleared the way for the naval policy. He was recalled before Salamis and commanded at Plataea.

  7. Name two engineering works Xerxes undertook for the invasion.
    Answer

    The canal through the Athos isthmus (Herodotus 7.22–24) and the twin ship-bridges over the Hellespont at Abydos (7.33–36).

  8. What was the "wooden wall" in the Delphic oracle, and how did Themistocles interpret it?
    Answer

    The oracle said the wooden wall alone would not fall; Themistocles argued it meant the ships, not the Acropolis palisade, and that Salamis would destroy Persian, not Greek, "children of women" (Herodotus 7.141–143).

  9. What happened to the inscription Pausanias put on the Serpent Column?
    Answer

    The Spartans erased his self-glorifying couplet and the allies replaced it with the names of the thirty-one cities that fought (Thucydides 1.132); the column stands in Istanbul.

  10. State Herodotus' rule for handling what he is told, and the passage where he states it.
    Answer

    He is obliged to report what is said but not to believe all of it, and this applies to his whole work (Herodotus 7.152).

Carry forward

  1. Herodotus is a single oral-tradition historian writing fifty years on: trust his sequence and his honestly reported disputes, not his numbers, speeches or Persian court scenes.
  2. The war created the Greek-barbarian polarity and the freedom-versus-slavery frame; the Athenian definition of Greekness at Herodotus 8.144 is where Hellenic identity is first stated.
  3. Athens converted Marathon and Salamis into a claim to lead; the Delian League and the Athenian empire of the next module rest on that claim, and Sparta's resentment of it is the road to the Peloponnesian War.
