What "lesser" means: cult and literature

The Greeks had no word for a "minor god". Every being below is a theos or daimon somewhere; what varies is whether anyone built an altar. The useful distinction is not rank but mode of existence: cult (sanctuary, festival, sacrifice, votives) against literature (Hesiod's genealogies, Homer's messenger scenes, tragedy, Hellenistic poetry), with a middle group of real but local cult and an outsized literary afterlife.

Two controls run through the survey: Hesiod, Theogony (c. 700 BC) for genealogy, and Pausanias, Description of Greece (2nd century AD) for who actually had altars. A figure in Hesiod but absent from Pausanias and inscriptions has an uncertain cult. Aristophanes, Peace 406–413, where Greeks sacrifice to the Olympians "whereas the barbarians sacrifice to Sun and Moon", shows how marginal even Helios was on the mainland.

Muses, Charites, Horai

The Muses

Hesiod names nine at Theogony 75–79, daughters of Zeus and Mnemosyne (Memory), Calliope "chief of them all" because she attends kings. He gives no spheres; the one-art-each scheme is Hellenistic and Roman systematisation.

Where sources disagree. Pausanias 9.29.2–4 records that the original Muses of Helicon were three (Melete, Mneme, Aoide: Practice, Memory, Song), that Mimnermus knew two generations, and that Alcman made them daughters of Uranus and Gaia. The canonical nine is Hesiod's victory, not a universal datum.

Their cult was genuine but institutional: the Valley of the Muses at Thespiae with the Mouseia contests (Pausanias 9.29–31), shrines in the Academy and Lyceum, the Alexandrian Mouseion with its priest.

The Charites

Hesiod, Theogony 907–911: Aglaea, Euphrosyne and Thalia, daughters of Zeus and Eurynome. Homer knows an older and a younger set, since Hera bribes Hypnos with Pasithea, "one of the younger Charites" (Iliad 14.267–276); Pausanias 9.35.1–7 reports two at Sparta and two at Athens. Their one serious cult centre was Orchomenus, where they were worshipped as stones fallen from heaven (Pindar, Olympian 14).

The Horai

In Hesiod (Theogony 901–903) they are daughters of Zeus and Themis with political names: Eunomia, Dike, Eirene (Good Order, Justice, Peace). In Attic cult they were seasonal: Thallo, Auxo and Carpo (Pausanias 9.35.2). In Homer they roll back the cloud-gates of Olympus (Iliad 5.749–751). The gap shows a poet rewriting a cult group to fit a theology.

Fate, order and retribution

The Moirai

Clotho, Lachesis and Atropos (Spinner, Apportioner, Unturnable) appear twice in the Theogony: at 217–222 as daughters of Nyx, at 904–906 as daughters of Zeus and Themis. Homer mostly uses moira as an impersonal portion; Plato gives them the spindle of Necessity (Republic 617c).

Where sources disagree. Hesiod's double genealogy is a real contradiction. West (1966) reads the Zeus–Themis version as the poet's re-assignment, subordinating fate to Zeus's justice; some suspect interpolation. The tension persists in Homer, where Zeus weighs saving Hector against what is fated (Iliad 22.167–185).

Cult was modest but real: a temple at Corinth (Pausanias 2.4.7), altars at Sicyon, Sparta and Olympia, where Zeus was Moiragetes, "Leader of the Fates" (5.15.5); at Delphi only two Moirai stood, with Zeus and Apollo Moiragetes as the third (10.24.4).

Themis and Dike

Themis, Titaness daughter of Gaia and Uranus (Theogony 135), is Zeus's consort and mother of the Horai and Moirai; her name means established right. She convenes the gods in Iliad 20.4–6 and holds the Delphic oracle between Gaia and Apollo (Aeschylus, Eumenides 1–8; Pausanias 10.5.6). She had cult: a small temple beside Nemesis at Rhamnous, sanctuaries at Thebes, Athens and Olympia.

Dike is the opposite case: enormous literary presence, almost no cult. In Works and Days 256–262 she sits beside Zeus reporting men's crooked judgements; Parmenides's proem gives her the keys of the gates of Day and Night; Aratus, Phaenomena 96–136, has her abandon the Bronze Age earth and become Virgo. Pausanias records no temple.

The Erinyes

Hesiod makes them spring from the blood of Uranus's castration (Theogony 183–187); Aeschylus calls them daughters of Night (Eumenides 321). In Homer they punish perjury (Iliad 19.259–260), enforce a mother's curse (9.571) and silence Achilles's talking horse (19.418): guardians of natural order as much as of blood-guilt. The names Alecto, Megaera and Tisiphone are late (Apollodorus, Library 1.1.4).

Where sources disagree. At Athens the goddesses of the Areopagus were the Semnai Theai (Pausanias 1.28.6), at Colonus and Sicyon the Eumenides. Aeschylus identifies them with the Erinyes who hound Orestes; Lloyd-Jones and A. L. Brown argued the identification was the playwright's own move, others that the euphemism always masked the Erinyes. The cult is certain; its original referent is not.

Nemesis and Tyche

Nemesis is a daughter of Nyx (Theogony 223). The Cypria made her, not Leda, the mother of Helen, laying an egg that Leda hatched (Apollodorus 3.10.7). Her cult at Rhamnous was substantial: a 5th-century temple with a statue by Agoracritus, which Pausanias 1.33.2–8 ties to the punished arrogance of the Persians at Marathon. Under Rome she presided over the arena.

Tyche begins as a name in the Oceanid catalogue (Theogony 360). Archilochus fr. 16 says "Tyche and Moira give everything"; Pindar, Olympian 12, hails her as Tyche Soteira of Himera. Her cult grew over time: temples at Thebes, Elis and Sicyon, a first statue by Bupalus (Pausanias 4.30.6), and in Hellenistic cities the civic Tyche modelled on Eutychides's Tyche of Antioch.

Nike and Eris

Nike is a daughter of Pallas and Styx, brought with Zelus, Kratos and Bia to Zeus's side before the Titanomachy (Theogony 383–403). Her cult is nearly always attached: the Acropolis temple is of Athena Nike, and Zeus at Olympia holds a Nike in his hand (Pausanias 5.11.1). She is a divine attribute given a body.

Eris, Ares's sister in Iliad 4.440–445, is Nyx's daughter in Theogony 225–232. Works and Days 11–26 splits her into a bad Eris who breeds war and a good one who makes potter envy potter. The golden apple belongs to the Cypria and Apollodorus, Epitome 3.2. She never received cult.

Nymphs are long-lived, not immortal: a Hesiodic fragment in Plutarch (Obsolescence of Oracles 415c) gives them ten phoenix-lives. Their cult is the most widespread in this module: cave sanctuaries at Vari in Attica, the Corycian cave above Delphi, the Pitsa cave near Sicyon with its painted votive panel of c. 540 BC. They are the opposite of literary gods.

Pan

Homeric Hymn 19 makes him the goat-legged son of Hermes and a daughter of Dryops; Herodotus 2.145 gives Penelope as mother. He was the great god of Arcadia (Pausanias 8.36.8, 8.38.5). Herodotus 6.105 tells how Philippides met Pan before Marathon and how Athens then founded his cave shrine below the Acropolis with a torch-race. A "lesser" god can hold a major cult.

Satyrs, Silenoi and Priapus

Hesiod fr. 123 M–W (in Strabo 10.3.19) calls the satyrs "worthless and good for nothing"; Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite 262 has Silenoi mating with nymphs in caves. In Attic painting they are horse-tailed, snub-nosed men in Dionysus's train; goat features come later. Silenus singular is the drunken sage captured by Midas (Herodotus 8.138). Satyr play kept them on stage; Euripides's Cyclops survives.

Where sources disagree. The older view distinguished horse-like Ionian Silenoi from goat-like Peloponnesian Satyrs; Hedreen and Lissarrague argue the terms were interchangeable in the classical period and the goat form Hellenistic. Satyrs had no cult of their own.

Priapus is absent from Homer and Hesiod: a god of Lampsacus, son of Dionysus and Aphrodite (Pausanias 9.31.2), whom Strabo 13.1.12 calls a recent god unknown to the old poets. Civic cult at Lampsacus was real; elsewhere he was a garden scarecrow with a Latin afterlife in the Priapea.

River gods

Hesiod lists twenty-five rivers as sons of Oceanus and Tethys (Theogony 337–345). Homer makes them actors: Scamander rises against Achilles (Iliad 21.211–382) and receives bulls and living horses (21.130–132); Peleus vows Achilles's hair to Spercheus (23.142–151); Achelous wrestles Heracles for Deianeira (Sophocles, Trachiniae 9–21). River cult was ubiquitous: hair offerings, bull sacrifice, altars of the Inachus and Cephisus, the bull-horned river head on coins across the Greek world.

The sea

Pontus, the Sea, is born of Gaia without a father (Theogony 131–132) and begets Nereus, Thaumas, Phorcys, Ceto and Eurybia (233–239). He is pure genealogy; no one sacrificed to Pontus.

Nereus, "truthful and gentle" (Theogony 233–236), is the Old Man of the Sea whom Heracles wrestles for the way to the Hesperides (Apollodorus 2.5.11). Proteus is his functional double: in Odyssey 4.351–570 he herds seals at Pharos and Menelaus pins him through his transformations to extract prophecy.

Where sources disagree. Herodotus 2.112–120 turns Proteus into a mortal king of Memphis who kept Helen while the Greeks fought at Troy for a phantom; Euripides's Helen follows. Most scholars see a rationalisation drawing on Stesichorus's Palinode or Egyptian informants; the direction of borrowing is uncertain.

Thetis, Nereid and mother of Achilles, is the most consequential of the group. Zeus and Poseidon desired her until Themis prophesied that her son would surpass his father, so she was married down to Peleus (Pindar, Isthmian 8.26–47; a different motive in Iliad 24.59–63). She had cult: a Thetideion near Pharsalus (Euripides, Andromache 20) and a hidden ancient image at Sparta (Pausanias 3.14.4). Slatkin's The Power of Thetis (1991) argues the Iliad presupposes a cosmic Thetis whose power the poem suppresses.

Amphitrite, a Nereid (Theogony 243), marries Poseidon and bears Triton (930–933); in the Odyssey her name is a metonym for the sea. She stands in Poseidon's Isthmian statue group (Pausanias 2.1.7) but has no shrine of her own. Triton guides the Argo out of Lake Tritonis (Apollonius, Argonautica 4.1551–1619); by Pausanias's day Tritons were a species, and he saw a pickled one at Tanagra (9.20.4).

Glaucus of Anthedon was a fisherman who ate a herb, became immortal and leapt into the sea (Pausanias 9.22.6–7; variants in Athenaeus 7.296–297). He prophesies to the Argonauts (Apollonius 1.1310–1328) and loves Scylla in Ovid (Metamorphoses 13). Anthedon honoured him; beyond that his cult is uncertain.

Sky and light

Helios, Selene and Eos are children of Hyperion and Theia (Theogony 371–374). Helios sees all and witnesses oaths (Iliad 3.277); his cattle doom Odysseus's crew (Odyssey 12). His cult was real but narrow: Rhodes, his portion at the division of the world (Pindar, Olympian 7.54–76), with the Halieia and the Colossus; Acrocorinth (Pausanias 2.4.6). Selene loves the ever-sleeping Endymion (Apollodorus 1.7.5); her cult is scarcely attested. Eos bears the winds and stars to Astraeus (Theogony 378–382) and carries off mortal lovers, Tithonus above all (Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite 218–238; Sappho's Tithonus poem). She had no cult.

Iris, daughter of Thaumas and Electra, sister of the Harpies (Theogony 265–269), is the gods' messenger throughout the Iliad and absent from the Odyssey, where Hermes takes over. Her one attested cult is Delian: Semus of Delos (in Athenaeus 14.645b) says the islanders offered her honey-cakes.

The winds. Hesiod gives Eos and Astraeus three sons, Boreas, Notus and Zephyrus, distinct from Typhon's destructive blasts (Theogony 378–380, 869–880). Achilles prays to Boreas and Zephyrus to kindle Patroclus's pyre (Iliad 23.194–216). When Boreas's storm wrecked the Persian fleet off Artemisium the Athenians built him an altar on the Ilissus (Herodotus 7.189); Pausanias 8.36.6 saw annual sacrifice to him at Megalopolis, and Xenophon's army sacrificed to him in Armenia (Anabasis 4.5.4). Wind cult was practical and real.

Pleiades and Hyades, daughters of Atlas, belong to the farmer's calendar: Hesiod times ploughing and sailing by them (Works and Days 383–384, 615–620). Apollodorus 3.10.1 names the seven: Alcyone, Merope, Celaeno, Electra, Sterope, Taygete, Maia. Aratus, Phaenomena 254–263, notes that only six are visible; the lost Pleiad is Merope, ashamed of her mortal husband, or Electra, grieving for Troy. The Hyades, "the rainy ones", were nurses of Dionysus or sisters who wept themselves to death for their brother Hyas (Hyginus, Fabulae 192). Both are stars with stories, never gods with altars.

Healer, attendants, twins

Asclepius

Homer knows him only as "the blameless physician", father of the army doctors Machaon and Podalirius (Iliad 2.731, 4.194). Pindar, Pythian 3, tells the story: Coronis, pregnant by Apollo, sleeps with Ischys and is killed; Apollo saves the infant from the pyre and gives him to Chiron; Asclepius heals until, bribed to raise the dead, he is struck by Zeus's thunderbolt. Then the reversal: from the 5th century he is a god with the largest healing cult in the Mediterranean. Epidaurus claimed his birth (Pausanias 2.26.3–7) and produced the 4th-century iamata inscriptions recording cures by incubation; Athens received him in 420/419 BC; Cos, Pergamum and, in 293 BC, Rome followed (Livy 10.47). Hygieia, Panacea, Iaso and the hooded child Telesphorus grew around him.

Where sources disagree. Hero raised to godhood, or god given a hero's biography? Pausanias 2.26 reports rival birthplaces (Epidaurus, Tricca, Messenia). The Edelsteins (1945) argued for a hero deified in the 6th–5th centuries; others reverse the sequence. The Thessalian origin in Strabo 9.5.17 is plausible but unprovable.

Hebe and Ganymede

Hebe (Youth), daughter of Zeus and Hera (Theogony 922), pours nectar in Iliad 4.2 and marries the deified Heracles (Odyssey 11.603). Her cult is small but striking: at Phlius, as Ganymeda, a cypress grove where freed slaves hung up their fetters (Pausanias 2.13.3–4).

Ganymede, son of Tros, "most beautiful of mortal men", is snatched up to be Zeus's wine-pourer, his father compensated with immortal horses (Iliad 20.231–235; Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite 202–217). Theognis 1345–1348 makes the motive erotic; Pindar, Olympian 1.44, follows; Plato's Laws 636c says the Cretans invented the tale to justify their customs.

Where sources disagree. Homer never says Zeus loved Ganymede; the Hymn says he was taken "for his beauty". Whether that is reticence or the erotic reading a 6th-century innovation is debated (Dover leaned to the latter). Ganymede never had cult.

The Dioscuri

Castor and Polydeuces are the limiting case: in Homer they are dead. Helen looks for them from the walls, not knowing that "the life-giving earth already held them" (Iliad 3.236–244); the Odyssey has them "alive and dead on alternate days" (11.298–304). Homeric Hymn 33 makes both sons of Zeus and saviours of sailors; Pindar, Nemean 10.55–90, has Polydeuces share his immortality rather than live without his brother.

Where sources disagree. The father is unstable (Tyndareus in Homer; Zeus for both in Hesiod fr. 24 M–W; Zeus for one in the Cypria). Comparatists (Ward 1968; West 2007) see inherited Indo-European horse-twins cognate with the Vedic Aśvins, old gods given a Spartan biography; others see hero cult elevated to godhood. The evidence does not settle it.

Their cult was strong: at Sparta, represented by the twin beams of the dokana, one twin accompanying each king on campaign until 506 BC (Herodotus 5.75); at Athens as the Anakes; and from 484 BC at Rome as Castor and Pollux (Livy 2.42). The theoxenia, a meal laid for the twins as guests, is attested in Pindar and in Cicero, De oratore 2.86.

Hesiod's abstractions, and the balance of cult

At Theogony 211–232 Night bears, alone, Moros (Doom), Ker, Thanatos (Death), Hypnos (Sleep), the Dreams, Momus (Blame), Oizys (Misery), the Hesperides, the Moirai and Keres, Nemesis, Apate (Deceit), Philotes, Geras (Old Age) and Eris. Eris then bears Ponos (Toil), Lethe, Limos (Famine), the Algea (Pains), Hysminai and Machai (Combats, Battles), Phonoi and Androktasiai (Murders, Manslaughters), Neikea (Quarrels), Pseudea (Lies), Logoi, Amphillogiai (Disputes), Dysnomia, Ate (Ruin) and Horkos (Oath), "who most afflicts men when anyone swears falsely".

This is cosmology by genealogy: dark things are kin because they arrive together. Hypnos and Thanatos are twins who carry Sarpedon's body home (Iliad 16.671–683) and live at the house of Night, Sleep gentle, Death with a heart of iron (Theogony 758–766). Euripides puts Thanatos on stage in Alcestis; Geras appears on vases as a shrivelled old man beaten by Heracles. Few crossed into worship: Hypnos had a statue in the Asclepieion at Sicyon and an altar at Troezen (Pausanias 2.10.2, 2.31.3), fitting a cult that healed by dream; Plutarch, Cleomenes 9, says Sparta had shrines of Phobos, Thanatos and Gelos (Laughter). The rest never left the page.

Cult clusters where a power touched daily life at a fixed place: a spring, a river, a cave, a sickbed, a storm, a battlefield. Literature clusters where a power is a concept or a stage in a story: the sea as such, the dawn, justice, discord, the beautiful boy. The middle column holds figures a single city made its own. Hesiod built one family out of both kinds, so that Dike and Pan, who never shared an altar, share a genealogy.

Self-test

  1. Name Hesiod's nine Muses and say who, in Hesiod, is their mother.
    Answer

    Clio, Euterpe, Thalia, Melpomene, Terpsichore, Erato, Polymnia, Ourania and Calliope (Theogony 75–79), daughters of Zeus and Mnemosyne. Their artistic spheres are not Hesiodic.

  2. Where does Hesiod contradict himself about the Moirai, and why does it matter?
    Answer

    Daughters of Nyx at Theogony 217–222 and of Zeus and Themis at 904–906. The second subordinates fate to Zeus's just order; the first keeps it among the primordial dark powers.

  3. Which Attic sanctuary gave Nemesis a major cult, and to what event did Pausanias connect it?
    Answer

    Rhamnous, with a cult statue by Agoracritus; Pausanias 1.33 linked it to the punished arrogance of the Persians at Marathon.

  4. How do Hesiod's Horai differ from the Horai worshipped at Athens?
    Answer

    Hesiod's are moral and political (Eunomia, Dike, Eirene); Athens' were seasonal (Thallo, Auxo, Carpo; Pausanias 9.35.2).

  5. What distinguishes a Hamadryad from other nymphs, and which sources make the point?
    Answer

    Her life is bound to one tree and ends with it: Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite 257–272 and Apollonius, Argonautica 2.476–489.

  6. How did Pan's cult reach Athens, according to Herodotus?
    Answer

    Philippides met Pan before Marathon, and Athens then founded his cave shrine below the Acropolis with a torch-race (Herodotus 6.105).

  7. Contrast Homer's Proteus with Herodotus's.
    Answer

    In Odyssey 4 a shape-shifting prophetic sea god herding seals at Pharos; in Herodotus 2.112–120 a mortal king of Memphis who kept Helen while the Greeks fought for a phantom.

  8. In what sense is Asclepius a "promoted" figure, and what evidence shows his cult at work?
    Answer

    Homer makes him a mortal physician and Pindar has Zeus kill him, yet from the 5th century he is a god at Epidaurus, Athens, Cos, Pergamum and Rome. The Epidaurian iamata inscriptions record cures by incubation.

  9. Which winds does Hesiod make sons of Eos and Astraeus, and which wind received Athenian cult and why?
    Answer

    Boreas, Notus and Zephyrus (Theogony 378–380). Boreas gained an altar on the Ilissus after his storm wrecked the Persian fleet off Artemisium (Herodotus 7.189).

  10. Give one of Night's children in Hesiod that did receive cult, and one that did not.
    Answer

    Hypnos had a statue at Sicyon and an altar at Troezen (Pausanias 2.10.2, 2.31.3), and Thanatos a Spartan shrine per Plutarch. Geras, Apate, Momus and Oizys had none.

Carry forward

  1. Cult and literature are separate axes. A figure's prominence in poetry (Eos, Dike, Eris) says nothing about whether anyone sacrificed to it; judge worship by Pausanias and inscriptions, not by Hesiod.
  2. Hesiod's Theogony is an argument, not a record: it rewrites existing cult groups (Horai, Moirai) into a theology of Zeus's order, and later poets and cities rewrote it back. Expect every genealogy to have a rival.
  3. Status moves in both directions. Asclepius rose from Homeric mortal to god of the largest healing cult in the Mediterranean; the Dioscuri are dead men in the Iliad and saviours of sailors in the Homeric Hymn; Tyche grew from a name in a list to patroness of Hellenistic cities. The same machinery applies when later modules meet Heracles, Dionysus and the hero cults.
