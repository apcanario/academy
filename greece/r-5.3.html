01The hoplite panoply and the phalanx

The hoplite took his name from his hopla, his arms, and the kit defined the man. Its centrepiece was the aspis: a round wooden shield about 90 cm across, bronze-faced, held by a central arm-band (porpax) and a hand-grip at the rim (antilabe). That double grip is the diagnostic feature. Worn on the forearm, the shield projects to the bearer's left and covers the exposed side of his neighbour, which only makes sense in a line. With it went a bronze helmet (the face-enclosing "Corinthian" type is classic), a bell cuirass, greaves, a thrusting spear of some 2 to 2.5 m with iron head and bronze butt-spike, and a short sword. The full kit weighed 20 to 30 kg and was costly: the hoplite was a man of property.

The archaeology dates the pieces. A warrior grave at Argos of about 720 BC held a bell cuirass and helmet; the double-grip shield appears around 700 to 675 BC. The earliest clear picture of massed ranks is the Protocorinthian Chigi olpe of about 650 to 640 BC. Tyrtaeus of Sparta, in the mid-seventh century, describes men standing "foot by foot, shield pressed on shield" (fr. 11 West); the Iliad mostly shows duels, but 13.130–133 has "shield on shield, helmet on helmet, man on man". The classical phalanx was a close-ordered block, usually eight deep, that advanced in step and broke the enemy by push and spear. Whether this already fits the seventh century, or is a fifth-century form read backwards, is the heart of the next debate.

02The "hoplite revolution" and the Lelantine War

Aristotle supplied the thesis. At Politics 4.1297b16–28 he says the first constitutions after the kingships were cavalry-based, since infantry was "useless without organisation"; as cities grew and "those with heavy arms grew stronger, more people came to share in the constitution". From this the modern "hoplite revolution" was built: a new class of middling farmers, indispensable in the phalanx, demanded political weight and broke the aristocratic monopoly, often by backing a tyrant. Antony Andrewes' The Greek Tyrants (1956) gave it classic form, noting that Nicolaus of Damascus makes Cypselus of Corinth polemarch before his coup.

Where sources disagree. Anthony Snodgrass (1965) showed that the panoply was adopted piecemeal over fifty years, which fits evolution rather than revolution. Hans van Wees (Greek Warfare: Myths and Realities, 2004) and Peter Krentz argue that archaic fighting stayed open and fluid, with archers and light-armed men in the line (so Tyrtaeus fr. 11.35–38), and that the rigid phalanx is classical. Victor Davis Hanson and Donald Kagan defend the orthodox view; the exchange is in Kagan and Viggiano, Men of Bronze (2013). The political half has fared worst: no archaic source shows hoplites acting as a class, and most tyrants are better explained as winners of aristocratic feuds. Aristotle's schema is a fourth-century theory, not a record.

The Lelantine War is the test case for early warfare. Chalcis and Eretria fought over the plain between them, conventionally around 700 BC. Thucydides 1.15.3 calls it the one pre-Persian war in which "the rest of Hellas" divided into alliances; Herodotus 5.99 explains that Samos had helped Chalcis and Miletus Eretria, so that in 499 Eretria repaid the debt. Hesiod won a tripod at the funeral games of Amphidamas at Chalcis (Works and Days 654–659), and later tradition made Amphidamas a casualty. Strabo 10.1.12 reports a stele at Amarynthus recording a pact to ban missiles, and Archilochus fr. 3 West speaks of fighting "not with bows or slings" but with swords, "the war-work of the spear-famed lords of Euboea". Chalcis seems to have won.

Where sources disagree. All of this is reconstruction from scraps. Victor Parker (1997) and others argue that the "great war with alliances" is a construct Thucydides built from a long local feud and scattered colonial rivalries, and that the missile ban, if real, belongs to aristocratic self-image rather than the phalanx. The date is uncertain within half a century.

03Lawgivers and the first written codes

Writing law down was political: it took the law out of the memory of aristocratic judges. Tradition names three archaic lawgivers of very different solidity.

Zaleucus of Locri in southern Italy, placed around 660 BC, was reputedly the first Greek to write a code; Ephorus (in Strabo 6.1.8) credits him with fixing penalties in advance rather than leaving them to the judge. Aristotle names him with Charondas of Catana (Politics 2.1274a22–31); Timaeus denied he had existed at all (Cicero, De Legibus 2.15).

Draco of Athens is solid in one respect: his homicide law was republished on stone in 409/8 BC and partly survives (IG I³ 104), distinguishing intentional from unintentional killing. His date is 621/0 (Athenian Constitution 4.1). Aristotle says his laws had "nothing distinctive except severity" (Politics 2.1274b15–18); Plutarch, Solon 17, preserves Demades' joke that they were written in blood. Solon repealed all but the homicide law.

Where sources disagree. Athenian Constitution 4 describes a full "constitution of Draco" with a hoplite franchise and a council of 401. Most scholars, P. J. Rhodes among them, regard the chapter as a late fifth- or fourth-century insertion, perhaps oligarchic propaganda of 411. Draco the law-writer is real; Draco the constitution-maker probably is not.

Lycurgus of Sparta is the limiting case. Plutarch opens his Life admitting that nothing about him is undisputed, including his existence. Herodotus 1.65–66 has him receive the constitution from Delphi or from Crete; Thucydides 1.18.1 dates Spartan good order to more than four centuries before 404; Aristotle makes him guardian of King Charillus (Politics 2.1271b25). Tyrtaeus' Eunomia (fr. 4 West) and the "Great Rhetra" in Plutarch, Lycurgus 6, show institutions being fixed in the seventh century but name no lawgiver. "Lycurgus" is best read as the name Sparta gave a long process so it could be called a single, god-given act.

What actually survives in stone is Cretan. The earliest Greek law inscription, from Dreros (c. 650–600 BC), forbids the kosmos, the chief magistrate, to hold office again for ten years. The Great Code of Gortyn, about 450 BC, is later than our period but the only extensive Greek code we have: some 600 lines in twelve boustrophedon columns on a public wall, regulating rape and adultery (fines graded by status), divorce and property division, the heiress and whom she must marry, adoption, debt and the position of the serf-like woikeus. Earlier Gortynian fragments reach into the seventh century. Both confirm that the first written laws were less about crime than about limiting magistrates and settling property.

Pheidon of Argos

Aristotle calls Pheidon a king who turned kingship into tyranny (Politics 5.1310b26). Ephorus (Strabo 8.3.33) makes him tenth from Temenus, says he recovered the whole "lot of Temenus", and credits him with the "Pheidonian" measures and even silver coinage. Herodotus 6.127 calls him the most arrogant of Greeks for expelling the Elean judges and running the Olympic games himself.

Where sources disagree. Pausanias 6.22.2 puts his Olympiad in 748; many emend to 668, beside the Argive victory over Sparta at Hysiae (669). Herodotus makes his son a suitor around 575, a century later. The coinage claim is anachronistic, since no Greek coined before the mid-sixth century. His date is unknown within 150 years.

Cypselus and Periander of Corinth

Herodotus tells the Cypselid story through the Corinthian Socles, warning Sparta against restoring Hippias (5.92). The Bacchiad clan ruled as a closed oligarchy; an oracle foretold that the child of the lame Bacchiad Labda, married out of the clan, would destroy them, and his mother hid the baby from ten assassins in a chest (kypsele). Cypselus overthrew the Bacchiads and ruled about thirty years; Aristotle classes him as a tyrant who rose by popular leadership and notes he kept no bodyguard (5.1310b29, 1315b27–28). Corinth under the Cypselids founded Ambracia, Leucas and Potidaea.

Periander is Herodotus' type of the tyrant turned monster. Thrasybulus of Miletus answered his request for advice by lopping every ear of corn that stood above the rest (5.92ζ). He killed his wife Melissa and burned the clothes of Corinth's women to warm her ghost (5.92η); he quarrelled with his son Lycophron and sent 300 Corcyraean boys to Lydia for castration (3.48–53). Yet the same man appears in lists of the Seven Sages, is credited with the diolkos across the Isthmus, and ruled forty years. Aristotle counts the dynasty at seventy-three and a half years (5.1315b22–29).

Cleisthenes of Sicyon

The Orthagorids lasted a century, the longest tyranny Aristotle knew, because they treated subjects moderately and largely obeyed the laws (Politics 5.1315b12–21). Cleisthenes fought Argos, banned rhapsodes for Homer's praise of Argives, and tried to oust the Argive hero Adrastus by importing his enemy Melanippus from Thebes (Herodotus 5.67). He renamed the Dorian tribes Hyatai, Oneatai and Choireatai, "pig-men, ass-men and swine-men", and his own Archelaoi, "rulers of the people" (5.68). The contest for his daughter Agariste, ending with Hippocleides dancing on a table (6.126–130), gave her to the Alcmaeonid Megacles; their grandson was the Athenian reformer Cleisthenes. Herodotus reads the tribe names as anti-Dorian contempt; some moderns think the insulting versions are hostile Argive distortions and the "anti-Dorian policy" a modern construct.

Pittacus of Mytilene

After the fall of the Penthilid kings and the tyrants Melanchrus and Myrsilus, Mytilene chose Pittacus as aisymnetes, which Aristotle defines as "an elective tyranny" (Politics 3.1285a30–b1). The exiled poet Alcaeus raged that the city had "set up base-born Pittacus as tyrant" (fr. 348). Pittacus had killed the Athenian Phrynon in single combat in the war over Sigeum (Strabo 13.1.38), ruled ten years, then laid down office (Diogenes Laertius 1.75). Aristotle preserves one law: double penalties for offences committed drunk (Politics 2.1274b18–23). He shows that "tyrant" could cover an elected emergency magistrate.

Polycrates of Samos

Polycrates seized Samos with his brothers about 538, killed one and exiled the other, and ruled a navy of a hundred penteconters (Herodotus 3.39). He plundered friend and foe alike; Amasis of Egypt renounced his friendship when a ring Polycrates threw into the sea came back in a fish (3.40–43). Sparta and Corinth besieged Samos on behalf of exiles and failed (3.54–56); Thucydides 1.13.6 records his dedication of Rheneia to Delian Apollo. He kept Anacreon and Ibycus at court, and the tunnel of Eupalinus, the mole and the Heraion that Herodotus admires (3.60) belong at least partly to his reign. In 522 the satrap Oroetes lured him to the mainland and crucified him (3.120–125).

The Peisistratids of Athens

Peisistratus took power three times. In 561/0, leading the "men beyond the hills" against the plain and coast factions, he wounded himself, was voted a bodyguard of club-bearers and seized the Acropolis (Herodotus 1.59; Athenian Constitution 14). Expelled, he returned by marrying Megacles' daughter and driving in beside a tall woman dressed as Athena (1.60); expelled again, he spent some ten years raising money in Thrace and allies in Eretria, and won at Pallene around 546 (1.61–64). He ruled until his death in 527/6.

The Athenian sources are strikingly kind. Herodotus says he governed "according to the existing laws" (1.59.6); Athenian Constitution 16 calls his rule an age of Cronus, with a tithe, loans to farmers and travelling rural judges. Thucydides insists the family "practised virtue and intelligence", exacted only a twentieth, adorned the city and fought its wars, interfering only to keep one of themselves in office (6.54). Hippias succeeded as eldest. In 514 Harmodius and Aristogeiton killed Hipparchus at the Panathenaea over a love affair (6.54–59); Thucydides' point, against Athenian belief, is that Hipparchus was never tyrant and the murder made Hippias harsher. The exiled Alcmaeonids worked on Delphi to push Sparta, and in 510 Cleomenes expelled Hippias (Herodotus 5.62–65), who ended by guiding the Persians to Marathon.

Where sources disagree. Athenian Constitution 15.4 has Peisistratus disarm the Athenians after Pallene; Thucydides 6.56–58 has Hippias do it after the murder. The chronology of the three attempts differs between Herodotus and Athenian Constitution 14–15, whose figures Rhodes finds internally inconsistent. Thucydides also corrects Herodotus 5.55 and popular memory that the lovers "freed Athens".

05What "tyrannos" meant then, and later

Tyrannos is probably a Lydian loanword, and it enters Greek with the Lydian king Gyges: its first attestation is Archilochus fr. 19 West, where the speaker does not care for Gyges' gold "nor desire great tyranny". In the seventh and sixth centuries it meant a sole ruler whose power was not hereditary kingship, without implying how he ruled. Herodotus uses tyrannos and basileus almost interchangeably; Sophocles could title a play Oedipus Tyrannos about a king his audience pitied; Pittacus the elected magistrate and Periander the murderer are both "tyrants". Solon refuses tyranny in his verse (fr. 32–33 West), so the office could be declined, but not yet simply abominated.

The pejorative sense hardened in democratic Athens after 510, when Harmodius and Aristogeiton received cult and statues and Hippias became a traitor at Marathon. By the fourth century the philosophers had a theory. Plato's Republic 8–9 makes the tyrant the final degeneration of soul and city. Aristotle defines tyranny as deviant kingship, rule for the ruler's benefit over unwilling subjects (Politics 3.1279b, 4.1295a), and lists its methods: cut down the outstanding, forbid clubs and common meals, keep the people poor and busy, use spies, make wars (5.1313a34–1314a29). English inherits this sense. Reading it back into Cypselus or Cleisthenes of Sicyon is the standard error.

06Why tyranny arose and how it ended

Thucydides 1.13.1 gives the oldest explanation: as Hellas grew stronger and "the acquisition of wealth became more of an object", revenues grew and tyrannies were set up. Aristotle sorts tyrants by origin (Politics 5.1310b): kings who overstepped (Pheidon), holders of high office in Ionia and Sicily, and demagogues who were also generals, such as Peisistratus and Theagenes of Megara. The modern hoplite theory has largely yielded to a view of the tyrant as the aristocrat who won: Cypselus against the Bacchiads, Peisistratus against rival factions, Polycrates against his brothers. Lydia offered a model of splendid monarchy next door. The one economic case with evidence is Athens, where Solon's poems describe debt-bondage a generation before Peisistratus.

Tyrannies ended for three reasons. First, succession: Aristotle observes that most barely outlived the founder, the sons being raised in luxury and arrogance, and he names the three longest, the Orthagorids at a hundred years, the Cypselids at seventy-three and a half and the Peisistratids at thirty-five (5.1315b). Second, Sparta: Thucydides 1.18.1 says most tyrannies were put down by Sparta, and later lists (Plutarch, On the Malice of Herodotus 21; the Rylands papyrus) credit her with Corinth, Sicyon and Athens. Third, Persia, which after 546 made the Ionian tyrants its clients. Thucydides' verdict (1.17), that tyrants cared only for their own safety and achieved nothing except against neighbours, sits awkwardly with the Athenian building and festival evidence.

07The Seven Sages

The earliest list is Plato's (Protagoras 343a): Thales of Miletus, Pittacus of Mytilene, Bias of Priene, Solon of Athens, Cleobulus of Lindos, Myson of Chen and Chilon of Sparta. Diogenes Laertius 1.40–42 records substitutes, Periander, Anacharsis, Epimenides and others, and that Demetrius of Phalerum dated their recognition to the archonship of Damasias, 582/1. Their wisdom was gnomic: "know thyself", "nothing in excess", Pittacus' "know the moment". Stories gathered around them: the golden tripod passed from sage to sage until it reached Delphi (Plutarch, Solon 4), and Solon's visit to Croesus (Herodotus 1.29–33), which Plutarch admits cannot fit the dates but refuses to give up (Solon 27).

The group overlaps tellingly with lawgivers and tyrants: Solon, Pittacus, Periander, Chilon the Spartan ephor. Dicaearchus already called them "shrewd men with a turn for legislation" rather than sages (Diogenes Laertius 1.40); Richard Martin reads them as performers of wisdom, a political role rather than a school. The list canonises the archaic ideal of the wise statesman.

08Coinage from Lydia

Herodotus 1.94 says the Lydians were the first to strike gold and silver coin and the first retailers; Xenophanes (fr. 4, in Pollux 9.83) agreed, though Pollux also records rival claimants including Pheidon. The earliest coins are electrum, a natural gold-silver alloy, stamped with a lion's head or other badge; the key deposit is the foundation fill of the Artemision at Ephesus, now dated by most around 600 BC. One early piece reads "I am the badge of Phanes". Croesus replaced electrum with separate gold and silver about 550. Greek cities followed: Aegina's silver "turtles" from about 550, Corinth's Pegasi, Athens' Wappenmünzen and, from about 515, the owls. Before coins, value was reckoned in cattle and in iron spits, from which obol and drachma, a "handful" of six spits, take their names.

Where sources disagree. Aristotle (Politics 1.1257a31–41) explains coinage as a convenience of trade. Numismatists have doubted it: Robert Cook (1958) proposed payment of mercenaries, Colin Kraay (1964) state payments and taxes, since early issues are too large for retail; Henry Kim (2001) countered with tiny early fractions. The Artemision date, and so the birth of coinage, has moved between about 640 and 560 and is not settled.

09Self-test

  1. What feature of the aspis ties the hoplite to a formation rather than to individual combat?
    Answer

    The double grip (porpax and antilabe) fixes the shield on the forearm so that its left half overhangs and protects the neighbour, which only works in a line.

  2. Which object is the earliest clear depiction of a phalanx, and roughly when?
    Answer

    The Protocorinthian Chigi olpe, about 650 to 640 BC.

  3. Which passage of Aristotle underlies the "hoplite revolution" thesis?
    Answer

    Politics 4.1297b16–28, on constitutions passing from cavalry to the heavy-armed as cities grew.

  4. Why does Thucydides single out the Lelantine War?
    Answer

    It was, he says, the only pre-Persian war in which the rest of Greece took sides in alliances (1.15.3).

  5. Which of Draco's laws survives, and how?
    Answer

    His homicide law, republished on stone in 409/8 BC (IG I³ 104).

  6. What is the earliest surviving Greek law inscription and what does it regulate?
    Answer

    The Dreros law, Crete, c. 650–600 BC, forbidding the kosmos to hold office again within ten years.

  7. What did Aristotle call Pittacus' position and why does it matter?
    Answer

    An aisymnetes, "an elective tyranny" (Politics 3.1285a), showing the word tyrant could cover a legitimately chosen ruler.

  8. What error about the Peisistratids does Thucydides correct in 6.54–59?
    Answer

    That Hipparchus was tyrant when murdered; Hippias was the eldest and ruler, and the murder made the tyranny harsher rather than ending it.

  9. Name the three longest tyrannies in Aristotle's reckoning.
    Answer

    The Orthagorids of Sicyon (100 years), the Cypselids of Corinth (73½) and the Peisistratids of Athens (35).

  10. What is the earliest attestation of the word tyrannos, and whose name is it linked with?
    Answer

    Archilochus fr. 19 West, in the mouth of a speaker rejecting the gold and "great tyranny" of Gyges of Lydia.

10Carry forward

  1. Archaic "tyrant" is a neutral word for an unconstitutional sole ruler, usually an aristocrat who won a factional fight; the moral sense is a construction of democratic Athens and fourth-century philosophy, and must not be read back.
  2. Aristotle's Politics and Athenian Constitution are theories built on thin tradition, not records: the hoplite revolution, the constitution of Draco and the typology of tyrants are fourth-century schemata to be tested, not assumed.
  3. Written law and coinage were both instruments for taking power out of aristocratic hands and fixing it in public, impersonal form; this is the ground on which Solon, Cleisthenes and the Athenian democracy will build.

The prompt this answers

Give me an in-depth report on Archaic Greek warfare and politics. Cover: the hoplite panoply and the phalanx, the debate over whether the "hoplite revolution" caused political change, and the Lelantine War; the lawgivers (Zaleucus, Draco, Lycurgus as legend) and the first written law codes (Gortyn); the age of tyrants, with Cypselus and Periander of Corinth, Pheidon of Argos, Polycrates of Samos, Cleisthenes of Sicyon, Pittacus of Mytilene and the Peisistratids of Athens; what "tyrannos" meant then versus later; why tyranny arose and how it ended; the Seven Sages; and the emergence of coinage from Lydia. Draw on Herodotus, Aristotle's Politics and Athenian Constitution, and Thucydides' Archaeology. Format: about 2000 words, headed sections, name sources, flag disagreements, end with a 10-question self-test with answers and the three ideas I must carry forward.
