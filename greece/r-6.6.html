The Spartan hegemony and its abuses, 404–395

When Lysander sailed into the Piraeus in spring 404 and the walls of Athens came down "to the music of flute-girls" (Xenophon, Hellenica 2.2.23), Sparta inherited an empire it had no apparatus to run. Lysander's answer was personal: in the former Athenian cities he installed boards of ten local oligarchs (dekarchies) backed by a Spartan governor, the harmost, with a garrison. Plutarch (Lysander 13) calls these regimes the harshest Greece had seen, and the harmosts quickly became a byword for brutality, extortion and lack of accountability. Tribute, which Sparta had gone to war promising to abolish, continued; Diodorus (14.10) puts it at more than a thousand talents a year.

At Athens, Lysander's protégés were the Thirty, whose rule (404–403) killed perhaps 1,500 citizens before Thrasybulus' return from Phyle and the amnesty of 403 (Xenophon, Hellenica 2.3–4). It was King Pausanias, not Lysander, who brokered that settlement: the Spartan establishment was already alarmed by Lysander's personal network and dissolved the dekarchies soon after. His scheme to make the kingship elective (Plutarch, Lysander 24–26) came to nothing, and he died at Haliartus in 395.

The deeper problem was structural. Sparta had perhaps 2,500 adult citizens by 400, a number that kept falling (oliganthropia); it could not garrison the Aegean, govern Asia Minor and hold down the Peloponnese at once. Agesilaus' Asian campaign of 396–394 provoked Persia into financing a coalition at home: the satrap Tithraustes' agent Timocrates carried gold to Thebes, Corinth and Argos (Xenophon, Hellenica 3.5.1–2).

Where sources disagree. Xenophon, hostile to Thebes, makes Persian bribery the cause of the Corinthian War. The Oxyrhynchus historian (Hellenica Oxyrhynchia 7) says anti-Spartan feeling at Thebes and Athens long preceded the gold, and that the Theban leaders deliberately engineered a border dispute between Phocis and Locris to force Sparta's hand. Most modern scholars follow the Oxyrhynchus account.

The Corinthian War and the King's Peace, 395–387

The coalition of Athens, Thebes, Corinth and Argos fought Sparta for eight years. On land Sparta won the set battles, at the Nemea River and Coronea in 394 (Xenophon, Hellenica 4.2–3), without being able to exploit them. At sea the decisive event was Cnidus (August 394), where the Athenian exile Conon, commanding a Persian fleet for Pharnabazus, destroyed the Spartan navy; the harmosts were expelled from the islands within months and Conon rebuilt Athens' Long Walls with Persian money (Xenophon, Hellenica 4.8.9–10). Iphicrates' peltasts destroyed a Spartan mora near Lechaeum in 390 (Hellenica 4.5.11–17).

Athens then overreached. Thrasybulus' campaigns of 389–388 revived a tithe at Byzantium and pushed Persia back to Sparta's side. The Spartan admiral Antalcidas blockaded the Hellespont, and the Greeks accepted Artaxerxes II's terms in 387/6. Xenophon preserves the text (Hellenica 5.1.31): the cities of Asia and Cyprus belong to the King; all other Greek cities, great and small, are to be autonomous, except that Athens keeps Lemnos, Imbros and Scyros; whoever refuses, the King will make war on "by land and by sea, with ships and with money".

The King's Peace, the first "common peace" (koine eirene), was a Persian document, but Sparta was its enforcer, and "autonomy" became a weapon: it dissolved the Boeotian federation and the union of Corinth and Argos while leaving the Peloponnesian League intact.

Sparta overreaches, 386–379

Agesilaus used the peace as licence. Mantinea was broken into villages in 385 (Xenophon, Hellenica 5.2.1–7); Phlius was besieged; a Spartan army marched north to dismantle the Olynthian federation (382–379). In 382, en route to Olynthus, Phoebidas accepted an invitation from the Theban oligarch Leontiades and seized the Cadmea, Thebes' acropolis, during the Thesmophoria (Xenophon, Hellenica 5.2.25–31). The democratic leader Ismenias was tried and executed; some 300 Thebans fled to Athens.

Where sources disagree. Xenophon says the Spartan authorities were angry and fined Phoebidas, but Agesilaus argued that only the outcome mattered ("was it useful?"), and Sparta kept the garrison. Diodorus (15.20) and Plutarch (Agesilaus 23–24, Pelopidas 6) report a widespread belief that Phoebidas acted on secret orders. Whether the seizure was policy or opportunism cannot be settled; that Sparta ratified it is not in doubt, and Xenophon himself (Hellenica 5.4.1) treats the fall of Spartan power as divine punishment for the oath-breaking.

In winter 379/8 the exiles struck back. Pelopidas and a handful of conspirators entered Thebes disguised, killed the pro-Spartan polemarchs at a drinking party, and with Athenian border forces in support forced the garrison to capitulate (Xenophon, Hellenica 5.4.2–12; Plutarch, Pelopidas 7–13). Then, in spring 378, the harmost Sphodrias launched an unprovoked night march on the Piraeus, failed to arrive before dawn, and was acquitted at Sparta through Agesilaus' influence (Hellenica 5.4.20–33). Athens, until then wavering, committed to Thebes.

The Second Athenian League, 378–

The league's charter survives on stone: the decree of Aristoteles (IG II² 43, 378/7). It is a point-by-point repudiation of the fifth-century empire: members are to be "free and autonomous", with no garrisons, governors or tribute; Athenians may not own land in allied territory; contributions were called syntaxeis, never phoros; a synod of allies sat at Athens alongside the assembly. Membership reached perhaps seventy cities, Thebes among the earliest, with the stated aim of compelling Sparta to let the Greeks live in peace under the King's Peace.

Where sources disagree. Xenophon (Hellenica 5.4.34) implies the Athenians built the league in reaction to Sphodrias' raid. Diodorus (15.28–29) places the founding before it. The inscription's dating by archon (Nausinicus, 378/7) fits either, and scholars divide between a league already in formation that the raid accelerated (Cawkwell) and one the raid created.

The league worked. Chabrias beat the Spartan fleet at Naxos in September 376, the first Athenian naval victory since the Peloponnesian War; Timotheus won at Alyzeia in 375, and Sparta accepted a renewed common peace recognising the league. But Theban strength now worried Athens as much as Spartan weakness pleased it.

Thebes: Epaminondas, Pelopidas and the Sacred Band

Theban power rested from 378 on three things. First, the re-founded Boeotian federation, now democratic, with seven elected boeotarchs. Second, the Sacred Band, 300 picked hoplites maintained at state expense on the Cadmea, organised, Plutarch says, in pairs of lovers (Pelopidas 18–19); Gorgidas had spread them along the front of the phalanx, Pelopidas concentrated them as one striking unit. Third, Epaminondas, a Pythagorean-trained aristocrat whom Nepos (Epaminondas) and Diodorus (15.39, 15.88) treat as the finest Greek of his age, and whom Xenophon almost ignores until 362.

At Tegyra in 375 Pelopidas with the Sacred Band and a few horsemen beat two Spartan morai in the open, killing both polemarchs (Plutarch, Pelopidas 16–17): the first time a Spartan force had been defeated by a smaller one. In 371 a peace conference at Sparta broke down when Epaminondas insisted on swearing for "the Boeotians", not "the Thebans", and Agesilaus struck Thebes from the treaty (Xenophon, Hellenica 6.3.18–20; Plutarch, Agesilaus 28).

Leuctra, Messenia, Megalopolis and Mantinea, 371–362

King Cleombrotus, already in Phocis, marched into Boeotia and met the Thebans at Leuctra in July 371. Xenophon (Hellenica 6.4.4–15) gives the fullest account. The Thebans put their phalanx fifty shields deep on the left, opposite the Spartan king, and left the rest of their line weak and held back; their cavalry drove the Spartan horse into their own infantry; Cleombrotus and 400 of the 700 Spartiates present fell. Diodorus (15.55–56) adds that Epaminondas deliberately refused battle with his right while his left advanced, the oblique order that later writers made his signature, and Plutarch (Pelopidas 23) has the Sacred Band charge at the run into the gap.

Where sources disagree. Xenophon never names Epaminondas at Leuctra and attributes the result to the cavalry mêlée and Spartan drunkenness at breakfast; Diodorus and Plutarch make it a tactical masterpiece. Numbers differ too: Diodorus gives the Spartans 10,000 against 6,000, Plutarch (Pelopidas 20) fewer. Modern debate (Cawkwell, Hanson, Buckler) turns on whether the deep left wing and the echelon were genuinely new or whether Xenophon's silence is simply his well-attested Theban bias.

Leuctra ended Spartan hegemony because Epaminondas followed it up. In winter 370/69 a Boeotian army with Arcadian, Argive and Elean allies invaded Laconia, the first enemy force there in centuries; unwalled Sparta survived because the Eurotas was in flood and Agesilaus held the city (Xenophon, Hellenica 6.5.22–32). Epaminondas then marched into Messenia, freed the helots who had worked Sparta's richest land for three centuries, recalled exiles from Italy, Sicily and Libya, and founded Messene on Mount Ithome (Diodorus 15.66; Pausanias 4.26–27). Sparta lost half its territory and the economic basis of the Spartiate way of life, and never recovered.

The Arcadians, encouraged by Thebes, had already united (370) and founded Megalopolis, a federal capital settled from some forty villages to block the road north from Sparta (Diodorus 15.72; Pausanias 8.27).

Where sources disagree. Pausanias dates Megalopolis to 371/0, immediately after Leuctra; Diodorus places it in 368. Whether Epaminondas personally planned the city, as Pausanias says, or merely approved an Arcadian initiative, is uncertain.

The Theban decade was never a hegemony in the Spartan sense. Epaminondas built a fleet in 364 and briefly won over Byzantium, Chios and Rhodes (Diodorus 15.78–79); Pelopidas was killed at Cynoscephalae the same year fighting Alexander of Pherae (Plutarch, Pelopidas 32). The Arcadians split over the use of sacred money from Olympia, and Mantinea went over to Sparta. Epaminondas marched south a fourth time in 362 and met Sparta, Athens, Mantinea and Elis at Mantinea; he won by the same deep left-wing attack and died of a spear wound as he did so (Xenophon, Hellenica 7.5.18–27; Diodorus 15.84–87). Xenophon ends his history there, judging that afterwards there was "even more confusion and disorder in Greece than before".

Jason of Pherae

The one new centralising power of the 370s was Thessalian. Jason, tyrant of Pherae, had by 375 made himself tagos, federal war-leader of all Thessaly, commanding (he claimed) 8,000 cavalry and 20,000 hoplites plus mercenaries. Xenophon reports his ambitions through his enemy Polydamas of Pharsalus, who went to Sparta for help and was told there was none to give (Hellenica 6.1.4–19). Jason arrived at Leuctra the day after the battle, allied to Thebes, and talked the Thebans out of storming the Spartan camp (Hellenica 6.4.20–26); they later suspected he wanted Sparta weakened, not destroyed. In 370 he announced he would preside over the Pythian festival with his whole army, and was assassinated by seven young men before he could go; many Greek cities honoured the killers (Hellenica 6.4.28–32). A northern monarch uniting Greece under a sacred festival and the prospect of a Persian war: Jason's project anticipates Philip's with uncanny precision, and his successors reduced Thessaly to a client of whoever could intervene, first Thebes, then Macedon.

Philip II: the army and the diplomacy, 359–352

Philip took power in 359 after his brother Perdiccas III and 4,000 Macedonians died fighting the Illyrians. Within two years he had bought off the Paeonians, beaten the Illyrian Bardylis (358) and taken Amphipolis (357), promising it to Athens and keeping it. Diodorus (16.3.1–2) says he at once reorganised the army and "devised the compact order and equipment of the phalanx". The infantry (pezhetairoi, "foot-companions") carried the sarissa, a pike of some 4.5 to 5.5 metres held in both hands, with a small shield slung from the shoulder, in files sixteen deep: five rows of points projected beyond the front rank. The Companion cavalry (hetairoi), heavy horse from the nobility, were the striking arm; the hypaspists linked them to the phalanx. Philip drilled year-round, banned wagons and most servants, and paid wages, creating something close to a professional national army. His engineers under Polyidus of Thessaly brought torsion catapults and siege towers to a new scale: Amphipolis, Methone (354, where Philip lost an eye), Olynthus and Perinthus were all besieged.

Where sources disagree. Diodorus credits the phalanx to Philip; a fragment attributed to Anaximenes credits an "Alexander" (probably Alexander II, Philip's elder brother) with naming the pezhetairoi. Whether Philip invented the sarissa phalanx or perfected it, and how much he owed to his years as a hostage in Thebes (Plutarch, Pelopidas 26) watching Epaminondas, remain contested; Hammond stressed the Theban debt, Ellis and Worthington Philip's originality.

His diplomacy was as inventive. He married seven times for policy: Phila of Elimeia, Audata the Illyrian, Philinna of Larissa, Olympias of Epirus (mother of Alexander, 357), Nicesipolis of Pherae, Meda of the Getae and, in 337, the Macedonian Cleopatra (Athenaeus 13.557b–e, quoting Satyrus). He bribed, promised, delayed and lied; Demosthenes' complaint that Philip took Pydna, Potidaea and Methone "while you were debating" (First Philippic 4.4–5) is also a tribute. The gold of Mount Pangaeum, won in 356, gave him a thousand talents a year (Diodorus 16.8.6–7).

The Third Sacred War, Olynthus, and the war of words, 356–346

In 356 the Amphictyonic Council fined Phocis for cultivating sacred land; Phocis under Philomelus seized Delphi and used its treasures to hire mercenaries (Diodorus 16.23–30). Thebes led the "Sacred War" against them, and Thessaly, ravaged by Phocian-backed Pherae, called Philip in. He was beaten twice by Onomarchus in 353, then crushed him at the Crocus Field in 352, drowning or crucifying 3,000 prisoners as temple-robbers (Diodorus 16.35), and was elected archon of Thessaly for life, acquiring its cavalry and its votes at Delphi. Athens blocked him at Thermopylae that summer.

In 349 Philip turned on Olynthus, head of the Chalcidian League, which had taken in his half-brothers. Demosthenes' three Olynthiacs begged Athens to send citizens, not mercenaries, and to touch the theoric fund. Athens sent too little, too late; Olynthus fell by treachery in 348 and Philip razed it and enslaved the population (Diodorus 16.53). In 346, while the Peace of Philocrates was being sworn, Philip marched through Thermopylae, accepted Phocian surrender, took Phocis' two Amphictyonic votes and presided at the Pythian Games (Diodorus 16.59–60; Demosthenes, On the Peace).

The intellectual argument was now clear. Demosthenes (First Philippic, 351; Second Philippic, 344; On the Chersonese and Third Philippic, 341) held that Philip was a barbarian despot, that every treaty was a stratagem, and that freedom meant a citizen army, war taxes and alliance with any Greek who would fight. Isocrates had called in the Panegyricus (380) for Athens and Sparta to lead a Persian crusade; in the Philippus (346) he urged Philip to reconcile Argos, Sparta, Thebes and Athens, lead them against Persia and settle Greece's landless mercenaries in Asia. Pan-Hellenism and Macedonian hegemony had converged.

Where sources disagree. Ancient judgement was divided: Theopompus called Philip the greatest man Europe had produced and also an incontinent drunk; Polybius (18.14) defended the Greeks who sided with Philip as patriots, not traitors, against Demosthenes' charge. Modern scholars split along the same line: Cawkwell saw Demosthenes as a brilliant orator and a disastrous strategist who misread Philip's limited aims in 346; Harris and Worthington are more sympathetic; Ellis argued Philip never sought to destroy Athens at all.

Chaeronea, the League of Corinth and the assassination, 340–336

War resumed in 340 when Philip seized the Athenian grain fleet at the Bosporus after failing to take Perinthus and Byzantium. In 339 an Amphictyonic dispute with Amphissa gave him a pretext to march south; he bypassed Thermopylae and seized Elatea. Demosthenes went to Thebes and, against Macedonian envoys and a century of enmity, secured the Theban alliance (On the Crown 18.169–179; Diodorus 16.84–85). At Chaeronea on 2 August 338 (Plutarch, Camillus 19) perhaps 35,000 allied Greeks faced Philip's 30,000 foot and 2,000 horse. Diodorus (16.85–86) has Philip on the right against the Athenians and the eighteen-year-old Alexander with the Companions against the Thebans; the Sacred Band died in place, and its grave beneath the lion monument yielded 254 skeletons in 1880 (Plutarch, Pelopidas 18; Pausanias 9.40.10). Athens lost 1,000 dead and 2,000 prisoners.

Where sources disagree. Polyaenus (4.2.2) says Philip feigned a retreat on his right to pull the Athenians forward and open the gap that Alexander exploited; Diodorus mentions no such manoeuvre, and Plutarch (Alexander 9) says only that Alexander "is said" to have been first to break the Sacred Band. The tactical detail of Chaeronea is reconstructed, not reported.

Philip garrisoned Thebes and installed an oligarchy; Athens, which had expected destruction, got its prisoners back without ransom and kept its fleet in exchange for dissolving the Second Athenian League (Diodorus 16.87). At Corinth in winter 338/7 he convened every mainland state except Sparta and created a common peace with a council (synedrion), a guarantee of existing constitutions, a ban on exile, confiscation and redistribution of land, and himself as hegemon of its forces; in 337 it voted war on Persia (Diodorus 16.89; IG II² 236). Autonomy, the slogan of 387, had become the legal basis of Macedonian control.

In 337 Philip married Cleopatra, niece of Attalus; Alexander and Olympias left court after the quarrel at the wedding (Plutarch, Alexander 9). In summer 336 at Aegae, during the wedding festival of his daughter to Alexander of Epirus, Philip entered the theatre without his guards and was stabbed by Pausanias of Orestis, a bodyguard who had been raped at Attalus' instigation and denied redress (Diodorus 16.91–94; Aristotle, Politics 1311b). Pausanias was killed as he reached his horse.

Where sources disagree. Diodorus and Aristotle treat the motive as personal. Justin (9.7) and Plutarch (Alexander 10) report suspicion of Olympias, who allegedly crowned the killer's corpse, and of Alexander; others pointed to Persian gold or to the Lyncestian brothers executed at Alexander's accession. No ancient source offers evidence beyond rumour, and modern opinion (Badian for conspiracy; Hammond, Ellis and Worthington for a lone killer with aristocratic grievances) remains unresolved.

Self-test

  1. What were the dekarchies and who installed them?
    Answer

    Boards of ten pro-Spartan oligarchs set up by Lysander in the former Athenian cities after 404, supported by a Spartan harmost and garrison; the Spartan authorities dissolved them within a few years out of suspicion of Lysander's personal power.

  2. Which sea battle ended Spartan naval supremacy in the Corinthian War, and who won it?
    Answer

    Cnidus, August 394: the Athenian Conon, commanding a Persian fleet with the satrap Pharnabazus, destroyed the Spartan fleet.

  3. State the two central provisions of the King's Peace of 387/6.
    Answer

    The Greek cities of Asia and Cyprus belong to the Persian King; all other Greek cities are to be autonomous (Athens keeping Lemnos, Imbros and Scyros), with the King threatening war on any who refuse.

  4. How was the Cadmea seized, and how did Agesilaus justify keeping it?
    Answer

    In 382 Phoebidas, invited by the oligarch Leontiades, occupied Thebes' acropolis during the Thesmophoria. Agesilaus argued that only whether the act was useful to Sparta mattered, so the garrison stayed, though Phoebidas was fined.

  5. Name three ways the Second Athenian League's charter differed from the fifth-century empire.
    Answer

    No garrisons, governors or tribute (contributions were called syntaxeis); members free and autonomous under their own constitutions; a ban on Athenians owning land in allied territory; and an allied synod sitting at Athens.

  6. What was tactically distinctive about the Theban deployment at Leuctra?
    Answer

    The phalanx was massed fifty deep on the left, opposite the Spartan king, while the weaker right was held back; the cavalry fought first and drove the Spartan horse into their own line. Diodorus and Plutarch credit Epaminondas with the oblique order; Xenophon does not name him.

  7. Why did the liberation of Messenia matter more than Leuctra itself?
    Answer

    Messenia's helot labour had funded the Spartiate way of life for three centuries; its loss, and the founding of Messene on Ithome, removed roughly half of Sparta's territory and made recovery of great-power status impossible.

  8. What did Jason of Pherae intend to do in 370, and what happened?
    Answer

    As tagos of Thessaly he announced he would preside over the Pythian festival at Delphi with his army; he was assassinated by seven young men before he could, and many Greek cities honoured the killers.

  9. What did Philip gain from the Third Sacred War beyond military victory?
    Answer

    Election as archon of Thessaly for life (its cavalry and Amphictyonic votes), the two Phocian votes on the Amphictyonic Council after 346, control of Thermopylae, and the presidency of the Pythian Games: a legal and religious standing inside Greece.

  10. What were the key terms of the League of Corinth?
    Answer

    A common peace among all mainland states except Sparta, a council (synedrion), guarantee of existing constitutions, prohibition of exile, confiscation and land redistribution, and Philip as hegemon commanding its forces; in 337 it voted war on Persia.

Carry forward

  1. "Autonomy" was the political vocabulary of the whole period, and every hegemon in turn, Sparta after 387, Thebes after 371 and Philip at Corinth in 338/7, used a common peace guaranteeing it as the legal instrument of control.
  2. Hegemony failed when it rested on a narrow citizen body: Spartan oliganthropia, Theban dependence on two men, and the fact that Philip's Macedon succeeded precisely because it was a monarchy with a mass, paid, professional army rather than a city-state.
  3. Our sources are partisan: Xenophon's silence on Epaminondas, Demosthenes' portrait of Philip and Diodorus' compression of Ephorus all shape what later generations, Alexander's historians included, took to be the facts of the fourth century.
