The shape of the land: a mental map

Hold the Greek world as a triangle of coasts around a single inland sea, the Aegean, with two long arms reaching west and north-east. The mainland forms the western side of the triangle; the Anatolian coast forms the eastern side; Crete closes the base. Everything else hangs off that frame.

To memorise it, walk it. Start at Mount Olympus, the northern hinge. South of it the Thessalian plain; then the Thermopylae gap; then the Boeotian basin around Lake Copais with Parnassus and Delphi to its west; then Attica, a thumb of thin soil pointing south-east into the Aegean; then the Isthmus, four miles wide, and beyond it the Peloponnese, a hand with Arcadia as its palm and the capes of Argolis, Laconia, Messenia and Elis as its fingers. Turn east across the water: the Cyclades as stepping stones from Attica and Euboea towards Samos and Miletus; Lesbos in the north opposite Troy; Rhodes in the south-east; Crete lying across the southern exit like a bar. Finally, two long sea roads: west past Ithaca and Corcyra to the Italian heel and Sicily, and north-east through the Hellespont and Bosporus into the Black Sea.

The regions of the mainland

Attica

Attica is small, roughly 2,400 square kilometres, hemmed by Parnes, Pentelicon and Hymettus, with the plains of Athens, Eleusis, Marathon and the Mesogaia between them. The soil is thin. Thucydides (1.2.5–6) made this the key to Athenian history: poor land attracted no invaders, so Attica kept the same population while richer regions churned, and the Athenians could claim to be autochthonous. What it lacked in grain it had in marble, olives, Laurion silver and a coast on every side but one. The twelve old communities were unified under Athens; Thucydides (2.15) credits this synoikismos to Theseus. The unusual thing about Attica is that it became one polis rather than several; Boeotia, of similar size, never did.

Boeotia

North of Attica, across Parnes and Cithaeron, lies a basin of good farmland around Lake Copais, drained by Mycenaean engineers and a marsh again by the classical period. Thebes dominated the south-east, Orchomenus the north-west, and their rivalry runs from the Heracles legends to the fourth century. Its plains made it the battlefield of Greece (Plataea, Leuctra, Coronea, Chaeronea), the orchestra of war in a phrase attributed to Epaminondas (Plutarch, Marcellus 21). Hesiod farmed at Ascra under Helicon and called it "bad in winter, hard in summer, good never" (Works and Days 639–640). It organised itself as a federation of cities, never a single polis.

Thessaly

Thessaly is the largest plain in Greece, ringed by Olympus, Ossa, Pelion, Othrys and Pindus and drained by the Peneus through the Vale of Tempe. It is horse and grain country, and its politics were correspondingly aristocratic: great houses such as the Aleuadae of Larisa, a serf class of penestai, a loose confederacy of four tetrads under an elected tagos. Myth makes it the oldest Greek landscape: Achilles' Phthia, Jason's Iolcus beneath Pelion, Cheiron and the Centaurs, the Titans on Othrys facing the gods on Olympus (Hesiod, Theogony 632–633). Homer's "Hellas" is a district of Achilles' kingdom here (Iliad 2.683–684), not the whole country.

The Peloponnese

Herodotus (8.73) counts seven peoples in the Peloponnese and treats the Arcadians, Cynurians and Achaeans as its original inhabitants and the Dorians as incomers. Geographically it is one block with several compartments:

The pattern to notice is that the cities which mattered in the Bronze Age (Mycenae, Tiryns, Pylos) are not the ones that mattered later (Sparta, Argos, Corinth). The Linear B archive from Pylos shows a palace governing all of Messenia; nothing on that scale reappeared until Sparta's conquest.

Epirus

Beyond Pindus on the Adriatic side, Epirus is high, wet and forested. Greeks found it strange and old. Its peoples (Chaonians, Thesprotians, Molossians) lived in villages under kings, organised as ethne, not poleis, until the Hellenistic period. Its sanctuaries are oracles of the old gods and the dead: Dodona, where Zeus answered from an oak and which Homer already calls Pelasgian (Iliad 16.233–235; Herodotus 2.52–57), and the Necromanteion on the Acheron, the river Greeks placed in the underworld. The Molossian kings claimed descent from Achilles' son Neoptolemus.

Macedon

Lower Macedon is the plain of the Haliacmon and Axios, with the old capital Aegae (Vergina) and later Pella; Upper Macedon is a ring of highland cantons. Olympus marks the border with Thessaly, so the gods' home sat at the edge of what southern Greeks counted as Greek. The Argead kings claimed Argive and Heraclid descent (Herodotus 8.137–139), and the Olympic judges admitted Alexander I only after examining it (Herodotus 5.22); Demosthenes (Third Philippic 31) still calls Philip II a barbarian. Macedon stayed a kingdom while the south fragmented into cities, and that difference is what mattered in the fourth century.

Where sources disagree. Ancient Greeks themselves disagreed whether Macedonians were Greek; the question was political as much as ethnic. Modern scholars mostly treat ancient Macedonian as a Greek dialect (the Pella curse tablet, c. 375 BC, is in a north-west Doric form), but the surviving glosses show non-Greek phonological features, and a minority still classes it as a sibling language rather than a dialect. The evidence is too thin to settle it.

The islands

The Cyclades circle Delos, where Leto bore Apollo on a floating rock that then became fixed (Homeric Hymn to Apollo 25–88). Naxos is Dionysus' island, where Theseus abandoned Ariadne; Paros supplied marble; Thera was blown apart in a Bronze Age eruption. Small, poor and exposed, the Cyclades were the natural members of the Delian League, and Delos its first treasury.

Crete is the largest island, a mountain spine (Ida, Dicte, the White Mountains) with fertile north-coast plains. Arthur Evans dug Knossos from 1900 and named its Bronze Age civilisation after the mythical king Minos. The myths are foundational: Zeus born in a Cretan cave and hidden from Cronus (Hesiod, Theogony 477–484), Europa and the bull (Apollodorus, Library 3.1.1), Minos, Daedalus, the Labyrinth, Theseus and Ariadne. Homer gives Crete "a hundred cities" in the Iliad (2.649) and "ninety" in the Odyssey (19.174). Classical Crete was a backwater of Dorian aristocracies with archaic law codes (Gortyn), which Plato treated as a model (Laws 1.624a).

Euboea lies so close to Boeotia and Attica that it is nearly mainland, joined at Chalcis by a bridge over the Euripus. Lefkandi has the richest Greek site of the tenth century, and Euboeans were the first Greeks west, at Pithecusae on Ischia and Cumae around 770–750. The Lelantine War between Chalcis and Eretria is the earliest Greek war Thucydides (1.15.3) found worth naming.

Lesbos faces the Troad and belonged to the Aeolic world: Mytilene and Methymna, Sappho, Alcaeus and Pittacus. Hesiod's father sailed from Aeolian Cyme on the opposite coast to settle in Boeotia (Works and Days 635–640), a reminder that this strait was a Greek inland water.

Rhodes at the south-east corner was Dorian; Homer's Catalogue gives it to Tlepolemus son of Heracles (Iliad 2.653–670), and Pindar's seventh Olympian tells how Helios received the island as it rose from the sea. Its three cities, Lindus, Ialysus and Camirus, united to found the city of Rhodes in 408, the great trading state of the Hellenistic Aegean.

Ionia and the Anatolian coast

The eastern side of the triangle is a coast of deep bays and river valleys (Hermus, Cayster, Maeander) opening on to the Anatolian interior. Greeks divided it by dialect: Aeolis in the north with Lesbos, Cyme and (originally) Smyrna; Ionia in the centre; the Dorian hexapolis (Halicarnassus, Cnidus, Cos and the three Rhodian cities) in the south, with Carians and Lycians behind. Herodotus (1.142) names the twelve Ionian cities, which met at the Panionion on Mycale. The migration story (Herodotus 1.145–147; Pausanias 7.2) has refugees from the Peloponnese gather in Athens and sail east under the sons of King Codrus; Herodotus adds that they took Carian wives whose fathers they had killed.

Ionia's wealth and exposure made it the intellectual front line: Thales, Anaximander and Anaximenes at Miletus, Heraclitus at Ephesus, Herodotus at Halicarnassus. The same exposure made it the first Greek land to fall to Lydia and then Persia (546), and the Ionian Revolt (499–494) drew Athens into the Persian Wars (Herodotus 5.97–6.32). In the far north, on the Hellespont, lay the mound of Hisarlik, identified by Schliemann in 1870 as Troy.

The West and the Black Sea

From the eighth century the Greeks planted cities along two corridors. Westward, Euboeans reached Ischia and Cumae; Achaeans founded Sybaris, Croton and Metapontum on the Italian instep; Spartans founded Taras (Strabo 6.3.2); Phocaeans went on to Massalia. Thucydides (6.3–5) gives a dated sequence for Sicily: Naxos first (734), Syracuse a year later by Corinthians, then Leontini, Catana, Megara Hyblaea, Gela and Acragas, with Phoenicians withdrawing to Motya and Panormus in the west and the native Sicels, Sicani and Elymians inland. "Magna Graecia" was a Roman name for the Italian cities. These were rich: Syracuse and Acragas built the largest temples in the Greek world, Croton housed Pythagoras' school, Elea Parmenides. Myth followed the colonists: Scylla and Charybdis were fixed in the Straits of Messina, the Cyclopes under Etna, Arethusa's spring at Syracuse; Persephone's abduction at Enna is first attested late (Cicero, Verrines 2.4.106–107).

North-eastwards, Megarians founded Byzantium and Chalcedon on the Bosporus, and Milesians founded Sinope, Trapezus, Olbia, Panticapaeum and dozens more along a sea first called Axeinos, "inhospitable", then renamed Euxeinos, "hospitable", by euphemism (Strabo 7.3.6). Its value was Scythian grain, salt fish, slaves and timber; Athens' fleet existed partly to keep this route open. Herodotus devoted Book 4 to Scythia; Xenophon's army saw the sea from Mount Theches and shouted "Thalatta!" (Anabasis 4.7.24). Myth placed the edge of the world here: the Argonauts at Colchis (Pindar, Pythian 4; Apollonius, Argonautica), Prometheus in the Caucasus, Iphigenia among the Taurians, the Amazons, and Achilles' cult on the White Island off the Danube.

Dialects and the "Dorian invasion"

Classical Greek was a family of mutually intelligible dialects. The traditional classification, which goes back to the ancients themselves (Strabo 8.1.2), has three groups, to which modern linguists add a fourth:

The map is odd. Arcadia and Cyprus, unconnected in historical times, share a dialect that most resembles the Bronze Age Greek of the Linear B tablets. Doric fills the Peloponnese around Arcadia and the southern islands. Aeolic appears in two separated blocks. The ancient explanation was migration. Thucydides (1.12.3) dates the Dorian conquest of the Peloponnese to eighty years after the fall of Troy; Herodotus (1.56) traces the Dorians through Phthiotis, Pindus and Doris; Tyrtaeus (fr. 2 West) has them come from Erineus. Mythology packaged it as the Return of the Heraclidae: the descendants of Heracles, exiled by Eurystheus, reclaimed the Peloponnese with Dorian allies and divided Argos, Sparta and Messenia by lot, while the Ionians fled to Attica and then to Asia (Apollodorus, Library 2.8.2–5; Pausanias 4.3). On this reading Arcado-Cypriot is a refugium: Mycenaean speech surviving in the mountains and in Cyprus, where Mycenaeans had settled before the collapse.

Where sources disagree. Archaeology has found no distinctive Dorian material culture arriving around 1100; the features once credited to them (cremation, iron, Protogeometric pottery, the single pin) appear in non-Dorian areas too. John Chadwick argued in 1976 that the Dorians were already in the Peloponnese as a subject population whose speech surfaced when the palaces fell; Jonathan Hall (1997) treats the Dorian identity as an archaic construction projected backwards. Others still accept small-scale movements from the north-west, because the dialect geography needs some explanation. The honest position is that the palace collapse is certain, a mass invasion is unproven, and the Return of the Heraclidae is Dorian self-justification rather than history.

Mountains, sea and the polis

The old textbook argument runs: Greece is eighty per cent mountain, its plains are small and separated, so each plain grew its own self-governing community, and the sea connected them just enough to make them rivals. The result was over a thousand poleis (Hansen and Nielsen's Inventory of 2004 lists about 1,035), most with a few thousand citizens. Plato pictured the Greeks "living round the sea like ants or frogs round a pond" (Phaedo 109b); Aristotle thought a polis should be surveyable at a glance (Politics 7.4, 1326b). Thucydides argued geographically too: poor soil kept Attica stable (1.2), and early cities were built inland for fear of piracy (1.7). Herodotus has Demaratus tell Xerxes that "poverty has always been Greece's foster-sister" (7.102).

Three qualifications matter. First, geography did not produce the polis everywhere: the plains of Thessaly and Boeotia produced federations, Epirus and Macedon kingdoms, so landscape shaped the kind of state rather than mandating the city. Second, the mountains did not isolate: shepherds, pilgrims and armies crossed them constantly, and the shared sanctuaries and dialect continuum prove it. Third, the sea was the real determinant: it made grain, olive and vine exportable, carried colonists, and let a small city punch above its size. Geography set the stage; it did not write the play.

Where sources disagree. Older scholarship (and Thucydides) treats Greek fragmentation as an environmental given. Robin Osborne and others have argued that the polis emerged from eighth-century social change (population growth, sanctuaries, hoplite land ownership) and that mountain terrain is a weak explanation, since mountainous Epirus produced no poleis while flat Ionia produced many. Horden and Purcell's The Corrupting Sea (2000) shifts the emphasis to connectivity and micro-regions. The disagreement is about weight, not fact.

Two features recur. Where there were visible Bronze Age ruins (Mycenae, Tiryns, Thebes, Knossos, Troy) the myths explain the ruins and attribute them to giants or heroes. Where there was a sanctuary at a frontier (Delphi, Olympia, Dodona) the myths explain why a god chose that spot and why no single city owns it. Eleusis is a local agrarian cult absorbed by Athens, whose Hymn makes Demeter herself order the temple built.

Where sources disagree. Troy. Hisarlik's Troy VI and VIIa were destroyed around 1300 and 1180; whether a Greek coalition did it is argued, not shown. Moses Finley (1964) held that nothing in Homer could be used as history; the Korfmann–Kolb quarrel over the size of Troy VI has its home in module 10.10. There is now broad agreement that Hisarlik is the Troy the Greeks meant and no agreement that the war happened.
Where sources disagree. Ithaca. Homer calls it "low-lying, farthest out to sea towards the dark" (Odyssey 9.25), which fits the modern island badly. Dörpfeld proposed Leucas; Bittlestone (2005) the Paliki peninsula of Cephalonia. Most scholars keep modern Ithaca and treat Homer's geography as poetic. Olympia too: Pausanias (5.7–8) records rival foundation myths, and the traditional 776 date for the first games is placed closer to 700 by some on the archaeology of the Altis.

Self-test

  1. Which Greek historian argued that Attica's poor soil explained Athenian stability and autochthony, and where?
    Answer

    Thucydides, 1.2.5–6.

  2. Name the seven traditional sub-regions of the Peloponnese.
    Answer

    Corinthia, Argolid, Laconia, Messenia, Elis, Achaea, Arcadia.

  3. Which dialect is spoken only in Arcadia and Cyprus, and why does that distribution matter?
    Answer

    Arcado-Cypriot; it is closest to the Mycenaean Greek of Linear B, suggesting it is a survival of Bronze Age speech in a mountain refugium and an overseas settlement, bypassed by Doric.

  4. What is the Return of the Heraclidae, and how does it relate to the Dorian invasion?
    Answer

    The myth in which Heracles' descendants, with Dorian allies, reconquer the Peloponnese and divide Argos, Sparta and Messenia by lot (Apollodorus 2.8.2–5). It is the mythological charter for the Dorian presence that Thucydides 1.12 dates eighty years after Troy.

  5. Give two pieces of evidence against a mass Dorian invasion.
    Answer

    No distinctive Dorian material culture appears around 1100; traits once called Dorian (cremation, iron, Protogeometric pottery) occur in non-Dorian regions too. Chadwick argued the Dorians were already present as a subject class.

  6. Which two mainland regions had large plains and did not develop into single poleis, and what did they become instead?
    Answer

    Thessaly (aristocratic confederacy of four tetrads under a tagos) and Boeotia (federal league of cities). Epirus and Macedon, mountainous and northern, became kingdoms.

  7. Who founded Syracuse, when, according to which source?
    Answer

    Corinthians under Archias, the year after Naxos, traditionally 733 BC, according to Thucydides 6.3.

  8. Why was the Black Sea renamed from Axeinos to Euxeinos, and who records this?
    Answer

    Euphemism: "inhospitable" became "hospitable" as Greeks settled it; Strabo 7.3.6.

  9. Which myths are attached to Olympia's foundation, and which source lists them side by side?
    Answer

    Heracles founding the games after Augeas (Pindar, Olympian 10), Pelops' race for Hippodameia, Zeus wrestling Cronus, and others; Pausanias 5.7–8 records the alternatives.

  10. What is the common pattern linking the myths of Mycenae, Thebes, Knossos and Troy?
    Answer

    Each is a site with visible Bronze Age ruins or walls that later Greeks explained by attributing them to heroes, giants or legendary kings.

Carry forward

  1. The Greek world is a sea with coasts, not a country: hold the Aegean triangle (mainland, Anatolia, Crete) with the western and Black Sea arms, and place every later event on it.
  2. Dialect geography (Ionic-Attic, Doric, Aeolic, Arcado-Cypriot) is real and ancient; the migration stories that explain it, above all the Dorian invasion and the Return of the Heraclidae, are later self-justifications whose historical core is uncertain.
  3. Landscape conditioned but did not dictate political form: small plains and a connecting sea favoured the polis, yet Thessaly, Boeotia, Epirus and Macedon show that federations and kingdoms were equally Greek answers to the same terrain.
