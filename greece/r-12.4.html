The life beyond the outline

Module 6.5 gives the frame, from Stagira in 384 to Chalcis in 322. The evidence is thin and late: Diogenes Laertius 5.1–35, with its gossip, catalogue and will; Dionysius of Halicarnassus (First Letter to Ammaeus 5), who dates each stage by Athenian archon; and the reverent Neoplatonic lives, all collected in Ingemar Düring's Aristotle in the Ancient Biographical Tradition (1957).

The Academy years (367–347) produced the published dialogues, now lost: the Eudemus on the soul, the Protrepticus, On Philosophy. Cicero's "golden river of speech" (Academica 2.119) probably praises them. Ingram Bywater (1869) recovered much of the Protrepticus from Iamblichus: it argues that thought is the end for which humans exist, a conviction Aristotle never dropped. Plato's death in 348/7 and Speusippus' succession ended his place in the school; whether the fall of Olynthus in 348 also made a man with Macedonian ties unwelcome in Athens is guessed, not attested.

Assos was a philosophers' court. Hermias, ruler of Atarneus and Assos, had taken in the Academics Erastus and Coriscus (Plato's Sixth Letter is addressed to all three); Aristotle and Xenocrates joined them, and Aristotle married Pythias, Hermias' niece or adopted daughter (Strabo 13.1.57; Diogenes Laertius 5.3). Around 341 the Persians seized Hermias by treachery and killed him; Aristotle's hymn to Virtue in his memory (Diogenes Laertius 5.7–8) was later used against him at Athens. Theophrastus of Eresus, his colleague for life, is first visible beside him here, and from about 345 the two worked on Lesbos.

Module 6.7 tells the years at Mieza, and modules 11.5 and 11.7 the fate of Aristotle's kinsman Callisthenes. A Delphic decree (FD III 1.400, before 329) crowns the two for compiling the list of Pythian victors; Aelian (Varia Historia 14.1) quotes a letter in which Aristotle takes the later loss of these honours calmly.

The Lyceum's novelty was organised research. Aristotle, a metic who could not own land, taught in a public gymnasium whose covered walk (peripatos) named the school (Diogenes Laertius 5.2). The catalogue lists the constitutions of 158 cities (5.27); the Constitution of the Athenians returned on a papyrus published in 1891, and P. J. Rhodes (1981) assigns it to a pupil. Other collections listed Olympic victors and dramatic productions, and the pupils divided the history of knowledge: Theophrastus on plants and physical opinions (module 12.1), Eudemus on geometry and astronomy, Meno on medicine. The will (Diogenes Laertius 5.11–16) names Antipater executor, provides for Herpyllis, his companion after Pythias died, and frees slaves.

Where sources disagree. Werner Jaeger (Aristoteles, 1923; module 6.5) mapped the corpus on to the life: Platonist in the Academy, critic at Assos, empiricist at the Lyceum, with the biology last; F. Nuyens (1939; French edition 1948) applied the method to the theory of the soul. H. D. P. Lee (1948) answered that the biology's place-names cluster around Lesbos and the Troad, placing it in the middle years, and G. E. L. Owen (1960) reversed the direction: the early logic is the most anti-Platonic, and the Metaphysics moves back towards Plato. Most now read the treatises as courses revised over decades.

Lecture notes and the cellar at Scepsis

Aristotle refers to his "exoteric" discussions (Nicomachean Ethics 1102a26; Politics 1278b31), the published dialogues. What survives is the other half, treatises (pragmateiai) for use inside the school, and they show it: cross-references in both directions, doublets (Metaphysics K repeats parts of B, Γ, E and the Physics), a philosophical lexicon (Metaphysics Δ), and three books shared by the two Ethics (NE 5–7 are EE 4–6). The catalogue in Diogenes Laertius (5.22–27) lists nearly 150 titles, many unknown to us, and lacks several treatises we read.

Strabo (13.1.54; module 6.5) tells how they came down. Theophrastus left the library, Aristotle's books with his own, to Neleus of Scepsis. Neleus' heirs hid the books in a trench from the Attalid kings, who were collecting for Pergamum, and damp and worms damaged them. Apellicon of Teos later bought them, filled the gaps badly and published faulty texts; Sulla took his library to Rome (module 7.3), where Tyrannion the grammarian handled it. Strabo's point is that the Peripatetics after Theophrastus, with few books and those mostly exoteric, could not philosophise systematically and merely declaimed on set themes. Plutarch (Sulla 26) adds that Andronicus of Rhodes obtained copies from Tyrannion, published them and drew up the tables now current.

Porphyry (Life of Plotinus 24) says Andronicus divided the works of Aristotle and Theophrastus into treatises, gathering related material together; our groupings, logic first and the Metaphysics named for its place after the physical works, probably descend from him. His date is open: Moraux (1973) put the work in the late 70s BC, Gottschalk (1987) in the 60s, Düring (1957) after Cicero's death, since Cicero never mentions it.

Where sources disagree. Athenaeus (1.3a–b) says Neleus sold Aristotle's books to Ptolemy II for Alexandria, which contradicts the cellar; and some treatises were plainly read in the Hellenistic school, since Eudemus asked Theophrastus for a sound text of a passage of the Physics (Simplicius, Commentary on the Physics). Barnes ("Roman Aristotle", 1997) argued that the two stories can both be true. Primavesi (2007) defends a modified Strabo: treatises absent from the Hellenistic catalogue keep a pre-Hellenistic system of book labels, so they reached our corpus from a collection out of circulation until about 100 BC.

Logic: categories, syllogism, demonstration

The six logical works were later grouped as the Organon, the instrument. The Categories sorts what is said without combination into ten kinds, from substance, quantity and quality to acting and being acted on (1b25–27). Its ontology is anti-Platonic (module 6.5): primary substance is the individual, this man or this horse; species and genus are secondary substances (2a11–19); and if primary substances did not exist, nothing else could (2b5–6).

The Prior Analytics defines a deduction (syllogismos) as an argument in which, certain things being laid down, something else follows of necessity from their being so (24b18–20). Its subject is the categorical syllogism, two premises sharing a middle term. The first figure is perfect, its validity evident: if A belongs to every B and B to every C, A belongs to every C (25b37–39), the mood medieval mnemonics called Barbara. Moods of the second and third figures are proved by conversion into the first or by reduction to the impossible, and invalid ones are rejected by counterexample. Aristotle claimed that on reasoning he found nothing done before him (Sophistical Refutations 34, 184b1–3); Stoic propositional logic later grew beside his (module 12.7).

Where sources disagree. Jan Łukasiewicz (Aristotle's Syllogistic, 1951) read each syllogism as a single conditional proposition and the system as an axiomatic theory. John Corcoran (1974) and Timothy Smiley (1973) read syllogisms as arguments and the system as natural deduction, closer to the text's practice of deriving conclusions from premises; most logicians since follow them.

The Posterior Analytics turns deduction into science: to know is to know the cause, through demonstration from premises that are true, primary, immediate, better known than, prior to and causes of the conclusion (71b19–22). First principles are grasped by induction and nous (2.19; module 6.5). The Topics supplies dialectic, which reasons from endoxa, the opinions of all, of most or of the wise (100b21–23). The treatises often open so, setting out the appearances and the puzzles (Nicomachean Ethics 1145b2–7); Owen ("Tithenai ta phainomena", 1961) argued that "appearances" there means what is said, not what is observed.

Physics: nature, change and ends

Module 6.5 gives the four causes; the Physics begins with the principles of change. Every change needs a persisting subject and a pair of contraries, a form gained and its privation: the unmusical man becomes musical (1.7). This was the answer to Parmenides that module 12.1 sets out. Nature is an internal principle of motion and rest (192b21–23). Motion is the actuality of the potential as such (201a10–11); place is the inner limit of the containing body (212a20–21); time is the number of motion with respect to before and after (219b1–2); and there is no void (4.8).

Book 2 defends final causes. Against those who say rain falls of necessity and only happens to ripen the corn, Aristotle argues that what happens always or for the most part cannot be chance, and that nature, like art, acts for an end without deliberating (2.8, 198b16–199b33). Sedley ("Is Aristotle's teleology anthropocentric?", 1991) adds Politics 1256b15–22, where plants exist for animals and animals for humans; most readers confine natural ends to the good of each kind. The heavens are made of aether, which moves naturally in circles (On the Heavens 1.2–3). A thrown stone keeps moving, Aristotle had to say, because the air pushes it (Physics 8.10); John Philoponus' sixth-century objection, that the thrower imparts a power to the stone itself, began the line to medieval impetus theory.

Metaphysics: substance and the unmoved mover

The Metaphysics is fourteen books of uneven date under an editor's title. Γ announces a science of being as being (1003a21): being is said in many ways, but all with reference to one, as everything healthy is called so with reference to health (1003a33–b10), what Owen named focal meaning. Γ also defends the principle of non-contradiction, the firmest of all (1005b19–20), which cannot be demonstrated, only defended against anyone willing to say something meaningful (1006a11–24).

Book Z starts again at Metaphysics 1028a10: being is said in many ways, and the old question what being is, is just the question what substance is (1028b2–4). The candidates are essence, universal, genus and substrate (1028b33–36). The substrate, stripped of attributes, collapses into bare matter, which cannot be primary, since substance must be separable and a this (1029a10–28). No universal is substance, since a universal is common and substance belongs to its owner alone (1038b8–12). What survives is form, the essence that makes this matter a man and the cause of its being (1041b27–28). The Categories made the individual primary; Z makes primary the form within it. Θ adds that actuality is prior to potentiality (Θ8, 1049b4–5).

Where sources disagree. If form is primary substance and no universal is substance, form must be particular: Socrates' form is his alone. Michael Frede and Günther Patzig (Aristoteles 'Metaphysik Z', 1988) took that line. Alan Code (1986) and Michael Loux (Primary Ousia, 1991) held that form is universal, shared by every man but predicated of matter rather than of individuals, so that Z13 does not exclude it.

Book Λ reaches god. If all substances were perishable, everything would be; but motion and time cannot have begun or ended, and the only continuous eternal motion is circular. So there is an eternal mover whose substance is actuality, without matter or potentiality (1071b3–22). It moves without being moved, as an object of desire and thought moves: it moves by being loved (1072b3). It thinks the best object, itself: thinking of thinking (1074b34–35). Λ8 multiplies the movers to match the spheres of Eudoxus and Callippus, fifty-five or forty-seven, as merely probable (1073b1–1074a14). The universe's good is like an army's, in its order and in its general, but more in the general (1075a11–15).

The biologist at Pyrrha and the soul

Module 6.5 gives the dogfish, the hectocotylus, the dolphins and Darwin's verdict. The lagoon of Pyrrha, the modern Gulf of Kalloni on Lesbos, is where much of that looking was done: the History of Animals names it for the fish that leave it in winter and the starfish that plague its fishermen, and Armand Marie Leroi's The Lagoon (2014) reconstructs the fieldwork from such notices. The method is first to collect differences and attributes, then to seek causes (History of Animals 491a7–14). The chick embryo, opened day by day, shows the heart as a beating speck of blood by the third day (6.3).

The Parts of Animals puts the final cause first (639b11–21): necessity in nature is hypothetical, as a saw must be of iron if it is to cut (Physics 200a10–13). The Generation of Animals makes the male supply form and motion, the female matter (729a9–11), calls the female a deformed male (737a27–28), and says that intellect alone enters from outside (736b27–28).

On the Soul defines soul as the first actuality of a natural body that has life potentially, a body with organs (412a27–b6). There is no more need to ask whether soul and body are one than whether the wax and its shape are (412b6–9); if the eye were an animal, sight would be its soul (412b18–22). Plants nourish, animals also perceive, humans also think (2.3). Perception takes on form without matter, as wax takes the seal's imprint without the gold (424a17–24). Before it thinks, the mind is actually nothing, a writing tablet with nothing yet written on it (429a24, 430a1). Then come the few lines of 3.5 (430a10–25): one intellect becomes all things, another makes all things, like light, and this one is separable, unaffected, and alone immortal and eternal.

Where sources disagree. Alexander of Aphrodisias identified the productive intellect with god, the first cause, so that nothing personal survives; Averroes made the receptive intellect, too, one and separate for all humans. Aquinas, On the Unity of the Intellect against the Averroists (1270), argued that both are powers of each individual soul. Moderns divide over perception as well: whether the eye-jelly literally turns red (Sorabji, 1974) or simply becomes aware of red (Burnyeat, 1992).

The Nicomachean Ethics beyond the mean

Module 6.5 gives the function argument, the mean, weakness of will and contemplation. Book 1 first fixes what the final good must be: complete, chosen always for itself, and self-sufficient, making life choiceworthy on its own (1097a25–b21). Happiness is activity over a complete life, for one swallow does not make a spring (1098a18–20). Fortune matters: Priam, virtuous and ruined, is not called happy, though the good man will never become wretched (1100a5–9, 1101a6–8). Pleasure is no process but the completion of an unimpeded activity, supervening like the bloom on those in their prime (1174b31–33).

Friendship fills two books because the city depends on it: lawgivers care more for it than for justice (1155a22–26). Its three kinds follow the three lovable things, the useful, the pleasant and the good (1155b17–1156a10). Friendships of use and pleasure end with the advantage; the complete kind joins good people alike in virtue, each wishing the other's good for the other's sake (1156b7–12). A friend is another self (1166a31–32). Why should the self-sufficient man need one? Because we contemplate our neighbours' actions better than our own, and a good friend's are good and akin to ours (9.9, 1169b33–1170a4). Book 8 maps constitutions on to household bonds: kingship on to fatherhood, aristocracy on to marriage, timocracy on to brotherhood (1160a31–1161a9).

Book 10 ranks the lives. Contemplation is the activity of the best part of us, the most continuous, pleasant, self-sufficient and leisured; so far as we can, we should make ourselves immortal (1177b33), and the life of practical virtue is happy in a secondary degree (1178a9–10).

Where sources disagree. W. F. R. Hardie ("The Final Good in Aristotle's Ethics", 1965) distinguished a dominant end from an inclusive one and found Aristotle wavering between them. J. L. Ackrill ("Aristotle on Eudaimonia", 1974) read Book 1 inclusively: happiness contains every intrinsic good, so nothing added could make it better. Richard Kraut (Aristotle on the Human Good, 1989) took Book 10 at its word: perfect happiness is contemplation, and the moral life a secondary happiness.

Household, slavery and constitutions

Module 6.5 gives the political animal, natural slavery with its admitted controversy, the six constitutions and the middle class. Book 1 builds the city from the household: master and slave, husband and wife, father and children (1253b4–11). Rule over a wife is political, over children royal (1259a37–b17); the woman has the deliberative part but without authority (akuron), the child has it undeveloped, the slave not at all (1260a12–14).

The argument for natural slavery runs through tools. The slave is a living tool for action; if shuttles wove by themselves, masters would need no slaves (1253b33–1254a1). Some people share in reason enough to perceive it but not to have it (1254b20–23), and for them being ruled is just and beneficial. Aristotle concedes that nature often fails to mark slaves' bodies and free men's souls as it intends (1254b27–1255a1), and that war enslaves the well-born (1255a3–b4); he names no natural slaves beyond hinting that barbarians fit the type (1252b5–9). Usury, money breeding money, is the most unnatural acquisition (1258b2–8).

What really divides oligarchy from democracy is wealth, not number: the rich happen to be few and the poor many (1279b34–1280a6). Book 3 grants the many a claim, since a crowd judging together may surpass the few best, as a feast to which many contribute beats one man's dinner (1281a40–b10). Polity mixes oligarchy and democracy (1293b33–34). The best city of Books 7 and 8 denies citizenship to farmers and craftsmen, whose work leaves no leisure for virtue (1328b33–1329a2).

Rhetoric and Poetics

Plato's Gorgias denied that rhetoric was an art, calling it a knack (462b–465a). Aristotle's Rhetoric opens by calling it the counterpart of dialectic (1354a1) and defines it as the capacity to see the available means of persuasion in each case (1355b25–26). Persuasion comes through the speaker's character, the hearer's emotion and the argument itself (1356a1–4). The argument proper is the enthymeme, a rhetorical deduction from probabilities and signs (1356b4–5). Speeches are deliberative, forensic or epideictic (1358b6–8). Book 2 gives the first systematic analysis of the emotions, each defined by its cause and object: anger is a desire, with pain, for revenge for an apparent slight (1378a30–32).

Module 6.5 gives tragedy's definition, plot, hamartia and the katharsis dispute. Behind them lies a theory of imitation. Humans are the most imitative of animals and learn first by imitating, and they enjoy exact images of things painful to see, such as corpses, because they learn from them (1448b4–19). A plot is a whole with beginning, middle and end (1450b26–27). Poetry is more philosophical than history, because it speaks of universals, what such a person would probably or necessarily do, where history says what Alcibiades did (1451b5–11). The lost second book, on comedy, drives Umberto Eco's Il nome della rosa (1980); Richard Janko (Aristotle on Comedy, 1984) argued that the Tractatus Coislinianus summarises it.

Afterlife: the commentators, Arabic and Latin

The school declined after Theophrastus (head 322–c. 287), and Strabo blamed the lost books. Alexander of Aphrodisias, whose On Fate is addressed to Septimius Severus and Caracalla, taught Aristotle's philosophy around 200 (module 7.4 has the chairs Marcus Aurelius endowed). The Neoplatonists (module 12.8) made Aristotle the road to Plato: Porphyry's Isagoge introduced the Categories, and Philoponus attacked the eternity of the world. Boethius' Latin Categories and On Interpretation carried the old logic into the Latin Middle Ages; Rome module 13.3 takes the Peripatetics at Rome.

Module 9.3 gives the Baghdad translators and the Toledo route. Al-Farabi was called the Second Teacher, after Aristotle. Avicenna (Ibn Sina, 980–1037) rewrote the system in his Healing, sharpening the distinction between essence and existence. Averroes (Ibn Rushd, 1126–1198) of Córdoba wrote short, middle and long commentaries, defending Aristotle against Avicenna's Platonising; the Latins called him the Commentator, and Dante set him in Limbo near the "master of those who know" (Inferno 4.131, 4.144).

Paris banned lectures on Aristotle's natural philosophy in 1210 and 1215; by 1255 its arts statutes prescribed nearly the whole corpus, which William of Moerbeke then translated or revised from the Greek. Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274) commented on the major treatises, called Aristotle simply the Philosopher, and rebuilt theology with his tools: the first of the five ways argues from motion to an unmoved first mover (Summa Theologiae I q.2 a.3). Where Aristotle conflicted with faith, Aquinas held that reason cannot settle the matter: the world's beginning in time is believed, not demonstrated (I q.46 a.2). In 1277 Bishop Tempier of Paris condemned 219 propositions, some of them Aristotelian. The physics fell in the seventeenth century; the logic lasted, and Kant could say it had not taken a step forward since Aristotle (Critique of Pure Reason, preface to the second edition, 1787).

Self-test

  1. How did Jaeger map the corpus on to the life, and how did Lee and Owen each undermine the map?
    Answer

    Jaeger (1923) placed a Platonist Aristotle in the Academy, a critic at Assos and an empiricist at the Lyceum, with the biology last. Lee (1948) argued that the biology's place-names point to Lesbos and the Troad in the middle years; Owen (1960) argued that the early logic is the most anti-Platonic and the Metaphysics moves back towards Plato.

  2. What does Strabo infer from the Scepsis story, and what evidence tells against the inference?
    Answer

    That the later Peripatetics, lacking the treatises, could not philosophise systematically (13.1.54). Athenaeus (1.3a–b) has Neleus sell the books to Ptolemy II, and Eudemus' request to Theophrastus for a sound text of the Physics shows treatises in use inside the school.

  3. How does Book Z of the Metaphysics revise the Categories on primary substance?
    Answer

    The Categories makes the individual, this man, primary substance and species secondary (2a11–19). Z rejects substrate and universal as candidates and makes form, the essence within the individual, primary substance and the cause of its being (1041b27–28), which raises the dispute whether form is particular or universal.

  4. Why is the first figure called perfect, and how are the other figures validated?
    Answer

    Its validity is evident without further steps, as in Barbara: if A belongs to every B and B to every C, A belongs to every C (Prior Analytics 25b37–39). Second- and third-figure moods are proved by converting premises into a first-figure syllogism or by reduction to the impossible.

  5. How does demonstration differ from dialectic, and what are endoxa?
    Answer

    Demonstration deduces conclusions from true, primary premises that are causes of the conclusion (Posterior Analytics 71b19–22); dialectic reasons from endoxa, the opinions of all, most or the wise (Topics 100b21–23). The treatises use dialectic to set out the appearances and puzzles; first principles themselves are grasped by induction and nous.

  6. Reconstruct the argument of Metaphysics Λ for an unmoved mover, and say how it moves the heavens.
    Answer

    Motion and time are eternal; the only continuous eternal motion is circular; so there must be an eternal mover whose substance is actuality without matter (1071b3–22). It moves as an object of love and thought moves those who desire it (1072b3), its own activity being thinking of thinking.

  7. Give three readings of the productive intellect of On the Soul 3.5.
    Answer

    Alexander of Aphrodisias identified it with god, the first cause. Averroes made the receptive intellect, too, one and separate for all humans. Aquinas (1270) made both powers of each individual soul.

  8. Why, by Nicomachean Ethics 9.9, does the self-sufficient happy man need friends?
    Answer

    Because we contemplate others' actions better than our own, and a good friend, another self, performs good actions akin to ours (1169b33–1170a4); the friend lets the good man see his own kind of activity.

  9. How does Aristotle argue for natural slavery, and what does he concede?
    Answer

    The slave is a living tool, and some people share in reason enough to perceive it but not to have it, so being ruled benefits them (1253b33–1254a1, 1254b20–23). He concedes that nature often fails to mark slaves and free men as it intends and that war enslaves the well-born (1254b27–1255b4).

  10. Why is poetry more philosophical than history, according to the Poetics?
    Answer

    Poetry speaks of universals, what such a person would probably or necessarily do, while history says what a particular person, such as Alcibiades, did (1451b5–11).

Carry forward

  1. The corpus is school material that came through a narrow, contested channel, from Theophrastus' library to Andronicus' arrangement. Read its doublets and tensions as stages of inquiry, and treat every developmental chronology as a hypothesis.
  2. Aristotle's method is to set out the appearances and opinions, find the puzzles and dissolve them by distinctions: being said in many ways, form and matter, potential and actual, the four causes. That vocabulary is what the Hellenistic schools, the commentators and the scholastics argued in.
  3. His most consequential texts are his shortest and most ambiguous: Λ on god and On the Soul 3.5 on intellect. Read as impersonal intellect by Alexander and Averroes, and as compatible with a personal soul and a creator by Aquinas, they decided what Aristotle meant to Islam and Christendom.
