01How a Homeric battle works

The Iliad looks like a string of single combats, but its battlefield is always crowded. The narrative follows the promachoi, the "fighters in front", who step out from the mass, throw their spears, kill or die, and fall back among their companions when pressed. The anonymous plēthus behind them fights as well. Joachim Latacz (Kampfparänese, 1977) and Hans van Wees (Status Warriors, 1992) argued that this fluid, open-order mass fighting is the poems' real tactical picture, and that the duels are close-ups within it.

An encounter follows a pattern. There is a boast or challenge, often with a genealogy (Glaucus at Iliad 6.145–211). Then comes a thrown spear, usually one of a pair, then a thrust, then the sword if the spear is lost. When weapons run out, heroes throw boulders. Diomedes lifts one "which two men could not carry, such as mortals are now" (5.302–304; compare 12.445–449). The phrase shows that the poet himself places his heroes in a stronger, vanished age.

Chariots, aristeiai and spoils

Chariots are mostly taxis. The hero rides up, dismounts, fights on foot, and keeps his driver close by for a quick retreat. The exception is telling. Nestor orders his Pylians to keep their chariots in line, saying "this is how men of old sacked cities" (4.297–309). The poet knows that chariots were once a massed arm but not how they worked. Linear B tablets from Knossos list hundreds of chariots, so the palaces certainly fielded them. Homer's taxi-chariot may be a memory that has lost its function, or, as P. A. L. Greenhalgh argued (Early Greek Warfare, 1973), the eighth-century practice of riding to battle projected back onto the past.

The aristeia, a run in which one hero dominates the field, structures the narrative: Diomedes in Book 5, Agamemnon in 11, Patroclus in 16, Achilles in 20–22. Each has an arming scene, a series of kills, a wound or a divine intervention, and a climax. Stripping armour is the visible proof of victory, and the struggle for corpses fills whole books. Book 17 is entirely about Patroclus' body, and Hector puts on the armour he has stripped from it (17.192–197). Nestor has to warn the Achaeans to kill first and strip later (6.66–71), which shows that the habit could become a tactical liability.

Anachronisms in both directions

Twice men stand packed "shield on shield, helmet on helmet, man on man" (13.130–133; 16.212–217), which is the language of close formation. Shields are often "round", as hoplite shields were. Yet Hector's shield bumps his neck and ankles as he walks (6.117–118), a Bronze Age body shield. Weapons are bronze, but iron appears in similes and tools, in one arrowhead (4.123), and as a prize at Patroclus' games (23.826–835).

Where sources disagree. Latacz took the massed passages as evidence that a phalanx already existed in the poet's day. Snodgrass ("The Hoplite Reform and History", Journal of Hellenic Studies 85, 1965) dated the hoplite panoply to c. 700–650, with the Chigi vase (c. 650) as the first clear image of a formation. Van Wees argues that the tight phalanx came later still, perhaps in the sixth century. Whether Homeric warfare is pre-hoplite, proto-hoplite or incoherent is uncertain.

02The economy of honour

Timē means both honour and price, and in Homer it is material. It shows in the best meat, the front seat and the full cup (Iliad 12.310–321), and above all in the geras, the prize of honour that the army allots from the spoils. Iliad 1 is a dispute over geras. Agamemnon refuses to be left alone without a prize (1.118–120), and Achilles protests that he does the fighting and gets the smaller share (1.163–168). Taking Briseis publicly marks down Achilles' worth.

Kleos, "what is heard", is fame in song. Achilles chooses between a short life with kleos aphthiton, "unwithering fame", and a long life without it (9.410–416). Sarpedon sets out the bargain: privilege at home is paid for by risk at the front (12.310–328). Aidōs is the restraining sense of other people's eyes. Hector stays outside the walls from aidōs before the Trojans (22.105–107). Nemesis is the indignation others feel when the code is broken (6.350–351).

Where sources disagree. E. R. Dodds (The Greeks and the Irrational, 1951) called Homeric society a "shame culture". Douglas Cairns (Aidōs, 1993) and Bernard Williams (Shame and Necessity, 1993) argued that aidōs is internalised, an imagined audience the hero carries within him, and that the shame/guilt contrast distorts the poems.

Gifts as a social system

Moses Finley, following Marcel Mauss (Essai sur le don, 1925), put gift exchange at the centre of the Homeric world. A gift creates an obligation, and an unreturned gift is a debt of status.

  • Glaucus and Diomedes (Iliad 6.119–236). The two enemies find that their grandfathers were guest-friends, so they refuse to fight and exchange armour. Glaucus gives gold for bronze, "a hundred oxen's worth for nine" (6.236). The comment is read as irony: Zeus has taken Glaucus' wits, since a gold cuirass for a bronze one is no exchange between equals. Either way, hereditary xenia overrides the war.
  • The Embassy (9.121–157). Agamemnon offers tripods, gold, horses and women, returns Briseis under oath, and adds a daughter without bride-price and seven cities. He also says Achilles should yield "since I am more kingly" (9.160–161), and Odysseus leaves that line out when he relays the offer. Achilles refuses because accepting would confirm the hierarchy. A gift this large is an assertion of superiority.
  • Priam's ransom (24.228–237, 553–595). The poem opens with a father's ransom refused (Chryses, 1.12–32) and closes with one accepted. That ring frames the Iliad around whether goods can still buy back what violence has taken.

03Xenia, the oikos and the limits of power

Xenia is the poems' international law. Zeus Xenios protects it (Odyssey 9.270–271), it passes down through families, and it has a protocol: welcome, bath and food come first, and questions about who the guest is come after (1.118–124; 3.69–74). It ends with guest-gifts, often heirlooms with pedigrees, such as the Sidonian mixing bowl Menelaus passes on (4.613–619). The Odyssey is a sequence of tests of xenia. The Phaeacians are ideal hosts. Polyphemus parodies the custom with his "gift" of eating Odysseus last (9.369–370). The suitors are permanent uninvited guests who eat the host's property. The war itself began when Paris stole his host's wife.

The basic unit is the oikos: land, herds, stored treasure (keimēlia), dependants and slaves. The suitors' crime is economic, the consumption of Odysseus' livelihood, and his wealth is counted in herds (14.96–104).

Basileis and the assembly

A basileus is a leading man among peers, not a monarch. Ithaca has many basileis (Odyssey 1.394–396), and Scheria has twelve, with Alcinous as the thirteenth (8.390–391). Agamemnon's authority rests on a sceptre said to come from Zeus (Iliad 2.100–108) and on the size of his contingent, not on any administration. Odysseus' "let there be one ruler" (2.204) is spoken just as one-man rule is visibly failing.

The agora matters but does not decide. The army shouts for Chryseis' return and Agamemnon overrides it (1.22–25). Thersites, the only commoner who speaks, is beaten to the army's laughter (2.211–277). Ithaca's assembly has not met in twenty years (Odyssey 2.26–27), and at the end of the poem it splits, with more than half arming against Odysseus (24.420–471). The Shield shows elders judging a blood-price case in the agora (Iliad 18.497–508). Hesiod's "gift-devouring basileis" (Works and Days 38–39, 263–264) show a similar world seen from below.

Where sources disagree. Kurt Raaflaub sees a polis in formation in these assemblies, in Scheria's walls and temples (Odyssey 6.7–10) and in the language of community. Finley saw a pre-political world of oikoi. The question is whether the scenes of communal action are vestigial or emergent.

04Slavery and the fate of captured women

Slaves come through capture, purchase and birth. Eumaeus was a king's son, carried off by a Phoenician slave-woman and sold to Laertes (Odyssey 15.403–484). Slavery is a fall in status, not an innate condition. Eumaeus owns a slave himself (14.449–452) and hopes for a house, land and a wife (14.62–66), which Odysseus later promises him (21.213–216). He also states the system's ideology: Zeus takes away half a man's worth on the day he is enslaved (17.322–323).

Eurycleia cost Laertes twenty oxen, and he never slept with her for fear of his wife's anger (1.429–433). The line shows both the master's entitlement and the wife's power in the house. Twelve of the fifty maids took up with the suitors (22.421–425). Telemachus hangs them, choosing a death that is "not clean" (22.462–473). The poem presents this as justice, and modern readers find it the hardest scene in the epic.

For women in war, capture was the expected end. Agamemnon offers captive women as gifts (Iliad 9.128–140). Briseis lost her husband and brothers to Achilles. Her lament recalls Patroclus' promise to make her Achilles' wife (19.282–300), which was a captive's best hope. Andromache foresees herself at an Argive's loom (6.454–458). The victims' side is imagined most sharply in a simile. Odysseus weeps over the song of Troy like a woman dragged into slavery from her dying husband (Odyssey 8.523–531), so the sacker of cities weeps like the women he enslaved.

05Marriage and the position of women

The marriage gifts do not form one system. Hedna usually means bride-price paid by the suitor. Iphidamas gave a hundred cattle (Iliad 11.241–245), Agamemnon offers a daughter anaednon, "without hedna" (9.146–148), and Hephaestus demands his hedna back after Aphrodite's adultery (Odyssey 8.317–320). But elsewhere Penelope's father "prepares the hedna" (1.277–278), and Telemachus speaks of Icarius endowing his daughter (2.52–54). That looks like dowry. Penelope also demands courtship gifts from the suitors (18.274–283).

Where sources disagree. Snodgrass ("An Historical Homeric Society?", Journal of Hellenic Studies 94, 1974) made the marriage gifts his star exhibit: no real society muddles bride-price and dowry, so the poems are an amalgam. Morris (1986) replied that real societies combine them, through indirect dowry and competitive courtship, so the "confusion" is aristocratic bargaining.

The women are elite women seen from inside the oikos, and they have more room to act than the rules suggest.

  • Andromache gives Hector tactical advice (Iliad 6.433–439) and is sent back to her loom: "war will be the concern of men" (6.492). Telemachus twice turns the line on Penelope (Odyssey 1.356–359; 21.350–353).
  • Penelope controls the timing of remarriage with the shroud trick (2.93–110) and sets the contest of the bow. She is the pivot of the succession.
  • Arete is the person Odysseus is told to supplicate (6.303–315; 7.53–77). She settles men's quarrels and is honoured "like a god".
  • Helen weaves the war into her web (Iliad 3.125–128) and drugs the wine at Sparta (Odyssey 4.219–232). She is the most self-aware figure in either poem.
  • Nausicaa thinks about marriage and fears gossip if she is seen with a stranger (Odyssey 6.273–288): aidōs governs where she goes.

06Religion as practised

The gods are characters, but human cult is recognisably historical. Sacrifice follows a fixed order: barley, prayer, slaughter, thigh bones wrapped in fat for the gods, entrails tasted, meat shared (Iliad 1.447–474; Odyssey 3.418–463). Prayer is reciprocal: Chryses reminds Apollo of the temple he roofed for him (Iliad 1.37–42). Theano lays a robe on the knees of Athena's seated statue in a temple, and the goddess refuses (6.297–311). Temple and cult image together make this an eighth-century scene. Omens are read by seers or ignored. Hector answers Polydamas: "one omen is best, to fight for one's country" (12.243). Oaths are sacrifices with a curse attached: the oath-breakers' brains are to be poured out like the wine (3.264–301). The dead are cremated and become strengthless shades (Odyssey 11). Hero cult and Delphic consultation are almost absent, perhaps by deliberate archaism.

07Which world? The historicity debate

After Schliemann, scholars read the poems as Mycenaean. Martin Nilsson (Homer and Mycenae, 1933), H. L. Lorimer (Homer and the Monuments, 1950) and Denys Page (History and the Homeric Iliad, 1959) catalogued the survivals. Linear B, deciphered in 1952, showed how much had been lost. The palaces ran scribal, redistributive economies. Homer has no palace administration, and he mentions writing once, in Bellerophon's "baneful signs" (Iliad 6.168–170).

The Mycenaean survivals

  • The boar's-tusk helmet (Iliad 10.261–265). It matches excavated examples, and the type was out of use by about the twelfth century.
  • Ajax's tower shield of seven hides and bronze (7.219–223). It matches Shaft Grave-era shields such as those on the Lion Hunt dagger.
  • Bronze weapons throughout, and Nestor's dove-handled cup (11.632–637).
  • The wanax. Linear B wa-na-ka was the palace king, and qa-si-re-u was a local official. Homer keeps anax as a title ("lord of men") but not the institution, and the basileus has risen to the top.
  • The Catalogue of Ships (2.494–759). Page read it as a Bronze Age map; Giovannini (Étude historique sur les origines du Catalogue des vaisseaux, 1969) derived it from seventh-century Delphic lists, and Visser (Homers Katalog der Schiffe, 1997) from the poet's own composition. The question is open.

Four modern positions

ScholarWorkClaimDate

Finley's tenth century rested on absences: Homer has no polis, no colonisation and no hoplites. Morris inverted the argument. A tradition that stays intelligible keeps updating its social world, and what looks archaic is mostly material culture used to signal "long ago". Later archaeology also gave the eighth century texture: temples, colonial foundations like Scheria's, Phoenician traders, and the "Nestor's cup" from Pithekoussai (c. 725).

Where sources disagree. The date of the poems is itself contested. Richard Janko's linguistic statistics (1982) place the Iliad around 750–725. Martin West argued for c. 680–640 ("The Date of the Iliad", Museum Helveticum 52, 1995). Gregory Nagy's evolutionary model (Homeric Questions, 1996) has the text fixing gradually into the sixth century, which makes the search for a single date misguided.

Oral tradition and the compression of centuries

Milman Parry and Albert Lord (The Singer of Tales, 1960) showed that noun-epithet formulas are a technology for composing in performance. Formulas are conservative: a phrase that scans survives long after its referent has gone, which is how a boar's-tusk helmet lasts four centuries in verse. The dialect agrees. It is an artificial mix of Ionic, Aeolic and older forms, some close to Arcado-Cypriot, and no one ever spoke it.

The layers are uneven. Objects and the formulas attached to them hold the oldest material. Social relations, which the audience had to recognise, renew themselves fastest. On this view Snodgrass and Morris are less opposed than they seem: the material inventory is an amalgam, while the institutions are largely recent and touched up to look old.

08Using Homer as a historical source

  1. Separate the layers. A weapon or title may be centuries older than its line. Material details are evidence of the tradition's memory, not of a period.
  2. Trust the incidental. Similes show the poet's world: iron tools, ploughing, horse-riding (Iliad 15.679–684). The plot's background assumptions are better evidence than the plot.
  3. Read the archaising filter. The poems exclude writing, coinage, Ionia and almost all Dorians (one mention, Odyssey 19.177). Something missing from Homer may still have existed in the poet's day.
  4. Remember whose voice it is. Thersites is mocked and slaves are loyal or punished. The poems give elite values, not a census.
  5. Avoid circularity. Date objects by excavation, then ask whether Homer knows them. Never date a site by the poem.
  6. Use controls. Where Homer agrees with Hesiod, Geometric art and early inscriptions, you are probably looking at the poet's present.

09Self-test

  1. What are promachoi, and how did Latacz and van Wees change the standard picture of Homeric battle?
    Answer

    Promachoi are the "front fighters" who step out from the mass to fight and fall back when pressed. Latacz and van Wees argued that the anonymous mass is always present and fighting, so the poems describe fluid open-order mass combat, and the duels are close-ups within it rather than the whole of the battle.

  2. Why is Nestor's advice at Iliad 4.297–309 significant for the chariot question?
    Answer

    It is the one passage in which chariots fight as a massed arm, and Nestor presents it as the practice of "men of old". Everywhere else chariots act as taxis. The poet seems to remember that chariots were once a tactical force without knowing how they worked.

  3. Give two passages that look like anachronistic hoplite or phalanx features, and one Bronze Age feature they clash with.
    Answer

    The close formation "shield on shield, helmet on helmet, man on man" (Iliad 13.130–133; 16.212–217) and the "round" shield vocabulary. They clash with Hector's body shield, which bumps his neck and ankles (6.117–118), and with Ajax's tower shield (7.219–223).

  4. Explain the double meaning of timē and how it drives the quarrel in Iliad 1.
    Answer

    Timē means both honour and price or value. Honour is expressed in material shares, above all the geras. When Agamemnon takes Briseis, Achilles' geras, he publicly lowers Achilles' worth, so the quarrel is about status made visible in goods.

  5. Why does Achilles refuse the gifts in Iliad 9, given how large they are?
    Answer

    Accepting them would acknowledge Agamemnon's superiority. Agamemnon explicitly says Achilles should submit because Agamemnon is "more kingly" (9.160–161), a line Odysseus leaves out. A gift that large asserts hierarchy, and Achilles will not be bought back into subordination.

  6. What ring structure do the ransoms of Chryses and Priam create?
    Answer

    The Iliad opens with a father's ransom for his child refused (Chryses, Book 1) and closes with a father's ransom accepted (Priam, Book 24). The poem is framed by the question of whether goods can still buy back what violence has taken.

  7. What does the story of Eumaeus show about how a person became a slave in the Homeric world?
    Answer

    Slavery was a fall in status, not an innate condition. Eumaeus was a king's son carried off by a Phoenician slave-woman and sold to Laertes. Slaves came through capture, purchase and birth, and a favoured slave could own a slave himself and hope for a house, land and a wife.

  8. Why did Snodgrass treat the marriage gifts as evidence against an "historical Homeric society", and what was the reply?
    Answer

    Hedna sometimes runs from suitor to father, which is bride-price, and sometimes from father with daughter, which looks like dowry. Snodgrass argued that no real society works this way, so the poems are an amalgam. Morris replied that real societies do combine the two (indirect dowry, competitive courtship gifts), so the mixture reflects elite bargaining rather than compilation.

  9. Contrast Finley's dating of Homeric society with Morris's, and state the principle behind Morris's view.
    Answer

    Finley placed it in the tenth to ninth centuries because the poems lack the polis, colonisation and hoplites. Morris placed it in the eighth century, the poet's own, on the principle that oral tradition must stay intelligible to its audience, so institutions are contemporary and archaism is a deliberate distancing device.

  10. Name three Mycenaean survivals in Homer and explain why the wanax is a survival of a word rather than an institution.
    Answer

    The boar's-tusk helmet (Iliad 10.261–265), Ajax's tower shield (7.219–223) and the consistently bronze weapons (Nestor's cup is another). Linear B wa-na-ka was the palace king at the head of a scribal bureaucracy. Homer keeps anax as an honorific, but there are no palaces or scribes in the poems, and his basileis, who were minor officials in Linear B, are now the ruling class.

10Carry forward

  1. Homer's layers are uneven. Objects and formulas preserve the Bronze Age, while social institutions mostly reflect the poet's eighth- or seventh-century present, dressed to look ancient.
  2. Homeric honour is material and relational. Timē, geras and gift exchange tie status to goods, and xenia structures relations between oikoi, a pattern that later Greek aristocratic culture inherits.
  3. Use Homer through controls: date things by archaeology, trust incidental details and similes over the plot, and read the poems as elite ideology rather than social description.

Give me an in-depth report on the society and warfare depicted in the Homeric poems and the debate about which real period they reflect. Cover: how a Homeric battle works (the promachoi and the mass, chariots as taxis, duels and aristeiai, spear and sword, the stripping of armour, the boulder-throwing, where the phalanx and the hoplite shield intrude as anachronisms); the economy of honour (time, geras, kleos, aidos, nemesis) and the gift exchange that structures it (Glaucus and Diomedes, the Embassy's gifts, Priam's ransom); xenia as institution; the oikos, the basileis, the agora and the limits of assembly; slavery in the poems (Eumaeus, Eurycleia, Briseis, the maids) and the fate of captured women; marriage, dowry and bride-price confusion; the position of women (Andromache, Penelope, Arete, Helen, Nausicaa); religion as practised in the poems (sacrifice, prayer, omens, oaths); the big debate: Finley's World of Odysseus and the tenth-century thesis, Snodgrass's amalgam, Morris's eighth-century thesis, Raaflaub, and the Mycenaean survivals (boar's-tusk helmet, Ajax's shield, bronze weapons, the wanax); the poems as oral tradition compressing centuries. End with the practical consequence: how to use Homer as a historical source without being fooled by him. Format: about 2500 words, headed sections, name sources, flag disagreements, end with a 10-question self-test with answers and the three ideas I must carry forward.
