Samos, Lampsacus and the Garden

Module 7.2 gives the dates (341–270), the garden bought in 306 and a community open to women and slaves. Epicurus was an Athenian of the deme Gargettus, brought up on Samos, where his parents Neocles and Chaerestrate had settled in the Athenian cleruchy (Diogenes Laertius 10.1). He came to Athens at eighteen, the age of ephebic service, then joined his father at Colophon after Perdiccas expelled the Athenians from Samos (10.1). He said he turned to philosophy at fourteen; Apollodorus says his schoolmasters could not tell him where Hesiod's Chaos came from (10.2; module 2.1 reads the line). He heard the Platonist Pamphilus and the Democritean Nausiphanes, then called himself self-taught and Nausiphanes a jellyfish (10.8, 10.13–14; Cicero, On the Nature of the Gods 1.72–73).

He taught at Mytilene and Lampsacus, where he gathered the core of the school: Metrodorus, Polyaenus, Colotes, Idomeneus, Leonteus and Themista, with Hermarchus, his successor, from Mytilene. In 307/6 he returned to Athens (10.2) and bought a garden for eighty minae (10.10). He died of a kidney stone in 271/0, aged seventy-two (10.15), writing on his last day to Idomeneus that against his pains stood the joy of remembering their conversations (10.22); Cicero's version is addressed to Hermarchus (On Ends 2.96).

The members lived simply and did not pool property, since sharing implies distrust (10.11). The will (10.16–21) leaves the garden, through two trustees, to Hermarchus and his successors, funds Epicurus' birthday on the tenth of Gamelion and a meeting on the twentieth of every month, provides for Metrodorus' children and frees four slaves. Romans later wore his portrait on rings (Cicero, On Ends 5.3), kept the twentieth as a feast (Pliny, Natural History 35.5) and were told to act as if Epicurus were watching (Seneca, Letters 25.5).

The sources: letters, maxims and charred rolls

Epicurus wrote some three hundred rolls (10.26), among them thirty-seven books On Nature (10.27). What survives whole is what Diogenes Laertius copied into book 10, which he ends with the Principal Doctrines (Kyriai Doxai, KD) as the colophon of his work (10.138).

The letters are epitomes, written so that the main heads could be held in memory (10.35–36) and practised day and night (10.135). The Vatican Sayings (SV), eighty-one maxims, were found by Carl Wotke in 1888 in a fourteenth-century manuscript (Vaticanus graecus 1950); some repeat the KD, and some are elsewhere ascribed to Metrodorus. The rest is carbonised. From 1752 the Villa of the Papyri at Herculaneum yielded an Epicurean library (Rome module 13.3 takes the villa, Philodemus and Piso). It held books of On Nature, among them book 25, on the mind and responsibility (PHerc. 419, 697, 1056, 1191, 1420, 1634), and book 28, on language and error (PHerc. 1479/1417), besides Philodemus' treatises, such as On Piety, On Signs (PHerc. 1065) and On Death (PHerc. 1050).

The canon: how anything is known

Epicurus replaced logic with "canonic", after his lost Kanon (The Rule). The criteria of truth are sensations, preconceptions (prolepseis) and feelings (pathe); dialectic is superfluous (Diogenes Laertius 10.31; Cicero, On Ends 1.22).

Every sensation is true. It adds nothing, and nothing can refute it: not a sensation of the same kind, which has equal standing, nor one of another kind, which judges different objects, nor reason, which depends on the senses (10.31–32). Error lies in added opinion (Letter to Herodotus 50–51). In Sextus' report of the Epicurean view (Against the Mathematicians 7.208–209), a distant square tower looks round: the sensation truly reports an image worn round in transit; the belief that the tower is round is false. An opinion is true if evident experience attests it or, for things unseen, does not contest it (7.211–216). Void passes, since without it motion, which is evident, would be impossible.

A preconception is a general notion laid down by repeated perception; without it we could not even seek what we seek (10.33), Epicurus' answer to the puzzle of Plato's Meno (80d–e). Celestial events may have several explanations consistent with appearances, and to insist on one is to fall into myth (Letter to Pythocles 86–87). Here Epicurus parted from Democritus' doubts about the senses (module 12.1; DK 68 B 9), and Plutarch defends Democritus against his Epicurean heirs (Against Colotes 1108f–1111e). KD 23 states the principle: fight all your sensations and you have no standard for judging even those you call false.

Atoms, void and the swerve

Module 7.2 gives the outline; module 12.1 hands on the system Epicurus revised. The Letter to Herodotus argues that nothing comes from or perishes into what is not (38–39); that the whole is bodies, attested by sensation, and void, inferred because bodies must have somewhere to move (39–40); and that atoms, void and worlds are unlimited (41–45).

The revisions answer objections. To Aristotle's complaint that the atomists never explained motion (module 12.1), Epicurus gives atoms weight besides shape and size (Letter to Herodotus 54); Aetius (1.3.18) calls weight his addition to Democritus. Atomic shapes are inconceivably many but not unlimited (42). Each atom has minimal parts, the smallest magnitudes, which cannot exist apart (56–59); David Furley (Two Studies in the Greek Atomists, 1967) read these as a reply to Aristotle's argument that no magnitude is built from partless points (Physics 6.1).

The swerve is not in the surviving letters. Lucretius (2.216–293) gives it two jobs: without it atoms would fall like raindrops and never collide, and the mind would be bound by fate. Cicero mocks an uncaused motion invented to save free will (On Fate 22–23; On Ends 1.19); Diogenes of Oinoanda credits Epicurus by name with the free movement in atoms that Democritus missed (fr. 54 Smith). Epicurus' target shows in the Letter to Menoeceus: better to follow the myths about the gods than be enslaved to the fate of the natural philosophers (134). Book 25 of On Nature, like SV 40, argues that the determinist refutes himself, since in arguing he treats his opponent as responsible for his beliefs.

Where sources disagree. Cyril Bailey (The Greek Atomists and Epicurus, 1928) read Lucretius as placing a swerve of the mind's atoms in every act of will. Furley (1967) answered that swerves are rare and serve only to save the will from necessity, so that action flows from character, as in Aristotle. David Sedley ("Epicurus' refutation of determinism", 1983), reading On Nature 25, makes the self an emergent cause and the swerve the physical condition that leaves room for it. Module 7.2's other question, whether the swerve is Epicurus' own, turns on Cicero and Diogenes of Oinoanda, who say it is.

The soul and death

Modules 7.2 and 8.4 give KD 2 and the Letter to Menoeceus (124–127), and module 7.2 Lucretius' symmetry argument. Beneath them lies the physics. The soul is a body of fine particles spread through the frame, most like breath mixed with heat (Letter to Herodotus 63); Aetius (4.3.11) adds a nameless fourth component that produces sensation. The soul perceives only while housed in the body and scatters when the body breaks up (64–66); it cannot be incorporeal, since only void is, and void neither acts nor is acted on (67).

Since all good and bad lies in sensation, death, its privation, is nothing to us (Letter to Menoeceus 124–125), and to fear its prospect is to be pained in vain (125). Unlimited time holds no more pleasure than limited time, if reason measures pleasure's limits (KD 19–20), so a complete life needs no more years. Against death, says SV 31, we all live in a city without walls. Thomas Nagel ("Death", 1970) revived the objection that death harms by deprivation; James Warren (Facing Death: Epicurus and His Critics, 2004) reconstructs the Epicurean replies.

Pleasure and the kinds of desire

Pleasure is the beginning and end of the blessed life (Letter to Menoeceus 128), and the cradle argument adds that animals seek it by nature from birth (Diogenes Laertius 10.137; Torquatus in Cicero, On Ends 1.30). Pleasures are weighed against pains (129–130). The end is not the profligate's pleasures but freedom from pain in the body and from disturbance in the soul; bread and water please most when one is in want, and sober reasoning, not drinking parties, makes life pleasant (131–132). Practical wisdom (phronesis) is the greatest good, since one cannot live pleasantly without living wisely, honourably and justly (132; KD 5).

The removal of all pain is the limit of pleasure (KD 3); after it bodily pleasure is only varied (KD 18). The scholion on KD 29 sorts desires: natural and necessary (drink when thirsty), natural but not necessary (costly food), neither (crowns and statues). Nature's wealth is limited and easy to get, the wealth of empty opinion unlimited (KD 15); whoever is not hungry, thirsty or cold, and expects to stay so, could rival Zeus in happiness (SV 33).

Module 12.5 gives the Cyrenaic doctrine and module 7.2 the two kinds of pleasure; Diogenes Laertius records the break. The Cyrenaics admit only pleasure in motion; Epicurus admits both kinds, quoting his On Choices: freedom from disturbance (ataraxia) and from pain (aponia) are katastematic, static, pleasures, while joy and delight are kinetic, in activity (10.136). The Cyrenaics hold bodily pains worse; Epicurus holds mental pains worse, since the flesh suffers only the present, the soul past, present and future (10.137). The deathbed letter is the doctrine at work.

Where sources disagree. Torquatus holds that the removal of all pain is itself the highest pleasure (Cicero, On Ends 1.37–38); Cicero answers that nobody calls painlessness pleasure and that Epicurus slides between two senses of the word (2.9–10). Bailey and John Rist (Epicurus: An Introduction, 1972) take Diogenes Laertius 10.136 as Epicurus' doctrine, static pleasure being the enjoyment of a sound body and mind. J. C. B. Gosling and C. C. W. Taylor (The Greeks on Pleasure, 1982) doubted the division, and Boris Nikolsky ("Epicurus on Pleasure", 2001) traced it to Carneades' classification of ends, reaching Cicero through Antiochus: Epicurus spoke of one pleasure with two aspects.

Friendship, and justice as a compact

Of all that wisdom provides for a blessed life, friendship is by far the greatest (KD 27). It begins in benefit but is choiceworthy for itself (SV 23, where the manuscript reads arete, "virtue", and editors commonly print hairete, "choiceworthy"); we need less our friends' help than confidence in it (SV 34). Torquatus reports three Epicurean accounts, from friendship valued for use, to friendship loved for itself once formed, to a compact among the wise (Cicero, On Ends 1.65–70).

Justice is a pledge of advantage, not to harm one another or be harmed (KD 31), and there is none with creatures that cannot make such compacts (KD 32); it is nothing in itself, but a compact (syntheke) made in some place at some time (KD 33). Injustice is bad only through the fear of detection (KD 34), and no secret violator can be sure of escaping (KD 35). A law that ceases to be useful ceases to be just (KD 37–38). This is Glaucon's contract (Republic 358e–359b; module 6.5 tells the ring of Gyges) with his conclusion reversed: the just man is the least disturbed, the unjust the most (KD 17), so the wise keep their compacts. Security comes from a quiet life withdrawn from the many (KD 14), the wise man stays out of politics (10.119), and the maxim "live unnoticed" survives because Plutarch wrote an essay against it.

The gods and the fourfold remedy

Module 7.2 gives KD 1. The Letter to Menoeceus adds that god is an imperishable and blessed living being, as the common notion outlines; that the gods exist, since knowledge of them is evident, but are not as the many think; and that the impious man is the one who fastens the opinions of the many on the gods (123–124). Universal belief is evidence because it springs from a preconception nature has given every people (Velleius in Cicero, On the Nature of the Gods 1.43–45). The gods are grasped by the mind through fine images and have not body but a sort of body, not blood but a sort of blood (1.49). A scholion on KD 1 says that some are numerically distinct and others exist by likeness of form, from a continuous flow of similar images to one place (10.139). Philodemus' On Piety records Epicurus taking part in the city's festivals and sacrifices.

Lactantius (On the Anger of God 13.20–21) ascribes to Epicurus the dilemma that god either wishes to remove evils and cannot, or can and will not, or neither, or both; only the last befits a god, so whence evils? Reinhold Glei ("Et invidus et inbecillus: das angebliche Epikurfragment bei Laktanz, De ira dei 13,20–21", Vigiliae Christianae 42, 1988) traced the argument to an Academic source, not to Epicurus.

Where sources disagree. Module 7.2 sets out the realist and idealist readings. A. A. Long and David Sedley (The Hellenistic Philosophers, 1987) revived the idealist one, the gods as our own idealisation of the blessed life, which the scholion's likeness of form suits, and Sedley ("Epicurus' theological innatism", 2011) adds that our preconception of the gods is innate, not drawn from experience. David Konstan ("Epicurus on the gods", 2011) defends the realist one: the gods are atomic compounds, and the physics can explain both how we come to know them and why they never perish.

Module 7.2 gives the fourfold remedy in English. Philodemus states it (tetrapharmakos, a doctors' word for a four-ingredient salve) in a work whose title survives only as Pros tous [...] (PHerc. 1005, column 5): aphobon ho theos, anypopton ho thanatos, kai tagathon men eukteton, to de deinon euekkartereton; god presents no fears, death no worries; the good is easy to get, the terrible easy to endure. The four clauses follow KD 1–4 in order. Diogenes of Oinoanda calls the same fears, of gods, death and pain, with desires beyond nature's limits, the roots of all evils (fr. 34 Smith).

Diogenes of Oinoanda's wall

In the second century AD, under Hadrian on epigraphic grounds, Diogenes of Oinoanda (Latin Oenoanda), in the uplands of northern Lycia, paid for a stoa wall inscribed with Epicurean philosophy: about 25,000 words over some 260 square metres, on Martin Ferguson Smith's estimate. The first blocks were found in 1884; Smith's Diogenes of Oinoanda: The Epicurean Inscription (1993) numbers the fragments. The wall held treatises on physics, ethics and old age, letters, and maxims of Epicurus along its lower margin (fr. 29 carries KD 1).

Diogenes explains why a doctrine of withdrawal needed a monument. Near the sunset of his life he wished to set out the remedies of salvation for the well-disposed, now and to come (fr. 3), and for so-called foreigners, since the whole earth is one country for all and the world one home (fr. 30). He asks that statues of the gods be made genial and smiling, so that we smile back instead of fearing them (fr. 19), and foresees a time when the life of the gods will pass to men, everything full of justice and mutual love, with no need of walls or laws (fr. 56).

The hostile tradition

Hostility began inside the school. Timocrates, Metrodorus' brother, defected and alleged gluttony, and Diotimus the Stoic circulated fifty obscene letters in Epicurus' name (10.3, 10.6); Cicero's Cotta sneers that even the courtesan Leontion dared to write against Theophrastus (On the Nature of the Gods 1.93).

In book 1 of On Ends Lucius Manlius Torquatus expounds the ethics (1.29–72); in book 2 Cicero replies that Epicurus equivocates on pleasure, that infants seek pleasure in motion, not painlessness (2.31–32), and that Torquatus' ancestors acted for honour (2.60–61; Rome module 3.2). Yet he allows that the dying Epicurus was better than his theory (2.96–99). Plutarch's Against Colotes (Moralia 1107d–1127e) answers Colotes' book arguing that one cannot even live by the doctrines of the other philosophers, and turns the charge round. Dante puts Epicurus and his followers, who make the soul die with the body, among the heretics (Inferno 10.13–15). Rome module 5.6 reads Lucretius, and Rome module 13.3 takes Philodemus, Cassius, Atticus and the other Roman Epicureans.

Where sources disagree. Cicero (On Ends 2) takes Epicurus' pleasure at its word and finds either vulgarity or equivocation. Seneca, a Stoic who quotes Epicurus throughout the early Letters, calls his precepts upright and, seen close, austere, and says the school is maligned for its name (On the Happy Life 12–13). Plutarch argues that Epicureans could not live by their principles; Diogenes Laertius, our main source, defends the man against the slanders (10.9–11).

Self-test

  1. Why does Epicurus hold that every sensation is true, and where does error come in?
    Answer

    A sensation adds nothing, and nothing can refute it: not a sensation of the same kind (equal standing), nor one of another kind (different objects), nor reason (which depends on the senses) (Diogenes Laertius 10.31–32). Error lies in the opinion added, as when the image of a distant square tower, worn round in transit, is taken to show a round tower.

  2. Name three ways Epicurus revised Democritus' atomism and the objection each answers.
    Answer

    Weight, against Aristotle's complaint that the atomists never explained motion (Letter to Herodotus 54; Aetius 1.3.18); minimal parts, against Aristotle's argument that magnitudes cannot be built from partless points (Furley); and truthful sensation, against Democritus' own doubts about the senses (DK 68 B 9). The swerve, against determinism, is a fourth.

  3. What two jobs does the swerve do in Lucretius, and why is its attribution to Epicurus questioned?
    Answer

    It lets falling atoms collide, and it breaks the chain of fate so that the mind is free (Lucretius 2.216–293). It appears nowhere in Epicurus' surviving letters; the attribution rests on later witnesses, Cicero (On Fate 22–23) and Diogenes of Oinoanda (fr. 54 Smith).

  4. Why, on Epicurus' account, can the soul neither survive the body nor be incorporeal?
    Answer

    It is a body of fine particles spread through the frame, which perceives only while housed in the body and scatters when the body breaks up (Letter to Herodotus 63–66). The only incorporeal is void, which neither acts nor is acted on, so an incorporeal soul could neither feel nor act (67).

  5. Classify desires with Epicurus' examples, and say why the classification matters for happiness.
    Answer

    Natural and necessary (drink when thirsty), natural but not necessary (costly food), neither (crowns and statues), in the scholion on KD 29. Natural wealth is limited and easy to get, while empty desires have no limit (KD 15), so happiness depends on satisfying the first and dropping the last.

  6. On which two points does Diogenes Laertius say Epicurus differed from the Cyrenaics?
    Answer

    They admitted only kinetic pleasure, he static pleasure (ataraxia, aponia) as well (10.136). They held bodily pains worse; he held mental pains worse, since the soul suffers past, present and future, the flesh only the present (10.137).

  7. What is justice for Epicurus, and why should the wise keep the compact even when they could break it unseen?
    Answer

    A compact for mutual advantage, not to harm or be harmed (KD 31, 33). Injustice is bad only through fear of detection, and no one can be sure of escaping (KD 34–35), so the unjust live in disturbance and the just in calm (KD 17).

  8. State the fourfold remedy as Philodemus gives it and relate it to the Principal Doctrines.
    Answer

    In PHerc. 1005, column 5: god presents no fears, death no worries; the good is easy to get, the terrible easy to endure. The clauses summarise KD 1–4 in order: the gods, death, the limit of pleasure, the endurance of pain.

  9. What are the realist and the idealist readings of the Epicurean gods, and who holds each?
    Answer

    For realists such as Konstan (2011) the gods are compounds of fine atoms whose indestructibility the physics can explain. For idealists, Long and Sedley (1987) and Sedley (2011), they are our own idealisation of the blessed life, a reading the scholion on KD 1, with gods existing by likeness of form, suits.

  10. Why is Diogenes of Oinoanda's inscription a paradox for the Garden, and how did he justify it?
    Answer

    A school that taught "live unnoticed" was proclaimed on a public wall. Diogenes says that near the end of his life he wished to set out the remedies of salvation for his own and future generations (fr. 3) and for foreigners, since the earth is one country for all (fr. 30).

Carry forward

  1. Epicureanism was built for memory and community: epitomes, forty maxims led by the fourfold remedy, a founder's birthday and portrait. Lucretius' poem and the wall at Oinoanda are delivery systems for a fixed core, and later Epicurean texts should be read as transmitting it.
  2. Epicurus' physics serves his ethics: weight, minimal parts, the swerve and truthful senses answer named objections, and an understanding of atoms and void is meant to remove the fear of gods and death that the remedy's first two clauses name.
  3. Most of the evidence is hostile (Cicero, Plutarch, Lactantius), late (Diogenes Laertius, Oinoanda) or charred (Herculaneum). Keep the reporter apart from the claim, as with the problem of evil that Lactantius ascribes to Epicurus and the division of pleasures that may belong to Cicero's sources.
