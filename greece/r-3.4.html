Pelias and the one-sandalled man

The Argo is the oldest ship in Greek literature. Homer calls her "Argo, known to all" and has Circe say that only she ever passed the Planctae, "because Hera loved Jason" (Odyssey 12.69–72); the Iliad knows Jason's son Euneus ruling Lemnos (7.467–469); Hesiod has Jason take Medea from Aeetes after "the painful tasks the overbearing king Pelias laid on him" (Theogony 992–1002). The frame is archaic. The connected narratives are later: Pindar's Pythian 4 (462 BC), Apollonius of Rhodes' Argonautica (mid third century BC), the summary in Apollodorus, Library 1.9.16–28, and the unfinished Latin epic of Valerius Flaccus (c. AD 70–90).

Pelias, son of Poseidon and Tyro, held Iolcus in Thessaly, having displaced his half-brother Aeson, Jason's father. An oracle warned him of a man from the hills wearing one sandal. In Pindar the stranger arrives at the market with two spears, a leopard skin and uncut hair, reared by Chiron on Pelion after his parents smuggled him out as a baby (Pythian 4.71–120). Apollonius compresses: Jason lost a sandal fording the swollen Anaurus and arrived at Pelias' sacrifice to Poseidon (Argonautica 1.5–17). Apollodorus adds a trap: Pelias asked Jason what he would do with a man destined to kill him, and Jason, prompted by Hera, said he would send him for the Golden Fleece; Hera wanted Medea brought to Greece to destroy Pelias, who had neglected her honours (Library 1.9.16). Pindar's Pelias pleads piety: Phrixus' ghost demands his soul and the fleece be brought home (Pythian 4.156–167).

Where sources disagree. Jason's mother is Alcimede in Apollonius (1.47, 1.233) and Pherecydes, but Polymede in Apollodorus (1.9.16) and Polymele elsewhere. Whether Jason volunteers the quest (Apollodorus, Valerius Flaccus 1.22–90) or has it imposed under a veneer of piety (Pindar) changes the moral balance of the whole epic; Apollonius leaves the mechanism almost unstated.

The Fleece: Athamas, Ino, Phrixus and Helle

The fleece came from Boeotian Orchomenus. Athamas, son of Aeolus, married the cloud-nymph Nephele and had Phrixus and Helle; he then took Ino, daughter of Cadmus, who hated her stepchildren. Ino had the women parch the seed-corn; when the crop failed and Athamas sent to Delphi, she bribed the messengers to report that Phrixus must be sacrificed to Zeus. At the altar Nephele (or Zeus through Hermes) sent a golden-fleeced ram; the children mounted it and flew east. Helle fell into the strait that took her name; Phrixus reached Aia in Colchis, where Aeetes, son of Helios, married him to his daughter Chalciope and hung the fleece of the sacrificed ram on an oak in the grove of Ares under a sleepless serpent (Apollodorus 1.9.1; Apollonius 2.1140–1156, 4.123–182).

Herodotus gives the story a living cult: at Alus in Achaea Phthiotis the eldest Athamantid, if caught entering the town hall, was sacrificed to Zeus Laphystius (7.197).

Where sources disagree. Whether the ram spoke (Hesiodic fragments in the scholia) is contested; Apollonius (2.1144–1147) says only that Hermes made the fleece golden. Why the fleece matters is also unstable: in Pindar it is Phrixus' soul that must be fetched; in Apollonius Pelias simply wants Jason dead; rationalisers (Diodorus 4.47, after Dionysius Scytobrachion) made the "ram" a tutor called Crius. No early source explains the fleece's power; it is uncertain whether it ever had one.

The ship and the crew

Argus built the ship at Pagasae under Athena's direction, with a speaking beam from the oak at Dodona in her keel (Apollonius 1.18–19, 4.580–591). Apollonius opens with a catalogue of some fifty-five men (1.23–233), modelled on the Iliadic Catalogue of Ships, and the roster is where the myth does its political work. It assembles the generation before Troy: Peleus and Telamon, fathers of Achilles and Ajax; Oileus, father of the lesser Ajax; Laertes in Apollodorus' list; Menoetius, father of Patroclus; Admetus; Augeas of Elis. The Dioscuri bring Sparta aboard; Meleager and the Thestiads bring Aetolia; Idas and Lynceus come from Messenia, the seers Idmon and Mopsus, the flying Boreads Zetes and Calais, Euphemus of Taenarum who runs on water, Periclymenus the shape-changer, and Tiphys the helmsman.

The list matters for three reasons. First, it is a pan-Hellenic charter: every region claims a bench, and local traditions kept adding names, which is why Apollodorus and Hyginus differ from Apollonius. Second, particular names serve particular patrons. Pindar's shorter list (Pythian 4.171–183) singles out Euphemus because Arcesilas of Cyrene descended from him; the clod of Libyan earth Triton gives Euphemus is the charter of Cyrene's foundation (4.13–56; Apollonius 4.1731–1764). Third, the roster signals the heroism being tested: the strength of Heracles and the song of Orpheus both come aboard, and Heracles is set aside before Colchis.

Orpheus, first in Apollonius' catalogue, keeps the rowing time, calms a quarrel with a cosmogonic song (1.494–515) and sings down the Sirens (4.891–919). Heracles is elected leader by acclamation and defers to Jason (1.341–349). Peleus is the steady counsellor of Book 4; Telamon the hot-tempered accuser when Heracles is left behind.

Where sources disagree. Atalanta is an Argonaut in Apollodorus (1.9.16) and Diodorus (4.41), but Apollonius excludes her: Jason feared quarrels over love among the men (1.769–773). Heracles' departure is told four ways: left in Mysia searching for Hylas (Apollonius 1.1207–1362); left at Aphetae in Thessaly (Herodotus 7.193); never embarked, because Argo declared she could not bear his weight (Pherecydes, in Apollodorus 1.9.19); walked to Colchis after losing Hylas (Theocritus, Idyll 13.73–75). Valerius Flaccus diverts him to free Prometheus (5.154–176). His strength makes Jason redundant, so each poet finds a way to remove him.

The voyage out

Lemnos

The Lemnian women had murdered their husbands, who had taken Thracian concubines after Aphrodite cursed the women with a foul smell. Hypsipyle, who had spared her father Thoas, received the Argonauts; they lingered until Heracles shamed them back to sea (Apollonius 1.609–909). Euneus, Jason's son by Hypsipyle, is Homeric, making Lemnos the oldest attested episode. In Pindar the stop is on the way home (Pythian 4.251–254).

Cyzicus

King Cyzicus of the Doliones welcomed them; Heracles killed the six-armed Earthborn who attacked the ship. Driven back by a night storm, the Argonauts fought the Doliones in the dark without recognising them and Jason killed Cyzicus; his wife Cleite hanged herself (1.936–1077). The episode sets the epic's note of blundering guilt.

Hylas

In Mysia Heracles snapped an oar and went to cut another; his squire Hylas, sent for water, was pulled into the spring by its nymph. Heracles raged through the woods; at dawn the others sailed without him. Telamon accused Jason of plotting it, until the sea-god Glaucus announced that Heracles was fated for other labours (1.1153–1362). Valerius Flaccus gives the loss most of Books 3 and 4.

Amycus

Amycus, king of the Bebryces, forced strangers to box him; Polydeuces killed him with a blow behind the ear (2.1–97). Theocritus, Idyll 22, lets him live after swearing to stop.

Phineus and the Harpies

On the Thynian coast the blind seer Phineus, wasted by the Harpies who snatched his food, begged the Boreads for help. Zetes and Calais chased them to the Floating Islands, where Iris swore the Harpies would trouble him no more; the islands became the Strophades, "turning". Phineus then prophesied the route as far as Colchis, withholding the end (2.178–530).

Where sources disagree. Apollonius says Zeus blinded Phineus for revealing the divine will too fully (2.181–184). A Hesiodic fragment has him choose long life over sight. Apollodorus (1.9.21, 3.15.3) and Sophocles' lost Phineus plays make it punishment for blinding his own sons at the instigation of their stepmother Idaea. Whether the Harpies died or were spared also varied.

The Clashing Rocks

At the Bosporus the Clashing Rocks (Symplegades) crushed whatever passed. On Phineus' advice Euphemus released a dove; it lost only its tail feathers, and the crew rowed through on the backwash as Athena shoved the ship with one hand, losing only the stern ornament. The rocks then rooted for ever (2.549–610). Homer's Planctae (Odyssey 12.59–72) are a different hazard; Apollonius keeps both, placing the Planctae on the homeward route.

Beyond the Bosporus Apollonius adds the Mariandyni, where Idmon dies of a boar's tusk and Tiphys of illness (2.815–898); the Amazons; the island of Ares, where the shipwrecked sons of Phrixus join them (2.1093–1230); and the Caucasus, where they hear Prometheus scream under the eagle (2.1246–1259).

Colchis

Book 3 of Apollonius moves to Olympus: Hera and Athena ask Aphrodite to have Eros make Medea, Aeetes' younger daughter and priestess of Hecate, fall in love with Jason (3.6–166). Aeetes, warned by prophecy of his own children's treachery, sets a task he himself performs: yoke the bronze-footed, fire-breathing bulls of Hephaestus, plough the plain of Ares, sow the dragon's teeth of Cadmus, and kill the armed men who spring up (3.401–421). Medea's sleepless soliloquies (3.616–824) made her, not Jason, the centre of the poem.

At the temple of Hecate Medea gives Jason the Promethean drug, grown from the Titan's blood, that makes him invulnerable for a day (3.844–866, 3.1013–1062). He yokes the bulls, ploughs, and throws a boulder among the Earthborn so that they kill each other (3.1278–1407). Aeetes plots to burn the ship; Medea flees at night, sings the serpent to sleep with a juniper sprig dipped in her drug, and Jason lifts down the fleece (4.123–182). Valerius Flaccus follows this outline but gives Medea a far longer resistance to Venus and inserts, in Book 6, a Colchian civil war against Aeetes' brother Perses in which Jason fights for Aeetes before being cheated.

Apsyrtus

Aeetes sends his son Apsyrtus in pursuit. In Apollonius the Colchians catch the Argonauts at the Brygean islands near the Danube mouth; the Argonauts are ready to surrender Medea for arbitration, and she, furious, extracts a promise from Jason. She lures Apsyrtus to a parley in a temple of Artemis, where Jason cuts him down and performs a murderer's rites, licking and spitting the blood (4.410–481). The Colchians give up, and Zeus decrees purification by Circe.

Where sources disagree. Apollodorus (1.9.24) and Pherecydes make Apsyrtus a small child whom Medea dismembers aboard, scattering the pieces so that Aeetes stops to gather them; they were buried at Tomi, "cuttings". Euripides has Jason say Medea "killed her brother at the hearth" (Medea 1334), placing the murder in Colchis, as in Sophocles' lost Colchian Women. Apollonius moves the deed far from home and gives Jason the blow, sparing Medea the pollution and deepening Jason's.

Apollonius' Book 4 carries the moral weight. Circe purifies the couple but drives them from her house (4.659–752). Hera summons Thetis to steer them past the Planctae, reminding her that Medea will one day be Achilles' wife in Elysium (4.783–832), a detail already in Ibycus and Simonides. Orpheus outsings the Sirens (4.891–921). At Drepane (Corcyra) a second Colchian fleet demands Medea; Queen Arete rules that a married woman cannot be returned, so Jason and Medea consummate the marriage hastily in the cave of Macris on the fleece (4.1098–1169): a wedding by necessity. A storm flings the ship into the Libyan Syrtis; the crew carry Argo twelve days to Lake Tritonis, lose Canthus and Mopsus, and are guided out by Triton, who gives Euphemus the clod (4.1228–1764). On Crete Medea kills the bronze giant Talos with the evil eye (4.1638–1693). The poem ends abruptly at Pagasae.

Iolcus: the death of Pelias

Apollodorus supplies the homecoming Apollonius omits (1.9.27). Pelias, assuming the Argonauts dead, had forced Aeson to drink bull's blood and had murdered Jason's infant brother Promachus. Jason handed over the fleece and waited. Medea went to Pelias' daughters and demonstrated rejuvenation by cutting up an old ram, boiling it with herbs, and drawing out a lamb. Persuaded, the daughters cut their father in pieces and boiled him; Medea had no intention of restoring him. Euripides dramatised the scene in his lost first production, the Peliades (455 BC). Pelias' son Acastus held funeral games (shown on the Chest of Cypselus, Pausanias 5.17.9–11) and expelled Jason and Medea. Diodorus (4.52) exempts Alcestis, who alone refused.

Where sources disagree. The Nostoi (fr. 6) said Medea boiled Aeson and made him young again; Simonides and Pherecydes said she rejuvenated Jason; Ovid gives the Aeson version (Metamorphoses 7.159–293). The cauldron is double-edged: healing for kin, butchery for an enemy. Nothing in Hesiod or Pindar hints at revenge on Pelias; the plot may be a sixth-century development.

Corinth: Glauce and the children

Euripides' Medea (431 BC, third prize) overwrote all other versions. Jason and Medea have lived some years in Corinth with two sons when Jason marries the daughter of King Creon; she is unnamed in the play, Glauce in Apollodorus (1.9.28) and Pausanias, Creusa in Seneca and Hyginus. Creon orders Medea into exile. She secures a day's grace, obtains from the passing Aegeus of Athens an oath of asylum (Medea 663–758), feigns reconciliation, and sends her sons to the bride with a poisoned robe and diadem; the girl burns, and Creon dies clinging to her (1136–1230). Medea kills the boys inside the house, appears above the roof in the chariot of Helios with the bodies, denies Jason their burial, and announces a cult for them at the sanctuary of Hera Akraia (1378–1383) before leaving for Athens.

That cult is the clue to the older Corinth. Eumelus' Corinthiaca, as reported by Pausanias (2.3.10–11), made Aeetes the first king of Corinth; the Corinthians later summoned Medea from Iolcus as rightful queen, and Jason reigned through her. Medea hid each newborn in Hera's temple, believing the goddess would make them immortal; when they died she was exposed, Jason left, and Medea handed Corinth to Sisyphus. The children die by a failed deification, the Demeter-and-Demophon pattern, not by murder. The scholia to Medea 264 preserve two more versions: Creophylus said Medea poisoned Creon and fled, leaving the children at Hera's altar, where Creon's kin killed them and blamed her; Parmeniscus said the Corinthian women, resenting a foreign sorceress as queen, killed her fourteen children in the temple, and a plague followed until Delphi ordered an annual expiation in which seven boys and seven girls served a year in the sanctuary with shorn hair and black clothes. Pausanias (2.3.6–7) saw the children's tomb and a statue of Deima, Terror. The scholiast also repeats the gossip that the Corinthians paid Euripides five talents to shift the guilt to Medea.

Where sources disagree. Whether Euripides invented deliberate child-murder is the central dispute. The ancient hypothesis says he borrowed it from Neophron of Sicyon; most modern scholars (following Page's 1938 edition) treat Neophron's fragments as a later reworking, though a minority revive Neophron's priority. What is clear is that the Akraia rite predates the play, and that every pre-Euripidean strand has the children killed by others or by a botched rite. The dragon-drawn chariot is also later: Euripides mentions only a chariot.

After Corinth: Athens and the Medes

In Athens Medea married Aegeus and bore him Medus (Apollodorus 1.9.28; Hesiod, Theogony 1001, already names a Medeius, son of Jason and Medea, reared by Chiron). When Theseus arrived unrecognised from Troezen, Medea persuaded Aegeus to poison him at a feast; Aegeus recognised the sword and dashed the cup aside, and Medea was driven out (Plutarch, Theseus 12; Apollodorus, Epitome 1.5–6; Euripides' lost Aegeus). Two destinations follow. In one she returns to Colchis with Medus, finds Aeetes deposed by his brother Perses, and restores him (Apollodorus 1.9.28; Diodorus 4.56). In the other she passes into the Iranian interior, where Medus names Media. Herodotus (7.62) says the Medes "were once called Arians, but when the Colchian Medea came to them from Athens they changed their name". It is Greek folk-etymology projected onto a real people, but it shows that by the fifth century an Athenian audience watching Medea flee to Aegeus knew where she would end up.

Her last home is the Isles of the Blest as Achilles' consort (Apollonius 4.811–815); the grand-daughter of Helios never dies in any source. Jason hanged himself (Neophron) or was crushed asleep beneath the rotting stern of the Argo at the Isthmus (Staphylus). The ship became a constellation.

Where sources disagree. Medea's son is Medus by Aegeus in Apollodorus and Diodorus, but Medeius by Jason in Hesiod. Whether the Medes were named from Medea or Medus splits Herodotus from the mythographers. Modern scholars treat the Athenian episode as fifth-century Attic appropriation, giving Athens a share in a Panhellenic heroine and a foil for Theseus; Euripides' Aegeus scene is its earliest trace.

Self-test

  1. What sign marked the man who would kill Pelias, and how did Jason acquire it in Apollonius?
    Answer

    A single sandal. In Argonautica 1.5–17 Jason lost the other crossing the flooded river Anaurus on his way to Pelias' sacrifice to Poseidon.

  2. How does Pindar's Pelias justify sending Jason to Colchis?
    Answer

    He claims the ghost of Phrixus has demanded that his soul and the fleece be brought home, and that he is too old to go himself (Pythian 4.156–167).

  3. Who engineered the near-sacrifice of Phrixus, and what did Herodotus find at Alus?
    Answer

    Ino, Athamas' second wife, by parching the seed-corn and bribing the Delphic messengers. Herodotus 7.197 reports a cult of Zeus Laphystius at Alus in which the eldest Athamantid was barred from the town hall and liable to sacrifice.

  4. Give the four distinct accounts of how Heracles left the expedition.
    Answer

    Left in Mysia searching for Hylas (Apollonius); left at Aphetae in Thessaly (Herodotus 7.193); never embarked because Argo refused his weight (Pherecydes); walked to Colchis after Hylas (Theocritus 13). Valerius Flaccus adds the freeing of Prometheus.

  5. Why does Apollonius exclude Atalanta?
    Answer

    Jason feared quarrels over love among the crew (Argonautica 1.769–773). Apollodorus and Diodorus include her.

  6. What is the oldest attested episode of the voyage, and where?
    Answer

    The stay on Lemnos: Homer knows Euneus, son of Jason and Hypsipyle, as king of Lemnos (Iliad 7.467–469), and the Odyssey (12.69–72) knows the Argo passing the Planctae.

  7. What were Aeetes' tasks?
    Answer

    Yoke the bronze-footed fire-breathing bulls of Hephaestus, plough the plain of Ares, sow the dragon's teeth of Cadmus, and kill the armed Earthborn who sprang up (Argonautica 3.401–421).

  8. How does the killing of Apsyrtus differ between Apollonius and Apollodorus?
    Answer

    In Apollonius Apsyrtus is an adult pursuer ambushed by Jason in a temple of Artemis near the Danube mouth; in Apollodorus he is a child taken aboard, dismembered by Medea and scattered so Aeetes would stop to gather the pieces. Euripides places the murder in Colchis.

  9. In Eumelus' Corinthiaca, how do Medea's children die?
    Answer

    She hides each newborn in the temple of Hera Akraia expecting immortality; they die. There is no murder (Pausanias 2.3.10–11).

  10. Which two different fathers are given for Medea's son who gave his name to the Medes?
    Answer

    Jason (Medeius, Hesiod Theogony 1001) and Aegeus (Medus, Apollodorus 1.9.28; Diodorus 4.56). Herodotus 7.62 derives the Medes' name from Medea herself.

Carry forward

  1. The Argonaut roster is a political charter: it assembles the fathers of the Trojan War generation and gives every Greek region a bench, which is why cities kept adding names and why Pindar singles out Euphemus for Cyrene.
  2. Medea the deliberate child-killer is Euripides' creation of 431 BC, laid over a Corinthian cult of Hera Akraia whose older stories blamed the Corinthians or a failed rite; always ask which Medea a source means.
  3. Greek mythic geography is poetic, not cartographic: the five incompatible return routes show poets rerouting the Argo through whatever edges of the known world their own century was exploring.
