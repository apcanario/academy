Hesiod's monster-tree

Greek monsters are not a loose bestiary. Most belong to one kin-group laid out in Hesiod, Theogony 233–336, and the organising principle is descent from Sea and Earth, not from Sky. Pontus, on Gaia, fathers Nereus, Thaumas, Phorcys, Ceto and Eurybia. From Thaumas come the Harpies; from Phorcys and Ceto comes almost everything with scales, snakes or surplus heads. A second line, Typhon, born of Gaia and Tartarus (Theogony 821–822), is grafted onto the first when he mates with Echidna. A third group, which Hesiod does not treat as monstrous, is the Cyclopes and Giants born of Gaia by Uranus. Everything else here (Centaurs, Minotaur, Talos, Argus, Sirens) stands outside the tree and comes from heroic saga rather than cosmogony. Keeping those four categories apart is the most useful thing one can do with the material.

Phorcys, Ceto and the children of the deep

Ceto's name simply means "sea-monster" (kētos). Hesiod gives the pair four broods (Theogony 270–336).

The Graiae are grey-haired from birth but "fair-cheeked" (270–273); Hesiod names two and knows no shared eye. Apollodorus, Library 2.4.2, has three with one eye and one tooth passed between them, which Perseus steals to extort the way to the nymphs holding the cap of Hades, the winged sandals and the kibisis. Nobody kills them; they are robbed.

The Gorgons live "beyond glorious Ocean" near the Hesperides (274–279). Two are immortal; Medusa is mortal and lay with Poseidon "in a soft meadow". Perseus beheads her and Chrysaor and Pegasus leap from the neck (280–283). Apollodorus 2.4.2–3 supplies the reflecting shield and Athena guiding the hand; Pindar, Pythian 12.6–21, has Athena invent the aulos from the sisters' wailing. In Homer the Gorgon is a face, not a creature with a biography: on the aegis (Iliad 5.741), on Agamemnon's shield (11.36), dreaded in Hades (Odyssey 11.633–635).

Where sources disagree. The beautiful Medusa raped by Poseidon in Athena's temple and punished with snake-hair is Ovid, Metamorphoses 4.790–803, and appears nowhere earlier. Hesiod implies the sisters were monstrous from birth, yet Pindar still calls her "fair-cheeked", and Apollodorus 2.4.3 records a variant in which Athena had her killed for rivalling the goddess in beauty. Burkert (The Orientalizing Revolution) compares the Archaic Gorgoneion to Mesopotamian Humbaba masks; others treat it as an independent apotropaic face.

Echidna (295–305) is half fair-cheeked nymph, half huge speckled snake, eating raw flesh beneath the earth at Arima. The pronoun at line 295 leaves her mother open: Ceto or Callirhoe; West's commentary prefers Ceto. Apollodorus 2.1.2 makes her a daughter of Tartarus and Gaia, and records her death: Argus Panoptes killed her asleep.

Ladon is Ceto's last child, the serpent guarding the golden apples (333–336), unnamed in Hesiod. The name first appears in Apollonius, Argonautica 4.1396–1407, where the Argonauts find him dead the day before, Heracles' arrow in him.

Where sources disagree. Apollodorus 2.5.11 makes the serpent a hundred-headed child of Typhon and Echidna, and in his main version Heracles never kills it: Atlas fetches the apples. The killing is in Pherecydes, Apollonius and Attic vase painting.

Typhon and Echidna's brood

Typhon is Gaia's last child by Tartarus, born after the Titans fall (Theogony 820–880): a hundred snake heads uttering every voice from a god's to a bull's. Zeus burns him with the thunderbolt and hurls him into Tartarus. Pindar, Pythian 1.15–28, pins him under Etna; Apollodorus 1.6.3 has the fullest fight, with Typhon cutting Zeus's sinews with the sickle.

Where sources disagree. The Homeric Hymn to Apollo 305–355 has Hera bear Typhon alone in anger at Zeus and hand him to the Delphic she-dragon to rear. The battle has close Hittite (Illuyanka) and Ugaritic (Baal and Yam) parallels; how direct the borrowing was is argued, with West, The East Face of Helicon, the maximalist case.

Typhon mates with Echidna (306–308) and the children follow in order.

  • Orthrus, Geryon's hound (309), two-headed in Apollodorus 2.5.10; Heracles clubs him at Erytheia.
  • Cerberus, fifty-headed and brazen-voiced (310–312), fawning on those who enter Hades and devouring those who leave (769–773). Apollodorus 2.5.12 gives three heads and a dragon's tail; Homer knows only "the hound of hateful Hades" (Iliad 8.368). Heracles wrestles him up to Eurystheus and returns him: the one brood-mate the hero does not kill.
  • The Hydra of Lerna, reared by Hera, killed by Heracles with Iolaus (313–318). Apollodorus 2.5.2: nine heads, the middle one immortal, two growing for each cut; Iolaus cauterises the stumps; Eurystheus refuses to count the labour because of the help; Heracles dips his arrows in the gall.
  • The Chimaera, lion, goat and snake, fire-breathing, slain by Pegasus and Bellerophon (319–325). Homer, Iliad 6.179–183, has Bellerophon kill her in Lycia at Iobates' command.
  • The Sphinx, Hesiod's Phix, "ruin of the Cadmeans" (326), fathered by Orthrus on an ambiguous "she". Apollodorus 3.5.8 makes her a child of Echidna and Typhon sent by Hera, who learnt her riddle from the Muses and throws herself from the acropolis when Oedipus answers "man". The one monster beaten by wit rather than a weapon.
  • The Nemean Lion, also reared by Hera, killed by Heracles (327–332). Apollodorus 2.5.1: hide proof against iron; Heracles blocks one mouth of its cave and strangles it.
  • The Crommyonian Sow, Phaea, is not in Hesiod. Apollodorus, Epitome 1.1, calls her offspring of Echidna and Typhon; Theseus kills her on the road from Troezen. Plutarch, Theseus 9, adds the rationalisation that Phaea was a murderous woman nicknamed Sow.
Where sources disagree. Hesiod's "she" at 326 may be Echidna or the Chimaera (West argues for the Chimaera), so the Sphinx and Lion are either Echidna's children by her own son or Orthrus's by his sister. Pausanias 2.37.4 notes that the earliest poets gave the Hydra a single head and Peisander first multiplied them. Epimenides and Hyginus, Astronomica 2.24, say the Lion fell from the Moon.

Medusa's children

Pegasus is named from the springs (pēgai) of Ocean where he was born; he flies to the immortals and carries Zeus's thunder (Theogony 280–286). Pindar, Olympian 13.63–92, has Bellerophon tame him with the golden bridle Athena brings in a dream, and Isthmian 7.44–47 has the rider thrown when he tries to reach heaven. Hippocrene on Helicon is struck from the rock by his hoof. Pegasus is the one child of a monster who becomes an instrument of the gods and is never hunted.

Chrysaor, "Golden Sword", exists only to father Geryon on Callirhoe (287–288). Geryon is three-headed in Hesiod, three-bodied in Apollodorus 2.5.10, winged in Stesichorus. Heracles shoots him at Erytheia "beside his shambling cattle" (289–294). Stesichorus's Geryoneis is the one text that pities a monster: Geryon dies with his head drooping "like a poppy".

Cyclopes and Giants

Hesiod's Cyclopes are three craftsmen, Brontes, Steropes and Arges, children of Gaia and Uranus with one round eye (Theogony 139–146). Later they forge Zeus's thunderbolt, Hades' cap and Poseidon's trident (Apollodorus 1.2.1), build the walls of Mycenae and Tiryns (Pausanias 2.16.5) and die at Apollo's hands in revenge for Asclepius (Euripides, Alcestis 5–7).

Homer's Cyclopes are a lawless pastoral people without assemblies or ships (Odyssey 9.105–115). Polyphemus is son of Poseidon and Thoosa, daughter of Phorcys (Odyssey 1.71–73), the single thread tying Homer's ogre to Hesiod's tree. Homer never says outright that he has one eye; the reader infers it from the blinding (9.382–394). Odysseus blinds him and does not kill him, and the curse drives the rest of the poem.

Where sources disagree. Ancient scholars already separated three kinds of Cyclopes: wall-builders, Sicilian herdsmen, divine smiths (Hellanicus in the Hesiod scholia). Mondi (1983) argues the blinded-ogre story is an international folktale secondarily attached to the name; Hesiod's and Homer's Cyclopes share a name and an eye and nothing else.

The Giants are born when the blood of castrated Uranus falls on Gaia, "in gleaming armour, holding long spears" (Theogony 183–186). Hesiod narrates no battle; Homer makes them a wild tribe under Eurymedon (Odyssey 7.58–60). The Gigantomachy proper is Apollodorus 1.6.1–2: no god can kill them without a mortal, so Heracles is enlisted; he drags Alcyoneus out of Pallene, Zeus and Heracles take Porphyrion, Athena throws Sicily onto Enceladus, Poseidon breaks off Nisyros onto Polybotes. Pindar, Nemean 1.67–69, treats this as Heracles' purpose.

Where sources disagree. In Hesiod and Homer the Giants are armoured warriors or an oversized tribe; the snake-legged form is Hellenistic (the Pergamon altar). Roman poets fuse Giants, Titans and Typhon into one revolt (Horace, Odes 3.4; Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.151–162); Greek sources keep three separate wars.

Centaurs: the sin of Ixion

Pindar, Pythian 2.21–48, is the earliest full account. Ixion, the first man to murder kin, was purified by Zeus and received on Olympus, then lusted after Hera. Zeus shaped a cloud in her likeness; Ixion lay with it and was bound to the wheel. The cloud bore Centaurus, "honoured neither among men nor in the laws of the gods", who mated with Magnesian mares on Pelion and sired a race "like the mother below, the father above". Apollodorus, Epitome 1.20, and Diodorus 4.69–70 repeat this. Chiron (son of Cronus and Philyra, Apollodorus 1.2.4) and Pholus (son of Silenus, 2.5.4) stand outside the line. Homer never describes a half-horse: Nestor fought "the mountain-dwelling beasts" (Iliad 1.268) and Eurytion's drunkenness began "the feud between Centaurs and men" (Odyssey 21.295–304). The horse-body is in art from the tenth century (the Lefkandi centaur).

Heracles is their principal killer: at Pholus's cave the smell of wine brings them down and he shoots many, wounding Chiron incurably by accident (Apollodorus 2.5.4); Nessus dies by his arrow and takes revenge through the poisoned blood (Sophocles, Trachiniae 555–581). The Lapiths under Pirithous and Theseus kill the rest at the wedding (Ovid, Metamorphoses 12.210–535).

Where sources disagree. Diodorus 4.69.5 records a version without the intermediary Centaurus, the Centaurs reared by nymphs on Pelion; Nonnus, Dionysiaca 14.193–202, derives a Cypriot race from Zeus's spilt seed. Whether Chiron dies (Apollodorus 2.5.4) or is set among the stars (Eratosthenes, Catasterisms 40) is also split.

The sea-road: Harpies, Sirens, Scylla and Charybdis, Cetus

The Harpies, Thaumas's daughters by Electra, sisters of Iris, are "fair-haired" racers of the winds (Theogony 265–269); nothing monstrous is said. In Homer the Harpy Podarge bears Achilles' horses (Iliad 16.149–151). Apollonius, Argonautica 2.178–300, has them foul Phineus's food until Zetes and Calais chase them to the Strophades. Nobody kills them.

The Sirens in Homer are two, sitting in a meadow heaped with bones, form never described (Odyssey 12.39–54, 158–200). Apollodorus, Epitome 7.18–19, makes them daughters of Achelous and Melpomene, fated to die when a ship passed unharmed; Lycophron, Alexandra 712–737, has them drown themselves. Apollonius 4.891–921 gives them bird bodies and has Orpheus drown their song.

Where sources disagree. Two, three or four Sirens; daughters of Achelous (Apollodorus), of Earth (Euripides, Helen 167–169) or of Phorcys (Sophocles fr. 861 Radt), which would pull them into Hesiod's tree.

Scylla has twelve feet, six necks, three rows of teeth in each head and a voice like a puppy; her mother is Crataeis (Odyssey 12.85–100, 124–125). Apollonius 4.828–829 makes Crataeis a name of Hecate and gives Phorcys as father. The nymph transformed by Circe is Ovid (Metamorphoses 13.730–14.74). Odysseus loses six men and sails on; Homer calls her immortal (12.118), but Lycophron 44–49 has Heracles kill her for stealing Geryon's cattle and Phorcys revive her with fire. Charybdis, who sucks down and spews the sea three times a day (12.101–110), has no form, parentage or killer in any Archaic source.

Cetus is the common noun for a sea-beast, and two heroes kill one. Perseus: Cassiepea boasts against the Nereids, Poseidon sends the beast, Ammon's oracle demands Andromeda, Perseus kills it and marries her (Apollodorus 2.4.3; Ovid, Metamorphoses 4.663–764). Heracles: Laomedon cheats Poseidon and Apollo, a beast ravages Troy and Hesione is exposed; Heracles kills it for the price of the divine mares and is cheated in turn (Apollodorus 2.5.9; the wall built to shelter him is at Iliad 20.144–148).

Where sources disagree. Apollodorus and Ovid have Perseus kill the beast with a blade; petrifaction with Medusa's head is later (Lucian, Dialogues of the Sea-Gods 14). Lycophron, Alexandra 33–37, has Heracles swallowed by the Trojan beast and cutting his way out, which Apollodorus omits.

Crete and Argos: the Minotaur, Talos, Argus Panoptes

The Minotaur, properly Asterios, is the child of Pasiphae and the bull Poseidon sent when Minos broke a vow; Daedalus builds the wooden cow and the labyrinth (Apollodorus 3.1.3–4). After Androgeus's death Minos imposes the tribute of seven youths and seven maidens; Theseus goes with the third tribute, Ariadne gives him the thread, and he kills the Minotaur "striking him with his fists" (Epitome 1.7–9). Homer knows Daedalus's dancing floor (Iliad 18.590–592) and Ariadne's death on Dia (Odyssey 11.321–325) but never the bull-man.

Where sources disagree. Fists in Apollodorus, sword or club on Attic vases from the seventh century. Plutarch, Theseus 16–19, reports Philochorus's rationalisation: the Minotaur was Taurus, Minos's general, beaten in funeral games.

Talos, the bronze guardian of Crete, is in Apollodorus 1.9.26 either of the bronze race or Hephaestus's gift to Minos, with a single vein from neck to ankle stopped by a bronze nail; he dies when Medea drives him mad, or when Poeas shoots the ankle. Apollonius 4.1638–1688 makes him the last of the bronze men; Medea's evil eye makes him graze his ankle on a rock and the ichor runs out "like molten lead". The one monster killed by a witch, not a hero.

Argus Panoptes, the all-seeing herdsman, killed the bull of Arcadia, a satyr, and Echidna before Hera set him to guard Io (Apollodorus 2.1.2–3). Hermes kills him with a stone, which the ancients read into the epithet Argeiphontes. Aeschylus calls him "the earthborn herdsman" (Prometheus Bound 567–573); Ovid lulls him with the tale of Syrinx, beheads him and sets the eyes in the peacock's tail (Metamorphoses 1.622–723).

Where sources disagree. Apollodorus lists four fathers: Agenor, Arestor, Inachus or Earth. The Hesiodic Aegimius (fr. 294 MW) gives him four eyes; Bacchylides 19.29–36 leaves open whether Hermes killed him or he simply slept.

What the monsters meant

Boundaries. Hesiod's monsters live where the map runs out: the Gorgons beyond Ocean, Geryon in the far west, Ladon with the Hesperides, Echidna underground, Cerberus at the gate of Hades, Scylla in a strait, the sea-beasts on the shores of Ethiopia and Troy. The hero who kills one extends the inhabited world. Vernant read the Gorgon's face as the look of death itself; Clay (Hesiod's Cosmos) argues the catalogue is placed so that Zeus's victory over Typhon and the heroes' clearing of Typhon's children form one continuous labour, with Heracles as Zeus's agent on earth. Hesiod says as much when Heracles reaches Olympus "having accomplished his great work" (Theogony 954).

Chaos and the wild. The monsters descend from Sea and Earth, not Sky, and are older than the Olympian order; they are what that order has to digest. Hybrid form is category violation in Mary Douglas's sense, and the Centaurs make the point socially: wine, lust and mountains against the wedding feast, with Chiron as the reminder that the wild holds its own wisdom (Kirk, Myth: Its Meaning and Functions).

The East. Typhon lies "among the Arimi" (Iliad 2.783), placed by Strabo 13.4.6 in Cilicia or Syria; the Chimaera is Lycian; Andromeda's beast is at Joppa, where Pliny 5.69 says the chains were still shown; the Sphinx is an Egyptian and Levantine image type, and Gorgoneion, Chimaera and Sphinx all enter Greek art in the seventh-century Orientalising wave. How much of the mythology, as opposed to the imagery, came east is contested: West and Burkert argue for direct transmission; sceptics note that the stories as told are Greek in every structural detail.

Why so many are female. The claim needs qualifying. Ceto, Echidna, the Graiae, Gorgons, Harpies, Sirens, Scylla, Charybdis, Sphinx, Chimaera and Hydra are female; Typhon, Ladon, Orthrus, Cerberus, Geryon, the Lion, Minotaur, Talos, Argus, Cyclopes, Giants and Centaurs are male. The pattern is function, not number. The female monsters are the mothers and the mouths: they breed, or they kill through gaze, voice and swallowing (Medusa's eye, the Sirens' song, the Sphinx's riddle, Scylla's six mouths, Charybdis's gulp). The male monsters are brute obstacles killed by force. Hesiod frames woman herself as a "beautiful evil" (Theogony 585–602), and Echidna, nymph above and snake below, literalises that image; Zeitlin (Playing the Other) reads the female monster as the male imagination's picture of what cannot be controlled from inside the household. A sober check: hydra, chimaira, sphinx, skylla and harpyia are grammatically feminine nouns, and gender followed grammar. Both things are true; hold them together.

Self-test

  1. Which two deities head the monster line in Hesiod, and from which primordial pair do they descend?
    Answer

    Phorcys and Ceto, children of Pontus and Gaia, Theogony 233–239.

  2. How many Graiae does Hesiod name, and what does Apollodorus add?
    Answer

    Two, Pemphredo and Enyo. Apollodorus 2.4.2 adds Deino and the shared eye and tooth.

  3. What is uncertain about Echidna's parentage in Hesiod, and who kills her in Apollodorus?
    Answer

    The pronoun at Theogony 295 could make her mother Ceto or Callirhoe. Argus Panoptes kills her asleep (Apollodorus 2.1.2).

  4. Which of Typhon and Echidna's children is not killed by Heracles, and what happens instead?
    Answer

    Cerberus; Heracles wrestles him up to Eurystheus and returns him (Apollodorus 2.5.12).

  5. Why does Eurystheus refuse to count the Hydra as a labour?
    Answer

    Because Iolaus helped by cauterising the heads (Apollodorus 2.5.2).

  6. What single genealogical thread links Homer's Polyphemus to Hesiod's tree?
    Answer

    His mother Thoosa is a daughter of Phorcys (Odyssey 1.71–73).

  7. Who is Centaurus, and why are Chiron and Pholus exceptions?
    Answer

    Child of Ixion and the cloud Nephele, who mated with Magnesian mares (Pindar, Pythian 2). Chiron is son of Cronus and Philyra; Pholus of Silenus and a nymph.

  8. Which two monsters die without any hero killing them?
    Answer

    The Sirens, who drown themselves after a ship passes (Apollodorus, Epitome 7.19), and the Sphinx, who leaps from the acropolis after Oedipus answers (Apollodorus 3.5.8).

  9. Name the two heroes who kill a sea-beast and the woman each rescues.
    Answer

    Perseus rescues Andromeda (Apollodorus 2.4.3); Heracles rescues Hesione at Troy (Apollodorus 2.5.9).

  10. Give one structural and one grammatical explanation for the prominence of female monsters.
    Answer

    Structural: female monsters breed and kill through gaze, song, riddle and mouth, fitting Hesiod's "beautiful evil". Grammatical: hydra, chimaira, sphinx, skylla are feminine nouns.

Carry forward

  1. Almost every classic monster descends from Sea and Earth through Phorcys, Ceto, Typhon and Echidna, and the Olympian order is defined by what it kills, cages or pushes to the edges of the map. Read each hero's monster-slaying as a continuation of Zeus's victory over Typhon.
  2. Hesiod, Homer and Apollodorus are not one voice. Hesiod gives genealogy without stories, Homer stories without genealogy, Apollodorus harmonises both a thousand years later and often adds what we now assume (three Graiae, three-headed Cerberus). Always ask which layer a detail comes from.
  3. Monsters encode categories under strain: boundary, wilderness, the foreign, the female. The female monster kills through eye, voice and mouth, the male through force; keep that distinction for the Gorgon, Sphinx and Siren episodes in the tragedy and Odyssey modules.
