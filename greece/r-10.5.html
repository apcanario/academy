01Two mothers, two eggs

Homer knows Helen only as "daughter of Zeus" (Iliad 3.199, 3.418) and sister of Castor and Polydeuces, "whom one mother bore with me" (Iliad 3.238). He never mentions a swan or an egg, and in the underworld Leda's sons are Tyndareus' children (Odyssey 11.298–304). The swan is later. Euripides gives it in full while letting Helen doubt it: Leda bore her to Zeus in swan's form, "if the story is true" (Helen 17–21), and she calls her own egg-birth a teras, a monstrosity (Helen 257–9).

The rival genealogy is older and stranger. The Cypria (fr. 9 Bernabé, in Athenaeus 8.334b–d) made Helen the daughter of Zeus and Nemesis, who fled him through sea and land as fish and beast and conceived against her will; the egg she laid was found by a shepherd and given to Leda, who hatched it and reared the child as her own (Apollodorus, Library 3.10.7, reporting both versions). At Rhamnous the base of Agoracritus' Nemesis showed Leda leading Helen to her true mother (Pausanias 1.33.7–8); at Sparta an egg bound with ribbons hung in the sanctuary of the Leucippides, "said to be the one Leda laid" (Pausanias 3.16.1). The tidy scheme of two eggs, one divine (Helen and Polydeuces) and one mortal (Clytemnestra and Castor), is late systematising (Hyginus, Fabulae 77); Pindar divides the twins by father without needing an egg (Nemean 10.79–82). The theology matters more than the biology: a child of Retribution is a different person from a child of Leda, and the Iliad seems to hear the pun when the Trojan elders say it is ou nemesis, "no cause for blame", that two peoples suffer for such a woman (3.156–7).

Where sources disagree. A scholion on Pindar, Nemean 10.150a, claims Hesiod made Helen the daughter of Zeus and an Oceanid, with no Leda at all; the fragment is lost and the attribution uncertain. Whether the Cypria's Nemesis is older than the swan or a learned alternative to it cannot be decided.

02Theseus, the suitors and the oath

Helen is abducted twice, the first time as a child of seven (Hellanicus) or ten (Diodorus 4.63), seized by Theseus and Peirithous while dancing at the sanctuary of Artemis Orthia (Plutarch, Theseus 31; Apollodorus, Epitome 1.23). Theseus left her at Aphidnae with his mother Aethra and went to Hades; the Dioscuri sacked Aphidnae, recovered their sister and enslaved Aethra, which is why "Aethra, daughter of Pittheus" can stand among Helen's handmaids at Troy without explanation (Iliad 3.144). Alcman already sang the Dioscuri's raid on Attica (fr. 21 Page), and Herodotus reports that Sparta spared Decelea throughout the Peloponnesian War because the Deceleans had shown the Tyndaridae where Helen was hidden (9.73).

Where sources disagree. Stesichorus and Euphorion made Helen bear Iphigenia to Theseus and hand her to Clytemnestra (Pausanias 2.22.6–7), so that Aulis sacrifices the daughter for the mother. It never displaced the Homeric line in which Iphianassa is Agamemnon's own child (Iliad 9.145).

The wooing survives best in the Hesiodic Catalogue of Women (frr. 196–204 M–W). Suitors send gifts from all Greece; Odysseus sends none, knowing Menelaus will win (fr. 198); Achilles is a boy with Chiron, and had he come "neither Menelaus nor any other" would have had her (fr. 204.87–92). Agamemnon woos for his brother (fr. 197), and Tyndareus chooses Menelaus "because he gave the most" (fr. 204.85–7). First he binds every suitor to defend the chosen husband if Helen is taken (fr. 204.78–85; Euripides, Iphigenia at Aulis 57–65). Apollodorus credits the idea to Odysseus, who traded it for help in winning Penelope (3.10.9); Pausanias places the ceremony at the Tomb of the Horse near Sparta, the suitors standing on the pieces of a sacrificed horse (3.20.9).

Where sources disagree. Thucydides 1.9 rejects the oath: Agamemnon raised the fleet "not so much through the oaths of Tyndareus as through superiority in power". Poetry needs the oath to explain why Odysseus and Achilles sailed; history needs hegemony.

03Helen in Homer

Iliad 3: the weaver on the wall

Iris finds Helen weaving into a purple cloak "the many struggles of the Trojans and Achaeans, which they were suffering for her sake" (3.125–8): she is composing the war as art while it is still being fought. On the wall Priam calls her "dear child" (3.162); she answers that she wishes "evil death had pleased me" when she followed his son, and calls herself kunōpis, dog-faced (3.173–80). She identifies Agamemnon, Odysseus and Ajax, then looks in vain for her brothers and supposes they are ashamed of her; the poet corrects her in his own voice, "but the life-giving earth already held them" (3.243). Her sight is sharp, her knowledge partial. After the duel Aphrodite summons her to Paris' bed, and Helen does what no other mortal in the poem does: she tells the goddess to go and sit beside him herself (3.399–412). The threat that follows silences her; she goes, but only to tell Paris she wishes he had died (3.428–36). Agency and compulsion share one scene.

Iliad 6 and 24: the dog who will be a song

To Hector she is harsher than anyone else is to her, "brother-in-law of me, a dog, an evil-contriving abomination", wishing a storm had swept her into the sea at birth (6.344–8). Then: Zeus set an evil doom on her and Paris "so that in time to come we may be a song for men" (6.357–8). She is the only character in the Iliad who knows she is in the Iliad. At the funeral she laments last, after Andromache and Hecuba, and for herself as much as for Hector: twenty years since she left home, he alone never spoke harshly to her, and now "all shudder at me" (24.762–75). Over all this hangs the poem's phrase for the war's cause, "Helen's struggles and groans" (2.356, 2.590), which cannot tell us whether the groans are hers or caused by her.

Odyssey 4: the drug and the horse

At Sparta Helen enters "like Artemis of the golden distaff" (4.122), recognises Telemachus before her husband does, and when grief threatens drops into the wine a drug "banishing pain and anger, bringing forgetfulness of every ill", from Polydamna of Egypt (4.219–32). She tells her story: she alone recognised the disguised Odysseus in Troy, bathed him, swore silence, and rejoiced, because her heart had turned homeward and she mourned the madness Aphrodite gave her (4.240–64). Menelaus replies, "Yes, wife, all that you have said is correct", and tells another: inside the Horse she walked round calling each hero in his wife's voice, "and a god must have bidden you, who wished to give glory to the Trojans"; Odysseus had to clamp his hand over Anticlus' mouth (4.266–89). The panels are paired and unadjudicated, and the drug is precisely what lets the room sit through the second story. That Hermione is their only child, "for the gods gave Helen no more" (4.12–14), has been stated flatly before either speaks.

04The phantom in Egypt

Stesichorus of Himera (sixth century) wrote a Palinode, a song of recantation, whose opening Plato preserves:

That story is not true; you did not go in the well-benched ships, nor reach the towers of Troy.

(fr. 192 PMG, at Plato, Phaedrus 243a.) Plato frames it with a blinding cured by recantation, repeated by Isocrates (Helen 64), and tells us what replaced her: men at Troy fought over "the eidolon of Helen, in ignorance of the truth" (Republic 586c). A commentary attributed to Chamaeleon reports two Palinodes, one rebuking Homer and one Hesiod (P.Oxy. 2506 fr. 26). Stesichorus had treated her guilt earlier: the Greeks were about to stone her after the fall and dropped the stones at her face (fr. 201 PMG).

Where sources disagree. A scholion on Lycophron 822 says Hesiod first put the phantom at Troy (Hesiod fr. 358 M–W), which would make Stesichorus populariser not inventor. Most scholars doubt the attribution, since nothing in the Catalogue supports it; the second Palinode is also contested.

Herodotus keeps Egypt and drops the phantom. Memphite priests told him Paris was blown to Egypt, where king Proteus confiscated Helen and the treasure and expelled him; the Greeks besieged Troy for ten years refusing to believe the Trojans did not have her; Menelaus recovered her after the war and repaid his host by sacrificing two Egyptian children for a wind (2.112–119). Homer, he argues, knew this and suppressed it as unepic, witness the Sidonian robes Paris collected en route (Iliad 6.289–92) and Helen's Egyptian drug (Odyssey 4.227–30). His verdict: the Trojans would certainly have given her back, and the god arranged their destruction to show that "great wrongs bring great punishments" (2.120). Helen is innocent and absent; the war is theodicy.

Euripides' Helen (412 BC) stages Stesichorus with Herodotus' map. Hera, cheated by Paris' judgement, fashions a Helen out of sky for him while Hermes carries the real one to Proteus (31–48). Proteus is dead; his son Theoclymenus wants to marry her; Teucer reports that Greece hates her; Menelaus arrives shipwrecked and in rags, and the phantom dissolves into air announcing that Tyndareus' daughter is blameless (605–15). The sophistic game runs in Gorgias' vocabulary: onoma against sōma, name against body (42–3, 588); seeming against being; a chorus asking "what is god, what is not god, what lies between?" (1137–8). Faced with two Helens, Menelaus trusts the memory of his labour over the fake more than his eyes. Aristophanes parodied it the next year (Thesmophoriazusae 850–928). Three years earlier Euripides had given the other Helen her trial in Trojan Women (914–1032), where she argues like a sophist and Hecuba answers that "your mind became Aphrodite" (988–9).

Where sources disagree. Verrall read Helen as comedy and the twentieth century called it tragicomedy or escape play; Allan and Wright argue that a play about a war fought for nothing, staged the year after the Sicilian disaster, is serious under its wit. At issue is whether Euripides endorsed the sophistic epistemology he dramatised or exposed it.

05Guilt as a set piece: Gorgias and Sappho

Gorgias' Encomium of Helen (DK 82 B11), the oldest surviving defence of a mythological defendant, is a disjunction. Helen went for one of four reasons: the will of the gods and necessity; force; persuasion by speech; or love. Gods or force excuse the weaker party. Speech makes her a victim, because "speech is a great dynast, which with the smallest and least visible body accomplishes the most divine works" (B11.8) and acts on the soul as drugs on the body (B11.14). Love is either a god she could not resist or a human sickness to be pitied. The last sentence gives the game away: he wrote "an encomium of Helen and a plaything (paignion) for myself" (B11.21). Isocrates' Helen (c. 370) opens by objecting that Gorgias wrote an apologia, and that to defend is to concede the charge (10.14). Helen became the schools' standard exercise in making the weaker argument strong; the Encomium is also a claim that logos is a fourth cause of the war, which no epic poet had named.

Sappho fr. 16 (Voigt), a century and a half earlier, speaks from inside Helen's desire rather than about her culpability. Some say cavalry, some infantry, some ships are the finest thing on the black earth; Sappho says it is whatever one loves, and proves it by Helen, "who far surpassed all mortals in beauty, left her most noble husband and sailed to Troy, with no thought at all for child or dear parents", led astray by something the lacuna at 12–13 has swallowed. The example then dissolves into absent Anactoria, whose walk Sappho would rather see than all the chariots of Lydia. Helen is a subject who chooses, and her choice is the paradigm of the one thing worth having. No apology is offered because none is felt to be needed.

06The sword that drops, and the goddess at Therapne

The reunion at the fall has a fixed archaic shape: Menelaus goes for Helen with drawn sword and drops it at the sight of her. Ibycus (fr. 296 PMG) and the Little Iliad are cited for the bared breasts; Aristophanes jokes about it as common knowledge (Lysistrata 155–6), and Peleus throws it at Menelaus: "when you saw her breast you threw away your sword and took her kiss" (Euripides, Andromache 627–31). Euripides tests the other ending twice. In Trojan Women Menelaus swears to kill her in Sparta and Hecuba warns him not to sail in the same ship, since "a lover never stops loving" (1049–59); in Orestes, Orestes and Pylades attack her in Argos and she vanishes from under the blade to be made a goddess by Apollo, a beacon for sailors beside her brothers (1629–42).

Goddess is what Sparta meant by Helen. At Therapne, on the bluff above the Eurotas, a nurse carried an ugly infant daily to "the shrine of Helen" until a woman appeared, stroked the child's head and promised she would be the most beautiful woman in Sparta; she became the mother of king Demaratus (Herodotus 6.61). Isocrates says the Spartans sacrifice there to Helen and Menelaus "not as heroes but as gods" (Helen 63); Pausanias was shown their tomb (3.19.9). Rhodes is darker: driven from Sparta after Menelaus' death by his bastard sons, Helen fled to Polyxo, widow of Tlepolemus, who sent maids dressed as Furies to hang her from a tree; the Rhodians worship her as Helen Dendritis, "of the Tree" (Pausanias 3.19.9–10). Hanging is a recurrent death for goddesses of young women (Artemis Apanchomene, Pausanias 8.23.6–7), and the tree returns in Theocritus' Spartan wedding song, where girls who ran oiled "like men" beside the Eurotas with Helen promise to garland a plane tree inscribed "Revere me: I am Helen's tree" (Idyll 18.22–48).

Alcman's Partheneion (fr. 1 Page), sung at dawn by a Spartan girls' chorus under Hagesichora and Agido, does not name Helen. Claude Calame (1977) argued that such choruses were the institutional form of girls' initiation, that Helen with the Leucippides and Artemis Orthia was among their patrons, and that Therapne was the terminus of the passage from parthenos to bride; Theocritus and the ugly child of Herodotus fit that reading exactly. Alcman does name Therapne elsewhere, with the Dioscuri (fr. 7 Page).

Where sources disagree. Calame's initiatory reading remains dominant, but the goddess of the Partheneion (Orthria or Aotis) is disputed, and Hutchinson and Ferrari see a wedding or an Orthia festival with no Helen at all. Her link to the Louvre Partheneion is inference, not text.

07Menelaus and his Laconia

Menelaus brings sixty ships from "Lacedaemon of the many ravines", with Pharis, Sparta, Messe, Amyclae and Helos (Iliad 2.581–90). He is the lesser Atreid and the poem knows it: Agamemnon stops him facing Hector because he would die (7.104–21), Apollo taunts that Trojans once called him "a soft spearman" (17.588), Agamemnon grumbles that he slackens and leaves the work to his brother (10.114–23). The duel is his: he wins on points, his sword shatters on Paris' helmet, he drags him by the chin-strap until Aphrodite snaps it (3.340–82). His wounding by Pandarus' arrow, turned into his belt by Athena, is the poem's first real injury, and draws Agamemnon's cry that he would go home in disgrace with Menelaus' bones rotting in Troy (4.104–82). His aristeia is Book 17, over Patroclus' body: he kills Euphorbus in the scene of the uprooted olive sapling (17.45–60), is given by Athena "the courage of a fly" (17.570), and brings the corpse home with the Ajaxes and Meriones. His best moment is off the field, yielding a disputed prize to Antilochus, "for my heart is never arrogant or harsh" (23.566–613).

The Odyssey gives him Egypt and eternity. Becalmed at Pharos, helped by Eidothea, he ambushes Proteus under seal-skins and holds him through lion, serpent, leopard, boar, water and tree (4.351–461). Proteus tells him the fates of Ajax, Agamemnon and Odysseus, then his own: not death in Argos but the Elysian plain where Rhadamanthys is, "because you have Helen and are the son-in-law of Zeus" (4.561–9). It is Homer's only promise of a blessed afterlife to a living man, and it is earned by marriage, not merit. The wealth Telemachus marvels at (4.71–5) Menelaus would return for the lives it cost (4.97). Athenian tragedy was less kind: the Menelaus of Sophocles' Ajax and of Euripides' Andromache and Orestes is a Spartan in the Athenian sense, petty and cold.

The Menelaion and Mycenaean Laconia

The Menelaion sits on the Therapne ridge. British excavations (Dawkins 1909–10; Catling 1973–6 and 1985) found two successive Mycenaean mansions, the first built around 1450 BC and the last destroyed by fire around 1200, then no use until the seventh century, when cult appears: a bronze aryballos of about 650 BC inscribed "Deinis dedicated these to Helen, wife of Menelaus", a bronze meat-hook for Helen, a fifth-century stele to Menelaus and a monumental stepped platform. A visible Bronze Age ruin was given to the royal couple five centuries after its fall, the normal pattern of Greek hero-cult.

Where sources disagree. Catling saw the mansions as the seat of a Mycenaean ruler of Laconia. Since 2009 excavation at Ayios Vasileios near Xirokambi, 12 km south of Sparta, has uncovered a palatial complex with Linear B tablets destroyed around 1200 BC, now generally taken as the true palace of Mycenaean Laconia, with the Menelaion a subordinate residence. Whether either preserved any memory into Homer's age, rather than being reinvented by it, is uncertain.

Hermione, Orestes and Neoptolemus

Hermione was promised to Neoptolemus at Troy and Telemachus arrives during her wedding feast (Odyssey 4.3–9); Menelaus' other heir Megapenthes is a slave's son (4.10–12), and Hesiod's Nicostratus as Helen's son (fr. 175 M–W) contradicts the Odyssey's only child. Euripides' Andromache dramatises the sequel: Hermione, barren and jealous of Andromache's son by Neoptolemus, plots murder, is carried off by Orestes, who arranges Neoptolemus' death at Delphi. Pindar had told that death two ways, as Apollo's punishment for killing Priam (Paean 6.112–20) and as a quarrel over sacrificial meat (Nemean 7.34–47). Orestes and Hermione's son Tisamenus inherited Sparta over Menelaus' slave-born sons (Pausanias 2.18.6), the last Pelopid before the Heraclid return.

Where sources disagree. Whether Tyndareus first betrothed Hermione to Orestes (Euripides, Andromache 966–70; Orestes 1653–7; Sophocles' lost Hermione) or Menelaus gave her straight to Neoptolemus (Odyssey 4) decides who is the wronged party at Delphi. The Odyssey knows nothing of Orestes' claim.

08Afterlives, and the problem Helen poses

Marlowe's Faustus asks "Was this the face that launched a thousand ships, / And burnt the topless towers of Ilium?" (Doctor Faustus, A-text 5.1), and the answer is no: the Helen he kisses is a devil in a woman's shape, Stesichorus' eidolon in a Lutheran key. Goethe built the third act of Faust II (1827) on Euripides: Helen before Menelaus' palace at Sparta, warned by Phorkyas-Mephistopheles that her husband means to sacrifice her, flees to Faust's Gothic castle, bears Euphorion, and when the boy falls dissolves, leaving robe and veil in Faust's arms. Yeats made the egg a political event, "the broken wall, the burning roof and tower / And Agamemnon dead" (Leda and the Swan, 1924). H.D.'s Helen in Egypt (1961), in three parts (Pallinode, Leuké, Eidolon), is the full modern Palinode: Achilles meets the real Helen on the Egyptian shore and the war is rebuilt from the woman who was never in it. Seferis' Helen (1953), spoken by Teucer on Cyprus, is the bitterest: so many dead "for an empty tunic, for a Helen". Christa Wolf's Kassandra (1983) rationalises as Herodotus did, with a twist: Paris returns without her and Troy fights to the death to protect a lie its leaders cannot admit. Giraudoux's The Trojan War Will Not Take Place (1935) has Helen bored on stage while men explain why she matters; Petersen's Troy (2004) restores her guilt by calling it love. Austin (1994), Hughes (2005), Maguire (2009) and Blondell (2013) agree on one thing: each age reinvents Helen by deciding how much agency it can bear to give her.

Antiquity had already split along that fault. One branch keeps her at Troy and clears her: Priam blames the gods (Iliad 3.164–5), Penelope says a god drove her (Odyssey 23.222), Gorgias proves no cause leaves her culpable. The other removes her and leaves a cloud or an Egyptian hostage in her place. Both solutions destroy the war they explain. If Helen is guiltless, ten years of dead men avenged nothing, because no wrong was done by the person for whom they died. If she was absent, they fought for a name, which is exactly what Euripides' Menelaus cannot accept when he weighs the woman in Egypt against his own toil. Homer's answer is the only one that does not flinch: Helen is at Troy, she chose and was compelled, she calls herself a dog and no one agrees, and she knows the whole thing exists so that there will be a song. The war was fought for a woman, and the woman was never adequate to the war. The Spartans, who built her a temple and brought her their ugly daughters, understood that this made her a goddess rather than a criminal.

09Self-test

  1. Which two mothers does the tradition give Helen, and which text makes Nemesis her mother?
    Answer

    Leda (the swan, Euripides Helen 17–21) and Nemesis, pursued by Zeus and laying an egg Leda hatched; in the Cypria (fr. 9 Bernabé, in Athenaeus 8.334b–d), reported by Apollodorus, Library 3.10.7.

  2. How does Aethra, Theseus' mother, come to be at Troy in Iliad 3.144?
    Answer

    The Dioscuri enslaved her when they sacked Aphidnae to recover the child Helen whom Theseus had hidden there (Plutarch, Theseus 31; Apollodorus, Epitome 1.23).

  3. What does Thucydides think raised the army against Troy, and what does the poetic tradition say?
    Answer

    Thucydides 1.9 credits Agamemnon's power, not the oath of Tyndareus; the Hesiodic Catalogue (fr. 204), Euripides (IA 57–65) and Apollodorus (3.10.9) make the suitors' oath the binding mechanism.

  4. What is unique about Helen's exchange with Aphrodite at Iliad 3.399–412?
    Answer

    She is the only mortal in the poem to defy a god to her face, telling Aphrodite to go and sit beside Paris herself; Aphrodite's threat forces compliance.

  5. What two stories do Helen and Menelaus tell in Odyssey 4, and why does the drug matter?
    Answer

    Helen says she alone recognised the disguised Odysseus in Troy and helped him; Menelaus "agrees" and recounts how she circled the Wooden Horse imitating the heroes' wives. She has just drugged the wine with a pain-killing Egyptian pharmakon, which lets the room sit through the second story.

  6. What is the Palinode, and what evidence is there for a phantom Helen in Stesichorus?
    Answer

    Stesichorus' recantation (fr. 192 PMG, at Plato, Phaedrus 243a) denies Helen sailed to Troy; Plato, Republic 586c, says men fought over her eidolon "as Stesichorus says". P.Oxy. 2506 suggests two Palinodes.

  7. How does Herodotus' Egyptian Helen differ from Euripides'?
    Answer

    Herodotus (2.112–120) has no phantom: king Proteus detains Helen and the Greeks refuse to believe the Trojans lack her. Euripides (Helen, 412 BC) has Hera make a phantom of air for Troy while Hermes carries the real Helen to Proteus.

  8. Name Gorgias' four possible causes for Helen's going to Troy, and what he calls the speech at its end.
    Answer

    Gods and necessity; force; persuasion by speech; love. He calls it an encomium of Helen and a paignion, a plaything, for himself (DK 82 B11.21).

  9. Why does Proteus tell Menelaus he will go to Elysium?
    Answer

    Because he has Helen and is therefore Zeus' son-in-law (Odyssey 4.561–9); the reward is for the marriage, not for any deed.

  10. What did the Menelaion excavations find, and how does Ayios Vasileios change the picture?
    Answer

    Two Mycenaean mansions (c. 1450 to c. 1200 BC), a gap, then seventh-century cult including the Deinis aryballos "to Helen, wife of Menelaus". The palace with Linear B tablets at Ayios Vasileios, dug since 2009, is now taken as Mycenaean Laconia's centre, making the Menelaion a subordinate residence later claimed for hero-cult.

10Carry forward

  1. Helen is the tradition's test case for responsibility: Homer gives her both choice and compulsion and refuses to resolve them, and every later author (Sappho, Stesichorus, Gorgias, Euripides) picks a side. Watch for the same move with Clytemnestra, Medea and Pandora.
  2. The phantom Helen lets the Trojan War be told as a war about nothing: a theodicy (Herodotus), a puzzle about names and bodies (Euripides), an allegory of propaganda (Wolf, Seferis). Keep the eidolon in hand for Plato's cave and the fifth-century debate on seeming and being.
  3. At Sparta Helen was a goddess with a cult at a Bronze Age ruin, patron of girls becoming women; the Menelaion is the model case of epic names attaching to Mycenaean remains, which recurs at Mycenae, Pylos and the eighth-century tomb cults.

The prompt this answers

Give me an in-depth report on Helen and the Spartan side of the Trojan War. Cover: Helen's birth (Leda and the swan, Nemesis and the egg, the two eggs with the Dioscuri); the abduction by Theseus and the rescue by her brothers; the suitors, the oath and Menelaus' selection; her agency in Iliad 3 and 6 and 24 (the teichoskopia, her self-reproach, her lament for Hector) and Odyssey 4 (the drug, the horse story, Menelaus' pointed reply); Stesichorus' Palinode and the phantom Helen in Egypt, Herodotus' Egyptian Helen, Euripides' Helen and the sophistic game it plays; Gorgias' Encomium of Helen and the question of her guilt as a rhetorical set piece; Sappho fr. 16; her reunion with Menelaus and the sword that drops; the Spartan cult of Helen at Therapne (the Menelaion), Helen Dendritis at Rhodes, Helen as a goddess of girls' initiation (Alcman); Menelaus himself: the lesser Atreid, his aristeia, the wounding, the wrestling with Proteus and Elysium as his reward for marrying Zeus' daughter; Mycenaean Laconia and the Menelaion site; Hermione, Orestes and Neoptolemus; Helen in Marlowe, Goethe's Faust, H.D., modern retellings. End with the problem Helen poses: a war fought for a woman who is either guiltless or absent. Format: about 2500 words, headed sections, name sources, flag disagreements, end with a 10-question self-test with answers and the three ideas I must carry forward.
