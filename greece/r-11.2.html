Granicus, 334 BC

Situation. The western satraps rejected Memnon's advice to burn the land and starve the invader, since Arsites would not see his satrapy burned (Arrian 1.12.9–10). An early battle suited Alexander, whose treasury was nearly empty. Ground. A river with steep, broken banks. The Persians lined the bank with cavalry and held the Greek mercenary infantry back on the rise, a deployment often read as a deliberate bid to kill Alexander as he climbed out.

Course. In Arrian (1.13–16) Parmenion urges waiting for dawn and Alexander attacks that afternoon: phalanx centre, Parmenion and the Thessalians left, Companions right. A probe by the Paeonians, prodromoi and one Companion squadron drew the Persian horse onto it. Alexander then led the Companions across in a slanting line against the current and struck the disordered Persians. The fight on the bank was a cavalry melee. Alexander's helmet was split; Cleitus severed Spithridates' arm as he struck (Arrian 1.15.8; Plutarch, Alexander 16). When the Persian centre broke, the mercenaries were encircled and almost annihilated. Lysippus cast statues of the 25 Companions killed in the first clash; Aristobulus, in Plutarch, gives 34 dead in all.

Where sources disagree. Diodorus (17.19) has an unopposed crossing at dawn and a battle on the plain. Peter Green took this as true: a failed afternoon assault, suppressed by Arrian's source, then the dawn crossing. Hammond and Bosworth defended Arrian, noting that Diodorus' version mirrors Parmenion's advice. Most follow Arrian; the question is open.

Issus, 333 BC

Situation. Delayed by illness at Tarsus, Alexander marched south towards Myriandrus. Darius crossed the Amanus by a northern pass, came down behind him at Issus, and killed the Macedonian sick (Arrian 2.7.1). Alexander confirmed it with a thirty-oared ship (2.7.2), turned about, and retook the pass by night. Ground. A narrow plain between sea and foothills along the Pinarus, where numbers counted for little.

Orders of battle. Darius massed heavy cavalry under Nabarzanes by the sea, Greek mercenaries and Cardaces in the centre, light troops on the hills to his left, and himself in the centre. Alexander deployed from column as the plain widened, sent the Thessalians behind the phalanx to Parmenion on the left, and cleared the hills with Agrianians and archers. Course. Alexander crossed the river at the run with Companions and hypaspists and broke the Persian left. The phalanx, disordered by the banks, opened a gap that the mercenaries exploited; Ptolemy son of Seleucus and about 120 notable Macedonians fell (2.10.7). Alexander wheeled onto the mercenaries' flank and towards Darius, who fled; the Persian right, holding the Thessalians, broke on the news. Alexander took a thigh wound, by Darius' hand according to Chares (Plutarch, Alexander 20).

Where sources disagree. The Pinarus is usually the Payas, though the Deli Çay has had defenders. Polybius (12.17–22) argued that Callisthenes' plain could not hold Callisthenes' numbers, so the topography was contested in antiquity. Curtius makes Darius flee early; Arrian only once his left had collapsed.

Gaugamela, 331 BC

Situation. Alexander refused Darius' offers, including everything west of the Euphrates (Arrian 2.25). Darius chose an open plain and levelled it for chariots. A captured Persian order of battle, cited from Aristobulus (Arrian 3.11.3), makes the Persian deployment unusually well known; an eclipse eleven days earlier fixes the date (3.7.6). Orders of battle. Bessus with Bactrians and Sacae on the Persian left, Mazaeus on the right, Darius in the centre with the Kinsmen, Greek mercenaries and elephants, chariots in front. Expecting envelopment, Alexander posted a second line able to face about and angled his flank guards back, forming something close to a hollow rectangle (3.12). He refused a night attack: he would not steal victory (3.10.2).

Course. Alexander inclined right, off the prepared ground. Bessus' horse extended to match, feeding squadrons in against Menidas and the mercenary cavalry. The chariots charged; Agrianians and javelin men shot the drivers, and the infantry opened lanes for the rest (3.13.5–6). As the Persian left stretched, a gap opened beside the centre. Alexander formed a Companion wedge, took the nearest phalanx battalions with him, and drove at Darius, who fled. On the left Mazaeus pressed Parmenion hard, and horsemen broke through a separate phalanx gap to the baggage, where the second line expelled them. Parmenion sent for help; Alexander turned back and collided with retreating Persian cavalry in the day's hardest fighting, losing 60 Companions (3.15.2). Darius escaped.

Where sources disagree. Callisthenes, via Plutarch (Alexander 33), blamed Parmenion as sluggish and envious, his appeal costing Alexander the king. Arrian is milder. Since Parmenion was murdered in 330, many scholars, Bosworth among them, see a hostile tradition built afterwards, and credit Parmenion with holding the wing on which the battle actually hung. Whether the pursuit could have caught Darius anyway is uncertain.

Hydaspes, 326 BC

Situation. Porus held the far bank of a river in spate, with elephants horses would not face. Alexander advertised that he would wait for low water while moving troops noisily along the bank night after night until Porus stopped reacting (Arrian 5.9–11). He then took a picked force about 150 stades upstream to a wooded headland and island, leaving Craterus in camp with orders to cross only if the elephants left. The crossing. Made at night in a thunderstorm; the force landed on a second island and forded the last channel chest-deep (5.13). Porus' son, sent to intercept, was killed and his chariots bogged.

Battle. Porus left a screen against Craterus and deployed elephants along his front, infantry behind and between, cavalry and chariots on the wings. Short of infantry, Alexander massed horse against the Indian left, led by the horse archers, and sent Coenus towards the other flank to fall on the Indian cavalry from behind once it engaged (5.16.3). The Indian horse, caught between them, sheltered among the elephants. The phalanx met the elephants with sarissas and javelins aimed at the mahouts; maddened animals trampled both sides, more often their own. Alexander's cavalry closed round the Indian mass, Craterus crossed, and the pursuit became slaughter. Porus surrendered and was kept as king (5.19.1–3). Bucephalas died soon after and gave his name to one of the two cities founded on the field. The battle was the army's last great victory: that summer, at the Hyphasis, the men refused to go further, and part of the reason was what elephants and monsoon fighting had cost them.

Where sources disagree. The crossing site is unresolved: Stein argued for Jalalpur, others for sites near Haranpur or Mong. Coenus' route is contested, round behind the Indian line or a direct attack on the Indian right; Arrian's text allows both. Plutarch's "letter of Alexander" (Alexander 60) differs on the son's force, and Arrian's Munychion date sits awkwardly with his own mention of solstice rains.

The sieges

Halicarnassus (334)

Memnon held the city with mercenaries and the Persian fleet at his back. Alexander filled the ditch, brought up towers and breached the wall; one night two drunk men of Perdiccas' battalion started an unplanned assault that nearly succeeded (Arrian 1.21.1). After a failed sortie Memnon fired the town and withdrew to citadels that held into 333: proof that a fortress supplied by sea could not be finished off from land.

Tyre (January to July/August 332)

Refused leave to sacrifice to Heracles-Melqart inside the island city (Arrian 2.15.7–16.8), Alexander argued that a Tyre left free gave the Persian fleet a base behind him (2.17). The mole. A causeway was built from the mainland across the four-stade channel. In deep water near the walls the builders came under fire, and two towers behind hide screens went up at its head. A fireship, a horse transport packed with combustibles, burned them (2.19). The naval war. Alexander widened the mole and went to Sidon, where the decisive change came: the Phoenician squadrons, their cities already surrendered, joined him, with 120 Cypriot ships (2.20). Outnumbered, Tyre closed its two harbours. Rams and towers. Rams were mounted on lashed ships and transports. The Tyrians sank stones to keep them off and cut anchor cables; the Macedonians answered with chains and cranes. A midday sortie from the northern harbour mauled the Cypriots until Alexander rounded the island and destroyed it (2.21–22). Assault and massacre. A breach was opened on the south side, bridges were dropped from ships, and the hypaspists went up under Admetus, who was killed, with Alexander beside them (2.23). Arrian counts 8,000 Tyrians killed, about 30,000 sold, and 400 Macedonian dead over the siege (2.24.4–5). Diodorus (17.46.4) and Curtius (4.4.17) add 2,000 men crucified along the shore; Arrian is silent.

Gaza (autumn 332)

The engineers judged the hill-top city impregnable. Alexander built a ramp round it, two stades wide and 250 feet high in Arrian (2.26.3), hauled up the Tyre machines, and took a catapult bolt in the shoulder (2.27.2). After about two months the men were killed and the women and children sold.

Where sources disagree. Curtius (4.6.29), like Hegesias of Magnesia, says the commander Batis was dragged alive behind Alexander's chariot in imitation of Achilles. Arrian omits it. Some scholars dismiss it as rhetoric; others suspect deliberate omission.

The Sogdian Rock (327) and Aornos (326)

Ariamazes' defenders jeered that Alexander needed winged soldiers. For a prize of twelve talents, 300 volunteers climbed the snow cliff at night with iron tent pegs and flax ropes; about 30 fell, and the garrison surrendered at dawn (Arrian 4.18.4–19.4; Curtius 7.11). Arrian puts Rhoxane's capture here; Strabo (11.11.4) puts it at another rock. Aornos, which legend said had defeated Heracles, fell after Alexander filled a ravine with a mound to bring artillery in range; the defenders slipped away by night and many were cut down (Arrian 4.28–30). Stein's identification with Pir-Sar (1926) is widely accepted but not certain. Each impossible fortress taken persuaded the next to negotiate.

The system behind the victories

Hammer, anvil and hinge

The sarissa phalanx (twelve cubits in Theophrastus, Enquiry into Plants 3.12.2) pinned the enemy centre; it needed only to hold. The Companion cavalry in wedges struck the weak point. Alexander attacked from his right, refused his left and advanced in echelon, so the enemy committed first; at Gaugamela he made the gap himself. The 3,000 hypaspists, faster than the phalanx and able to keep pace with the horse, were the hinge that stopped a gap opening between them and led assaults on walls. Agrianians, archers and javelin men cleared hills, killed chariot crews and screened flanks; they appear at every turning point.

Leadership and its costs

Leading the wedge gave Alexander local control and unlimited loyalty, but nobody commanded the whole field once he charged, hence the stranded left at Gaugamela. The wounds accumulated: the Granicus, Issus, Gaza, a stone to the head at Cyropolis (Arrian 4.3.3), and an arrow through the chest among the Malli (6.10) that nearly ended the expedition.

Intelligence and speed

He questioned prisoners and locals constantly and checked reports before acting. Speed was a weapon: in 335 he reached Boeotia from Illyria before Thebes believed him alive (Arrian 1.7.5); chasing Darius in 330 he covered about 400 stades in a night with mounted infantry (3.21).

Logistics: Engels

Donald Engels (Alexander the Great and the Logistics of the Macedonian Army, 1978) argued that food, water and fodder governed every movement. Philip had banned carts and made men carry their own flour (Frontinus, Stratagems 4.1.6), shrinking the train, but the army could still carry only a few days' supply. Hence its timing by harvests, its hugging of rivers and coasts with the fleet, and its reliance on surrenders negotiated in advance; Gedrosia becomes a failed rendezvous with Nearchus. Critics accept the framework but dispute many of his figures.

Engineers, Bematists, doctors

Diades, pupil of Philip's engineer Polyidus, served at Tyre; Vitruvius (10.13.3) credits him with sectional towers, the borer and a boarding bridge. The Bematists, among them Baeton and Diognetus (Pliny, Natural History 6.61), paced and recorded each march, data later used by Eratosthenes. Philip of Acarnania treated Alexander at Tarsus (Arrian 2.4.8–11); Critodemus of Cos, or Critobulus in Curtius (9.5.25), drew the Mallian arrow. No medical corps can be shown, but care was expected.

Persian failures

No unified command at the Granicus; Memnon's death in 333 ending the naval counter-stroke; cramped ground at Issus; faith in chariots at Gaugamela; and Darius twice fleeing while his right was winning. Briant and others rightly reject the "decadent empire": the cavalry was excellent. The structural gap was line infantry able to face the phalanx: the Greek mercenaries were too few and were lost at the Granicus and Issus, and the Cardaces never proved themselves. Persian command also centred on the king's person, so his flight dissolved the army in a way Alexander's wounds never did; Macedonian battalion and squadron commanders could fight on without orders.

Comparisons and the honest assessment

Philip, Epaminondas, Hannibal

Philip built the instrument: phalanx, expanded Companions, pay, the cart ban, engineers, combined arms (Diodorus 16.3). At Chaeronea his feigned withdrawal (Polyaenus 4.2.2) drew the Athenians on while Alexander broke through on the other wing (Diodorus 16.86). But Philip preferred diplomacy and limited war; Alexander made the decisive battle the aim. Epaminondas, watched by the hostage Philip at Thebes, supplied the weighted wing and refused flank: fifty shields deep at Leuctra (Xenophon, Hellenica 6.4.12), driving "like a trireme" at Mantinea (7.5.23). Alexander turned the infantry wedge into a cavalry wedge. Hannibal is the contrast: at Cannae (Polybius 3.113–117) he enveloped, letting the Romans push into the trap, while Alexander penetrated and hunted the enemy commander. Hannibal won with mercenaries, no support from home, commanding from behind the line. In the story of his talk with Scipio he ranks Alexander first (Livy 35.14; Plutarch, Flamininus 21), though the anecdote may be invented.

How much was Alexander?

The army and most of its 334 officers were Philip's; Parmenion, Craterus and Coenus ran independent commands well. Persian choices eased the Granicus and Issus, and Darius' flight turned a close fight at Gaugamela into a rout. Yet three things were Alexander's alone: his reading of ground and timing (the Hydaspes feints, the rightward drift, the swift turn at Issus); his range, beating cavalry armies, a sea fortress, elephants, mountain strongholds and steppe raiders within a decade, which no other ancient commander matches; and his will, which took Tyre and Aornos. Against that: risk out of proportion to gain, lost control once he charged, brutality at Tyre, Gaza and among the Malli, and ambitions the army finally refused at the Hyphasis. A fair verdict: a supreme tactician with a supreme instrument against an enemy whose king lacked nerve. Remove any one and the outcome changes, though not necessarily to defeat.

Self-test

  1. What advice did Memnon give the satraps before the Granicus, and why was it rejected?
    Answer

    He advised a scorched-earth retreat to starve the Macedonians. Arsites refused to see his own satrapy burned (Arrian 1.12.9–10), and the satraps chose to fight.

  2. State the Granicus timing problem in one sentence for each source.
    Answer

    Arrian: Alexander rejected Parmenion's advice to wait and attacked across the river the same afternoon. Diodorus: he crossed unopposed at dawn and fought on the plain beyond. Green favoured Diodorus; Hammond and Bosworth defended Arrian.

  3. How did Darius come to be behind Alexander at Issus, and how did Alexander confirm it?
    Answer

    Darius crossed the Amanus by a northern pass while Alexander moved south towards Myriandrus, and reached Issus on Alexander's line of communication. Alexander sent a thirty-oared ship up the coast to check (Arrian 2.7.2).

  4. Where did the Macedonian line come closest to breaking at Issus, and who paid for it?
    Answer

    In the centre, where the river banks broke up the phalanx and the Greek mercenaries drove into the gap. Ptolemy son of Seleucus and about 120 Macedonians of rank were killed (Arrian 2.10.7).

  5. How were the scythed chariots at Gaugamela neutralised?
    Answer

    The Agrianians and javelin men shot the drivers and horses, and the infantry opened lanes so that the surviving chariots passed through harmlessly to be dealt with in the rear (Arrian 3.13.5–6).

  6. What is the dispute over Parmenion's role at Gaugamela?
    Answer

    Callisthenes, via Plutarch (Alexander 33), portrays him as slow and his appeal for help as costing Alexander the capture of Darius. Many modern scholars see a tradition hostile to him, built up after his murder in 330, and credit him with holding the hard-pressed left wing.

  7. How did Alexander get across the Hydaspes?
    Answer

    Weeks of noisy feints along the bank dulled Porus' reactions. Alexander then took about 5,000 horse and 6,000 foot to a screened crossing about 150 stades upstream, crossed by night in a storm, and forded a final channel after landing on a second island, while Craterus held the main camp opposite Porus.

  8. What turned the naval situation at Tyre?
    Answer

    The Phoenician squadrons, whose home cities had already submitted, and 120 Cypriot ships came over to Alexander at Sidon (Arrian 2.20). That gave him command of the sea, allowed him to blockade both harbours, and let him use ship-mounted rams against the walls.

  9. What does Engels' study argue, and what is its main limitation?
    Answer

    That food, water and fodder dictated the army's routes and timing: soldiers carried their own rations, the army could carry only a few days' supply, and it therefore depended on rivers, coasts, the fleet, harvests and local surrenders arranged in advance. Critics accept the framework but question his figures for the army's size and train.

  10. How does Alexander's decisive method differ from Hannibal's at Cannae?
    Answer

    Hannibal enveloped the enemy and let them advance into a trap, commanding from behind his line. Alexander broke into the enemy line at a gap, usually with the Companion wedge he led himself, and went for the enemy commander.

Carry forward

  1. Alexander's battles follow one pattern: the phalanx fixes, the hypaspists hold the hinge, the light troops screen, and the Companion wedge led by Alexander breaks through a gap that the oblique approach has created, aimed at the enemy commander.
  2. Almost every tactical reconstruction depends on Arrian, writing from Ptolemy and Aristobulus. Where the vulgate (Diodorus, Curtius) differs, as on the Granicus timing, Parmenion, Batis or the Tyrian crucifixions, the disagreement is often about reputation as much as fact.
  3. The victories came from three causes together: Alexander's own reading of ground and timing, the army and officers Philip left him, and the Persians' strategic and personal failures, Darius' above all. A balanced judgement of him has to give weight to all three.
