01From avenger to heir: Hyrcania to Arachosia, 330

In summer 330 Bessus, satrap of Bactria, and his fellow conspirators stabbed Darius III and left him dying (Arrian, Anabasis 3.21.10; Curtius 5.13). That changed what the war was about. At Ecbatana Alexander had already sent home the allied Greek contingents (Arrian 3.19.5): the League of Corinth's war of revenge had in effect ended at Persepolis. Bessus then took the upright tiara and the throne name Artaxerxes (Arrian 3.25.3). That made him a usurper whom Alexander, as Darius' self-declared avenger and heir, could hunt in Achaemenid terms.

Aria set the pattern for the next five years. Its satrap Satibarzanes submitted, was confirmed in post, then killed his Macedonian escort and went over to Bessus. A lightning return to Artacoana crushed him (Arrian 3.25.5–7). Arrian does not record the foundation of Alexandria in Aria, at or near Herat. It is known from Strabo (11.10.1), Pliny (Natural History 6.61) and Isidore of Charax. The city commanded the junction of the roads to Bactria and Drangiana. At Phrada in Drangiana Philotas was executed and Parmenion murdered (Arrian 3.26). The army wintered among the Ariaspians, whom Alexander honoured as "Benefactors" because they had fed Cyrus the Great's army (Arrian 3.27.4–5). Cyrus would return as a model again and again. In Arachosia Alexander founded the city Isidore calls Alexandropolis, usually placed at Old Kandahar.

Where sources disagree. The derivation of "Kandahar" from "Iskandar" is a popular etymology, not a proven one. Old Kandahar had an Achaemenid predecessor, and Ashoka's Greek edicts there show Greek readers by the mid-third century, but no ancient author describes the foundation. The identification is probable, not certain.

02The Hindu Kush and the end of Bessus, 329

After wintering near Begram, where he founded Alexandria in the Caucasus, Alexander crossed the Hindu Kush in early spring 329, probably by the Khawak pass. The troops lived on raw meat from the pack animals, seasoned with silphium (Arrian 3.28.4–7; Curtius 7.3). Bessus had counted on the mountains. He scorched the country and retreated across the Oxus, burning his boats. The army crossed on tent-skins stuffed with chaff (Arrian 3.29.4), and the coalition broke up. Spitamenes and Dataphernes offered to give up Bessus. Ptolemy's own account turns the handover into a daring raid that he led. Aristobulus says Bessus was simply handed over (Arrian 3.29.6–30.5).

Bessus was set naked and collared by the roadside, asked why he had killed his king and kinsman, and flogged. At Bactra a council sentenced him to lose his nose and ear-lobes, and he was sent to Ecbatana to be executed before the Medes and Persians (Arrian 4.7.3). This was the punishment Darius I had inflicted on the rebel Fravartish, as recorded at Behistun. Arrian (4.7.4) condemns it as barbarian and links it to Alexander's adoption of Median dress. The point is the role it reveals: Alexander was punishing a rebel against the Great King as the Great King.

Where sources disagree. Arrian has mutilation followed by execution at Ecbatana. Curtius (7.10.10) has crucifixion. Diodorus (17.83.9) has Bessus handed to Darius' brother Oxyathres and cut to pieces.

03Sogdiana, 329–327: the guerrilla war and its settlement

Bessus' fall did not pacify the north-east. In summer 329 Alexander founded Alexandria Eschate, "the Furthest", at Khujand on the Jaxartes (Syr Darya). Arrian (4.4.1) says its walls rose in twenty days. The Sogdian towns, Cyropolis among them, immediately rose. Alexander was wounded in the head and neck during the sieges, and crossed the river under a catapult barrage to beat the Saca horsemen. Meanwhile Spitamenes laid siege to Maracanda (Samarkand).

The Polytimetus

The relief column, under the Lycian interpreter Pharnuches with Andromachus, Menedemus and Caranus, was wiped out on the Polytimetus (Zeravshan) by horse-archers (Arrian 4.5–6; Curtius 7.7.30–39). Curtius gives about 2,000 infantry and 300 cavalry killed, and says Alexander ordered the survivors to keep silent. It was the worst defeat of the reign. Alexander answered by slaughtering the people of the valley (Arrian 4.6.5). From then on it was a war of terror and counter-terror. It produced the drunken killing of Cleitus at Maracanda in 328 (Arrian 4.8–9) and overlapped with the proskynesis crisis of 327 (4.10–14).

Where sources disagree. Arrian gives two versions. In the first, probably Ptolemy's, Pharnuches loses his nerve in open battle. In Aristobulus' version, there is an ambush and a chaotic river crossing ordered by Caranus with no agreed command. Both blame a non-Macedonian with a doubtful commission, which looks apologetic.

The Branchidae

Curtius (7.5.28–35) tells of a town of Greeks descended from the Branchidae, the priests of Apollo at Didyma, whose ancestors had supposedly handed the temple treasure to Xerxes and moved east with him. They welcomed Alexander. He had them massacred anyway, for their ancestors' sacrilege. Strabo (11.11.4) and Plutarch (Moralia 557b) know the story. Arrian is silent.

Where sources disagree. Tarn dismissed the story as fiction. Others accept it, perhaps as an atrocity justified by Callisthenes, who was promoting the revived Didyma oracle (Strabo 17.1.43). Herodotus (6.19) makes things worse: in his account Didyma was sacked by Darius' generals in 494, not betrayed to Xerxes. Uncertain.

Spitamenes, the Rocks and Roxane

In 328 five columns swept Sogdiana. After Coenus defeated Spitamenes at Gabae, Spitamenes' Massagetae allies sent his head to Alexander (Arrian 4.17.7). Curtius (8.3) says his wife killed him, a more novelistic version. The resistance ended at mountain fortresses. At the Sogdian Rock the defenders jeered that Alexander would need soldiers with wings. About three hundred volunteers climbed the cliff at night with tent-pegs and ropes, and some thirty fell to their deaths (Arrian 4.18.4–19.4). Among the captives were the family of the Bactrian noble Oxyartes.

Alexander married Oxyartes' daughter Roxane. Plutarch (Alexander 47.7–8) calls it a love match that also served his policy. Curtius (8.4.23–30) records the Macedonians' disgust that the king had taken a captive's daughter. The marriage tied the most powerful Bactrian family to the regime, and Oxyartes later became satrap of the Paropamisadae. It also made an Iranian woman the probable mother of the heir.

Where sources disagree. Curtius (7.11) dates the "Rock of Arimazes" to 329/8, while Arrian puts it in 327. Strabo (11.11.4) has Roxane captured at the rock of Sisimithres, whom Curtius seems to treat as Arrian's Chorienes. The order of the sieges cannot be fixed.

Cities as garrisons

Justin (12.5.13) credits Alexander with twelve foundations in Bactria and Sogdiana, and Strabo (11.11.4) with eight. Most were garrison towns, not poleis: veterans, mercenaries and local people placed on river crossings and oasis routes. Amyntas was left with 10,000 infantry and 3,500 cavalry (Arrian 4.22.3). The settlers rose on a false report of Alexander's death in 325, and again in force in 323 (Diodorus 17.99.5–6; 18.7). Whether Ai Khanoum was Alexandria Oxiana or an early Seleucid city is uncertain.

04Into India, 327–326: Taxiles, the hill peoples, Aornos, Nysa

The ruler of Taxila, called Taxiles by the Greeks (perhaps Ambhi; Curtius' Omphis), had already offered an alliance against his rival Porus (Diodorus 17.86.4). In 327 Hephaestion and Perdiccas took the Kabul valley road with Taxiles' help to bridge the Indus. Alexander turned north into the hills to secure the flank. Against the Aspasians he was wounded in the shoulder, and Ptolemy, who is again his own witness, killed their chief in single combat (Arrian 4.23–24). At Massaga, among the Assacenians, an arrow hit Alexander in the ankle. After the town surrendered, 7,000 Indian mercenaries agreed to serve him and were then cut down. Arrian (4.27.3–4) says they tried to desert. Diodorus (17.84) and Plutarch (Alexander 59.6–7) call it treachery against men under truce.

Aornos, a crag above the Indus, was said to have defeated Heracles himself. Arrian (4.28.1–2) suspects Macedonian boasting. Alexander filled a ravine and built a mound across to the summit, then took it as the defenders slipped away (4.29–30). Aurel Stein identified Aornos as Pir-Sar. More recent work in Swat favours Mount Ilam. It remains uncertain.

At Nysa the people, led by Acouphis, claimed Dionysus as their founder and pointed to the ivy on Mount Meros, whose name echoed mēros, the thigh of Zeus from which the god was born. Alexander, moved by pothos (Arrian 5.2.1), spared the city, and the Companions held a Bacchic revel. Arrian (5.1.2) advises against looking too closely into such stories, and Eratosthenes treated them as flattery (Arrian 5.3.1–4; Strabo 15.1.7–9). The expedition was starting to measure itself against gods.

05Hydaspes and Hyphasis, 326

Porus held the swollen Hydaspes (Jhelum) with elephants that horses would not face. After many nights of feints, Alexander crossed upstream in a storm with about 5,000 cavalry and 6,000 infantry, leaving Craterus to hold the camp (Arrian 5.11–14). In the battle Coenus' cavalry took the Indians in the rear. The phalanx attacked the elephants' drivers and trunks until the maddened animals trampled their own side (5.15–18). Asked how he wished to be treated, the wounded Porus said "as a king" (5.19.2). Alexander restored him with more territory and reconciled him with Taxiles. A grateful Porus could hold the Punjab more cheaply than a satrap. Nicaea and Bucephala, named for the dead horse, were founded on the battlefield.

Where sources disagree. Arrian (5.14.3–6) sets Aristobulus' sixty chariots under Porus' son against Ptolemy's 2,000 cavalry and 120 chariots. Porus' elephants are put at 200 (Arrian 5.15.4), 130 (Diodorus 17.87.2) and 85 (Curtius 8.13.6).

The army pushed east past the Acesines and Hydraotes and stopped at the Hyphasis (Beas). It had had some seventy days of monsoon rain (Diodorus 17.94.3), and there were reports of the Nanda kingdom on the Ganges with thousands of elephants (Curtius 9.2; Plutarch, Alexander 62). In Arrian, Alexander argues that the eastern Ocean is near and that conquest has no limit but the earth's end (5.25–26). Coenus answers for the men: they have done enough, they want to see their parents, wives and children, and the finest success is knowing when to stop (5.27). Alexander sulked in his tent for three days. The sacrifices then proved unfavourable, and he turned back, leaving twelve altars as high as towers (5.28–29.1). Coenus died within weeks (6.2.1).

Where sources disagree. The speeches are Arrian's compositions. Most scholars read the episode as a real defeat of the king's will. Some, notably P. O. Spann, argue Alexander engineered or exploited the refusal and used the omens to save face.

06Down the Indus and three routes west, 326–324

The Mallian town

In November 326 the fleet left the Hydaspes, with Nearchus as admiral and Onesicritus as royal helmsman. Ptolemy put it at nearly 2,000 craft (Arrian 6.2.4), Nearchus at 800 (Indica 19.7). The voyage was a campaign of conquest. At a town of the Malli, often identified with Multan but uncertainly, Alexander climbed onto the wall. The ladders broke behind him, and he jumped down inside with Peucestas, Leonnatus and Abreas. Abreas was killed. An arrow pierced Alexander's chest while Peucestas covered him with the sacred shield from Troy, until the army broke in and killed everyone (Arrian 6.9–11).

Where sources disagree. Arrian (6.11.3–8) corrects the record. Ptolemy was not present, whatever Clitarchus and Timagenes said, because Ptolemy himself wrote that he was elsewhere. Curtius (9.5.21) notes the same contradiction. Who removed the arrowhead (Critodemus of Cos or Perdiccas) is also disputed.

Further down, rulers who submitted and then revolted, such as Musicanus, were hanged, along with the Brahmans who had encouraged them (Arrian 6.17.2). At Patala, the head of the delta, in summer 325, the tides stranded the boats and astonished the Macedonians (6.19.1–2). In open sea Alexander poured a libation to Poseidon from a golden cup (6.19.5).

Craterus and Gedrosia

The force was split. Craterus, sent ahead with the elephants, baggage and veterans, took the inland route through Arachosia and Drangiana (Arrian 6.17.3). He brought the rebel Ordanes to Carmania in chains. Alexander took the coast through the Oreitae, leaving Leonnatus at a new Alexandria at Rhambacia, and then entered Gedrosia. The march to Pura took sixty days (6.24.1). Arrian gives two motives. The practical one was to dig wells and lay down depots for the fleet (6.23). The other, from Nearchus, was rivalry: Semiramis had come back that way with twenty survivors and Cyrus with seven, and Alexander wanted to outdo them (6.24.2–3; Strabo 15.1.5). Heat and thirst were followed by a night flash flood that swept away most of the women and children (6.25.5). Plutarch (Alexander 66.4–5) says barely a quarter of 135,000 men survived.

Where sources disagree. Donald Engels argued that the fighting force was far smaller than Plutarch implies and that most of the dead were non-combatants and animals. A minority, Peter Green among them, suspect the march was a punishment for the Hyphasis. Most see a sound plan ruined by the monsoon's timing and the fleet's delay, and treat the Cyrus and Semiramis motive as Nearchus' gloss.

Nearchus and Onesicritus

Driven out early by hostile locals, Nearchus sailed the Makran coast. His account survives in Arrian's Indica (20–42). It describes Fish-Eaters who made meal from dried fish and built houses of whale bones (29), a school of whales scattered by trumpets (30), and his refusal of Onesicritus' plan to sail for Cape Maketa (Musandam). His orders, he said, were to survey the coast, not to take risks (32.9–13). Reaching Harmozia, he went inland to Alexander, who wept because he thought the fleet lost (33–36). Arrian (6.2.3) accuses Onesicritus of claiming the command when he was only a helmsman, and Strabo (15.1.28) calls him the arch-pilot of marvels.

Carmania and Susa

The vulgate tradition describes a seven-day Bacchic procession through Carmania, in imitation of Dionysus' triumph (Plutarch, Alexander 67; Curtius 9.10.24–29; Diodorus 17.106.1). Arrian (6.28.1–2) rejects it because neither Ptolemy nor Aristobulus mentions it. The purge, by contrast, is certain. Cleander and Sitalces, Parmenion's killers, were executed (Arrian 6.27.4), and Harpalus fled. At Pasargadae Aristobulus was ordered to restore Cyrus' plundered tomb (6.29). At Susa in early 324 Nearchus and Onesicritus were crowned, and 30,000 Iranian Epigoni arrived, trained in Macedonian fashion (7.5.6; 7.6.1).

07The world as they imagined it

The army set out with Aristotle's map. According to Aristotle, from the Hindu Kush, his "Parnassus", one could see the outer Ocean (Meteorologica 1.13). For Herodotus, India was the last inhabited land to the east (3.98). The Hindu Kush became the "Caucasus", complete with Prometheus' cave, which Eratosthenes dismissed as flattery (Arrian 5.3; Strabo 11.5.5). The Jaxartes became the Tanais, so that Alexander could be said to have reached Europe's edge. Arrian separates it from Herodotus' Tanais (3.30.7–9). Strabo (11.7.4) blames Polycleitus for merging the Caspian with the Sea of Azov to make this work. Alexander treats the Caspian as a gulf of Ocean (Arrian 5.26.1–2) and later sent Heraclides to check (7.16).

Crocodiles in the Indus and Egyptian beans on the Acesines convinced Alexander he had found the upper Nile. He wrote as much to Olympias, then deleted the passage when he learned the river reached the sea (Arrian 6.1.2–5; Strabo 15.1.25). He apparently ignored or did not know that Scylax had sailed from the Indus to Egypt for Darius I (Herodotus 4.44).

The legacy lasted. The bematists Diognetus and Baeton produced measured distances (Pliny 6.61). Nearchus charted the sea route from India to the Gulf. Megasthenes, around 300, opened up the Ganges, and India became a subcontinent instead of a fringe. Eratosthenes drew one continuous chain from the Taurus through the "Caucasus" to the Himalaya, and moved the eastern Ocean far beyond the Hyphasis. Patrocles still persuaded him that the Caspian opened into the northern Ocean (Strabo 11.11.6).

08Assessment: the war that became something else

These six years strip the expedition of its first purpose in stages. The League of Corinth war ended when the Greeks were sent home from Ecbatana. The pursuit of Bessus was an Achaemenid war of succession, fought with Persian punishments. Sogdiana was a frontier pacification of the kind any Great King would have had to fight. Even the Indus valley could be presented as recovering the old satrapies of Gandhara and Hindush. The Hyphasis was the first point clearly beyond any Persian frontier, where only conquest for its own sake remained, and there the army refused to go on.

By then the explanations are heroic ones. Arrian calls it pothos. The landmarks are Heracles at Aornos, Dionysus at Nysa, Cyrus and Semiramis in Gedrosia, and the sacred shield from Troy at the Mallian town. At the same time the army was turned into an imperial force: Iranian cavalry, Median dress, Roxane, the Epigoni. Cleitus, Callisthenes and Coenus each voiced the Macedonian protest. What reached Susa was the personal empire of a king whose authority rested on conquest and emulation of the gods, served by an army loyal to him rather than to Macedon.

Where sources disagree. Tarn saw a vision of human unity here. Bosworth (Alexander and the East) stresses massacre and the "tragedy of triumph". Briant sees the last of the Achaemenids. Holt emphasises how coercive and fragile the Bactrian settlement was.

09Self-test

  1. Why did Darius' death change the basis of Alexander's war, and what event at Ecbatana marks the shift?
    Answer

    Bessus claimed the throne as Artaxerxes, so Alexander could hunt him as Darius' avenger and heir, which made it an Achaemenid war of succession. The dismissal of the allied Greek contingents at Ecbatana (Arrian 3.19.5) had in effect ended the League of Corinth's war of revenge.

  2. What was Persian about Bessus' punishment, and how does Arrian judge it?
    Answer

    Mutilation of the nose and ear-lobes, then execution before the Medes and Persians: Darius I's treatment of rebels, as at Behistun. Arrian (4.7.3–4) condemns it as barbarian and links it to Alexander's Median dress.

  3. How do Arrian's two sources explain the Polytimetus disaster?
    Answer

    The main version blames Pharnuches for losing his nerve in open battle. Aristobulus describes an ambush and a chaotic crossing ordered by Caranus with no agreed command. Both lay the blame on others, not Alexander.

  4. Why do scholars doubt the Branchidae massacre?
    Answer

    Arrian does not mention it. Herodotus 6.19 has Didyma sacked in 494, not betrayed to Xerxes. And it may come from Callisthenes' propaganda for the revived oracle. Its historicity is uncertain.

  5. What political purposes did the marriage to Roxane serve?
    Answer

    It bound Oxyartes' family, the leading Bactrian aristocrats, to the regime. It also made an Iranian woman the probable mother of the heir, and Curtius records the Macedonians' disgust at the match.

  6. How does Arrian treat Heracles at Aornos and Dionysus at Nysa?
    Answer

    Sceptically. He suspects the Macedonians attached Heracles to Aornos to inflate the feat (4.28.1–2). He advises against looking too closely into the Nysa claims (5.1.2), and he records Eratosthenes' view that the Nysa stories were flattery.

  7. Why did Alexander restore Porus?
    Answer

    A powerful, grateful native king could hold the Punjab more cheaply than a satrap. Alexander also enlarged his territory and reconciled him with Taxiles.

  8. What did Coenus argue at the Hyphasis, and what is the modern debate?
    Answer

    That the army had done enough, wanted to go home to its families, and that knowing when to stop was the finest success. Most scholars see a real defeat of Alexander's will. Spann and others argue he engineered or exploited the protest and used omens to save face.

  9. What two motives does Arrian give for the Gedrosian march, and where does the Cyrus and Semiramis comparison come from?
    Answer

    Supporting the fleet with wells and depots, and outdoing Semiramis and Cyrus, who had barely got through. The comparison comes from Nearchus (Arrian 6.24.2–3; Strabo 15.1.5).

  10. Name two geographical misidentifications and one lasting gain for Greek geography.
    Answer

    The Jaxartes taken for the Tanais, the Indus for the upper Nile, the Hindu Kush called the Caucasus, the Caspian thought a gulf of Ocean. Lasting gains include the bematists' measured distances, Nearchus' survey of the sea route, and the later picture of India and the Ganges.

10Carry forward

  1. After 330 the expedition's justification moved from Panhellenic revenge to Achaemenid succession, and at the Hyphasis to open-ended personal conquest. The army's refusal there was, on most scholars' reading, a real limit that Alexander's own troops imposed on him.
  2. The eastern campaign comes to us through competing filters: Ptolemy's self-promotion, Aristobulus' apologetics, the rival accounts of Nearchus and Onesicritus, and the moralising vulgate. Arrian's corrections at the Mallian town and in Carmania show the source problem directly.
  3. Myth and geography worked together. Heracles, Dionysus, Cyrus and Semiramis gave the campaign its meaning. Misnamed rivers and mountains shaped its decisions. Its surveys founded Hellenistic geography.

The prompt this answers

Give me an in-depth report on Alexander's campaigns from the death of Darius to the return to Susa, 330 to 324 BC. Cover: the pursuit of Bessus through Hyrcania, Aria, Drangiana and Arachosia, and the foundations (Alexandria in Areia, Kandahar); the crossing of the Hindu Kush; Bessus' capture and mutilation; Spitamenes' revolt and the two years of guerrilla war in Sogdiana, the massacre at the Polytimetus, the Branchidae, the city foundations as garrisons; the Sogdian Rock and the marriage to Roxane as policy; the descent into India: the alliance with Taxiles, the Aspasians and Assacenians, the storming of Aornos, Nysa; the Hydaspes campaign and Porus restored; the march to the Hyphasis and the mutiny, Coenus' speech; the river voyage down the Indus, the Mallian town and the near-fatal wound; Patala and the division of the force; the Gedrosian desert march (why he chose it, the losses, the Cyrus and Semiramis comparison); Nearchus' voyage along the Makran coast to the Persian Gulf and Onesicritus; Craterus' route through Arachosia; the Carmanian revel. Then the geography: what Alexander and his men thought the world looked like (the Jaxartes as the Tanais, the Indus as the Nile, the eastern Ocean) and how the campaign changed Greek geography. End with an assessment of the eastern campaign as the place where the expedition stopped being a war of revenge and became something else. Format: about 2500 words, headed sections, name sources, flag disagreements, end with a 10-question self-test with answers and the three ideas I must carry forward.
