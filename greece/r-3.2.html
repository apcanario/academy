01Birth: Alcmene, Amphitryon and the hatred of Hera

Heracles is a Perseid on both sides. Alcmene was the daughter of Electryon of Mycenae, son of Perseus; Amphitryon, her husband, was the son of Alcaeus, another son of Perseus. When Amphitryon killed Electryon, by accident in Apollodorus (Library 2.4.6), he was driven from the Argolid and took Alcmene to Thebes. She refused him until he had avenged her brothers, killed by the Taphians. While he was away Zeus came to her in Amphitryon's shape and, Apollodorus says, made the one night three times its length (2.4.8). Amphitryon returned the next day and lay with her too; the twins were Heracles, by Zeus, and Iphicles, a night younger, by the mortal husband. Hesiod's Shield of Heracles 1–56 tells the same story and already says Zeus intended the child as a defender of gods and men.

Hera's hatred begins before the birth. In Iliad 19.95–133 Zeus boasts that a man of his blood will be born that day to rule all his neighbours; Hera makes him swear it, holds back the birth goddesses from Alcmene and hurries on the labour of Sthenelus' wife, so that Eurystheus arrives in the seventh month and the oath falls to him. The child was first called Alcides; Apollodorus (2.4.12) says the Pythia renamed him Heracles, while Diodorus (4.10.1) explains the name as glory won through Hera's persecution. The infant suckled by an unwitting Hera, whose spilt milk became the Milky Way, is Hellenistic (Diodorus 4.9.6; the Catasterisms).

The serpents are the first labour in miniature. Pindar, Nemean 1.33–72, is the fullest early telling: Hera sends two snakes into the nursery, the infant strangles one in each hand, Amphitryon rushes in with drawn sword, and Teiresias prophesies the beasts he will kill, the war against the Giants and his final rest with Hebe. Theocritus, Idyll 24 makes him ten months old, Apollodorus (2.4.8) eight.

Where sources disagree. Apollodorus also records, from Pherecydes of Athens, that Amphitryon himself put the serpents in the cradle to learn which twin was his: Iphicles fled, Heracles stood his ground. The Hera version became canonical, but the Pherecydes version is older than most surviving texts and removes the goddess entirely.

02Youth, Thebes and the killing of the children

Apollodorus (2.4.9) lists the teachers: Amphitryon for the chariot, Autolycus for wrestling, Eurytus for archery, Castor for fighting in armour, Linus for the lyre. Heracles killed Linus with a blow of the lyre and was acquitted under a law of Rhadamanthys permitting the return of a blow. Sent to the cattle-farms, at eighteen he killed the lion of Cithaeron while staying with Thespius, who gave him his fifty daughters. Returning, he mutilated the heralds of Erginus of Orchomenus who were collecting tribute from Thebes, then killed Erginus in the war that followed, losing Amphitryon in the fighting. Creon gave him his daughter Megara.

The madness is where the biography forks. In Apollodorus (2.4.12) and Diodorus (4.11.1–2) Hera drives him mad soon after the marriage; he throws his children into the fire, is purified by Thespius and goes to Delphi, where the Pythia tells him to serve Eurystheus for twelve years and perform ten labours, after which he will be immortal. The madness causes the labours. Euripides reverses the order. In Heracles (c. 416 BC) the hero returns from Cerberus to find Lycus about to kill Megara and the children; he saves them, and only then do Iris and Lyssa descend at Hera's command (822–873). He kills Megara and the three boys thinking them Eurystheus' family, is stopped from killing Amphitryon by Athena's thrown stone, and wakes to be talked out of suicide by Theseus, whom he has just freed from Hades. The point is deliberate: madness strikes a man who has finished the civilising work and has nothing left to earn, and the rescue is by human friendship, not divine favour.

Where sources disagree. Madness before the labours makes them expiation; madness after (Euripides) makes them irrelevant to it. Most scholars treat the Euripidean placement as his own invention; Wilamowitz argued he preserved an older sequence. The children number three in Euripides and Apollodorus, five in Pherecydes, eight in Pindar, Isthmian 4.63–64. Megara is killed in Euripides but given to Iolaus in Apollodorus 2.6.1.

03The Twelve Labours

The canonical order is Apollodorus, Library 2.5.1–12; Diodorus 4.11–26 has the same twelve with the hind and boar, and the birds and stables, swapped, and Cerberus before the apples. The earliest complete set is not a text but the twelve metopes of the temple of Zeus at Olympia (c. 460 BC), where the Augean stables come last, a local compliment. The two lost epics that fixed the saga, Peisander of Camirus' and Panyassis' Heracleia, survive only in fragments. Apollodorus explains the number: the Pythia ordered ten, but Eurystheus disallowed the Hydra, because Iolaus helped, and the stables, because Heracles had demanded wages, so two more were added (2.5.11).

The pattern is visible once the table is laid out. The first six labours clear the Peloponnese of beasts: the hero as culture-bringer, the alexikakos who makes the land habitable. Labours seven to nine push to the edges of the Greek world. The last three are not about monsters but about the boundary of mortality: the west where the sun goes down, the garden holding the fruit of eternal life, and the house of Hades. Burkert read Geryon's cattle as a death-journey older than the heroic frame, a raid on the herds of the dead; whatever one makes of that, the ordering already makes the labours a descent and return, which is why the apotheosis follows so naturally. Hera reared the Hydra, gave the apples at her wedding and appears among the Amazons: the labours are her war against him by proxy.

Where sources disagree. Pausanias 2.37.4 says the Hydra had one head until Peisander gave it many. The apples: Apollodorus 2.5.11 reports both that Heracles sent Atlas and held up the sky, and (from Pherecydes) that he killed the serpent himself; Euripides, Heracles 394–399 and Sophocles, Women of Trachis 1099–1100 follow the second. The Cretan bull is either Europa's or the Minotaur's father; Apollodorus gives both. Heracles comes up from Hades at Troezen in Apollodorus, Taenarum or Heraclea Pontica elsewhere.

04The parerga

The side-deeds stitch Heracles into every other saga. The Argonauts. He joined the Argo but did not reach Colchis. Apollonius (Argonautica 1.1207–1357) leaves him in Mysia searching for Hylas, snatched by a nymph; Herodotus 7.193 has him put ashore at Aphetae to fetch water. Apollodorus 1.9.19 collects the variants: Herodorus says he never sailed, being enslaved to Omphale at the time; Demaratus says he reached Colchis. The reason for the ejection is structural: a crew that includes Heracles has no need of Jason.

Prometheus. Hesiod, Theogony 526–534 already has Heracles kill the eagle with Zeus' consent, to increase his son's glory. Apollodorus 2.5.11 sets it on the Caucasus during the apple quest and adds the unbinding; Prometheus in return told him to send Atlas for the apples, and Chiron, incurably wounded in the boar episode, gave up his immortality so that Prometheus might be freed (2.5.4). Antaeus. A Libyan son of Earth who forced strangers to wrestle and roofed Poseidon's temple with their skulls (Pindar, Isthmian 4.52–55); Heracles, finding that he drew strength from the ground, lifted him and broke him in the air (Apollodorus 2.5.11). Busiris. An Egyptian king who sacrificed a foreigner each year to end a drought; Heracles let himself be led to the altar and killed Busiris, his son and the herald (Apollodorus 2.5.11; Diodorus 4.27.2–3). The Pillars. On the Geryon journey he set up two pillars at the straits as the mark of how far he had gone. Pindar makes them the limit of human achievement, beyond which not even the wise can pass (Olympian 3.43–45; Nemean 3.21–23); Diodorus 4.18.5 reports both that he narrowed the strait to keep out sea-monsters and that he cut it open.

Where sources disagree. Herodotus 2.45 rejects the Busiris story outright: the Egyptians abhor human sacrifice, and the tale of Heracles slaughtering thousands at the altar is simple-minded. He also insists there are two Heracleses, an old Egyptian god and a Greek man, a problem he cannot resolve (2.43–44).

Add the Gigantomachy, where the oracle required a mortal ally (Pindar, Nemean 1.67–69; Apollodorus 1.6.1–2), and the help given to the Dorian king Aegimius against the Lapiths in return for a third of his land, which Apollodorus 2.7.7 and Diodorus 4.37.3–4 record as the origin of the Dorian claim through Hyllus.

05Omphale, Troy and Oechalia

After the labours Heracles won Iole at Oechalia in an archery contest and was refused her by Eurytus, his old teacher. When Iphitus, the one son who had taken his side, came to Tiryns searching for stolen cattle, Heracles in a second fit of madness threw him from the walls (Apollodorus 2.6.1–2). The Odyssey treats this as plain crime: he killed a guest in his own house with no regard for the gods or the table (21.22–30), and Iphitus' bow, passed to Odysseus, is the weapon of the suitors' slaughter. Sophocles, Women of Trachis 270–273 makes it an ambush. Disease followed, the Pythia refused to answer, and Heracles carried off the tripod and fought Apollo until Zeus parted them with a thunderbolt. The oracle then came: he must be sold into slavery and the price paid to Eurytus as blood-money. Hermes sold him to Omphale, queen of Lydia: three years in Apollodorus (2.6.3), one in Sophocles (Trach. 248–253). In Lydia he caught the Cercopes, killed Syleus, and fathered the son from whom Herodotus 1.7 derives the Lydian Heraclid dynasty. The exchange of clothes, Heracles in Omphale's gowns, is Hellenistic and Roman (Ovid, Heroides 9; Lucian, Dialogues of the Gods 13); the fifth century had him working, not spinning.

Troy. Laomedon had cheated him of the immortal horses promised for saving Hesione. Heracles sailed with eighteen ships in Apollodorus 2.6.4, six in Iliad 5.638–642, where Tlepolemus taunts Sarpedon that his father took Troy with a fraction of the present force. Laomedon and all his sons died except Podarces, ransomed by Hesione's veil and so renamed Priam, the bought one. Hera's storm then drove Heracles to Cos (Iliad 14.249–256). Peloponnesian campaigns followed: Augeas killed and the Olympic Games founded; Pylos taken from Neleus (Iliad 11.690–693); Hippocoon of Sparta destroyed and Tyndareus restored (Apollodorus 2.7.3), the first link between Heracles and the Spartan throne.

Oechalia. Years later, settled at Trachis after killing Eunomus, he marched on Oechalia, killed Eurytus and his sons and took Iole (Apollodorus 2.7.7). Sophocles sets Oechalia in Euboea (Trach. 74, 237); Homer's catalogue puts it in Thessaly (Iliad 2.730); Pausanias 4.2.2–3 admits nobody could agree.

06Deianira, Nessus and the robe

Deianira, daughter of Oeneus of Calydon, had been courted by the river-god Achelous; Heracles wrestled him for her, and her account of that terror opens Sophocles' play (Trach. 9–27). Crossing the Evenus, Heracles entrusted her to the centaur ferryman Nessus, who tried to rape her midstream; Heracles shot him from the bank with a Hydra-poisoned arrow. Dying, Nessus told her to keep the blood from his wound as a charm that would hold Heracles' love. Apollodorus 2.7.6 specifies seed mixed with blood; Sophocles, more decorous, has only the clotted blood round the arrow (572–577). The poison of the second labour has been waiting for decades.

Sophocles' tragedy is the version that matters. Heracles has been away fifteen months; Lichas brings home captives from Oechalia, among them a silent girl. Deianira learns she is Iole and that she is to share the house. She does not rage; she reasons that love is a disease no one chooses, then reaches for the charm, anoints a robe and sends it to Heracles, who is sacrificing to Zeus at Cape Cenaeum. The wisp of wool she used, left in the sun, crumbles and foams (695–704); she knows. Hyllus arrives to describe the robe eating into his father's flesh and Lichas hurled onto the rocks, and curses his mother. Deianira kills herself with a sword on the marriage bed (930–931). Heracles is carried in raving; when he learns it was Nessus' blood he understands the old oracle that he would die by nothing living, and becomes calm. He orders Hyllus to carry him to Oeta and burn him, and to marry Iole. The play's last line reads the whole catastrophe as the will of Zeus.

Where sources disagree. Deianira dies by the sword in Sophocles but hangs herself in Apollodorus 2.7.7 and Diodorus 4.38.3. Modern critics divide on whose play it is: for some she is the true subject and the title names the women who witness her; for others Heracles' late entrance is the point. The date is unknown, with guesses from the 450s to the 420s.

07The pyre on Oeta, the apotheosis and Hebe

The pyre on Oeta above Trachis is a real place: excavation on the summit found an ash altar with Geometric and Archaic offerings and later inscriptions to Heracles, a cult older than any literary account. In Apollodorus 2.7.7 nobody will light the pyre until Poeas does so and receives the bow; Sophocles, Philoctetes 801–803 gives it to Philoctetes; Diodorus 4.38.4–5 to Iolaus. All agree that a cloud carried him up with thunder, and Diodorus adds (4.39.1) that no bones were found. Heracles was reconciled with Hera, who in Diodorus 4.39.2 adopted him by a ritual rebirth, and married Hebe, Youth, by whom he had Alexiares and Anicetus (Apollodorus 2.7.7).

The apotheosis is old. Hesiod, Theogony 950–955 says Heracles, his grievous labours completed, made Hebe his wife on Olympus and lives untroubled and unaging among the immortals. Pindar closes Nemean 1 with Teiresias prophesying the same, and in Nemean 3.22 calls him heros theos, hero-god, the only figure of Greek myth who is fully both. Herodotus 2.44 approves of the Greeks who keep two cults, sacrificing to one Heracles as an Olympian and offering to the other as a dead hero.

Where sources disagree. Homer is the awkward witness. In Iliad 18.117–119 Achilles says not even Heracles escaped death, but was overcome by fate and Hera's anger. In Odyssey 11.601–627 Odysseus meets his shade in Hades, "but he himself feasts with the immortal gods". Ancient critics already flagged lines 602–604 as an insertion, attributed by some to Onomacritus under the Peisistratids. Whether the split-Heracles compromise is Homeric or sixth-century Athenian remains disputed.

08The politics: Heracleidae, Sparta, Macedon and Athens

Heracles left no city. He was born at Thebes, belonged by blood to Argos, served at Tiryns, died in Thessaly, and his children were refugees. That is precisely what made him usable: an ancestor without a homeland gave every claimant the same thing, a right that pre-dated the current occupants.

The Heracleidae. After his death Eurystheus pursued the children. They fled to Athens, where Demophon took them in; Eurystheus marched on Attica, Macaria gave her life for the victory, and Eurystheus was killed (Euripides, Children of Heracles; Apollodorus 2.8.1). Hyllus then tried to re-enter the Peloponnese, misread an oracle, and was killed at the Isthmus by Echemus of Tegea (Herodotus 9.26). The great-grandsons, Temenus, Cresphontes and Aristodemus, returned with the Dorians, crossed from Naupactus and drew lots: Argos to Temenus, Messenia to Cresphontes, Sparta to the twin sons of the dead Aristodemus, Procles and Eurysthenes, founders of the two royal houses (Apollodorus 2.8.2–5; Diodorus 4.57–58; Pausanias 3.1.5–6). This is the Greeks' own explanation of what we call the Dorian migration; Thucydides 1.12.3 dates it eighty years after Troy and calls it the Dorians with the Heracleidae. The two names are not interchangeable: the Dorians are the people, the Heracleidae their kings, and the link between them is Aegimius' adoption of Hyllus, from whom one of the three Dorian tribes, the Hylleis, took its name.

Sparta. This explains an otherwise strange fact: the two kings were not, in their own view, Dorians. Barred from the Athenian Acropolis as a Dorian, Cleomenes answered, "I am no Dorian but an Achaean" (Herodotus 5.72). Herodotus gives Leonidas' pedigree back to Heracles in twenty generations (7.204) and Leotychides' in 8.131. Tyrtaeus (fr. 2 West), in the seventh century, already sings that Zeus gave this city to the Heracleidae. The ideology is one of restoration: Heracles had held the Peloponnese by right as Zeus' son and been cheated by Eurystheus; his descendants took back their own. It also gave Sparta a title to the whole peninsula, on which Spartan foreign policy drew for three centuries, and the story of Heracles restoring Tyndareus meant the hero had already decided once who ruled Sparta.

Macedon. The Argead kings traced themselves to Temenus of Argos and so to Heracles. Herodotus 8.137–139 tells how Perdiccas came from Argos to Macedonia, and how Alexander I, challenged at Olympia as a barbarian, proved his Argive descent and was admitted to the stadium (5.22). Heracles in the lion-scalp on nearly every silver tetradrachm of Alexander the Great made the ancestor visible across the empire. Alexander besieged Tyre for seven months when refused the right to sacrifice to Heracles-Melqart (Arrian 2.15.7–16.8), stormed Aornos because Heracles was said to have failed there (Arrian 4.28.1–4), and named a son Heracles. The Ptolemies then claimed the same ancestor through Alexander (Theocritus, Idyll 17.26–27). For a Macedonian king Heracles did three things: proved Greekness to Greeks who doubted it, justified rule by conquest, and offered a precedent for a mortal becoming a god.

Athens. Athens claimed no descent but kept him anyway, and in the sixth century he appears on Attic vases more often than any other figure, far more than Theseus. The ties were specific. Heracles was the first foreigner initiated at Eleusis, and the Lesser Mysteries at Agrae were said to have been founded for his purification (Apollodorus 2.5.12; Diodorus 4.14.3). He freed Theseus from Hades, and in Euripides' Heracles 1322–1337 Theseus repays him with Athenian land and cult; Plutarch, Theseus 35.2–3 says Theseus dedicated to Heracles all his own sanctuaries but four. The Athenians sheltered his children, and cited that service at Plataea (Herodotus 9.27). At Marathon the army camped in his precinct, and after the battle in his precinct at Cynosarges (Herodotus 6.108, 6.116). For a democracy he offered a hero who belonged to no one's aristocracy, who worked and suffered, and who had been Athens' guest.

Where sources disagree. John Boardman argued (1972–1989) that the Peisistratids adopted Heracles as personal propaganda, with his introduction to Olympus mirroring Peisistratus' return to Athens in a chariot beside a woman dressed as Athena (Herodotus 1.60), and that the fall in Heracles scenes and rise of Theseus after 510 marks the democracy's rebranding. R. M. Cook and others replied that Heracles was popular everywhere in sixth-century art and that painters followed buyers, not tyrants. The shift to Theseus after 510 is real; its cause is uncertain.

09Self-test

  1. Why was Eurystheus, and not Heracles, born to rule all his neighbours, according to Homer?
    Answer

    In Iliad 19.95–133 Zeus swore that a man of his blood born that day would rule all his neighbours; Hera delayed Alcmene's labour and hastened Eurystheus' birth in the seventh month, so the oath fell to him.

  2. In Apollodorus, does the madness come before or after the labours, and what difference does it make?
    Answer

    Before: the killing of the children leads to Delphi and the sentence of service to Eurystheus, so the labours are expiation. Euripides puts it after, so the labours are irrelevant to it: the madness strikes a man who has nothing left to earn.

  3. Why twelve labours when the Pythia ordered ten?
    Answer

    Eurystheus disallowed the Hydra (Iolaus helped) and the Augean stables (Heracles demanded wages), so two more were set (Apollodorus 2.5.11).

  4. Which three labours are about mortality rather than monsters, and why?
    Answer

    Geryon's cattle (the far west, the sunset), the apples of the Hesperides (the fruit of immortality) and Cerberus (Hades itself). Each takes him to a boundary of death and back, preparing the apotheosis.

  5. Give two incompatible ancient accounts of why Heracles did not reach Colchis with the Argonauts.
    Answer

    Apollonius: left in Mysia searching for Hylas. Herodotus 7.193: put ashore at Aphetae to fetch water. Herodorus: he never sailed, being enslaved to Omphale at the time.

  6. What was the Hydra's connection to Heracles' death?
    Answer

    He dipped his arrows in its venom; the arrow that killed Nessus carried it, Nessus' poisoned blood became Deianira's love-charm, and the robe anointed with it burned Heracles alive.

  7. How does Deianira die in Sophocles, and how in Apollodorus?
    Answer

    Sophocles, Women of Trachis 930–931: by the sword, on the marriage bed. Apollodorus 2.7.7 and Diodorus: she hangs herself.

  8. What does Herodotus 2.44 say about the Greek worship of Heracles?
    Answer

    That the Greeks who keep two cults act rightly: sacrificing to one Heracles as an immortal Olympian and offering to the other as a hero, that is, a dead man.

  9. Why could Cleomenes of Sparta say "I am no Dorian but an Achaean"?
    Answer

    The Spartan kings descended from Heracles through the Heracleidae, not from the Dorian people they led; the Dorians were the followers whom Aegimius' adoption of Hyllus had bound to the Heraclid line.

  10. Name three concrete Athenian ties to Heracles despite the absence of any claim of descent.
    Answer

    Any three of: initiation at Eleusis and the founding of the Lesser Mysteries; the shelter given to the Heracleidae; Theseus' dedication of his shrines to Heracles (Plutarch, Theseus 35); the Athenian camp in his Marathon precinct (Herodotus 6.108); his precinct at Cynosarges (Herodotus 6.116); his rescue of Theseus from Hades.

10Carry forward

  1. Heracles is the only Greek figure who is both hero and god, heros theos, and the Greeks knew the contradiction: the split between the Olympian bridegroom of Hebe and the shade in Hades (Odyssey 11) is the model for every later argument about whether a man can become divine, from Alexander to the Hellenistic kings.
  2. The labours read as a sequence: clearing the Peloponnese, pushing to the edges of the Greek world, then three assaults on the frontier of death. Keep that shape; the colonising Heracles of the western Greeks and the Heracles of Stoic moral philosophy both grow from it.
  3. Descent from Heracles is a political instrument, not a tribal fact. The Heracleidae are kings, the Dorians a people, and the "return" legitimates rule as restoration rather than conquest; Sparta, Macedon and the Ptolemies all drew on it, while Athens shows that a city could own the hero through cult and hospitality without owning his blood.

The prompt this answers

Give me an in-depth report on Heracles, the complete life. Cover: birth (Alcmene, Amphitryon, Hera's hatred, the serpents), youth, the madness and the killing of his children; the Twelve Labours in the canonical order with the location, the monster or task, and what each labour is "about"; the parerga (side adventures: the Argonauts, Prometheus, Antaeus, Busiris, the Pillars); the servitude to Omphale, the sack of Troy and Oechalia; Deianira, Nessus and the poisoned robe; the pyre on Oeta and the apotheosis; his marriage to Hebe. Then the politics: why Dorian Sparta and the Macedonian kings claimed descent from him, the Heracleidae, and why Athens kept him too. Sources: Apollodorus, Diodorus, Euripides' Heracles, Sophocles' Women of Trachis, Pindar. Format: about 3000 words, headed sections, flag disagreements, end with a 10-question self-test with answers and the three ideas I must carry forward.
