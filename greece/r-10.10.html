01Finding Hisarlik: Calvert, Schliemann and the rival sites

The Greek town of Ilion stood on the mound now called Hisarlik ("place of fortresses"), about 6 kilometres from the Dardanelles, and showed visitors the relics of Priam's city. Xerxes sacrificed a thousand oxen to Athena of Ilion (Herodotus 7.43); Alexander did the same in 334 BC. The doubt was ancient too. Demetrius of Scepsis, followed by Strabo (Geography 13.1.25–27, 13.1.35), placed Homer's Troy at the "Village of the Ilians" inland, because the plain before Ilion seemed too young and small for the Iliad's battles.

By the late eighteenth century the favourite candidate was Bunarbashi (Pınarbaşı), whose springs travellers matched to Iliad 22.147–56. Charles Maclaren argued for Hisarlik in 1822, but the groundwork was done by Frank Calvert, a British consular agent whose family owned part of the mound. Calvert dug trial trenches in 1863–65 and in 1868 persuaded Heinrich Schliemann, then touring the Troad with Bunarbashi in mind, that Hisarlik was the place. Schliemann's books played down Calvert's part; modern histories give him the credit.

Where sources disagree. Strabo and Demetrius denied that Ilion was Homer's Troy; the Ilians insisted it was. Modern archaeology has sided with the Ilians, but Strabo's point about the plain has been partly answered by geology: coring by John Kraft and colleagues showed that the Karamenderes delta has silted up since the Bronze Age, so the shoreline lay much closer to the citadel in the late second millennium.

02Schliemann's trench and Priam's Treasure

Schliemann dug without a permit in 1870 and in proper campaigns in 1871, 1872 and 1873, returning in 1878–79, 1882 and 1890. Believing Homer's Troy lay at the bottom, he drove a great north–south trench to bedrock, cutting away much of the Late Bronze Age citadel that would later matter most. He identified Troy II, a burnt Early Bronze Age citadel, as Priam's city, its ramp as the Scaean Gate. He was wrong by more than a thousand years, and from 1882 his architect Wilhelm Dörpfeld began to correct him.

In 1873, beside the Troy II wall, Schliemann found the hoard he named Priam's Treasure: copper and silver vessels, a gold sauceboat, silver ingots, thousands of gold beads and two elaborate diadems. Photographs of Sophia Schliemann wearing the "Jewels of Helen" made it world-famous. He smuggled it to Athens and in 1881 gave his Trojan collection to Berlin. The treasure disappeared in 1945. Reports in 1991 traced it to Moscow, taken by a Soviet trophy brigade, and on 25 August 1993 the Russian culture minister Yevgenii Sidorov admitted it was there; the Pushkin Museum, which holds the gold, put it on display in 1996, and the Hermitage holds further material. Germany and Turkey both have claims; Russia treats it as compensation for wartime losses under its 1998 law on displaced cultural property.

The treasure belongs to Troy II, in the mid-third millennium BC (roughly 2550–2250 BC). It has nothing to do with Priam on any chronology.

Where sources disagree. Schliemann's account of the find is unreliable. He wrote that Sophia carried the objects away in her shawl, yet his own letters show she was in Athens at the time. William Calder ("Schliemann on Schliemann", Greek, Roman and Byzantine Studies 13, 1972) opened the case against Schliemann's own accounts, and David Traill (Schliemann of Troy: Treasure and Deceit, 1995) argued that Schliemann put the "treasure" together from several findspots, perhaps adding purchased pieces. Donald Easton ("Schliemann's mendacity: a false trail?", Antiquity 58, 1984; "Priam's gold: the full story", Anatolian Studies 44, 1994) and others accept that the core is a genuine Troy II hoard or hoards, with Schliemann dramatising the circumstances. The objects are genuinely Early Bronze Age; how they were found remains uncertain.

03The nine cities

Dörpfeld divided the mound into nine main settlements; Carl Blegen's Cincinnati team (1932–38) split them into some fifty phases. Dates approximately follow Korfmann's Tübingen chronology; every boundary is debated.

CityApproximate dateKey features
Troy Ic. 2900–2550 BCSmall walled citadel about 90 m across; long megaron houses; handmade dark pottery
Troy IIc. 2550–2250 BCLarger citadel, monumental ramped gate, great megaron; wheel-made pottery; Priam's Treasure; ended in fire
Troy IIIc. 2250–2200 BCCrowded, poorer settlement rebuilt on Troy II; much continuity
Troy IVc. 2200–1900 BCLarger area, domed ovens; Anatolian rather than Aegean connections
Troy Vc. 1900–1750 BCNeater houses, new wall circuit; transition to the Middle Bronze Age
Troy VIc. 1750–1300 BCNew culture; great limestone walls with towers and gates; grey wheel-made ware, horse bones, imported Mycenaean pottery; lower city; end (VIh) in earthquake or attack
Troy VIIa (VIi)c. 1300–1180 BCSame people rebuild; small crowded houses against the walls, storage jars sunk into floors; destroyed by fire
Troy VIIbc. 1180–950 BCReoccupation; handmade "knobbed ware" with Balkan links (VIIb2); Luwian seal; gradual decline
Troy VIIIc. 950–85 BCGreek Ilion; sanctuary and later temple of Athena; visits by Xerxes and Alexander; sacked by Fimbria in 85 BC
Troy IX85 BC–c. AD 500Roman Ilium, rebuilt and favoured by Augustus as the city of Aeneas; declined in late antiquity

Only two layers are serious candidates for Homer's city: the end of Troy VI and the end of Troy VIIa. Both fall in the Late Bronze Age, when Mycenaean Greece was at its height and the Hittites were the great power of Anatolia.

04Troy VI and Troy VIIa: Dörpfeld, Blegen, Korfmann

Dörpfeld's walls

In 1893–94 Dörpfeld exposed the walls of Troy VI on the south and east, beyond Schliemann's trench: dressed limestone faces that slope inwards, with shallow vertical offsets every nine or ten metres, a mudbrick superstructure, massive towers and several gates. The citadel, about 200 metres across, held large free-standing houses on terraces. Dörpfeld declared Troy VI Priam's city, because imported Mycenaean pottery lay in its levels and none in Troy II: the citadel that traded with the Greeks of the Late Bronze Age was the one the Greek story could be about. Andromache's warning that the wall is weakest by the fig tree (Iliad 6.433–39) has been matched to a weaker western stretch, but such matches prove little.

Blegen's earthquake and fire

Blegen concluded that Troy VIh ended by earthquake, not enemy action: toppled walls, shifted masonry, no general burning. Survivors built Troy VIIa among the ruins: small, packed houses against the citadel wall, with large pithoi sunk into the floors, which Blegen read as a population sheltering and storing food against a siege. Troy VIIa ended in a violent fire. Blegen found human remains in the streets and a bronze arrowhead of a type usually called Aegean; Korfmann's team later added a few more arrowheads and spearheads, piles of sling-stones and a partly burnt skeleton of a girl. Blegen named VIIa as Priam's Troy and dated its fall to about 1250 BC.

Where sources disagree. The earthquake that ended Troy VI is Blegen's reading (Troy III, 1953: leaning walls, fallen masonry, no burning); Penelope Mountjoy (Studia Troica 9, 1999) and others note that the evidence does not exclude human destruction following it, or both. The date of VIIa's fall has moved: on Mountjoy's study of the Mycenaean pottery it falls around 1190–1180 BC, after many Mycenaean palaces had already burnt. That is awkward for a grand expedition led from Mycenae. The arrowheads are too few to identify an attacker; the fire could be accidental, internal conflict, or raiders from anywhere.

Korfmann's lower city and the Kolb controversy

Manfred Korfmann of Tübingen excavated from 1988 until his death in 2005. Magnetometer survey revealed a ditch cut into bedrock about 400 metres south of the citadel, and later a second ditch further out. Korfmann argued that Troy VI had a lower city, the whole covering perhaps 27 hectares with several thousand inhabitants, and presented Troy as a regional capital and trading centre between the Aegean, the Black Sea and Anatolia. He also proposed renaming VIIa as VIi to stress continuity.

In 2001 the Tübingen ancient historian Frank Kolb attacked these claims: the lower city was barely attested by buildings, the ditch was no fortification, the reconstruction in the exhibition "Troia: Traum und Wirklichkeit" showed a dense town the evidence did not support, and large-scale trade was unproven. Troy, for him, was a modest provincial seat. The quarrel became public and personal; a symposium in Tübingen in February 2002 settled nothing.

Where sources disagree. Kolb and Dieter Hertel (Hertel, Troia: Archäologie, Geschichte, Mythos, 2001; Hertel and Kolb, "Troy in clearer perspective", Anatolian Studies 53, 2003) held that the plateau south of the citadel was all but empty before Hellenistic times and the population at most a few thousand; Donald Easton, J. David Hawkins, Andrew and Susan Sherratt ("Troy in recent perspective", Anatolian Studies 52, 2002) answered that a lower settlement of some kind is accepted by everyone and that the truth lies nearer Korfmann's end. Later work under Ernst Pernicka and Peter Jablonka uncovered Late Bronze Age houses in the lower city, and most archaeologists now accept that one existed. Its density, and therefore the population, remains uncertain. On trade, Kolb is partly right: imported Mycenaean pottery is only a small share of Troy VI ceramics, and direct evidence for Black Sea traffic in the Bronze Age is thin.

05Troy in the Hittite texts

The strongest evidence that Troy mattered comes from the archives at Hattusa. Emil Forrer proposed in 1924 that Ahhiyawa was Homer's Achaioi and Wilusa was Ilios (originally Wilios); Ferdinand Sommer rejected both in 1932. In 1998 J. David Hawkins's reading of the Karabel inscription fixed Mira and the Seha River Land, leaving Wilusa in or near the Troad. Most specialists now accept both equations; the texts are collected in Gary Beckman, Trevor Bryce and Eric Cline, The Ahhiyawa Texts (2011).

Wilusa and Alaksandu

Wilusiya and Taruisa (perhaps Troia) appear among twenty-two places in the Assuwa coalition that Tudhaliya I/II defeated in the late fifteenth century. Around 1280 BC Muwatalli II made a treaty (CTH 76) with Alaksandu of Wilusa. It says Wilusa had been loyal to Hatti since early times, names an earlier king Kukkunni, binds Alaksandu to send troops for Hittite wars, and lists Wilusa's gods, including a deity whose name has been restored as Appaliunas and an underground watercourse (KASKAL.KUR). Korfmann's team then found a rock-cut water system under the edge of the Troy citadel. Alaksandu looks very like Alexandros, the second name of Paris.

Where sources disagree. Appaliunas is a partly restored reading (Beckman, Hittite Diplomatic Texts, 2nd ed. 1999, no. 13 prints the restoration), so the claim, pressed by Latacz (Troy and Homer, 2004), that it is Apollo rests on a damaged word. Alaksandu as a Greek name at Wilusa is striking but may reflect contact rather than a Greek dynasty, and no specialist identifies him with Homer's Paris. Taruisa as Troia is linguistically weaker than Wilusa as Ilios. The Luwian-language question (Watkins on the Trojans' speech) belongs to module 10.3.

Ahhiyawa, Piyamaradu and Millawanda

About thirty Hittite texts mention Ahhiyawa. Its king is addressed as a "brother", a great king, and its Anatolian foothold was Millawanda (Milawata), almost certainly Miletus, where a Mycenaean settlement has been excavated. It worked through clients and adventurers, above all Piyamaradu, a western Anatolian prince who raided Hittite vassals for decades with Ahhiyawan backing.

  • The Manapa-Tarhunta letter (CTH 191), probably to Muwatalli II from the vassal king of the Seha River Land, reports that Hittite troops have gone to attack Wilusa and that Piyamaradu has raided Lazpa (Lesbos), with men from there going over to Atpa, Piyamaradu's son-in-law, in Millawanda. Why Hittite troops were moving against Wilusa is not clear: to put down a revolt, or to drive off attackers.
  • The Tawagalawa letter (CTH 181), probably from Hattusili III around 1250 BC to an unnamed king of Ahhiyawa, complains that Piyamaradu escaped by ship from Millawanda and asks the Ahhiyawan king to hand him over or rein him in. It mentions Tawagalawa, the addressee's brother (perhaps Greek Etewoklewes, Eteocles). It also refers to "the matter of Wilusa" over which the two kings had been hostile and have now made peace.
  • The Milawata letter (CTH 182), probably from Tudhaliya IV to Tarkasnawa of Mira late in the thirteenth century, shows Millawanda now under Hittite control and concerns Walmu, a king of Wilusa who had been driven out; the Hittite king wants him restored as a vassal.

Tudhaliya IV's treaty with Sausgamuwa of Amurru lists the king of Ahhiyawa among the great kings and then strikes the name out, perhaps marking the end of Ahhiyawan power in Anatolia. The cumulative case is real: a Mycenaean power was active in western Anatolia for two centuries; Wilusa, a Hittite vassal on its edge, was attacked at least once, lost a king, and caused hostility between Hatti and Ahhiyawa.

The Luwian seal

In 1995 Korfmann's team found a small bronze biconvex seal in a Troy VIIb2 context of the twelfth century BC. It carries Luwian hieroglyphs, read by Hawkins and Donald Easton (1996) as a man's name with the title "scribe" on one side and a woman's name on the other. It is the only Bronze Age writing yet found at Troy. It shows someone at Troy used Anatolian writing, not that Trojans spoke Luwian, and its context postdates both candidate destructions. No archive has been found at Troy, so Wilusa's own side is missing.

06Wind, current and the economics of Troy

Homer's stock epithet for the city, Ilios ēnemoessa, "windy Ilios", is accurate. Through most of the summer sailing season a strong north-easterly wind blows down the Dardanelles, and a surface current flows out of the Sea of Marmara towards the Aegean at one to three knots. A Bronze Age square-rigged ship, poor at sailing into the wind, could wait days or weeks for a southerly. Korfmann argued that ships anchored in Beşik Bay, south-west of Troy, where he excavated a Late Bronze Age cemetery with Mycenaean pottery, and that Troy grew rich by supplying, piloting or taxing them. The citadel also commanded the crossing between Europe and Asia.

Wealth also came from horses (horse bones appear in Troy VI; Homer's Trojans are "horse-tamers"), textiles (spindle whorls abound) and the plain's grain and fish. A strategic site on a contested frontier needs no stolen queen to explain a war; Herodotus already reported Persian and Phoenician versions that turned the abductions into tit-for-tat raiding (Herodotus 1.1–5).

07Dating the war and the scholarly positions

Ancient and archaeological dates

Herodotus put the war rather more than 800 years before his time (2.145), around 1250 BC. The Parian Marble gives 1209/8, Duris of Samos 1334/3. Eratosthenes' 1184/3 became canonical. All rest on genealogies counted at fixed years per generation, not records. The archaeological windows are the end of Troy VIh around 1300 and the end of VIIa around 1190–1180 on current pottery dating. Eratosthenes' figure lands close to the second, but given his method that is probably coincidence.

Where sources disagree. Blegen's early date for VIIa (c. 1250) suited Herodotus and a Mycenaean expedition before the palaces fell. The later date (c. 1180) suits Eratosthenes but puts the destruction in the disorder of the collapse, when attackers were more likely to be refugees, raiders or northern newcomers than a coalition from Mycenae. The Hittite texts point to trouble over Wilusa in the early-to-mid thirteenth century, which fits neither destruction exactly.

Finley's scepticism

Moses Finley, in The World of Odysseus (1954) and the 1964 Journal of Hellenic Studies debate "The Trojan War" with John Caskey, Geoffrey Kirk and Denys Page, argued that Homer's society belongs to the tenth and ninth centuries, that oral tradition preserves little reliable over five centuries, and that the war should be removed from Greek history and treated as myth. His dating rested on institutions rather than objects: a world of gift exchange and the household (oikos), with no polis, no coinage and no writing, fits the Dark Age and not the palaces, so the poems could not be carrying a Bronze Age memory intact. That is the argument Latacz had to answer.

Latacz's maximalism

Joachim Latacz, in Troia und Homer (2001; English Troy and Homer, 2004), argued the reverse. With Wilusa equal to Ilios and Ahhiyawa equal to the Achaeans, he held that the Catalogue of Ships (Iliad 2.494–759) preserves a Mycenaean political map, that the hexameter tradition reached back into the Bronze Age, and that the story's core is historical: Greeks fought for Wilusa. Page, in History and the Homeric Iliad (1959), had made an earlier version of this case.

The middle ground

Most current specialists, for example Trevor Bryce in The Trojans and Their Neighbours (2006) and Eric Cline in The Trojan War: A Very Short Introduction (2013), take a middle position. The setting is historical: Troy was a substantial Late Bronze Age city, Achaeans were active in western Anatolia, and Wilusa was fought over. Whether any single event lies behind the Iliad cannot be shown, and there may have been several "Trojan Wars". Homer himself knows an earlier sack, by Heracles in the reign of Laomedon (Iliad 5.640–42).

What a historical kernel might look like

A plausible kernel is a series of conflicts in the thirteenth and early twelfth centuries: raids by Ahhiyawan lords and allies such as Piyamaradu, attacks on Wilusa, a dethroned king, perhaps a sack, with the Hittites as distant overlord. There is no evidence of a ten-year siege, a coalition of 1,186 ships under one high king, a war fought over one woman, or Greek-speaking Trojans who worship Greek gods. The poem's material world is also mixed: the boar's-tusk helmet (Iliad 10.261–65) and Ajax's tower shield are Bronze Age survivals, while cremation, iron and much of the social world belong to later centuries. Homer's Troy is a composite built up over five hundred years of performance.

08What archaeology can and cannot confirm about a poem

Archaeology at Hisarlik has confirmed that the place existed where tradition put it, that it was large, walled and prosperous in the Late Bronze Age, that it had Mycenaean contacts, and that it was destroyed around 1300 and again around 1180. With the Hittite texts it shows Wilusa was a cause of dispute between great kings. That is more than Finley allowed.

What it cannot do is confirm events, agents or motives. A burnt layer does not record who lit it or why, and a few arrowheads do not identify an army. Even the texts name Wilusa but not Priam, Hector or Agamemnon. Agreement between poem and site is also partly circular: Schliemann's Troy II, Dörpfeld's VI and Blegen's VIIa were each "confirmed" by the excavator who wanted them. The lasting lesson is that oral poetry keeps names, places and a sense of former greatness much longer than it keeps narrative facts. Homer remembered that Ilios was rich, walled and windy and that Achaeans once fought there; the rest of the story is epic.

09Self-test

  1. Who first dug at Hisarlik and convinced Schliemann it was Troy, and what rival site did many earlier travellers favour?
    Answer

    Frank Calvert, the British consular agent whose family owned part of the mound, dug trial trenches in 1863–65 and persuaded Schliemann in 1868. Many earlier travellers favoured Bunarbashi (Pınarbaşı), on the strength of its springs.

  2. Which ancient authors denied that Greek Ilion was Homer's Troy, and on what grounds?
    Answer

    Demetrius of Scepsis, followed by Strabo (Geography 13.1), placed Homer's Troy at the "Village of the Ilians", partly because the plain in front of Ilion seemed too new and small. Coring has since shown that the coastline was much closer to the mound in the Bronze Age.

  3. To which city does Priam's Treasure belong, roughly when, and where is it now?
    Answer

    Troy II, c. 2550–2250 BC, more than a thousand years before any Trojan War. Taken from Berlin by Soviet forces in 1945, the gold is in the Pushkin Museum in Moscow, with other material in the Hermitage.

  4. What did Blegen see as the cause of Troy VIh's end, and why did he think Troy VIIa was under siege?
    Answer

    An earthquake (collapsed masonry, no general fire). In VIIa he found small crowded houses against the walls with storage pithoi sunk into the floors, which he read as a population sheltering inside the citadel and stockpiling food. VIIa then burnt, with human remains and at least one arrowhead in the debris.

  5. Why is the newer date for the destruction of Troy VIIa awkward for a Mycenaean expedition?
    Answer

    Mountjoy's pottery dating puts it around 1190–1180 BC, after many Mycenaean palaces had already been destroyed, so a coalition led from Mycenae is unlikely at that point.

  6. What was Korfmann's main new claim about Troy VI, and what were Kolb's objections?
    Answer

    Korfmann argued from a bedrock-cut ditch found by magnetometry that Troy VI had a large lower city (the whole site perhaps 27 hectares, with several thousand people) and was a regional trading centre. Kolb said the lower city was barely attested, the ditch was not a fortification, the exhibition reconstruction was misleading and the trade was unproven.

  7. What does the Alaksandu treaty tell us, and which Homeric parallel does it suggest?
    Answer

    Around 1280 BC Muwatalli II bound Alaksandu of Wilusa as a loyal vassal who had to supply troops; it lists Wilusa's gods, perhaps including Appaliunas and an underground watercourse. The name recalls Alexandros, the other name of Paris.

  8. What is the significance of the Tawagalawa letter for the Trojan question?
    Answer

    Probably from Hattusili III to the king of Ahhiyawa, whom he treats as an equal "brother", it concerns the renegade Piyamaradu and refers to the matter of Wilusa over which the two had been hostile and have made peace, the nearest text to a Hittite-Ahhiyawan dispute over Troy.

  9. Why did the Dardanelles make Troy economically important?
    Answer

    Prevailing north-easterly winds and an outward current made the strait hard to enter under sail, so ships may have waited in Beşik Bay, and Troy could profit from servicing or taxing them, as well as from controlling the crossing to Europe.

  10. Contrast Finley's and Latacz's positions in one sentence each.
    Answer

    Finley held that Homeric society is post-Mycenaean and that the war should be treated as myth rather than history. Latacz held that the Hittite texts and the Catalogue of Ships show a historical Mycenaean conflict over Wilusa behind the epic.

10Carry forward

  1. Troy (Hisarlik, Wilusa) was a real and substantial Late Bronze Age city on a contested frontier between the Hittite and Mycenaean worlds, and the Hittite texts show it was fought over.
  2. No layer, text or object confirms the Iliad's story; destructions around 1300 and 1180 BC have no named attackers, and Homer's Troy is a composite built up over centuries of oral poetry.
  3. Archaeology can confirm places, scale, dates and contacts but not agents, motives or plots; oral epic keeps names and a sense of former greatness much longer than it keeps events.

Cover: the identification of Hisarlik (Calvert, Schliemann), Schliemann's excavation and its destructions, Priam's Treasure and its true date and modern whereabouts; Dörpfeld and Troy VI, Blegen and Troy VIIa, Korfmann and the lower city, and the Korfmann-Kolb controversy about Troy's size and importance; the nine cities and their dates, the Troy VI walls and the earthquake, the Troy VIIa destruction by fire and the arrowheads; the Hittite texts in detail: Wilusa, Alaksandu, the Ahhiyawa letters, the Tawagalawa letter, Piyamaradu, Milawata, the Manapa-Tarhunta letter, and the case for a Mycenaean-Hittite conflict over western Anatolia; the Luwian seal from Troy; the Dardanelles winds and the economic logic of a Troy; the dating problem (Eratosthenes' 1184, Herodotus' 1250, the archaeological windows); the positions: Finley's scepticism, Latacz's maximalism, the middle ground; what a historical kernel might look like and how different it would be from Homer. End with the methodological lesson: what archaeology can and cannot confirm about a poem. Format: about 2500 words, headed sections, name sources, flag disagreements, end with a 10-question self-test with answers and the three ideas I must carry forward.
