First contact: Pyrrhus and the Illyrian wars (280–219)

Pyrrhus in Italy

Rome met the Hellenistic world in southern Italy, not Greece. In 281 Tarentum invited Pyrrhus of Epirus, a cousin of Alexander and the ablest general of his generation, to defend it against Rome. He landed in 280 with some 25,000 men and twenty elephants. He beat the legions at Heraclea (280) and Asculum (279), but each victory cost veterans he could not replace while Rome simply levied new legions. Plutarch, Pyrrhus 21, preserves the remark that gave us "Pyrrhic victory":

If we win one more battle against the Romans, we shall be utterly ruined.

After a Sicilian detour against Carthage (278–276) he returned, was checked at Beneventum (275) and sailed home; Tarentum fell in 272. The fuller accounts are Plutarch, Pyrrhus 13–26, and Appian, Samnite Wars 10–12. The lesson stood: a Hellenistic professional army could win battles against Rome and still lose the war to Roman manpower.

The Illyrian wars

Rome's first armed crossing of the Adriatic came in 229, against Queen Teuta of Illyria, whose fleets had been raiding Italian shipping. Polybius 2.2–12 presents the war as a police action against piracy; Rome crushed Teuta, installed Demetrius of Pharos as a client, and sent embassies to the Greek leagues announcing what it had done; Demetrius later switched to Macedon and was expelled in a second war (219), fleeing to Philip V.

Where sources disagree. Polybius' piracy explanation has been doubted. Maurice Holleaux (1921) held that Rome had no eastern policy before 200; W. V. Harris (War and Imperialism, 1979) saw habitual aggression; Erich Gruen (1984) a reactive Rome; Arthur Eckstein (2006) an anarchic interstate system in which every power behaved like this.

Philip V and the Second Macedonian War (215–196)

In 215 Philip V allied with Hannibal (treaty text in Polybius 7.9). Rome kept him busy through the Aetolians until the Peace of Phoenice (205), then returned in 200 on an appeal from Rhodes and Pergamon alarmed by a reported pact between Philip and Antiochus III to carve up the possessions of the child-king Ptolemy V (Polybius 15.20; Livy 31.14).

The decisive battle came at Cynoscephalae in Thessaly in 197. Polybius 18.18–27 and Livy 33.7–10 describe a confused engagement among foggy hills: Philip's right-wing phalanx drove the Roman left downhill, but his left never formed, and an unnamed tribune peeled twenty maniples off the victorious Roman right and struck the phalanx from behind. It could not turn; some 8,000 Macedonians died. Polybius 18.28–32 explains why the manipular legion, able to reform in sub-units on any terrain, beats a phalanx invincible only on flat ground.

At the Isthmian Games of 196 Titus Quinctius Flamininus had a herald proclaim that the Greeks formerly subject to Philip were to be "free, ungarrisoned, untaxed, and governed by their own laws" (Polybius 18.46; Livy 33.32; Plutarch, Flamininus 10–12). By 194 every Roman soldier had left Greece.

Where sources disagree. Plutarch and Livy present Flamininus as a sincere philhellene; Polybius, who admired him, notes his vanity. Ernst Badian (Foreign Clientelae, 1958) read "freedom" as the language of Roman patronage: the Greeks became clients bound by gratitude, a Roman and not a Greek notion of liberty. Gruen replied that Rome was borrowing an old Hellenistic slogan and meant roughly what the Antigonids and Ptolemies had meant by it.

Antiochus III and the Syrian War (192–188)

Antiochus III "the Great", fresh from reconquering Seleucid Iran and Coele-Syria, crossed to Thrace in 196 and was told by Roman envoys to leave Europe and free the Asian Greek cities. Talks failed; in 192 the Aetolians, disappointed in their reward from Rome, invited him into Greece. He came with only 10,000 men and was swept out of Thermopylae in 191 by Manius Acilius Glabrio, with Cato the Elder as legate.

Lucius Cornelius Scipio, with his brother Africanus as adviser, crossed the Hellespont and met Antiochus at Magnesia ad Sipylum in the winter of 190/189. Livy 37.37–44 and Appian, Syrian Wars 30–36, both resting on lost Polybius, describe scythed chariots panicking Antiochus' own left wing, Eumenes II of Pergamon's cavalry rolling it up, and the Seleucid centre of phalanx and elephants broken by missile fire. Livy's 50,000 Seleucid dead is a propaganda figure. The Treaty of Apamea (188; Polybius 21.42; Livy 38.38) pushed the Seleucids beyond the Taurus, imposed an indemnity of 15,000 talents, limited fleet and elephants, and demanded the surrender of Hannibal, at Antiochus' court since 195, who fled to Bithynia.

Perseus, Pydna and the end of the Antigonids (171–167)

Philip's son Perseus rebuilt Macedonian strength quietly. Eumenes denounced him in the Senate in 172 (Livy 42.11–13) and Rome declared war in 171; Livy 42.47 preserves older senators deploring the "new wisdom" of deceiving Perseus during negotiations.

Lucius Aemilius Paullus took command in 168. At Pydna on 22 June (the date is fixed by the lunar eclipse of the night before, which the tribune Sulpicius Gallus had predicted to the troops: Livy 44.37; Plutarch, Aemilius 17) the battle began by accident over a stray horse. The phalanx advanced magnificently across level ground, lost cohesion on broken terrain, and Paullus fed cohorts into the gaps. Plutarch, Aemilius 20, says 20,000 Macedonians died in an hour. Perseus surrendered on Samothrace and died in Italian custody. The monarchy founded by Antigonus Gonatas was abolished and Macedon split into four tributary republics (Livy 45.29). The same year, outside Alexandria, the envoy Popillius Laenas drew a circle in the sand around Antiochus IV and demanded an answer before he stepped out of it (Polybius 29.27). No Hellenistic monarch could now act without reference to Rome.

The settlement was brutal where it chose to be. On senatorial orders Paullus sacked seventy towns of Epirus in a day and enslaved 150,000 people (Polybius 30.15; Livy 45.34). In Achaea, technically loyal, the pro-Roman Callicrates supplied a list of a thousand leading men suspected of sympathy for Perseus; they were summoned to Rome "for trial" in 167 and detained in Italian towns for seventeen years without charge (Polybius 30.13, 30.32; Pausanias 7.10.7–12). Among them was Polybius of Megalopolis, former cavalry commander of the League. Kept in Rome through his friendship with Paullus' son Scipio Aemilianus (Polybius 31.23–25), he wrote the history that remains our best account of how Rome won. When the survivors were released in 150, fewer than 300 of the thousand were alive.

The last wars in Greece and the first provinces (150–129)

Andriscus and the province of Macedonia

In 149 a pretender, Andriscus, claiming to be Perseus' son, overran the four republics and beat a Roman praetor. Quintus Caecilius Metellus destroyed him in 148, and Macedonia became Rome's first province east of the Adriatic.

The Achaean War and the sack of Corinth

When Sparta tried to leave the Achaean League, the Senate (146) ordered the League to release not only Sparta but Corinth, Argos and other members; the assembly rioted, and the strategoi Critolaus and then Diaeus led the League into war. Metellus beat Critolaus at Scarpheia; Lucius Mummius destroyed Diaeus at Leucopetra, entered Corinth, killed the men, sold the women and children, shipped the art to Rome and burned the city (Polybius 38.9–18, 39.2–6; Pausanias 7.14–16; Strabo 8.6.23). Polybius was present and records seeing Roman soldiers using masterpieces as dice-boards. The League was dissolved; Polybius himself was asked to arrange the new order.

Where sources disagree. Whether "Achaea" became a province in 146 is uncertain. Pausanias 7.16.9–10 implies direct rule from then on, but no governor of Achaea is attested before Augustus constituted the province in 27 BC, and most scholars now think southern Greece was supervised by the Macedonian governor for a century, with free cities such as Athens and Sparta nominally autonomous.

The Attalid bequest

Attalus III of Pergamon died childless in 133 and left his kingdom to the Roman People by will (Strabo 13.4.2; Livy, Periochae 58–59; Justin 36.4). Tiberius Gracchus at once proposed using the Attalid treasure for his land commission (Plutarch, Tiberius Gracchus 14). A pretender, Aristonicus, was crushed by 129 and Pergamon became the province of Asia, the richest in the empire and the one whose tax-farmers would do most to make Mithridates welcome.

Where sources disagree. The letter of Mithridates in Sallust, Histories 4.69 M, alleges the will was forged by Rome. The Pergamene decree OGIS 338, passed within weeks of Attalus' death, refers to the will's ratification as fact, which most historians take as decisive; the motive, probably to pre-empt a succession war, remains conjecture.

Mithridates, Sulla and Pompey (89–63)

Mithridates VI Eupator of Pontus, Persian in descent and Greek in self-presentation, struck while Rome was distracted by the Social War. In 88 his armies overran Roman Asia and he ordered the massacre of every Italian resident in a single day: 80,000 according to Valerius Maximus 9.2 ext. 3 and Memnon (FGrH 434 F 22.9), 150,000 in Plutarch (Sulla 24); the cities' eager participation says how Roman rule had been experienced. His general Archelaus crossed to Greece, where Athens under the tyrant Aristion joined him.

Sulla arrived in 87, besieged Athens through the winter, felled the groves of the Academy and Lyceum for siege timber, and stormed the city on 1 March 86 (Plutarch, Sulla 12–14; Appian, Mithridatic Wars 30–39; Pausanias 1.20.4–7). The killing in the Kerameikos stopped, Plutarch says, when Sulla declared he forgave "the living for the sake of the dead". Sulla then beat Archelaus at Chaeronea and Orchomenus (86) and in 85 made the Peace of Dardanus, restoring Mithridates to Pontus because his own enemies in Rome mattered more. Among the spoils he shipped home was Apellicon's library, containing the manuscripts of Aristotle and Theophrastus (Strabo 13.1.54; Plutarch, Sulla 26): the Aristotelian corpus reached the Latin West as loot.

Two further wars followed. Lucullus drove Mithridates from Pontus (73–67) but lost control of his army; Pompey, given the command in 66, finished him in a year, and the king killed himself at Panticapaeum in the Crimea in 63 when his son Pharnaces revolted (Appian, Mithridatic Wars 111–113; Cassius Dio 37.11–14). Pompey then turned south. The Seleucid dynasty had shrunk to a few cities of northern Syria. In 64 Pompey declined to recognise Antiochus XIII Asiaticus, remarking that a king who could not hold his realm against Arabs and Jews was unfit to have it back, and annexed Syria as a province (Appian, Syrian Wars 49–50, 70; Justin 40.2). The house of Seleucus ended by administrative decision, without a battle.

Cleopatra, Actium and the end of Ptolemaic Egypt (51–30)

Egypt survived because it was rich and already half-controlled: Ptolemy XII had bought his throne back from Rome with 6,000 talents. His daughter Cleopatra VII, queen from 51, saw that the only path was personal alliance with whoever held Roman power. She bore Caesar a son, Caesarion, in 47, and after his murder allied with Mark Antony from 41. At the Donations of Alexandria in 34 Antony distributed eastern kingdoms to her children and proclaimed Caesarion Caesar's legitimate heir (Plutarch, Antony 54; Cassius Dio 49.41), a direct challenge to Octavian's adoptive legitimacy.

Rome declared war on Cleopatra, not Antony, in 32, and the fleets met off Actium on 2 September 31. Antony's larger ships, undermanned by disease and desertion, failed to break Agrippa's line; Cleopatra's squadron broke out and Antony followed, abandoning the rest (Plutarch, Antony 62–68; Cassius Dio 50.12–35). The army surrendered a week later. Octavian entered Alexandria on 1 August 30; Antony killed himself, and Cleopatra died about ten days later, aged thirty-nine. Egypt became the emperor's personal possession under an equestrian prefect. Horace, Odes 1.37, gives the Roman triumph and, remarkably, the queen's dignity:

No humble woman, she scorned to be led in a proud triumph.
Where sources disagree. Plutarch, Antony 86, admits no one knew how Cleopatra died: the asp was one story, a poisoned hairpin another; Strabo 17.1.10 likewise gives two versions. On the battle, W. W. Tarn (1931) argued that Actium was hardly fought, Antony's fleet being cover for a planned escape, and that Augustan poets (Virgil, Aeneid 8.675–713; Propertius 4.6) inflated it into a cosmic clash of East and West. Most recent accounts accept a real but short battle, decided when Cleopatra's departure broke Antony's nerve.

Captive Greece: the reverse conquest

Horace, Epistles 2.1.156–157, put the paradox in a line every educated Roman knew:

Captive Greece took captive her savage conqueror and brought the arts to rustic Latium.

Tutors and philosophers

Latin literature began as a Greek import: Livius Andronicus, a Greek from Tarentum, translated the Odyssey and staged the first Latin tragedies; Ennius, who said he had three hearts (Oscan, Greek, Latin), wrote the Annales in Homer's hexameter. The Athenian philosophers' embassy of 155 so alarmed Cato that he urged it be sent home before Roman youth forgot how to obey (Plutarch, Cato Maior 22). Within a generation the Stoic Panaetius lived in Scipio Aemilianus' house, and every senatorial boy had a Greek paedagogus and finished his education at Athens or Rhodes, as Cicero did.

Loot

The art came as spoil. Marcellus' sack of Syracuse in 211 filled Rome with Greek statues for the first time (Livy 25.40; Plutarch, Marcellus 21); Polybius 9.10 criticises the policy: a conqueror should take gold and leave the conquered their ornaments. Paullus' triumph of 167 took three days, the first for statues and paintings alone (Plutarch, Aemilius 32–33); Pliny, Natural History 35.24, records that when Attalus II bid 600,000 denarii at Mummius' Corinthian auction for a painting by Aristides, Mummius assumed it held some hidden virtue and withdrew it to dedicate in Rome. A century later Verres was prosecuted (Cicero, Verrines 2.4) for stealing Greek art privately: the loot was now understood.

Gods and ancestors

Roman religion had received Greek gods long before the conquest: Apollo in 431, Asclepius fetched from Epidaurus in 293 (Livy 10.47), the twelve-god lectisternium of 217 pairing Roman and Greek deities in six couples (Livy 22.10.9), the Great Mother in 204 (Livy 29.10–14). The interpretatio that identified Jupiter with Zeus, Juno with Hera, Minerva with Athena, Mars with Ares, Venus with Aphrodite, Mercury with Hermes, Neptune with Poseidon and Diana with Artemis was standard when Ennius listed the twelve Di Consentes. What Rome mostly took was not the gods but their stories: Latin cult had names, rituals and functions but little narrative, and Greek myth filled the vacuum.

The Trojan ancestry was the master-stroke. Hellanicus of Lesbos in the fifth century already had Aeneas founding Rome (Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Roman Antiquities 1.72), and Etruscan statuettes of disputed date from Veii show Aeneas carrying Anchises, so the legend was in Italy early. Naevius, Ennius and Cato's Origines built it into Roman history; the gens Iulia claimed descent from Aeneas' son Iulus and so from Venus; Virgil's Aeneid and Livy 1.1–3 fixed the canonical form. The genius of the choice is that Troy placed Romans inside the Homeric world as equals of the Greeks yet not Greeks, and even gave them a grievance. Andrew Erskine (Troy between Greece and Rome, 2001) shows Greek cities exploiting the kinship to win Roman favour.

Where sources disagree. Georges Dumézil held that early Rome was a society "without mythology", its gods abstract functions, which is why Greek myth was absorbed wholesale; T. P. Wiseman (The Myths of Rome, 2004) argued that Rome had plenty of native story, told in drama and ritual, which the literary record failed to preserve.

Why Rome won: Polybius' answer

Polybius opens his Histories (1.1.5) by asking who could be so idle as not to want to know "by what means and under what kind of constitution" the Romans subjected almost the whole inhabited world in less than fifty-three years (220–167). His answer in Book 6 is constitutional. Rome's politeia mixes monarchy (consuls), aristocracy (Senate) and democracy (assemblies), each checking the others (6.11–18), and this mixture resists anacyclosis, the natural cycle by which pure constitutions degenerate (6.3–9).

Three supporting arguments matter more to a modern reader. First, the army (6.19–42), with the phalanx comparison at 18.28–32 explaining Cynoscephalae and Pydna together. Second, culture: the aristocratic funeral with its ancestral masks (6.53–54) bred young men who would die for glory, and Roman religious scruple, which Polybius frankly calls deisidaimonia, superstition, kept the masses honest and magistrates accountable where Greek officials embezzled (6.56). Third, manpower: his census figures for 225 (2.24) give Rome and its allies over 700,000 foot and 70,000 horse, which is why Pyrrhus and Hannibal could win battles and still lose. What his later books show rather than say is that Rome also won by exploiting Greek states that could never combine against it, and after 168 by terror as policy.

Where sources disagree. Modern explanations divide along lines already met. Harris stresses an aristocracy whose careers required war; Gruen Roman reluctance to annex and the Greek habit of inviting Rome in; Eckstein argues that every Hellenistic state was equally bellicose and Rome simply had the deeper manpower base and a more inclusive citizenship, which is Polybius' own answer in modern dress. His constitutional thesis is now generally judged his weakest argument, his military and social observations his strongest.

Self-test

  1. Which two battles did Pyrrhus win against Rome, and which battle ended his Italian campaign?
    Answer

    Heraclea (280) and Asculum (279); he was checked at Beneventum in 275 and withdrew to Epirus.

  2. What Roman manoeuvre decided Cynoscephalae, and where does Polybius explain its general significance?
    Answer

    A tribune detached twenty maniples from the victorious Roman right and attacked the phalanx from behind; Polybius 18.28–32 explains why the flexible manipular legion beats the phalanx on uneven ground.

  3. What did Flamininus proclaim at the Isthmian Games of 196, and how have Badian and Gruen interpreted it differently?
    Answer

    That the Greeks formerly under Philip V were free, ungarrisoned, untaxed and self-governing. Badian saw Roman patronage creating client states; Gruen saw Rome borrowing a standard Hellenistic slogan in good faith.

  4. Name the treaty that ended the Syrian War and two of its terms.
    Answer

    The Treaty of Apamea (188): Antiochus III withdrew beyond the Taurus, paid 15,000 talents, limited his fleet and elephants, and was to surrender Hannibal.

  5. How is the date of Pydna fixed, and what happened to the Macedonian monarchy afterwards?
    Answer

    By the lunar eclipse of 21 June 168, the night before the battle. The Antigonid monarchy was abolished and Macedon divided into four tributary republics.

  6. Who compiled the list of the thousand Achaeans, how long were they held, and how many survived?
    Answer

    The pro-Roman Achaean politician Callicrates; they were held in Italy from 167 to 150, and fewer than 300 returned.

  7. What evidence supports the authenticity of Attalus III's will?
    Answer

    The Pergamene decree OGIS 338, passed just after his death in 133, treats the will and its ratification by Rome as fact; the forgery allegation appears only in Mithridates' letter in Sallust's Histories.

  8. What famous library reached Rome as a result of Sulla's sack of Athens?
    Answer

    Apellicon's library, containing the manuscripts of Aristotle and Theophrastus (Strabo 13.1.54; Plutarch, Sulla 26).

  9. How did the Seleucid dynasty end?
    Answer

    Without a battle: in 64 Pompey refused to recognise Antiochus XIII Asiaticus and annexed Syria as a Roman province.

  10. According to Polybius Book 6, what three elements made up Rome's mixed constitution, and what did he think its religion did?
    Answer

    Consuls (monarchic), Senate (aristocratic) and assemblies (democratic), each checking the others; Roman religious scruple, which he calls deisidaimonia, kept the masses in order and magistrates honest (6.56).

Carry forward

  1. Rome did not conquer the Greek world in one campaign but by a century of interventions, each invited or provoked by Greek states that would not combine against it; the step from arbitration to terror came in 168–146, with Epirus, the thousand Achaeans and Corinth.
  2. The Hellenistic monarchies ended differently: Macedon by battle and abolition, Pergamon by bequest, the Seleucids by Pompey's decree, Egypt by Actium and annexation to the emperor personally. Each became a province of a different legal shape.
  3. The conquest ran both ways. Greek literature, art, philosophy and myth became Rome's own, the Trojan ancestry letting Romans enter the Homeric world as rivals rather than pupils; Polybius, himself a hostage of the conquest, wrote the first serious analysis of why it happened.
