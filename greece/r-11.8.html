The last year: from Ecbatana to Babylon

The Exiles' Decree

At the Olympic Games of 324 Nicanor of Stagira read out a royal letter to a crowd that Diodorus puts at over 20,000 exiles: the Greek cities were to take back their banished citizens, except those guilty of sacrilege or murder (Diodorus 18.8.2–5; 17.109.1; Curtius 10.2.4). The order overrode the autonomy guaranteed by the League of Corinth. It hit Athens hardest, because Athens would lose Samos, settled by its cleruchs since 365, and it hit the Aetolians, who would lose Oeniadae. Athens prepared for war while it debated divine honours for Alexander. The decree is the direct background to the Lamian War of 323–322.

Hephaestion

Hephaestion died of fever at Ecbatana in autumn 324, after eating a boiled fowl and drinking wine against his doctor's orders (Plutarch, Alexander 72; Arrian, Anabasis 7.14). Alexander had the physician Glaucias crucified, cut the manes of the horses, and ordered a pyre at Babylon: 12,000 talents in Diodorus (17.114–115), 10,000 in Plutarch. Plutarch presents the winter massacre of the Cossaeans as an offering to the dead man.

Where sources disagree. The oracle of Ammon was asked what honours Hephaestion should receive. Arrian (7.14.7; 7.23.6) says it allowed hero cult. Diodorus (17.115.6) says it allowed sacrifice as to a god. The difference bears on how far Alexander was then pressing claims to divinity.

Arabia and the "last plans"

At Babylon Alexander dug a harbour for a thousand ships and had vessels carried overland from Phoenicia to the Euphrates (Arrian 7.19.3–6). The target was Arabia. The pretext was that the Arabs had sent no embassy, and Arrian (7.20.1) reports that Alexander hoped to be worshipped as their third god, after Ouranos and Dionysus. After the death Perdiccas read the army Alexander's memoranda (hypomnemata; Diodorus 18.4.4–6): a thousand warships against Carthage, a road along North Africa to the Pillars of Heracles, six great temples, a pyramid for Philip, and exchanges of population between Asia and Europe. The army voted to cancel all of them.

Where sources disagree. Tarn called the last plans a forgery. Badian defended them, and most scholars now follow him. Even a genuine document reached us through a manoeuvre, however, since Perdiccas presented it in order to have it rejected.

The Chaldaeans and the omens

Chaldaean priests warned Alexander not to enter Babylon, or at least not facing west (Arrian 7.16.5–17.6, from Aristobulus; Diodorus 17.112). He suspected they wanted to keep the revenues of the temple of Bel (Marduk), whose rebuilding they had delayed. Even so, he tried to circle round to another gate and was blocked by the marshes. Then the omens began. A gust blew his diadem into the reeds by the Assyrian royal tombs, and the sailor who retrieved it put it on his own head (Arrian 7.22). The seer Peithagoras found a liver without a lobe (7.18). A tame ass kicked the king's finest lion to death (Plutarch 73). An unknown man sat on the empty throne wearing the royal robe and diadem and said only that the idea had come to him; he was executed (Arrian 7.24; Diodorus 17.116; Plutarch 73–74). Assyriologists have read this as a garbled memory of the substitute-king ritual (šar pūḫi), in which a stand-in absorbed an evil omen and was then killed. The idea is attractive but uncertain.

Eleven days in Daesius

The illness followed a drinking party given by Medius of Larissa. Arrian (7.25–26) and Plutarch (Alexander 76) both reproduce the Ephemerides ("Royal Journals"). Plutarch dates the entries by the Macedonian month.

A Babylonian astronomical diary records the king's death on 29 Ayyaru, that is, 11 June 323 BC. It is the firmest date in his life.

Where sources disagree. Plutarch (75) also cites Aristobulus for a different ending: wine drunk in the fever, delirium, and death on 30 Daesius. The journal's status is contested. Pearson thought it a literary fake. Bosworth (1971) argued that it was published in the early Successor period to establish a natural death against the poison rumours. Hammond defended it as an authentic court record. The reference to Sarapis looks anachronistic, since the cult is usually thought to be Ptolemaic.

"To the strongest"

Asked who should have the kingdom, Alexander is said to have answered tōi kratistōi, "to the strongest", and to have foreseen great funeral games over his body (Arrian 7.26.3; Diodorus 17.117.4). Curtius (10.5.5) has optimo, "to the best". He gave his ring to Perdiccas (Curtius 10.5.4; Justin 12.15). Arrian offers the saying as hearsay, outside the journal, which has the king already speechless. It reads like an epigram composed afterwards to explain forty years of war among the Successors.

What killed him

All the accounts describe roughly twelve days of fever after heavy drinking, with growing weakness, loss of speech and terminal coma. Diodorus (17.117) and Justin (12.13) add a sudden stabbing pain after he drained a great cup of unmixed wine to Heracles. Plutarch (75) dismisses this as tragic embellishment.

The ancient poisoning story is political. Antipater, about to be replaced as regent, sent his son Cassander with water from the Styx at Nonacris, so corrosive that only a mule's hoof could hold it. His younger son Iolaus, the royal cupbearer, poured it, and some versions say Aristotle advised, embittered by the death of his relative Callisthenes (Arrian 7.27; Plutarch 77; Curtius 10.10.14–17; Diodorus 17.118; Justin 12.13–14). The Liber de morte attached to the Alexander Romance names Medius' guests as conspirators. It is a pamphlet aimed at Antipater's house, probably from Perdiccas' or Polyperchon's circle.

Where sources disagree. Arrian (7.27.3) records the poison story only so as not to seem ignorant of it. Plutarch (77.5) says most writers think it invented. Diodorus claims that historians suppressed it out of fear of Cassander. Justin accepts it. Most modern scholars favour natural causes, made worse by drink, the chest wound at the Malli town, and exhaustion, though Bosworth kept poisoning open. The honest verdict is that the cause is unknown. The poisoning story tells us about Successor politics, not about toxicology.

The body

Curtius (10.10.9–13) says the corpse lay six days in the summer heat while the generals quarrelled and was found uncorrupted. Egyptian and Chaldaean embalmers then placed it in a gold coffin filled with spices. Building the funeral carriage took two years. Diodorus (18.26–28) describes a gold-roofed Ionic shrine on wheels, hung with bells and painted panels of the army and fleet and drawn by sixty-four mules. In 321 Ptolemy met it in Syria and took it to Egypt, with the connivance of Arrhidaeus, its escort, while Perdiccas' officers failed to stop him (Diodorus 18.28; Strabo 17.1.8; Arrian, Events after Alexander fr. 1.25). The body gave Ptolemy a claim to legitimacy, and it gave Perdiccas a reason to invade Egypt, where his own officers murdered him.

Where sources disagree. The intended destination was either Siwa (Diodorus 18.3.5) or the royal tombs at Aegae (Pausanias 1.6.3). Curtius (10.10.20) and Pausanias (1.7.1) put the body first at Memphis, with the move to Alexandria under Ptolemy II. Diodorus implies that Ptolemy I took it to Alexandria directly.

In Alexandria it lay in the Sema or Soma. Zenobius (3.94) says Ptolemy IV built a mausoleum there in which the Ptolemies were buried around Alexander. Strabo (17.1.8) says a later Ptolemy melted down the gold coffin and replaced it with one of glass or alabaster. The visitors are part of the story.

  • Caesar came in 48 BC. Lucan (10.14–52) makes the visit an indictment of both men.
  • Augustus crowned the body and strewed flowers (Suetonius, Augustus 18). Dio (51.16.5) adds that he broke off part of the nose. Offered the Ptolemies too, he said he had come to see a king, not corpses.
  • Caligula wore Alexander's breastplate from the tomb (Suetonius, Caligula 52). Septimius Severus closed the tomb to visitors (Dio 76.13.2).
  • Caracalla, in 215, left his cloak, rings and belt on the coffin (Herodian 4.8.9).

Possible ends for the tomb are Aurelian's destruction of the palace quarter in the 270s, the tsunami of 365, and the anti-pagan violence of the 390s. Libanius (Oration 49.12), writing around 390, implies the body was still on show. Within a few years John Chrysostom (Homily 26 on 2 Corinthians) could ask where the tomb was as though no one knew. More than 140 official searches, from the Nebi Daniel mosque to Siwa, have found nothing confirmed.

Image as currency: the Successors and Rome

The Successors

The generals ruled in the Argead name while it lasted, through Philip III Arrhidaeus and Alexander IV, both murdered by about 310. After that, Argead brides served instead. Cassander married Philip's daughter Thessalonike, and Alexander's sister Cleopatra was killed in 308 before she could marry a rival. Ptolemy encouraged the rumour that he was Philip's bastard (Pausanias 1.6.2; Curtius 9.8.22). Eumenes held councils before Alexander's empty throne (Diodorus 18.60–61). Coinage made the image portable. From about 319 Ptolemy's tetradrachms showed Alexander in an elephant scalp with the ram's horn of Ammon, and from 297 Lysimachus put the horned, diademed Alexander on silver that circulated for centuries. This horned image matters for the Quran debate below.

The Roman Alexander

  • Scipio Africanus was rumoured, like Alexander, to have been fathered by a serpent (Livy 26.19).
  • Pompeius took the name Magnus and copied Alexander's raised forelock (Plutarch, Pompey 2). At his triumph of 61 he wore a cloak said to be Alexander's (Appian, Mithridatic Wars 117).
  • Caesar, at Gades, wept before Alexander's statue at having achieved nothing at the age by which Alexander had conquered the world (Suetonius, Julius 7). Plutarch (Caesar 11) has him reading a book instead.
  • Antony named his son Alexander Helios and dressed him as a Macedonian at the Donations of Alexandria (Plutarch, Antony 54).
  • Augustus sealed with a sphinx, then with Alexander's head, and then with his own (Suetonius, Augustus 50; Pliny, Natural History 37.10).
  • Trajan, at the Persian Gulf in 116, said he would sail to India were he younger, and sacrificed in the room where Alexander died (Dio 68.29–30).
  • Caracalla raised a "Macedonian phalanx" of 16,000 and persecuted Alexandria's Aristotelians because Aristotle had supposedly poisoned Alexander (Dio 78.7–9; Herodian 4.8).

The moralists pushed back. Livy's digression (9.17–19) argues that Alexander would have lost to Rome's consuls, and it is aimed at Greeks who rated even the Parthians above Rome. Seneca makes Alexander the type of the man ruined by anger and appetite (De ira 3.17; De beneficiis 1.13; Epistles 94.62). Lucan (10.20–46) calls him the mad son of Philip and a fortunate brigand, standing in for Caesar.

Jerusalem, the Quran, Persia, the medieval West

In Josephus (Jewish Antiquities 11.317–345) the high priest Jaddus meets Alexander in white robes. The king bows to the divine name, recognises a figure from his dreams, sacrifices in the Temple and is shown Daniel's prophecy. No Greek historian mentions the visit, and the story is almost universally regarded as a legend that served as a charter for Jewish privileges.

Quran 18:83–98 tells of Dhul-Qarnayn, "the two-horned one", who travels to the setting and the rising sun and builds an iron barrier against Gog and Magog. Most classical exegetes took him to be Alexander. Kevin van Bladel and others point to close parallels with the Syriac Alexander Legend of about 630, in which Alexander builds a gate against the northern peoples.

Where sources disagree. Because Dhul-Qarnayn is a righteous monotheist, many Muslim scholars reject identifying him with a pagan son of Ammon. Abul Kalam Azad and Maududi proposed Cyrus the Great instead, and others decline to name any historical king. Whether the Syriac Legend came before or after the Quranic passage is itself disputed.

Zoroastrian texts such as the Arda Viraz Namag call Alexander "the accursed", the man who burned the Avesta. Firdausi's Shahnameh (around 1010) absorbs him instead. Iskandar is the son of the Persian king Darab by a daughter of Filqus (Philip), which makes him half-brother to Dara, so the conquest becomes a family succession. Nizami's Iskandarnameh (late twelfth century) goes further and makes him conqueror, then philosopher among the Greek sages, then prophet.

The medieval West received him through the Greek Alexander Romance (Pseudo-Callisthenes) in Latin versions by Julius Valerius and Leo of Naples, and through Curtius. Walter of Châtillon's Alexandreis and the Old French Roman d'Alexandre (late twelfth century) made him a chivalric hero who explores the sea in a glass bell and the sky in a griffin-drawn basket. The poem's twelve-syllable line gave French the alexandrine. In Jacques de Longuyon's Vœux du paon (1312) he stands among the Nine Worthies with Hector and Caesar, alongside three Jewish and three Christian heroes.

The modern Alexanders

Droysen (1833; Geschichte des Hellenismus, 1836–43) coined "Hellenism" for a fusion of Greek and Eastern culture that prepared the way for Christianity, and his Macedon unifying Greece looks very like Prussia. Tarn (1926, 1948) made Alexander a visionary of the unity of mankind, built largely on the prayer for homonoia at Opis (Arrian 7.11.9) and on Plutarch's rhetorical On the Fortune of Alexander. Badian demolished that view (Historia, 1958) and portrayed a paranoid autocrat who destroyed his marshals. Bosworth (Conquest and Empire, 1988; Alexander and the East, 1996) documented the massacres in Sogdiana and India. Green (1974) gave a brilliant, ruthless politician. Lane Fox (1973) gave a Homeric romantic driven by longing (pothos). Worthington (2004) stresses drink, megalomania and a drained Macedon. Cartledge (2004) offers a balanced portrait built around the problem of the sources. The post-colonial turn, led by Pierre Briant, makes Alexander "the last of the Achaemenids", who took over Persian structures, and shows how European empires used a civilising Alexander to justify themselves.

Where sources disagree. Tarn wrote amid the internationalism that followed 1918. Badian, a refugee from Nazi Austria, wrote with recent experience of charismatic dictators. Both men read the same Arrian. Current work largely abandons the choice between good and bad Alexander and asks how late sources constructed each portrait.

Oliver Stone's Alexander (2004), with Lane Fox as adviser, is narrated by an aged Ptolemy writing his memoirs, so the film stages the source problem. Greek lawyers threatened to sue over the treatment of Hephaestion.

In the Balkans Alexander became a matter of state. Greece contested the newly independent Macedonia's name and the Vergina Sun on its flag, which was dropped under the 1995 Interim Accord. VMRO-DPMNE governments from 2006 pursued "antiquisation": Skopje's airport was named for Alexander and a huge "Warrior on a Horse" went up in 2011. Rallies in Thessaloniki and Athens in 2018 answered with "Macedonia is Greek". The Prespa Agreement (2018, in force 2019) created North Macedonia. It states that the country's Slavic language and identity are unrelated to ancient Hellenic Macedonia, and the airport and statues were renamed.

Why readers project onto Alexander

The first reason is the sources. Nothing written in Alexander's lifetime survives intact. Ptolemy, Aristobulus, Callisthenes and Cleitarchus reach us through writers three to five centuries later, so every reconstruction means choosing between incompatible witnesses, and the choices reveal the historian making them. The second is the shape of the life. He conquered the known world and died at thirty-two with his plans open, leaving no old age to settle what he was. The third is need. The Successors needed legitimacy, Romans a rival and a warning, Jews a king who bowed to their God, Persians a lost son, Muslims a monotheist builder of walls, knights a model, Droysen a unifier, Tarn a brotherhood, Badian a tyrant, Briant an Achaemenid, and the Balkans an ancestor. With the body lost there is no fixed point, and every age has filled the empty tomb with its own Alexander.

Self-test

  1. What did the Exiles' Decree require, and why did it threaten Athens in particular?
    Answer

    Proclaimed at Olympia in 324, it ordered the Greek cities to take back their exiles, overriding the League of Corinth. Athens would have lost Samos, held by its cleruchs since 365. The decree helped bring on the Lamian War.

  2. On what point do Arrian and Diodorus disagree about Ammon's reply concerning Hephaestion?
    Answer

    Arrian says Ammon allowed hero cult; Diodorus says it allowed worship as a god.

  3. What did the Chaldaeans warn, and what motive did Alexander suspect?
    Answer

    Not to enter Babylon, or not facing west. He suspected they wanted to keep the revenues of the temple of Bel (Marduk) (Arrian 7.16–17).

  4. How have modern scholars interpreted the stranger who sat on Alexander's throne?
    Answer

    As a garbled memory of the Mesopotamian substitute-king ritual (šar pūḫi), in which a stand-in absorbed an evil omen and was then killed. The reading is plausible but uncertain.

  5. Which ancient authors preserve the Ephemerides, and what did Bosworth argue about them?
    Answer

    Arrian (7.25–26) and Plutarch (Alexander 76). Bosworth argued that the account was published early in the Successor period to establish a natural death against the poison rumours.

  6. Why is the Veratrum album hypothesis preferred to strychnine?
    Answer

    White hellebore was known to Greek medicine and produces a slow decline over days. Strychnine kills within hours with convulsions, and none are recorded.

  7. Outline the ancient poisoning story and Arrian's attitude to it.
    Answer

    Antipater sent Cassander with Styx water carried in a mule's hoof; Iolaus the cupbearer gave it to Alexander; some versions say Aristotle advised. Arrian records the story but does not believe it.

  8. Trace the body from Babylon to Alexandria and name one dispute about the route.
    Answer

    The funeral carriage left Babylon in 321 and was diverted by Ptolemy in Syria to Egypt. The body lay at Memphis, then in the Sema at Alexandria. Curtius and Pausanias date the move to Alexandria to Ptolemy II, while Diodorus implies that Ptolemy I took it there directly. Whether the intended destination was Siwa or Aegae is also disputed.

  9. What did Augustus and Caracalla do at the tomb?
    Answer

    Augustus crowned the body and strewed flowers (Suetonius); Dio says he broke off part of the nose. Caracalla left his cloak, rings and belt on the coffin (Herodian 4.8.9).

  10. How does Firdausi make Alexander an Iranian king?
    Answer

    He makes Iskandar the son of the Persian king Darab by a daughter of Philip, and so half-brother to Dara. This turns the conquest into a dynastic succession and reverses the Zoroastrian image of "the accursed".

Carry forward

  1. The stories of the death, including the omens, the journal, "to the strongest" and the poison plot, were shaped by Successor propaganda. Read each first as evidence for who needed it told.
  2. Alexander's corpse and portrait were political property. The pattern runs from Ptolemy's theft of the body and the horned coinage to Roman emperors at the Sema.
  3. Every reception rebuilt Alexander for its own needs, and the modern scholarly Alexanders belong to that history of reception rather than standing outside it.
