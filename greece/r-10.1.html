Birth and the Thetis prophecy

Achilles exists because Zeus was frightened. The clearest statement is Pindar, Isthmian 8.26–48: Zeus and Poseidon both want the Nereid Thetis, and Themis stops them with a prophecy that any son Thetis bears to a god will be mightier than his father, wielding a weapon stronger than the thunderbolt or the trident. The solution is to marry her downwards, to the mortal Peleus, so that the child inherits a mortal ceiling. Achilles is therefore a contained cosmic threat: the god who never happened.

Aeschylus, Prometheus Bound 755–770 and 907–927, makes the same prophecy the secret Prometheus withholds from Zeus until he is released; the lost Prometheus Unbound presumably traded the secret for his freedom. Apollodorus, Library 3.13.5, records both this and a rival explanation: Thetis refused Zeus out of loyalty to Hera, who had reared her, and was condemned to a mortal husband. Homer knows the second strand without the prophecy (Iliad 24.59–63; 18.432–434).

Where sources disagree. Pindar and Aeschylus make the marriage a precaution against a usurper; the Cypria (as reported in scholia) and Apollodorus' second version make it a punishment for refusing Zeus. The Iliad suppresses the succession theme almost entirely. Laura Slatkin (The Power of Thetis, 1991) argues the suppression is deliberate and audible: Thetis once saved Zeus from being bound (1.396–406), and her power to compel him for Achilles is the residue of a cosmic plot the poem declines to tell.

Making him invulnerable: Styx versus fire

The Styx-dipping is late. Its first surviving statement is Statius, Achilleid 1.133–134, where Thetis regrets that she did not arm him completely in the Styx's waters; the heel as the held, unwetted part is spelled out only by Servius (on Aeneid 6.57) and Fulgentius (Mythologiae 3.7). The older story is fire and ambrosia. Apollonius, Argonautica 4.869–879, and Apollodorus, Library 3.13.6, have Thetis burn away the infant's mortal part by night and anoint him with ambrosia by day, until Peleus sees the child in the flames and cries out; Thetis abandons both and returns to the sea. The template is Demeter's attempt on Demophoon in the Homeric Hymn to Demeter 231–262. A grimmer variant in Lycophron, Alexandra 178–179, with Ptolemy Hephaestion (via Photius, Bibliotheca 190), has Thetis destroy six sons in the fire; Achilles, the seventh, is snatched out with a burnt ankle, which Peleus replaces with the ankle-bone of the giant Damysus. That is the root of the vulnerable heel: not a dry spot but a graft.

Homer has none of this. His Achilles bleeds when Asteropaeus grazes his forearm (Iliad 21.166–167) and needs Hephaestus' armour because his body is ordinary. Invulnerability is a folk-tale accretion the epic resisted, and it makes the arrow story necessary: an unwoundable man needs a loophole.

Pelion and Scyros

After Thetis leaves, Peleus hands the boy to Chiron on Pelion. Pindar, Nemean 3.43–63, has the six-year-old killing lions and boars and dragging the carcasses to the centaur, who raises him with his wife Philyra. Apollodorus 3.13.6 adds the diet of lion and boar entrails and bear marrow, and a folk etymology: he is Achilles because his lips (cheile) never touched a breast. Homer keeps a thinner Chiron: the source of Peleus' ash spear (Iliad 16.143–144) and of Achilles' medicine (11.831–832). Statius, Achilleid 2.96–167, lets Achilles narrate his own curriculum.

Where sources disagree. Homer's Phoenix claims to have raised Achilles from infancy, cutting his meat and holding him on his knees (Iliad 9.485–495), a human nurture with no room for a centaur. The lyric tradition preferred Chiron because it makes Achilles half-wild from the start; the Iliad prefers Phoenix because its Achilles must be socialised enough to be persuadable.

Scyros is where the tradition contradicts itself most openly. Homer's Achilles took Scyros by force (Iliad 9.667–668) and left a son, Neoptolemus, raised there (19.326–327; Odyssey 11.506–509). The Cypria, per Proclus, has him land there after a storm and marry Deidamia, Lycomedes' daughter. The famous version, in which Thetis hides him dressed as a girl among Lycomedes' daughters and Odysseus unmasks him with a trumpet and a spear, is first attested in the lost Scyrians of Sophocles and Euripides, then in Ovid, Metamorphoses 13.162–170, Hyginus, Fabulae 96, Apollodorus 3.13.8, and above all Statius, Achilleid 1.207–960. The son's alternative name Pyrrhus derives from Achilles' alias Pyrrha. The concealment is incompatible with the Iliad's Achilles, who sailed knowing the two fates; a hidden boy does not choose.

The two fates and the economy of kleos

In the embassy scene Achilles reports his mother's words: he carries two fates (dichthadias keras) to his death; stay and fight at Troy and his homecoming is lost but his kleos is imperishable (kleos aphthiton); go home and the fame is lost but he lives long (Iliad 9.410–416). The passage sits inside a refusal of Agamemnon's gift list, and its real argument is at 9.401–409: no quantity of cattle or tripods is worth a life, because the psyche once past the teeth cannot be bought back.

This is a crisis in the heroic economy. The norm is Sarpedon's at 12.310–328: kings eat the fat and drink the sweet wine because they stand in the front line, and since death waits anyway, one should go and win glory or give it. Prizes (time) and song (kleos) are supposed to be one currency, honour paid now and honour paid forever. Agamemnon broke the exchange by taking Briseis, and Achilles discovers that the two cannot be reconverted: gifts cannot restore honour, and honour cannot be eaten. Gregory Nagy (The Best of the Achaeans, 1979) read kleos aphthiton as an inherited Indo-European formula for what epic itself confers, so that the choice of fates is the poem naming its own function; Margalit Finkelberg (1986) objected that the phrase is not formulaic in the strict sense. The dispute matters less than the fact that Achilles treats his fame as something he could decline.

The choice is never made in Book 9. Achilles says he will sail tomorrow and does not. The decision comes at 18.98–126, after Patroclus' death, and it is vengeance over life, not glory over life: he will accept death when Zeus wishes, provided he can first kill Hector. Glory is a by-product of grief.

Achilles and Patroclus

The Iliad never names the bond. It gives Patroclus as the older man sent to advise (11.786–787), as the one person Achilles weeps for without limit (18.22–35), as the subject of the vow to Menoetius that he would be brought home alive (18.324–327), and as the ghost who asks that their bones share one urn (23.83–92), honoured in Odyssey 24.76–77. Both men sleep with captive women on the same night (9.663–668). The words used are philos and hetairos, which cover kin, comrade and beloved without deciding.

The fifth century decided. Aeschylus' Myrmidons (frr. 135–136 Radt, in Athenaeus 13.601a–b) had Achilles address the corpse with the words sebas de meron, the reverence of your thighs, and reproach it for being ungrateful for many kisses. In Plato, Symposium 179e–180b, Phaedrus accepts the erotic reading but says Aeschylus talks nonsense in making Achilles the lover: Homer makes him the younger and more beautiful, so he was the beloved who died for his lover, the rarer and nobler act, rewarded with the Isles of the Blest. Xenophon, Symposium 8.31, has Socrates deny it: Homer shows Patroclus as a comrade, not a lover. Aeschines, Against Timarchus 141–150, quotes the laments of Books 18 and 23 and argues that Homer concealed the eros because educated hearers would understand.

Where sources disagree. Four readings coexist in antiquity: silence (Homer), lover Achilles (Aeschylus), beloved Achilles (Plato's Phaedrus), chaste comradeship (Xenophon). Moderns split the same way. W. M. Clarke (1978) and Kenneth Dover (Greek Homosexuality, 1978) see the Iliad as compatible with a sexual bond it does not name; David Halperin ("Heroes and their Pals", 1990) groups the pair with Gilgamesh and Enkidu and David and Jonathan as a type of heroic friendship that is intense, exclusive and not pederastic. The Iliad is undecidable here; the Athenian debate tells you about Athens.

Menis, Briseis, and the Shield

The poem's first word is menin. Leonard Muellner (The Anger of Achilles, 1996) showed that menis is not ordinary rage (cholos) but a sanction normally reserved for gods when cosmic order is breached: Apollo's at 1.75, Zeus', Demeter's. Applied to a mortal it belongs to Achilles alone. This is the theology of the plot. Apollo's menis over the dishonoured priest opens the poem, Achilles' over his dishonoured time mirrors it, and Thetis carries the second to Olympus where Zeus' nod (1.524–530) makes a mortal grudge an instrument of divine will. The man whose birth was arranged to keep him from threatening Zeus becomes the one mortal whose anger operates at Zeus' scale. He lays the menis down at 19.67–68 and then exceeds it.

Briseis is the price-tag that turns out to be a person. Taken from Lyrnessus after Achilles killed her husband Mynes (2.689–694; 19.291–296), she is led from the hut unwilling, aekousa (1.348). In Book 9 Achilles claims to have loved her from the heart although she was won by the spear (9.341–343). Her single speech, the lament over Patroclus (19.282–300), reveals that Patroclus had promised to make her Achilles' wedded wife in Phthia. The scholia name her Hippodameia; Briseis is a patronymic. Through Dictys, Benoit de Sainte-Maure and Boccaccio she becomes Briseida and then Chaucer's Criseyde, which is how she reaches Shakespeare detached from Achilles.

The Shield (18.478–608) is the poem's view of what the war excludes: cosmos, then two cities, one at peace with a wedding and a lawsuit over a killed man's blood-price (18.497–508), one at war with a siege and an ambush; then ploughing, reaping, vintage, lions on cattle, a dance, Ocean on the rim. Oliver Taplin (1980) read it as ordinary life carried into battle on the arm of the man who has left it; the lawsuit answers Ajax's complaint at 9.632–636 that even a brother's killer is forgiven once the poine is paid, which Achilles alone refuses.

Hector, Priam, and the death

Achilles' treatment of Hector is a sustained refusal of the shield's world. He pierces the ankles and drags the corpse (22.395–404), drags it three times round Patroclus' tomb each dawn (24.14–18), and is kept from succeeding only because Aphrodite and Apollo preserve the body (23.184–191). Apollo's indictment at 24.33–54 is the poem's verdict: Achilles has destroyed pity (eleos) and has no aidos. To Lycaon he had put it in his own terms: Patroclus died, who was far better than you, and I too shall die (21.99–113). The cruelty is a levelling: no death matters because his own does not.

Book 24 undoes this without cancelling it. Priam kisses the hands that killed his sons (24.478–479); the two men weep for different dead (24.507–512); Achilles speaks of the two jars of Zeus and of Peleus, who like Priam has one son who will not tend his old age (24.527–551). Colin Macleod's commentary (1982) treats this as the poem's real conclusion, pity learnt through a shared condition. But Achilles still warns Priam not to provoke him (24.559–570). Pity is a truce, not a conversion.

The death is outside the Iliad, which only predicts it: by a god and a man (19.416–417), by Apollo's arrows (21.277–278), by Paris and Apollo at the Scaean Gates (22.359–360). Proclus' summary of Arctinus' Aethiopis gives the sequence: Achilles kills Memnon, routs the Trojans, bursts into the city and is killed by Paris and Apollo; Ajax carries the body while Odysseus holds off the Trojans; Thetis, the Nereids and the Muses mourn; Thetis snatches him from the pyre to the White Island.

Where sources disagree. Neither Homer nor, as far as the summary shows, the Aethiopis mentions the heel. A Chalcidian amphora of about 540 BC showed the dead Achilles with an arrow in the ankle, so the motif is archaic in art; in texts it appears in Apollodorus, Epitome 5.3, Hyginus, Fabulae 107, Quintus Smyrnaeus 3.60–63 and Statius 1.134. Dictys and Dares replace the battlefield death with an ambush in the temple of Thymbraean Apollo, where Achilles is lured by a promise of Polyxena; this unheroic version dominated the medieval West and lies behind Shakespeare.

After death: games, arms, the shade and the island

Agamemnon's ghost describes the funeral at Odyssey 24.36–94: seventeen days of mourning, the Nereids, the Muses singing the dirge, the bones placed with Patroclus' in a golden amphora Dionysus gave Thetis, a tomb on the headland over the Hellespont, and prizes set out by Thetis for the games. The Aethiopis adds the quarrel of Odysseus and Ajax over the arms; the Little Iliad (scholia to Aristophanes, Knights 1056) has it settled by overhearing Trojan girls on the wall, Pindar, Nemean 8.23–34, by secret ballot, the Odyssey (11.543–567) by Trojan captives and Athena. The first act of Achilles' afterlife is the destruction of the second-best Achaean.

Two afterlives then compete. In Odyssey 11.467–540 the shade rejects consolation: he would rather be a hired labourer (thes) to a landless man than king over all the dead (11.488–491). The usual rendering "slave" is too strong; a thes is a free man with nothing, and that is the point. The Achilles who refused gifts because life cannot be bought confirms it from the other side. The other tradition removes him from Hades: the Aethiopis sends him to Leuke; Pindar puts him in the Isles of the Blest, taken there by Thetis (Olympian 2.79–80), or on a bright island in the Euxine (Nemean 4.49–50); Euripides, Andromache 1259–1262, has Thetis promise Peleus he will see their son living there.

Leuke is a real island, modern Zmiinyi off the Danube mouth, and the cult is documented. Arrian, Periplus of the Euxine 21–23, describes a temple, offerings left by sailors, and Achilles appearing in dreams to give sailing directions; Olbian inscriptions call him Achilles Pontarches; Alcaeus already called him lord of Scythia (fr. 354). Pausanias 3.19.11–13 tells of Leonymus of Croton, who saw Achilles on the island married to Helen; Philostratus, Heroicus 53–57, elaborates the household. Guy Hedreen (1991) and Hildebrecht Hommel (1980) debate whether this is hero-cult for a dead man or an originally divine Achilles the epic demoted.

Where sources disagree. His consort on the island is Helen in Pausanias and Philostratus; Medea in Ibycus (fr. 10) and Simonides, per the scholia on Apollonius 4.814, and Apollonius' Hera promises exactly that (4.811–815); Iphigenia, renamed Orsilochia, in Nicander as excerpted by Antoninus Liberalis 27. The Polyxena sacrifice attaches the tomb to this afterlife: Proclus' summary of the Iliou Persis ends with her slaughter at Achilles' grave, a Tyrrhenian amphora of about 570 BC shows Neoptolemus doing it, and Euripides, Hecuba 37–41 and 521–582, makes the ghost demand her as his prize. Whether he wanted a bride, a blood-offering or a wind is left open.

Reception

Alexander crossed to Troy in 334 BC and, according to Arrian, Anabasis 1.12.1, garlanded Achilles' tomb while Hephaestion garlanded Patroclus'; Plutarch, Alexander 15.8–9, adds that he anointed the stele, ran naked round it, and called Achilles fortunate to have had a faithful friend in life and a great herald after death. Olympias' Aeacid line claimed descent from Neoptolemus, and after Hephaestion's death Alexander cut his hair over the corpse as Achilles had (Arrian 7.14.4).

Catullus 64.338–370 gives the Fates' wedding-song for Peleus and Thetis and lets it prophesy the child: a hero who will choke the Scamander with corpses and whose final witness will be Polyxena, her blood soaking his high tomb. The whole career becomes an atrocity foreseen at the moment of joy. Statius' unfinished Achilleid (c. AD 95) turns to the boy: the dress, Deidamia, Odysseus' unmasking, the account of Chiron. P. J. Heslin (The Transvestite Achilles, 2005) reads it as a meditation on how masculinity is put on, and it is the main reason the Scyros story, not the fire or the Styx, dominated the medieval Achilles.

Shakespeare's Troilus and Cressida (c. 1602) inherits the Dictys–Dares–Caxton Achilles: proud, idle, sulking for love of Polyxena, manipulated by Ulysses, and finally having his Myrmidons kill an unarmed Hector while he watches (5.8). Thersites calls Patroclus Achilles' "masculine whore" (5.1). This is Achilles with the kleos drained out. Madeline Miller's The Song of Achilles (2011) reverses the polarity: narrated by Patroclus, it assembles Chiron, Scyros and Deidamia from Statius and Apollodorus, follows Phaedrus in making Achilles the beloved rather than the lover, and ends with the shared urn. Its popularity shows which ancient reading the present prefers.

What Achilles is for

Achilles is the figure through whom Greek culture priced its own ideal. Sarpedon's logic is that since death is certain, the only rational use of a life is to spend it for honour. Achilles accepts the premise and tests the arithmetic, and the tradition keeps both results: the Iliad 9 Achilles who knows the fame is not worth it and takes it anyway for Patroclus' sake, and the Odyssey 11 Achilles who confirms from Hades that it was not. The island cult is the third answer, the one that cheats: the hero who did not really die. Socrates in Plato, Apology 28c–d, took the first Achilles as his model for refusing to abandon his post; Jean-Pierre Vernant's "La belle mort" (1982) named the ideology, the beautiful death that freezes a man at his peak and hands him to song. Achilles is for asking whether a short, remembered life beats a long, unremembered one, and for refusing to let either answer stand unchallenged.

Self-test

  1. Which source first states that Thetis' son by a god would overthrow his father, and how is the problem solved?
    Answer

    Pindar, Isthmian 8.26–48, where Themis prophesies it and advises marrying Thetis to the mortal Peleus so the son is mortal.

  2. Which version of Achilles' infancy is older, Styx-dipping or fire-and-ambrosia, and where is each first attested?
    Answer

    Fire-and-ambrosia is older (Apollonius, Argonautica 4.869–879; Apollodorus 3.13.6, modelled on the Demeter–Demophoon story). Styx-dipping first appears in Statius, Achilleid 1.133–134, with the heel specified only by Servius and Fulgentius.

  3. What is the strongest evidence that Homer's Achilles is not invulnerable?
    Answer

    Asteropaeus grazes his forearm and draws blood at Iliad 21.166–167, and he needs divine armour.

  4. How do Homer's and the later tradition's accounts of Achilles' arrival on Scyros conflict?
    Answer

    Homer says he conquered Scyros (Iliad 9.667–668); the Cypria has a storm landing; Sophocles, Euripides, Ovid, Hyginus and Statius have Thetis hide him there disguised as a girl, which is incompatible with his knowing choice of fates.

  5. In which book does Achilles actually commit to dying at Troy, and what motivates him?
    Answer

    Book 18 (18.98–126), after Patroclus' death; the motive is vengeance on Hector, not glory for its own sake.

  6. Name the four ancient positions on the Achilles–Patroclus bond and one source for each.
    Answer

    Silence or ambiguity (Homer); Achilles as lover (Aeschylus, Myrmidons); Achilles as beloved (Plato, Symposium 179e–180b); chaste comradeship (Xenophon, Symposium 8.31). Aeschines, Against Timarchus 141–150, holds Homer concealed an erotic bond.

  7. Why does the word menis matter theologically?
    Answer

    Following Muellner, menis is the anger of gods at breaches of cosmic order; Achilles is the only mortal to whom it is applied, so his wrath mirrors Apollo's and is ratified by Zeus' nod (1.524–530).

  8. What does the lawsuit on the Shield (18.497–508) have to do with Achilles' refusal in Book 9?
    Answer

    It shows a community settling a killing by blood-price, the ordinary poine that Ajax (9.632–636) says even a brother's killer accepts and that Achilles alone refuses.

  9. Where did the Aethiopis send Achilles after death, and which later text makes the shade regret death?
    Answer

    The Aethiopis has Thetis snatch him from the pyre to the White Island (Leuke); Odyssey 11.488–491 has the shade say he would rather be a landless man's hired labourer than rule the dead.

  10. What did Alexander and Hephaestion do at Troy in 334 BC, and who reports it?
    Answer

    Alexander garlanded Achilles' tomb and Hephaestion garlanded Patroclus' (Arrian, Anabasis 1.12.1); Plutarch, Alexander 15, adds the anointing and naked race.

Carry forward

  1. Achilles is a contained cosmic threat: the Thetis prophecy means his mortality is a design decision by Zeus, and the Iliad's menis is the residue of the god he was prevented from being.
  2. The two fates expose the heroic economy: time and kleos are supposed to be interchangeable, and Achilles' career, from refusing the gifts to the regretful shade in Odyssey 11, is the tradition's own audit of that exchange.
  3. Every later Achilles chooses among inherited variants rather than inventing: the Scyros concealment (Statius), the treacherous ambush (Dictys to Shakespeare), the beloved rather than lover (Phaedrus to Miller). Knowing which variant a text picks tells you what that culture wanted from him.
