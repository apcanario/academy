Two festivals: the City Dionysia and the Lenaea

Attic drama was competitive worship, staged twice a year for Dionysus and financed as a public duty. The City Dionysia fell in Elaphebolion (late March), when the seas reopened and allies and foreigners filled the city; the Lenaea in Gamelion (January/February), when the audience was Athenian only, as Dicaeopolis says when he claims he may abuse the city because "no strangers are present" (Aristophanes, Acharnians 502–8). Comedy entered the Lenaea around 440, tragedy about 432, and the Lenaea stayed comedy's home: Acharnians, Knights, Wasps and Frogs were Lenaean.

The Dionysia began with the statue of Dionysus Eleuthereus brought back by torchlight from the Eleutherae road, and a proagon at which each poet presented his unmasked actors. Then the pompe, with sacrificial bulls, phalloi carried by the allies and the choregoi in finery. Before the plays the city displayed itself: the allies' tribute was carried through the orchestra, war orphans paraded in new armour, benefactors were crowned (Isocrates, On the Peace 82; Aeschines, Against Ctesiphon 41–8).

The eponymous archon chose three tragic poets months ahead ("granting a chorus") and assigned each a choregos, a rich citizen who paid for the chorus's training, costume and upkeep; Lysias 21.1–5 prices a tragic chorus at thirty minae. The state paid poet and, from 449, actors. Programme: twenty dithyrambic choruses, five comedies (three in wartime, probably), and three days of tragedy, one poet per day with a tetralogy of three tragedies and a satyr play. Aeschylus preferred connected tetralogies (the Oresteia plus the lost satyr play Proteus); Sophocles and Euripides usually did not. Judging was built against bribery: names from each tribe were sealed in urns, one judge drawn from each at the festival, and of their ten tablets only five were counted (Lysias 4.3). Plutarch, Cimon 8, records the archon in 468 making Cimon's generals judge, and Sophocles beating Aeschylus. The inscribed results, through Aristotle's lost Didascaliae and the hypotheses prefixed to our texts, give us most of our dates.

Where sources disagree. Simon Goldhill ("The Great Dionysia and civic ideology", 1987) read the pre-play ceremonies as a civic frame that tragedy then questioned, making the genre democratic to its core. Jasper Griffin (CQ 1998) replied that the plays concern mythical kings and families and the reading is a projection; Scott Scullion ("Nothing to do with Dionysus", 2002) doubted the Dionysiac setting shaped the plays at all.

Theatre, masks, chorus and the three actors

The stone Theatre of Dionysus on the Acropolis's south slope is Lycurgan (330s) and later. The fifth-century theatre had wooden benches (theatron), an orchestra not yet the circle of Epidaurus, and from at least 458 a wooden skene whose central door served as palace, tent or cave, flanked by two side entrances (eisodoi). Two machines matter: the ekkyklema, a platform rolled out to show an interior tableau (the bodies in Agamemnon 1372ff), and the mechane, a crane for gods, which Trygaeus on his dung beetle begs the operator to handle carefully (Peace 174–6). Plato's "more than thirty thousand" spectators (Symposium 175e) is a boast; the Lycurgan theatre held perhaps fifteen thousand, and Eric Csapo argues the fifth-century cavea nearer six thousand.

All performers were masked, in full-head pieces of stiffened linen, cork or wood, naturalistic beside the later towering onkos; Pollux's twenty-eight tragic masks (Onomasticon 4.133–54) are Hellenistic. Masks let three men play twelve roles and men play women, and fixed the face, so that voice, posture and text carried meaning. The chorus numbered twelve under Aeschylus, fifteen from Sophocles (Life of Sophocles 4), twenty-four in comedy; the dancers were citizen amateurs, and the poet was their didaskalos, "teacher". The three-actor rule is Aristotle's: Aeschylus added the second actor and "gave the lead to speech", Sophocles the third and scene painting (Poetics 1449a15–18). No surviving tragedy needs more than three speakers at once; the doublings are interpretative (one actor may have played both Clytemnestra and Cassandra). From 449 protagonists had their own prize.

Origins: dithyramb, Thespis and the goat

Aristotle derives tragedy "from those who led off the dithyramb", comedy from the leaders of phallic songs, and says tragedy grew out of something "satyr-like" before acquiring dignity (Poetics 1449a9–21). Herodotus 1.23 credits the dithyramb's shaping to Arion at Corinth; the Suda adds that Arion "invented the tragic mode". Herodotus 5.67 mentions "tragic choruses" for the hero Adrastus at Sicyon, transferred to Dionysus by the tyrant Cleisthenes. The Peloponnesians claimed both genres (1448a29–b2).

The Athenian story centres on Thespis of Icaria. The Marmor Parium places his first tragic victory between 538 and 528, under Pisistratus. Tradition gives him the first actor stepping out to "answer" the chorus (hypokrites), masks of white lead and then linen, and a wagon-stage (Horace, Ars Poetica 275–7); Plutarch has Solon rebuke him for "telling such lies" (Solon 29.6). Nothing genuine survives. Of his successors, Pratinas of Phlius is credited with the satyr play, and Phrynichus was fined for making the theatre weep with his Capture of Miletus (Herodotus 6.21). Tragoidia is "goat-song": a goat prize, a goat sacrifice (Walter Burkert, 1966) or singers dressed as goats; nobody knows which.

Where sources disagree. Gerald Else (The Origin and Early Form of Greek Tragedy, 1965) rejected the dithyramb and the ritual model, arguing that Thespis and Aeschylus invented a literary form from epic and Solonian elegy. Most scholars keep a choral-Dionysiac background, but the Cambridge ritualists' dying-god drama is dead and Winkler's ephebe thesis unproven. Between Thespis and Persians lie sixty years we cannot see.

Aeschylus

Born at Eleusis c. 525/4, Aeschylus fought at Marathon (his epitaph, in the ancient Life and Pausanias 1.14.5, names Marathon and not poetry) and probably Salamis; first victory 484, thirteen in all; death at Gela 456/5. Of seventy to ninety plays, seven survive, the oldest drama we possess. His signature is the connected trilogy, in which a curse or divine purpose unfolds across generations and justice itself changes shape. His choruses are vast and theologically searching; his stagecraft, as Oliver Taplin showed (The Stagecraft of Aeschylus, 1977), works through entrances, exits and silences: Cassandra's muteness, the crimson cloth, the Furies' torchlit exit. Aristophanes's Aeschylus in Frogs is the maker of towering diction who taught Athenians to fight (1019–27).

Sophocles

Sophocles (c. 496–406/5), of Colonus, was the most successful dramatist Athens had and a public man: Hellenotamias in 443/2, general with Pericles against Samos in 441/0, one of the probouloi after Sicily in 413, and after death honoured as the hero Dexion. Some 123 plays, eighteen Dionysia victories, never third (Life). He beat Aeschylus at his first attempt in 468 and in 406 brought his chorus on in mourning for Euripides. Reported innovations: the third actor, scene painting, the chorus raised to fifteen, a treatise On the Chorus (Suda), and the self-contained play in place of the trilogy. His seven plots hinge on a single, isolated will meeting a limit, whose refusal is at once catastrophe and distinction; he is the master of peripeteia and late recognition, and the Poetics is in effect a theory of Oedipus Tyrannus. He is also the most steadily religious of the three: oracles come true, gods are present and opaque.

Euripides

Euripides (c. 485/480–406) was by tradition born on Salamis, knew Anaxagoras, Protagoras and Socrates, won only four times in life, and died at the court of Archelaus of Macedon. He was the most performed dramatist of later antiquity, which is why nineteen plays survive: seven from the same school selection as the other two sevens, and the "alphabetic" ten (Helen to Hippolytus, with Cyclops) from a chance-preserved volume of a complete edition. He is the great disturber. A prologue monologue lays out the backstory; a god on the crane often imposes an ending the action has not earned; between come formal debates, monody in the new music, slaves and women at the centre, heroes in rags. Aristophanes spent three comedies on him for teaching the people "to chatter" (Frogs 954–8). Aristotle calls him "the most tragic of the poets" while faulting his plots (Poetics 1453a29).

Where sources disagree. The Macedonian exile rests on late sources; Scott Scullion ("Euripides and Macedon", CQ 2003) argued it is spun from the plays. Rhesus, transmitted as his, was doubted by an ancient hypothesis; most moderns place it in the fourth century, though Ritchie (1964) defended it.

The satyr play and Cyclops

The fourth play of a tetralogy was a satyr play: shorter, in tragic metre, with a chorus of satyrs led by their father Silenus in horse-tails and phalloi, treating a heroic myth as burlesque with stock motifs of captivity, monster, marvellous invention and cowardly bragging. Horace (Ars Poetica 220–50) calls it relief for an audience "drunk and lawless". Papyri preserve much of Aeschylus's Net-Haulers and Sophocles's Trackers. Euripides's Cyclops is the only complete one; date uncertain, perhaps 408 with Orestes (Seaford), perhaps the 420s. Silenus and the satyrs are Polyphemus's slaves on Etna. Odysseus trades wine for food, is betrayed by Silenus, watches two men eaten, gets the Cyclops drunk and blinds him with the heated stake while the satyrs cheer and invent excuses not to help. Odyssey 9 as farce, an hour after Agave's severed head.

Aristotle's Poetics: plot, hamartia, catharsis

The Poetics (c. 335) is lecture notes, incomplete (the book on comedy is lost), by a reader of plays seventy years after our last tragedy. Tragedy is "an imitation of an action that is serious, complete and of magnitude ... through pity and fear accomplishing the katharsis of such emotions" (1449b24–8). Of its six parts, plot is "the soul" (1450a38), spectacle "least artistic". The best plot is complex, with peripeteia and anagnorisis coinciding as in Oedipus (1452a22–33); its hero is "between the extremes" and falls "not through vice but through some hamartia" (1453a7–17).

Hamartia is not "tragic flaw". In the Nicomachean Ethics (1135b) hamartema is a mistake made in ignorance of a particular: Oedipus did not know the man at the crossroads. Bremer (Hamartia, 1969) and Stinton (1975) dispute whether it shades into culpable error; Stinton's "range of meanings" is the consensus. Catharsis appears once in the Poetics, unexplained; Politics 8.1341b–1342a describes a musical katharsis that relieves "as if by medical treatment". Bernays (1857) read purgation; Lessing purification; Golden and Nussbaum (The Fragility of Goodness, 1986) clarification; Halliwell (Aristotle's Poetics, 1986) a medical metaphor for emotions properly felt, not evacuated.

Where sources disagree. How far to trust it: it is the only criticism by someone who read hundreds of lost tragedies, and it ignores Dionysus, the festival, politics, the gods and the chorus (1456a25). Its model is Oedipus; it has no room for the trilogy, for Persians or for Bacchae. Halliwell defends it as a theory of a core type; Vernant and Goldhill call it a fourth-century misreading. The "unities" are not in it: 1449b12 only notes tragedy "tries" to stay within one day; Castelvetro invented the rest in 1570.

Old Comedy and Aristophanes

Comedy entered the Dionysia in 486 (Suda); Cratinus and Eupolis survive in fragments. Aristophanes (c. 446–c. 386) wrote about forty plays from 427; eleven survive, the only complete Old Comedies. Costume was padded belly and buttocks with a leather phallus; the chorus of twenty-four might be men, wasps, birds or clouds. The form has set parts: a prologue states a fantastic idea; the parodos brings on a usually hostile chorus; the agon is a formal debate the hero wins. Then the parabasis: with the actors gone, the chorus "steps forward" and addresses the audience in a fixed sequence (kommation; anapaests, often in the poet's own voice; pnigos; ode, epirrhema, antode, antepirrhema). Here Aristophanes talks about himself: Cleon's prosecution over Babylonians (Acharnians 628–64), his career (Knights 507–50), his third place with Clouds (518–62). Episodes then test the idea on a parade of intruders, and an exodos of feast or wedding closes. The parabasis shrinks in Frogs and vanishes in Assemblywomen and Wealth: Old Comedy turning Middle before our eyes.

The satire is personal, onomasti komoidein, abusing real men by name, above all Cleon. Yet Cleon was elected general weeks after Knights won, and Socrates lived twenty-four years after Clouds. The alleged decree of Syracosius (415) banning abuse by name rests on a scholion to Birds 1297 and may be invented.

Where sources disagree. Was Aristophanes a conservative with a programme, a festival safety-valve, or democratic accountability in costume? Jeffrey Henderson (1990) sees a citizen-adviser meant to be taken seriously; Stephen Halliwell stresses the ritual frame that made abuse unreal; Malcolm Heath (Political Comedy in Aristophanes, 1987) doubts persuasion was intended. Plato's Apology 18b–19c already names Aristophanes among Socrates's "old accusers".

Menander, New Comedy, and six plays to read first

By the 380s comedy had shed its political target, its parabasis and most of its chorus ("Middle Comedy": Antiphanes, Alexis). New Comedy, from about 320, is the comedy of private life in a city that no longer governs itself: a lover, a girl who proves to be a citizen's lost daughter, a soldier, a courtesan, a clever slave, an angry father, a recognition by tokens, a wedding. The chorus is a band of revellers whose entrances mark five acts (manuscripts write only XOPOY); prologues are spoken by Chance (Aspis) or Ignorance (Perikeiromene). Menander (342/1–291/0), pupil of Theophrastus, wrote over a hundred plays and won eight times; Aristophanes of Byzantium asked, "O Menander, O life, which of you imitated the other?" His text vanished from the Byzantine schools and returned on papyrus, above all the Bodmer codex (1958) with the complete Dyskolos (316; the misanthrope Cnemon falls down his well and is reconciled), most of Samia, and much of Epitrepontes. Through Plautus and Terence his plots reach Shakespeare and Molière; Menander, not Aristophanes, is the ancestor of European comedy.

Six to read first

  1. Aeschylus, Agamemnon, then the rest of the Oresteia: the only complete trilogy, the densest poetry, and the template every later Orestes answers.
  2. Sophocles, Oedipus Tyrannus: Aristotle's model; read it before the Poetics so you can test the theory against the play.
  3. Sophocles, Antigone: the cleanest clash of two kinds of right, and the tragedy later philosophy and politics have used most.
  4. Euripides, Medea: his whole method in one evening, prologue, debate, a woman at the centre, a superhuman exit, no consolation.
  5. Euripides, Bacchae: the one tragedy about the festival's god, with the chorus restored to power.
  6. Aristophanes, Frogs: the play that discusses the others, Aeschylus against Euripides judged by Dionysus, with every Old Comedy convention on show and the city about to fall.

A second six: Persians, Hippolytus, Trojan Women, Philoctetes, Lysistrata, Dyskolos.

Self-test

  1. In which months did the City Dionysia and the Lenaea fall, and why did that matter for the audience?
    Answer

    Dionysia in Elaphebolion (March/April), when the open seas brought allies and foreigners; Lenaea in Gamelion (January/February), Athenians only, as Acharnians 502–8 exploits.

  2. What did a choregos pay for, and what did Lysias 21 say a tragic chorus cost?
    Answer

    Training, costume, upkeep and victory feast of the chorus; about thirty minae. The state paid poet and actors.

  3. How were the ten judges chosen, and how many votes were counted?
    Answer

    One drawn at the festival from each of ten sealed tribal urns; only five of their ten tablets were drawn and counted.

  4. Which innovations does Aristotle give to Aeschylus and to Sophocles in Poetics 1449a?
    Answer

    Aeschylus: second actor, reduced chorus, primacy of speech. Sophocles: third actor and scene painting.

  5. What does the Marmor Parium tell us about Thespis, and what does Horace add?
    Answer

    A first tragic victory between 538 and 528, under Pisistratus. Horace (Ars Poetica 275–7) adds the wagon.

  6. Why do many scholars doubt that Aeschylus wrote Prometheus Bound?
    Answer

    Griffith (1977) showed its metre, vocabulary, chorus and stagecraft differ from the other six; West proposed Euphorion, c. 440–430. Antiquity never doubted it.

  7. Why do nineteen plays of Euripides survive against seven each of the others?
    Answer

    Seven come from the selected school edition like the other sevens; ten more (Helen to Hippolytus, with Cyclops) survived by chance in one volume of an alphabetical complete edition.

  8. What does hamartia mean in Aristotle, and what does it not mean?
    Answer

    An error made in ignorance of a particular fact, like Oedipus at the crossroads; not a moral "tragic flaw". Bremer and Stinton debate how far it reaches toward culpable error.

  9. Name the parts of a parabasis and say what Aristophanes uses it for.
    Answer

    Kommation, anapaests, pnigos, ode, epirrhema, antode, antepirrhema. The chorus speaks for the poet: Cleon's prosecution (Acharnians), his career (Knights), his defeat with Clouds.

  10. What replaced the chorus in New Comedy, and which Menander play survives complete?
    Answer

    Revellers whose entrances mark the five acts, noted only as XOPOY. Dyskolos (316), from the Bodmer papyrus, 1958.

Carry forward

  1. Attic drama was competitive civic worship of Dionysus, paid for by liturgy and judged by lot; read every play as one entry on one day of a festival the whole city attended.
  2. The three tragedians are three answers to the same inherited myths: Aeschylus's justice evolving across a trilogy, Sophocles's single will meeting its limit, Euripides's unconsoled scrutiny of cruelty and gods. Keep the Theban and Argive cycles as the spine on which all three hang their variations.
  3. Aristotle's Poetics is a fourth-century theory of one kind of plot; use it as a tool for Oedipus, not the law of the genre, and treat hamartia and catharsis as contested words, not settled doctrine.
