01What "renaissance" means here

The label comes from a 1981 Athens symposium published as The Greek Renaissance of the Eighth Century B.C. (ed. Robin Hägg, 1983), and Anthony Snodgrass gave it analytical shape in Archaic Greece (1980) as a "structural revolution". The claim is not that Greece recovered what Mycenae had lost; the eighth century produced things the palaces never had: an alphabet with vowels, the polis, Panhellenic festivals, monumental temples built by communities rather than kings, written poetry and a conscious relationship with a heroic past. Almost every strand below has its first secure archaeological horizon between roughly 800 and 700 BC, and almost every strand is also contested in its date or cause. Keep the two facts together.

02The alphabet: borrowing and earliest evidence

Herodotus 5.58 says the Phoenicians who came with Cadmus brought letters to Boeotia and that the Ionians still called them phoinikeia grammata. The letter names (alpha, beta, gamma) are meaningless in Greek and transparent in Semitic (aleph "ox", beth "house"), the letter order is Phoenician, and the earliest Greek letter-forms match Phoenician forms of the ninth and early eighth centuries. The Greek contribution was to reassign the Phoenician consonant signs the language did not need (aleph, he, yod, ayin, waw) to vowels, making the script fully phonetic for the first time anywhere.

The earliest inscriptions cluster around 770–720 BC and are already literary in ambition:

  • The Dipylon oinochoe (Athens, c. 740–730), a wine jug scratched with a hexameter and a garbled second line: a prize for "whoever of all the dancers now dances most nimbly". It uses the Attic local script.
  • Nestor's cup (Pithekoussai, Ischia, c. 740–720), a Rhodian kotyle from a child's cremation, inscribed in Euboean letters with one iambic-like line and two hexameters:
    Nestor's cup I am, good to drink from.
    The joke, that whoever drinks will be seized by desire for Aphrodite, presupposes an audience that knows an epic Nestor with a famous cup (compare Iliad 11.632–637).
  • Scraps from Lefkandi, Eretria, Methone and the Osteria dell'Osa jar in Latium (c. 775, five letters, reading disputed) push the date of first use back towards 800.

Within two generations the script had split into local varieties (Kirchhoff's "red", "blue" and "green" groups), which implies either very rapid diffusion from one point or several near-simultaneous adaptations.

Where sources disagree. Joseph Naveh (1973) argued from letter-forms that the borrowing happened c. 1100 and that three centuries of writing on perishable material are simply lost; most epigraphists (Jeffery, Johnston) hold to c. 800 or a little before, because the alphabet shows no trace of the changes Phoenician script underwent between 1100 and 800. The place is equally open: Euboea, Cyprus, Crete, Rhodes and the Syrian coast all have advocates. Barry Powell (Homer and the Origin of the Greek Alphabet, 1991) proposed that one Euboean adapter devised the vowels specifically to record hexameter poetry; the earliest texts being verse fits his thesis, but most scholars think commercial and ownership uses simply do not survive.

03Euboeans abroad: Al Mina and Pithekoussai

Euboea, with Lefkandi (the rich Toumba "heroön" burial of c. 950 BC), Eretria and Chalcis, was the Greek region least affected by the post-palatial collapse and the first to re-enter long-distance exchange. Euboean pendent-semicircle skyphoi turn up on Cyprus, at Tyre and along the Levantine coast from the ninth century.

Al Mina, at the mouth of the Orontes in north Syria, was excavated by Leonard Woolley in 1936–37. Its lowest levels (c. 800–700) contain large quantities of Euboean and Cycladic pottery alongside Cypriot and Levantine wares. John Boardman treated it as a Greek trading station, perhaps the point where Greeks learned the alphabet and the Near Eastern motifs that flood into Orientalising art. Others (Graham, Waldbaum, Luke) read it as a native port that Greek traders visited and where some Greek pottery was consumed by locals; there are no Greek burials, no Greek cult and no Greek graffiti securely of that date.

Pithekoussai on Ischia is different: an actual settlement. Strabo 5.4.9 says Eretrians and Chalcidians founded it and prospered from the fertility of the soil and from gold-working until internal quarrels and volcanic activity drove them out; Livy 8.22.5–6 says the Chalcidians moved from Pithekoussai to Cumae on the mainland. Giorgio Buchner's excavations from 1952 revealed the San Montano cemetery (over a thousand graves), the Mazzola metalworking quarter with iron slag and crucibles, and imports from Corinth, Rhodes, Phoenicia, Egypt and Etruria. Nestor's cup came from grave 168. A Late Geometric krater from the site carries the earliest Greek shipwreck scene. Foundation c. 770 makes it the oldest Greek settlement in the west, and its mixed material culture (Semitic graffiti, Levantine amphorae) suggests Phoenicians lived alongside Greeks.

Where sources disagree. Was Pithekoussai a colony (apoikia) or a trading post (emporion)? Buchner and Ridgway stressed its metal trade with Etruria; Strabo's emphasis on agriculture, the size of the cemetery and the family burials point to a permanent community. The Euboean primacy itself is sometimes questioned: the Lelantine War tradition (Thucydides 1.15.3; Herodotus 5.99) has Chalcis and Eretria destroy each other's strength around 700, which would explain why Corinth, not Euboea, dominates the seventh century.

04Homer fixed in writing, and the Peisistratid question

The Iliad and Odyssey are oral-formulaic in technique (Parry, Lord) but reached us as written texts. Three questions follow: when, where, and how much Athens had to do with it.

Ancient dating was guesswork. Herodotus 2.53 puts Homer and Hesiod "four hundred years before my time", about 850; the Contest of Homer and Hesiod makes them contemporaries and rivals. Modern estimates diverge. Richard Janko (Homer, Hesiod and the Hymns, 1982) used the statistical frequency of archaic linguistic features to place the Iliad c. 750–725 and the Odyssey a generation later; Martin West (The Making of the Iliad, 2011) argued for a poet working c. 680–640 who composed with writing over years. Gregory Nagy's evolutionary model denies any single fixing: the poems crystallised gradually through Panhellenic performance, reaching a stable text only in the Hellenistic period.

The Athenian strand rests on late sources. Cicero, De oratore 3.137, says Peisistratus "is said to have first arranged the books of Homer, previously confused, as we now have them"; Pausanias 7.26.13 and Aelian, Varia Historia 13.14 agree. The pseudo-Platonic Hipparchus 228b credits Peisistratus' son Hipparchus with bringing the poems to Athens and compelling the rhapsodes at the Panathenaia to perform them in sequence; Diogenes Laertius 1.57 attributes the same relay rule to Solon. Josephus, Against Apion 1.12, claims Homer left nothing in writing and that the songs were later stitched together from memory. A scholiast on Pindar, Nemean 2.1, says Cynaethus of Chios, of the Homeridae, interpolated many verses and first recited Homer at Syracuse in 504/501.

Where sources disagree. Friedrich Wolf (1795) took the Peisistratid recension as the moment the text was created; Minna Skafte Jensen (1980) revived a strong version, dating the written poems to sixth-century Athens. Most Homerists now accept a weaker form: the texts existed earlier in Ionia (the dialect is Ionic, the Atticisms thin and superficial), but Athens was the "Panathenaic bottleneck" through which the vulgate passed, which is why nearly all medieval manuscripts descend from an Attic exemplar. Whether an eighth-century poet wrote, dictated, or never touched writing remains uncertain, and the Nestor's cup joke is the only external evidence that epic Nestor was known to Greeks by c. 720.

05Hesiod: a person with a biography

Hesiod is the first Greek who tells us his name, his father's history and his grievances. In Works and Days 633–640 his father left Aeolian Cyme in Asia Minor fleeing poverty and settled at Ascra under Helicon in Boeotia, "bad in winter, hard in summer, never good". In Theogony 22–34 the Muses met him pasturing lambs on Helicon and gave him a laurel staff. In Works and Days 650–662 he crossed to Chalcis for the funeral games of Amphidamas and won a tripod with a song; Plutarch, Moralia 153f, says Amphidamas died fighting Eretria, which would tie Hesiod to the Lelantine War c. 700. The poem is addressed to his brother Perses, who bribed "gift-devouring kings" to win more than his share of their inheritance (27–41; the phrase at 38–39 and 263–264).

The world of Works and Days is a village of independent farmers under local basileis who judge in the agora. The farm has oxen, a few slaves (dmoes), a hired man and woman taken on seasonally, a ploughman ideally forty years old (441). Time is read from the stars: plough when the Pleiades set (383–384), reap when they rise; sail only in the fifty days after the solstice (663–694). Hesiod recommends one son to keep the estate whole (376–380), a glimpse of land pressure. His theology is moral: Zeus watches the crooked judgement, and the just city flourishes while the unjust starves (225–247). The five races of men (109–201) place the heroes who fought at Thebes and Troy as a separate race before our iron age.

Where sources disagree. West (1966) dated the Theogony c. 730–700 and made Hesiod older than Homer; Janko's linguistic statistics put both Hesiodic poems after the Odyssey, c. 700–650. Attempts to date the star calendar astronomically give results spread over two centuries. Some (Lamberton, Nagy) treat "Hesiod" as a traditional persona like "Homer"; the specificity of Ascra, Perses and Amphidamas persuades most that a real poet stands behind at least Works and Days. Pausanias 9.31.4 reports that the Boeotians of Helicon accepted only Works and Days as genuine.

06Panhellenic sanctuaries

Catherine Morgan (Athletes and Oracles, 1990) showed that Olympia and Delphi grew from local cult places into inter-regional meeting grounds during the eighth century, with bronze dedications (tripods, figurines) multiplying sharply after c. 800. François de Polignac (1984) argued that extra-urban sanctuaries defined the territory of emerging poleis; the Panhellenic ones did the same for the Greek world as a whole.

The 776 date comes from the victor list compiled by Hippias of Elis around 400 BC, naming Coroebus of Elis as first stadion winner (preserved via Eusebius' Chronicle). Plutarch, Numa 1.4, already doubted its reliability. Pausanias 5.8.5 records a tradition that the games had lapsed and were refounded by Iphitus of Elis with Lycurgus of Sparta, whose names stood on a bronze discus at Olympia (5.20.1; Plutarch, Lycurgus 1.1, citing Aristotle).

Why festivals mattered: Greeks had no shared state, king or capital, and Iliad 2.684 still uses "Hellenes" for Achilles' Thessalians only (Thucydides 1.3 noticed this). What they had was shared gods and shared competition. Herodotus 8.144 later defines Greekness as common blood, language, "shrines of the gods and sacrifices", and customs; the eighth century is when the third item acquired places. The sacred truce (ekecheiria) let men from warring cities meet; the games ranked them by a common standard; the oracle gave colonists (Thera to Cyrene, Herodotus 4.150–159) a neutral authority. Hesiod's Panhellenes (Works and Days 528) is the first appearance of the word.

Where sources disagree. Alfred Mallwitz (1988) argued from the stratigraphy of the stadium and wells that organised games cannot predate c. 700, making 776 a back-calculation. Paul Christesen (Olympic Victor Lists, 2007) treats Hippias' early entries as reconstruction. Morgan accepts that cult activity intensified around 776 even if the formal festival is later. The Olympia and Delphi evidence is good; Isthmia and Nemea are eighth-century only in the loosest sense.

07The first temples and the Geometric style

Mycenaean religion had no freestanding temple. The eighth century invented the type: a long hall housing a cult image, oriented to an altar outside, built by a community. The earliest are mudbrick on stone socles with wooden posts, not stone buildings; the all-stone Doric temple belongs to the seventh century (Isthmia, c. 690–650; Corinth's old temple; Thermon). Key eighth-century examples:

  • Samos, Heraion, Hekatompedon I (c. 800): a hundred-foot hall with a central row of posts; Hekatompedon II (c. 670) added the first peristyle.
  • Eretria, Apollo Daphnephoros: an apsidal hut of c. 800 (the "Daphnephoreion"), then a hekatompedon c. 750.
  • Dreros, Crete (c. 750–700): small temple with a central hearth and three hammered bronze statuettes of Apollo, Leto and Artemis, the earliest surviving Greek cult images.
  • Terracotta temple models from Perachora and the Argive Heraion (c. 750–700) show steep thatched or tiled roofs over apsidal and rectangular plans.

The painted pottery of the period gives it its name. After Protogeometric (c. 1050–900), Attic workshops built decoration from compass-drawn circles, meanders and zigzags; around 760 the Dipylon Master reintroduced the human figure on monumental funerary vases. The Dipylon amphora (Athens NM 804) shows the prothesis, the laying-out of the dead woman, with mourners in silhouette with triangular torsos. Late Geometric kraters add chariot processions, sea battles and the occasional mythical candidate (the conjoined "Siamese twins" read as the Aktorione-Molione of Iliad 11.709). Nicolas Coldstream's Geometric Greece (1977) remains the handbook; Snodgrass (Homer and the Artists, 1998) argued that very few Geometric scenes illustrate Homer specifically, and most are generic heroic imagery.

08Hero cult at Mycenaean tombs

From about 750 BC Greeks began leaving offerings at Bronze Age tombs that had stood unvisited for four centuries: the tholos at Menidi in Attica, chamber tombs at Mycenae and Prosymna in the Argolid, tholoi in Messenia, the Thorikos tombs. The deposits are Late Geometric cups, kraters and figurines, often broken deliberately. At about the same time cults of named heroes appear with built shrines: Agamemnon at Mycenae, Menelaus and Helen at Therapne near Sparta (both c. 700), Pelops at Olympia, Odysseus at Polis Bay on Ithaca, and the warrior burials at Eretria's West Gate (c. 720–680) over which a triangular heroön was later raised.

The phenomenon has three principal explanations.

  1. Coldstream (JHS 96, 1976) argued that the newly circulating Homeric epics taught Greeks that the great tombs belonged to heroes, and veneration followed. Hector's own tomb is imagined as a landmark (Iliad 7.86–91).
  2. Snodgrass (1982, 1988) reversed the causation: a growing population of farmers moving onto new land encountered old tombs and claimed the dead as ancestors, legitimating possession. The epics reflect the same interest rather than creating it.
  3. Carla Antonaccio (An Archaeology of Ancestors, 1995) separated "tomb cult" (episodic, anonymous, tied to local groups) from "hero cult" proper (named figures, continuous, often polis-sponsored) and denied that the former needs epic at all. James Whitley (JHS 108, 1988) noted the regional contrast: dispersed rural cults in Attica, state-focused ones in the Argolid.

For myth the implications are large. Hesiod's scheme makes the heroes a distinct race, "demigods", who lived before us (Works and Days 156–173). Homer calls them "men of former times" and gives them strength no living man has. The eighth century is when Greeks decided that the ruins around them belonged to that race and that its members could still be reached by sacrifice: myth became local history, anchored to visible places, and cities began to compete for ownership of particular heroes.

Where sources disagree. The chronological hinge is whether offerings precede or follow the likely diffusion of epic. If Janko's early Homer is right, Coldstream's sequence works; if West's seventh-century Homer is right, tomb cult comes first and epic cannot be its cause. The Lefkandi Toumba burial (c. 950) with its later tumulus shows that honouring extraordinary dead predates both.

09The population surge

Snodgrass (Archaic Greece, 1980) counted datable Attic graves and found a roughly sevenfold rise across the eighth century, implying growth rates near 4 per cent a year; he proposed a demographic explosion driving colonisation, land hunger, hero cult and the polis itself. The colonising dates support a surge in the second half of the century: Cumae c. 750, Naxos 734, Syracuse 733, Leontini and Catane 729, Megara Hyblaea 728, Sybaris c. 720, Taras 706 (Thucydides 6.3–5 for Sicily). Odyssey 9.116–141, the empty island off the Cyclopes with soil that "would bear all things in season", reads like a colonist's survey. Hesiod's advice to raise one son and the Theran tradition of drought-driven emigration (Herodotus 4.151) point the same way.

Where sources disagree. Ian Morris (Burial and Ancient Society, 1987) argued that the grave count measures burial visibility, not population: before c. 750 only an elite received formal burial, and afterwards the practice extended to all adults, so the "explosion" is partly social. John Camp (1979) read a cluster of abandoned Agora wells around 700 as a drought and famine, suggesting the surge overshot. Settlement surveys in the Argolid, Boeotia and Euboea nonetheless show real increases in site numbers. The consensus is growth, sustained and significant, but at rates far below Snodgrass's figure; 1 per cent a year compounded over a century would still double the population and strain a village economy of the kind Hesiod describes.

10Self-test

  1. What did Greek adapters do with Phoenician signs such as aleph and he, and why did it matter?
    Answer

    They reassigned consonant signs Greek did not need to write vowels, making the first fully phonetic alphabet; Phoenician had written consonants only.

  2. Where was Nestor's cup found, what script is it in, and what does its joke presuppose?
    Answer

    Grave 168 at Pithekoussai on Ischia, c. 740–720, in Euboean letters; it presupposes an audience familiar with an epic Nestor and his famous cup.

  3. Which ancient author says Eretrians and Chalcidians founded Pithekoussai, and what is the main modern dispute about Al Mina?
    Answer

    Strabo 5.4.9. The dispute is whether Al Mina was a Greek trading post (Boardman) or a native port that Greek traders visited and where some Greek pottery was consumed by locals.

  4. Name the Latin source for the Peisistratid recension and state the weak form of the hypothesis most scholars now accept.
    Answer

    Cicero, De oratore 3.137. Weak form: the poems existed earlier in Ionia but passed through sixth-century Athenian Panathenaic performance, which shaped the surviving vulgate.

  5. Give two biographical facts Hesiod states about himself and the passages that contain them.
    Answer

    His father migrated from Aeolian Cyme to Ascra (Works and Days 633–640); he won a tripod at the funeral games of Amphidamas at Chalcis (650–662). Also the Muses on Helicon (Theogony 22–34) and the dispute with Perses (Works and Days 27–41).

  6. Where does the 776 date come from, and who doubted it in antiquity and in modern scholarship?
    Answer

    Hippias of Elis's victor list, naming Coroebus; Plutarch (Numa 1.4) doubted it, as do Mallwitz (games not before c. 700) and Christesen.

  7. According to Herodotus 8.144, what four things made someone Greek?
    Answer

    Common blood, common language, shared shrines and sacrifices, and shared customs.

  8. Were the first Greek temples built of stone? Give one eighth-century example and its construction.
    Answer

    No; they were mudbrick on stone socles with wooden posts. Samos Hekatompedon I (c. 800) or the Eretria hekatompedon (c. 750). Stone Doric temples begin in the seventh century at Isthmia.

  9. Contrast Coldstream's and Snodgrass's explanations of eighth-century offerings at Mycenaean tombs.
    Answer

    Coldstream: Homeric epic inspired veneration of tombs as heroic. Snodgrass: expanding farming populations claimed old tombs as ancestral to legitimate land; epic reflects the same interest rather than causing it.

  10. What was Ian Morris's objection to Snodgrass's sevenfold population increase?
    Answer

    Grave counts track who received formal burial, not how many people lived; after c. 750 burial extended from an elite to all adults, so much of the rise is a change in practice.

11Carry forward

  1. The eighth century created, rather than restored: alphabet with vowels, polis, temple, Panhellenic festival and written epic all have their first horizon here, and Euboea is the hinge between the Near East and the West.
  2. The Homeric and Hesiodic texts are datable only within a window of roughly 750–650, and Athens shaped their transmission in the sixth century; every later module that cites Homer rests on this uncertainty.
  3. Hero cult at Bronze Age tombs is the moment myth became local history: Greeks fixed the heroic age as a separate race, attached it to visible places, and cities began to own heroes. That link between landscape, cult and story governs everything from Sparta's Menelaion to Athenian tragedy.
