01A religion with no name

Classical Greek had no word for "religion". It had ta hiera ("the sacred things"), ta nomizomena ("the customary things"), eusebeia (right conduct towards gods, parents and guests alike) and therapeia theon, "tending the gods". The gap in vocabulary reflects the absence of the thing. There was no scripture: Homer and Hesiod were poets, not revelation; it was they, Herodotus says, who "gave the gods their epithets and told the Hellenes where each came from" (Herodotus 2.53). There was no creed, because nobody was asked to believe anything, only to do the customary things; Socrates was charged with "not acknowledging the gods the city acknowledges" (Xenophon, Memorabilia 1.1.1), not with false belief. There was no priestly class with doctrine, because priests were office-holders attached to a single sanctuary. Myths contradicted one another freely; this troubled philosophers, not worshippers.

Walter Burkert's Greek Religion (1977; English 1985) starts here: Greek religion is ritual transmitted by imitation, with myth as loose commentary. Robert Parker (Polytheism and Society at Athens, 2005) adds that the rituals belonged to groups existing for other purposes: city, deme, phratry and household were the congregations. Herodotus defines Greekness by "common blood, common language, common shrines of the gods and sacrifices, and like customs" (Herodotus 8.144.2).

Where sources disagree. Burkert treats ritual as primary and belief as nearly irrelevant. Henk Versnel (Coping with the Gods, 2011) and Julia Kindt (Rethinking Greek Religion, 2012) argue that Greeks held beliefs that shaped action, and that Socrates' trial shows belief could become a public issue. Parker is intermediate: belief existed but was never a test of membership.

02Animal sacrifice, step by step

The central rite was thysia: slaughter of a domestic animal followed by a shared meal. The fullest early descriptions are Nestor's heifer for Athena (Homer, Odyssey 3.430–463) and the hecatomb at Chryse (Iliad 1.447–474); Aristophanes parodies it in Peace 948–1022.

  1. Procession. The garlanded animal is led to the altar; a girl carries the basket (kanoun) with the knife hidden under barley. The beast must seem willing.
  2. Lustral water (chernips). Carried round the altar; hands washed; the animal sprinkled, its start read as assent.
  3. Barley (oulai). Thrown at altar and victim; forehead hairs cut and burnt, the first offering and first violence.
  4. The prayer. Standing, arms raised: name the god, state the claim, make the request (model: Chryses, Iliad 1.451–456).
  5. The cut. Axe-blow, then the throat cut with head pulled back so blood sprays upward; the women raise the ololyge; blood splashed on the altar.
  6. The division. The splanchna (heart, liver, lungs, kidneys) are roasted at once and eaten by the inner circle. The thigh bones (meria) are wrapped in fat, topped with pieces from every limb and burnt. The rest is boiled and distributed equally or by lot; the hide went to the priest or was sold (dermatikon accounts, IG II² 1496).
  7. The gods' portion. Bones, fat, smoke (knise), and the tail and sacrum (osphys), whose curling in the fire was an omen. Menander's cook grumbles that men give the gods "the tail-end and the gall-bladder" (Dyskolos 447–453).

Sphagia before battle or at an oath drained blood into the earth uneaten; the holokaustos burnt the whole victim; enagismos for the dead and some heroes went into a pit.

Where sources disagree. Karl Meuli (1946) saw in thysia a survival of hunters' ritual, a "comedy of innocence" disclaiming guilt; Burkert's Homo Necans (1972) built on this: sacrifice channels aggression and binds the group through shared killing. Vernant and Detienne (The Cuisine of Sacrifice, 1979) read the rite instead as culinary and political, defining humans between beasts and gods. F. S. Naiden (Smoke Signals for the Gods, 2013) argues all of them overweight the killing; the guilt reading has little ancient support beyond the Bouphonia aetiology (Porphyry, On Abstinence 2.28–30). Whether the Olympian/chthonian split is an ancient category (Scott Scullion) or a modern schema (Renate Schlesier) is also open.

03Prometheus at Mecone

Hesiod explains the division through a story. At Mecone, when gods and mortals were being separated, Prometheus set out two portions of an ox: the meat hidden in the stomach, and the white bones dressed in gleaming fat. Zeus, seeing through the trick (Hesiod insists), chose the bones, "and from this the tribes of men on earth burn white bones for the immortals" (Hesiod, Theogony 535–557). Zeus withholds fire, Prometheus steals it, and Zeus answers with Pandora and with Prometheus bound (Theogony 558–616; Works and Days 42–105).

The myth is a strict aetiology: it explains a rite whose logic looks odd (why do the gods get the inedible part?) and fixes sacrifice at the moment the human condition was set: men eat meat, cook with stolen fire, labour and die; the gods take the smoke. Burkert noted that Hesiod's insistence that Zeus was not fooled betrays the problem: the rite must honour the god, yet looks like a swindle.

Where sources disagree. The Theogony makes Prometheus's act the origin of sacrifice, the Works and Days the origin of toil and of woman, with no butchering. Aeschylus's Prometheus Bound drops the trick altogether; Apollodorus (Library 1.7.1) keeps only the fire theft. Whether Mecone (later identified with Sicyon) had a commemorative cult is unknown.

04Libations, votives and first-fruits

Libation (sponde) was the commonest ritual act: a few drops of wine from a shallow phiale before drinking, at meals, departures, the symposium and treaties, which is why spondai came to mean "truce". The choe emptied the vessel completely, for the dead and the powers below; Odysseus pours milk-and-honey, wine and water into a pit (Odyssey 11.26–28). Wineless libations (nephalia) went to the Eumenides (Aeschylus, Eumenides 107; Sophocles, Oedipus at Colonus 100, 481).

Votives (anathemata, "things set up") stayed with the god: statuettes, pots, weapons, clothing, hair, or Croesus's gold at Delphi (Herodotus 1.50–51). The logic was charis, pleasure that creates obligation: Hecuba promises Athena twelve heifers and a robe "if she will pity the city" (Iliad 6.302–311). Brauron's inventories list garments dedicated after childbirth (IG II² 1514–1531); Epidaurus and Corinth yield terracotta limbs recording cures.

First-fruits (aparchai) gave the god a share before humans took theirs: a handful of harvest, part of a catch, the first cut of hair. Athens made this policy: the Eleusinian decree (IG I³ 78, about 435–415) orders demes and allies to send a fraction of their grain to Eleusis, and one sixtieth of imperial tribute was an aparche to Athena. Hesiod's household version: "sacrifice to the immortals according to your means" (Works and Days 336–341).

05The god's house, the altar outside, and the civic priest

A temple (naos) housed the god's image and treasure; it was not a hall for worshippers, and the cella was dark and often locked. Worship happened at the altar (bomos), outdoors before the east end, within the temenos. The altar is the older and indispensable element: Zeus at Olympia was worshipped for centuries at a mound of ash before any temple stood, and the ash altar remained the cult centre afterwards (Pausanias 5.13.8–11). Many cults never had a temple; Burkert calls the temple "a votive offering writ large". Inside stood the cult image, an ancient wooden figure like the olive-wood Athena Polias or a showpiece like Pheidias's Athena Parthenos (Pausanias 1.24.5–7): the god's presence, not the god.

Where sources disagree. No text records a priestess or regular sacrifice for the Parthenon, and the old image of Athena Polias was housed elsewhere. Some conclude the Parthenon was chiefly treasury and monument; others think the evidence is simply thin. Uncertain.

Priests (hiereis) and priestesses (hiereiai) belonged to one cult and were defined by office, not learning: they kept the keys, tended the image, presided at sacrifice and took perquisites. They did not preach or monopolise sacrifice; any head of household could kill for Zeus. Some priesthoods were hereditary in a genos (the Eteoboutadai for Athena Polias, the Eumolpidai and Kerykes at Eleusis); the priestess of Athena Nike was chosen by lot "from all Athenian women" (IG I³ 35, about 448); in some Ionian cities priesthoods were sold. Women held office on a scale unmatched elsewhere in public life; Lysimache served Athena Polias for sixty-four years (Pliny, Natural History 34.76). Beside priests stood manteis (seers) and exegetai (advisers on sacred law); the archon basileus oversaw the whole (Aristotle, Constitution of the Athenians 57).

06The household cults

The oikos had its own gods at boundary and centre. Archon-candidates were asked whether they had "an Apollo Patroos and a Zeus Herkeios, and where these shrines are" (Aristotle, Constitution of the Athenians 55.3).

  • Zeus Herkeios, "of the fence", had his altar in the courtyard, the place of household sacrifice and refuge (Odyssey 22.334–336).
  • Hestia, the hearth, took the first and last libation at meals; newborn, bride and new slave were each brought to it and so admitted to the house. She has no myths and no temple of consequence; she is the fire.
  • Zeus Ktesios, "of possessions", lived in the storeroom as a wool-wreathed jar of water, oil and seeds (Autocleides' Exegetikon, at Athenaeus 11.473b–c).
  • Hermes at the door: a herm stood outside, often with an Apollo Agyieus pillar and a small Hecate shrine. Hipparchus set up herms along the roads (Plato, Hipparchus 228d–229a); their mutilation in 415 was sacrilege against the whole city (Thucydides 6.27).
  • Zeus Meilichios, the snake-form Zeus of wealth and purification, took ancestral family sacrifice; Xenophon, told he had neglected it, sacrifices whole pigs as his father did (Anabasis 7.8.1–6).

The kyrios conducted these rites. Plato's hostility to private shrines (Laws 909d–910e) shows how common and uncontrolled they were.

07Miasma and purification

Pollution (miasma) was a condition, not a sin: a contagious disorder that made a person unfit to approach the gods and dangerous to others. Parker's Miasma (1983) is the standard study. Birth polluted mother and house: the Cyrene sacred law (SEG 9.72) rules that the woman in childbed pollutes the roof, and those in the house for three days; births and deaths were forbidden on Delos (Thucydides 3.104.2). Death polluted the household; a bowl of water from another house stood at the door for visitors (Euripides, Alcestis 98–100), and the funerary law of Iulis on Keos (IG XII.5 593) fixes who is polluted and for how long. Homicide was gravest: the killer was barred from shrines, market and city, and even unintentional killers went into exile (Demosthenes 23.72); the pollution attached to the land, as at Thebes under Oedipus (Sophocles, Oedipus Tyrannus 95–101), and to Orestes until Apollo purifies him with pig's blood (Aeschylus, Eumenides 280–283). Athens even tried and expelled the axe or stone that had killed a man (Constitution of the Athenians 57.4). Sex polluted briefly: one washed before entering a sanctuary; priests in office observed stricter abstinence.

Purification (katharmos) used water (the perirrhanteria at sanctuary entrances, sea water for grave cases), sulphur (Odysseus fumigates his hall, Odyssey 22.481–494) and piglet's blood, dripped over the killer or carried round the Pnyx before the assembly met; scapegoats (pharmakoi) were driven out at the Thargelia. Athens purified Delos in 426/5 by removing its graves (Thucydides 3.104.1–2).

Where sources disagree. Louis Moulinier (1952) held that archaic pollution was quasi-physical dirt that later acquired moral content; E. R. Dodds (The Greeks and the Irrational, 1951) traced a shift from "shame-culture" to "guilt-culture". Parker, drawing on Mary Douglas's Purity and Danger (1966), rejected both: pollution and moral fault were entangled from the start but never identical. Ancient writers disagreed too: the Hippocratic On the Sacred Disease (1) mocks purifiers who treat epilepsy as pollution, and Euripides' Iphigenia asks why a goddess who shrinks from a mourner delights in human slaughter (Iphigenia in Tauris 380–391).

08Prayer, oaths, curses and magic

Prayer was spoken aloud, standing, arms raised with palms to the sky (Iliad 1.450; Odyssey 9.526–527); to the powers below one pointed the hands down or struck the earth (Iliad 9.568–569). Kneeling was foreign or servile; Theophrastus's Superstitious Man, who kneels at crossroads and oils smooth stones, is ridiculous (Characters 16). The structure was invocation with cult titles, argument from charis, request.

An oath (horkos) was a conditional self-curse with the gods as enforcers; Hesiod makes Horkos a child of Strife, "who most afflicts men who swear falsely" (Theogony 231–232). Solemn oaths were sworn standing on the cut pieces (tomia) of a victim so the swearer's fate should match the beast's: the Areopagus oath over boar, ram and bull (Demosthenes 23.67–68); the truce-oath at Iliad 3.276–301, wine poured as the perjurer's brains should spill. The ephebic oath calls the boundary stones and crops as witnesses (Lycurgus, Against Leocrates 76–77).

Public curses were a state weapon: the Teos inscription (ML 30, about 470) curses poisoners and traitors with death for them and their line. Private curses took the form of binding tablets (katadesmoi): lead sheets inscribed "I bind X, his tongue, his hands, his soul" before a lawsuit, rivalry or love affair, rolled, sometimes nailed, and dropped into a grave of the untimely dead or a well. Over 1,600 are known, the earliest Sicilian of the later sixth century, with a dense Athenian cluster of legal curses. Plato knew the trade and the fear of it (Republic 364b–c; Laws 933a–e). Erotic spells like Simaetha's (Theocritus, Idyll 2) and itinerant purifiers (goetes) belong to the same world.

Where sources disagree. Frazer and Tylor opposed coercive magic to supplicatory religion; Fritz Graf (Magic in the Ancient World, 1997), Christopher Faraone and Versnel show that the tablets borrow prayer formulae and invoke the same gods, so the line is one of social approval, not technique. The Greek word mageia, from the Persian Magi, was polemical.

09The Athenian year

The lunar year began with the first new moon after the summer solstice, with a thirteenth month intercalated as needed. Each month had fixed sacred days: the first (noumenia), the third for Athena, the fourth for Heracles, Hermes and Aphrodite, the sixth for Artemis, the seventh for Apollo, the eighth for Poseidon and Theseus. The state calendar stood on the Solonian axones, controversially revised by Nicomachus around 400 (Lysias 30). Jon Mikalson's Sacred and Civil Calendar of the Athenian Year (1975) is the guide; Parker counts roughly 120 festival days a year, as the "Old Oligarch" complained (Pseudo-Xenophon, Constitution of the Athenians 3.2).

10State, deme, household, individual

The four levels nested, worshipping largely the same gods under local titles. The state was a corporate worshipper: the demos voted sacrifices, financed hecatombs, appointed hieropoioi and received the gods' charis for all. Impiety (asebeia) was a public crime because divine anger fell on the city. Christiane Sourvinou-Inwood's "polis religion" model (1990) made the city the frame within which all Greek religion had meaning.

The deme was a miniature polis. The Erchia calendar (SEG 21.541, early fourth century) lists some fifty sacrifices a year, roughly 550 drachmas, to gods from Zeus to local heroines. The Rural Dionysia was a deme affair, parodied in Dikaiopolis's private procession (Aristophanes, Acharnians 237–279). Phratries, gene and orgeones occupied the same middle ground.

The household had the kyrios as priest and the family dead tended at the Genesia. The individual had more room than the model allows: people vowed and dedicated, asked Dodona about marriage and money, slept in Asclepius's precinct for a dream cure, bought a binding tablet, or sought initiation into the Mysteries or a Bacchic group whose gold leaves promised a better lot below.

Where sources disagree. Kindt (2012) and Esther Eidinow argue that the polis-religion model marginalises what did not fit the city: magic, Bacchic initiation, private oracles, household superstition. Parker accepts much of this while holding that the city remained the dominant frame. Burkert treated the polis as a late container for rituals far older than any city.

11Self-test

  1. Which Greek terms came closest to "religion", and what do they emphasise?
    Answer

    Ta hiera (the sacred things), ta nomizomena (the customary things), eusebeia (right conduct) and therapeia theon (tending the gods). All emphasise practice and custom, not belief or doctrine.

  2. In what order do the chief acts of a thysia occur?
    Answer

    Procession with garlanded animal and basket; lustral water and hand-washing; barley thrown and forehead hairs burnt; prayer with raised hands; the cut and the ololyge; roasting and sharing of the splanchna, burning of thigh bones in fat; boiling and distribution of the meat.

  3. What exactly do the gods receive from a sacrifice, and which text explains why?
    Answer

    The thigh bones wrapped in fat, the tail and sacrum, and the rising smoke. Hesiod, Theogony 535–557: Prometheus's division at Mecone, where Zeus took the bones dressed in fat.

  4. Distinguish sponde from choe.
    Answer

    A sponde pours a few drops of wine and the rest is drunk; it accompanies meals, departures and treaties. A choe empties the vessel completely and is poured for the dead and chthonic powers, often with milk, honey or water.

  5. Why is it misleading to call a Greek temple a place of worship?
    Answer

    The temple housed the image and treasure and admitted no congregation. Worship, above all sacrifice, happened at the altar outside, which was older and indispensable; many cults had an altar but no temple (Zeus's ash altar at Olympia).

  6. Give two ways a priesthood might be filled in classical Athens.
    Answer

    Hereditarily within a genos (Eteoboutadai for Athena Polias; Eumolpidai and Kerykes at Eleusis) or by lot from all Athenian women (priestess of Athena Nike, IG I³ 35). Elsewhere, by purchase.

  7. Which two household gods did an archon-candidate have to possess, and where did they stand?
    Answer

    Apollo Patroos and Zeus Herkeios (Aristotle, Constitution of the Athenians 55.3). Zeus Herkeios had his altar in the courtyard.

  8. Name the four main sources of miasma and one purifying agent for each.
    Answer

    Birth (waiting days per the Cyrene law); death (water bowl at the door, the fixed days of the Iulis law); homicide (exile, piglet's blood as for Orestes); sex (washing before entering a sanctuary).

  9. How was a solemn oath physically enacted, and what did the gesture mean?
    Answer

    The swearer stood on the cut pieces (tomia) of a sacrificed animal, as in the Areopagus oath over boar, ram and bull (Demosthenes 23.67–68). The gesture is a conditional self-curse: may I perish like the beast if I swear falsely.

  10. In which Athenian month did the Eleusinian Mysteries fall, and in which the City Dionysia?
    Answer

    Boedromion (September/October) for the Mysteries; Elaphebolion (March/April) for the City Dionysia.

12Carry forward

  1. Greek religion is a set of inherited practices, not a set of beliefs: judge later material (mystery cults, philosophy, Christian polemic) by asking what people did, not what they professed.
  2. Sacrifice is the master rite, and its structure (procession, prayer, kill, divide, eat) and its Promethean aetiology recur wherever gods and humans meet; the unburnt meat is the human share and the smoke is the divine one.
  3. The worshipping unit is a social unit that exists for other reasons (city, deme, phratry, house), so changes in Greek politics are always also changes in Greek religion, and pollution, oath and curse are the mechanisms by which the gods police those units.
