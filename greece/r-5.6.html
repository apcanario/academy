01The lyric age and its genres

Between roughly 700 and 450 BC the Greek world produced two things it had not produced before: short poems in which a named individual speaks about the present, and prose arguments about what the world is made of. Both arose on the same Aegean coasts and were carried by the same institutions. The Greeks did not call the first group "lyric" in our sense. For Alexandrian scholars lyrikos meant song to the lyre, and their canon of nine (Alcman, Alcaeus, Sappho, Stesichorus, Ibycus, Anacreon, Simonides, Pindar, Bacchylides) excluded Archilochus, Tyrtaeus, Solon and Theognis, whose metres were recited or sung to the pipe.

Five genres matter. Elegy uses the elegiac couplet, was accompanied by the aulos, and ranges from battlefield exhortation (Tyrtaeus) to political reflection (Solon) and erotic complaint (Mimnermus). Iambus is defined less by its iambic and trochaic metres than by occasion and tone: abuse, obscenity, mock-narrative. Its home was Paros and the cult of Demeter, where ritual insult was licensed. Monody is solo song to the lyre in short stanzas, perfected on Lesbos by Sappho and Alcaeus in Aeolic dialect. Choral lyric was composed for a trained chorus that sang and danced at public festivals; its idiom is Doric-coloured and its scale larger, with myth and prayer woven round the occasion. Epinician, the victory ode for an athlete at the four great games, emerges last, in the late sixth century, as choral lyric commissioned by the very rich.

Where sources disagree. M. L. West (Studies in Greek Elegy and Iambus, 1974) argued that iambos names a genre of performance context, not a metre, since Archilochus used elegiacs for abuse too. Others keep the metrical definition. Whether Sappho's songs were solo pieces for a circle of girls or choral pieces for public occasions is also contested; André Lardinois has argued for a larger choral share than the traditional "monodic Sappho" allows.

02The symposium as setting

Most of this poetry was first heard at the symposium, the after-dinner drinking party of aristocratic men. Seven to fifteen guests reclined in the andron, wine was mixed with water in a krater under a symposiarch, and the evening moved through libations, songs passed round with a myrtle branch, kottabos, flute-girls and talk. Xenophanes fr. 1 describes the clean floor, garlands and incense, and the rule that one should drink only as much as lets one walk home unaided. Alcaeus fr. 346 opens:

Let us drink. Why wait for the lamps? There is a finger of daylight left.

Theognis addresses fellow drinkers; Anacreon's whole persona is sympotic; the Attic skolia, including the Harmodius song, were sympotic rounds.

The setting explains the poems' brevity, their address to a named companion, their audience of equals, their factional politics and their erotic code. It also explains preservation: songs that pleased were resung for generations, which is why the Theognidean corpus accreted. Oswyn Murray's Sympotica (1990) is the standard collection.

Archilochus is dated by his mention of a total eclipse (fr. 122), almost certainly that of 648. He fought as a mercenary on Thasos, threw away his shield and said so (fr. 5), preferred a short bow-legged captain to a tall preening one (fr. 114), and feuded with Lycambes, who had broken off a marriage to his daughter Neobule; the Cologne epode (fr. 196a) narrates the seduction of her younger sister. Tyrtaeus wrote for Spartans in the Second Messenian War: fr. 10 and 12 reshape Homeric vocabulary into an ethic of dying in the front rank for the city. Alcman's Louvre Partheneion (fr. 1), sung by a chorus of girls, praises their leaders Hagesichora and Agido with sunrise and racehorse imagery.

Sappho composed nine books, of which one complete poem (fr. 1, the prayer to Aphrodite, preserved by Dionysius of Halicarnassus) and a few near-complete ones survive, swelled by papyri published in 2004 and 2014. Fr. 16 argues that the most beautiful thing is whatever one loves, taking Helen as proof; fr. 31, quoted in On the Sublime 10, inventories the body's collapse at the sight of a beloved girl. Alcaeus, her contemporary in Mytilene, fought the tyrants Melanchrus and Myrsilus and then his former ally Pittacus; fr. 332 exults over Myrsilus' death, fr. 129 curses Pittacus from exile, and fr. 6 gives the first ship-of-state allegory.

Mimnermus is the poet of youth's brevity: we are like leaves in spring (fr. 2). Solon answered him, wishing for eighty years rather than sixty (fr. 20), and used verse for politics: fr. 4 analyses Athenian dysnomia, fr. 13 asks the Muses for honest wealth, and fr. 36, in iambic trimeters, calls the black Earth to witness that he pulled up her boundary stones. Aristotle, Constitution of the Athenians 5–12, quotes him as evidence. Theognis addresses the boy Cyrnus with aristocratic advice about trusting no one; the 1,400 surviving lines are an anthology grown around a sixth-century core.

Anacreon served Polycrates of Samos and then the Pisistratids, singing of Eros throwing a purple ball (fr. 358) and a Thracian filly who needs a rider (fr. 417). Simonides is the first professional across genres: epinicians, laments (the Danae fragment, fr. 543), Persian War epigrams and the recovered Plataea elegy (fr. 11 West). Pindar's forty-five epinicians survive complete because schools read them: Olympian 1 for Hieron of Syracuse (476) retells and corrects the Tantalus myth; Pythian 1 (470) describes Etna's eruption. Bacchylides, Simonides' nephew, was almost lost until a papyrus reached the British Museum in 1896; Ode 5 celebrates the same Hieron victory as Olympian 1.

Where sources disagree. Theognis' date is uncertain: the Suda places him c. 540, but internal allusions have been used for dates from the 630s to the 480s, and West treats the corpus as stratified. The Thermopylae couplet "Go tell the Spartans" is ascribed to Simonides only by late sources; Herodotus 7.228 names him only for the epigram to the seer Megistias.

04What is new compared with epic

Four things. First, the present: epic narrates a heroic past in a timeless formulaic dialect; lyric speaks of this war, this party, this girl, in local dialect. Second, the speaking I: Homer never names himself; Archilochus, Sappho and Alcaeus speak in their own names about their own feelings, and Sappho fr. 31 is the first sustained description of an inner physiological state in Western literature. Third, revaluation: Archilochus' lost shield, Sappho's preference of the beloved to cavalry, Xenophanes' claim (fr. 2) that a wise man is worth more to a city than an Olympic victor, Tyrtaeus' transfer of arete from the champion to the hoplite line, all bend Homeric values to the polis and the person. Fourth, form: dozens of metres instead of one, strophic music instead of recitation, a scale in lines rather than books.

Where sources disagree. Bruno Snell (The Discovery of the Mind, 1946) read this as the birth of individual self-consciousness. Kenneth Dover and West replied that the lyric "I" is often a performed persona: Lycambes and Neobule may be stock iambic figures, and sympotic song lends its singer a borrowed voice. Gregory Nagy adds that reperformance detaches the "I" from any one historical speaker. The truth is probably in between, and differs poet by poet.

05Why philosophy began in Ionia

Miletus around 600 was the richest Greek city: colonies across the Black Sea, Lydia next door, a trading post at Naucratis in Egypt, overland routes from Mesopotamia. Babylonian eclipse records and Egyptian land-measurement were available to anyone who asked, and Herodotus says Thales did (2.109; 1.74 for his prediction of the 585 eclipse). But borrowed data does not explain the leap to asking what everything is, and three further conditions are usually cited. Ionia had no priestly class with a monopoly on cosmology; Hesiod's Theogony already offered a systematic account, and the Milesians dropped the gods from the system. The polis gave public argument prestige: G. E. R. Lloyd (Magic, Reason and Experience, 1979) argued that open debate in assembly and lawcourt bred a culture in which rival accounts competed before an audience, and Jean-Pierre Vernant linked Anaximander's cosmos, with the earth held at the centre by symmetry, to the ideal of isonomia. Alphabetic writing let a short prose book travel and be criticised by name: Heraclitus attacks Hesiod, Pythagoras, Xenophanes and Hecataeus in one fragment (B40).

Where sources disagree. How much is Near Eastern inheritance is still argued: Walter Burkert and M. L. West (Early Greek Philosophy and the Orient, 1971) stress Iranian and Babylonian parallels; others hold that the move to an impersonal, argued cosmology has no clear eastern precedent. The eclipse prediction is doubted by most historians of astronomy, since no method then available could fix a solar eclipse's visibility at a given place.

06The Milesians and Pythagoras

The Milesians are known through Aristotle (Metaphysics A 3, 983b–984a), Theophrastus' lost Physical Opinions and later compilers such as Simplicius and Diogenes Laertius, cited by Diels–Kranz (DK) numbers. "Presocratic" is a modern label; Democritus outlived Socrates.

Thales (fl. 585): everything comes from water, the earth floats on water, and the magnet shows that soul-like power pervades matter: "all things are full of gods" (Aristotle, On the Soul 405a, 411a). No writing of his survived even in antiquity. Anaximander (c. 610–546): the source is the apeiron, the boundless, which is no named stuff; from it opposites separate out and pay each other penalty for injustice "according to the assessment of time" (B1, via Simplicius). The drum-shaped earth stays put because it is equidistant from everything and has no reason to move (Aristotle, On the Heavens 295b), the first argument from sufficient reason. Anaximenes (fl. 546): the source is air, which becomes fire by rarefaction and wind, cloud, water, earth and stone by condensation. This is the first mechanism: a process by which one stuff becomes the appearances of many.

Pythagoras (c. 570–495) left Samos for Croton about 530 and founded a community with rules of secrecy, diet and conduct. He wrote nothing. Two doctrines are securely his: transmigration of souls, mocked already by Xenophanes (B7), who has him recognise a dead friend's voice in a beaten puppy; and the belief that number and ratio underlie reality, which Aristotle attributes to "the so-called Pythagoreans" (Metaphysics 985b–986a) and connects to the discovery that musical concords are whole-number ratios.

Where sources disagree. Aristotle imposes his own category of material cause on Thales; whether Thales meant that things originated from water or still are water is unknowable. On Pythagoras, Burkert's Lore and Science in Ancient Pythagoreanism (1972) argued that the historical man was a religious wonder-worker and the mathematics was credited to him by Platonists; Leonid Zhmud has defended a genuinely mathematical Pythagoras. The theorem's attribution rests on sources a thousand years later.

07From the critique of the gods to the denial of change

Xenophanes of Colophon (c. 570–475), a wandering poet, turned Ionian inquiry on religion. Homer and Hesiod attributed theft, adultery and deceit to the gods (B11); Ethiopians make their gods snub-nosed and black, Thracians blue-eyed and red-haired (B16); if oxen and horses had hands they would draw gods like oxen and horses (B15). Against this he set one god, greatest among gods and men, unlike mortals in body or thought, who sees, hears and thinks as a whole and shakes everything by thought without moving (B23–26). He observed sea-shells inland (Hippolytus, Refutation 1.14) and inferred that the earth had once been mud. B34 is the first statement of epistemic humility: no man has seen the clear truth about the gods, and one who spoke it would not know.

Heraclitus of Ephesus (fl. c. 500) wrote aphorisms designed to be hard. The logos, the account according to which all things happen, is common to all, yet men live as if they had private understanding (B1, B2). Opposites are one: the road up and down (B60), sea water purest and foulest (B61). Everything is in exchange like goods for gold (B90), and the exchange is fire, "kindling in measures and going out in measures" (B30). Strife is justice (B80); war is father of all (B53). The river fragment B12 says that on those who step into the same rivers different waters flow, a statement about identity through change rather than its denial. He despised polymathy without understanding (B40) and held the soul deep beyond finding (B45).

Where sources disagree. Plato (Cratylus 402a) made Heraclitus the philosopher of universal flux, and the slogan panta rhei and the "cannot step twice" version (B91, via Plutarch) are probably Platonic sharpenings. G. S. Kirk and Miroslav Marcovich argued that the genuine fragments emphasise measure and stability within change; Karl Popper and Jonathan Barnes defended the radical reading. Whether Xenophanes was a monotheist or merely posited a supreme god among lesser ones (B23 says "among gods and men") is also unresolved; Aristotle complained that he "made nothing clear" (Metaphysics 986b).

Parmenides of Elea (born c. 515) wrote a hexameter poem in which a goddess instructs the poet after a chariot ride to the gates of Night and Day. The Way of Truth (B2, B8, preserved by Simplicius) argues from one decision: either it is, or it is not; "it is not" cannot be thought or said, since one cannot think nothing (B3). Therefore what-is is ungenerated (from what could it have come?), imperishable, continuous, unmoving, complete, like a well-rounded ball. Coming-to-be, perishing and change are names mortals have laid down (B8.38–41). The Way of Opinion that follows gives a cosmology of Light and Night which the goddess calls deceptive; it contains the first correct statement that the moon shines with borrowed light (B14–15). Zeno of Elea (c. 490–430), his pupil, defended this by reducing plurality and motion to absurdity. Aristotle (Physics 239b) preserves the four motion paradoxes: the Dichotomy (to cross a stadium one must first cross half, then half the remainder, without end), the Achilles, the Arrow (at each instant it occupies a space equal to itself, so is at rest) and the Moving Rows. Simplicius preserves the plurality arguments: if things are many they are both infinitely small and infinitely large (B1–2).

Where sources disagree. G. E. L. Owen and Barnes read Parmenides as a strict numerical monist (only one thing exists); Patricia Curd's "predicational monism" holds that each real thing must be one and unchanging, which makes the pluralists his heirs rather than opponents. The status of the Doxa, best-possible physics or demonstration of error, is also open. Aristotle's answer to Zeno, that a line is only potentially infinitely divisible, has not satisfied everyone.

08Empedocles, Anaxagoras, Democritus

After Parmenides, a physicist had to explain change without letting anything come from nothing. Three answers followed.

ThinkerUnchanging realsSource of changeKey fragments
Empedoclesfour roots: earth, water, air, fireLove and Strife, in a cosmic cycleB6, B17, B115, B117
Anaxagorasinfinitely many seeds, "everything in everything"Mind (Nous) starts a rotationB11, B12, B17

Empedocles of Acragas (c. 492–432) kept Parmenides' ban on generation by making four eternal "roots" (B6) mix and separate under Love and Strife, which alternately dominate in a cycle (B17). In the stage of separating Strife, limbs wander alone, "faces without necks" (B57), and only viable combinations survive. Perception works by effluences fitting pores (B89). He also wrote as a fallen god, exiled thirty thousand seasons for bloodshed (B115), who has been "boy and girl, bush, bird and silent fish" (B117), and forbade eating flesh as kin-murder (B137). The tale that he died leaping into Etna (Diogenes Laertius 8.69) is legend.

Anaxagoras of Clazomenae (c. 500–428) moved to Athens, taught Pericles, and was prosecuted for impiety for saying the sun is a fiery stone larger than the Peloponnese (Plutarch, Pericles 32). His core claim: nothing comes to be or perishes, but things are mixed and separated (B17); in everything there is a portion of everything (B11), so hair can grow from food; and the original mass was set spinning by Mind, "unlimited, self-ruling and mixed with nothing" (B12). Socrates in Plato's Phaedo (97b–98c) records his disappointment that Anaxagoras introduced Mind and then explained everything mechanically.

Democritus of Abdera (c. 460–370), building on the shadowy Leucippus, answered Parmenides and Zeno at once: reality is uncuttable bodies (atoma) moving in void, differing only in shape, arrangement and position, as A and N, AN and NA, N and Z differ (Aristotle, Metaphysics 985b). Atoms collide, rebound and tangle into infinite worlds by necessity. Sensible qualities are not in things: "by convention sweet, by convention bitter; in reality atoms and void" (B9), to which the senses reply, "wretched mind, you take your evidence from us and then throw us down" (B125). He would rather find one causal explanation than gain the kingdom of Persia (B118).

Where sources disagree. The Strasbourg papyrus of Empedocles (published 1999) has reopened whether On Nature and Purifications were two poems or one; Catherine Osborne and Brad Inwood argue for one. Anaxagoras' trial is placed anywhere from the 450s to 431 and is doubted outright by Jaap Mansfeld. Epicurus denied that Leucippus ever existed (Diogenes Laertius 10.13). Whether Democritus' atoms were physically or conceptually indivisible, and so whether he had really answered Zeno, is argued by David Furley and others.

09Self-test

  1. Which lyric poets did the Alexandrian canon of nine exclude, and why?
    Answer

    Archilochus, Tyrtaeus, Solon and Theognis, because elegy and iambus were recited or sung to the pipe, not sung to the lyre, so were not lyrikos in the strict ancient sense.

  2. How is Archilochus dated, and to what year?
    Answer

    By his reference to a total solar eclipse in fr. 122 West, almost certainly that of 648 BC.

  3. What is the argument of Sappho fr. 16?
    Answer

    That the most beautiful thing is whatever one loves, with Helen as proof; the speaker prefers the beloved to cavalry.

  4. In which fragment and metre does Solon defend his reforms, and whom does he call as witness?
    Answer

    Fr. 36 West, in iambic trimeters; he calls the black Earth, from whom he pulled up the boundary stones, as witness.

  5. Beyond borrowed Egyptian and Babylonian knowledge, name the three conditions usually cited for the birth of philosophy in Ionia.
    Answer

    The absence of a priestly monopoly on cosmology; the polis culture of open public argument (Lloyd, Vernant); and alphabetic literacy enabling short prose books to circulate and be criticised by name.

  6. What is Anaximander's argument for why the earth stays where it is?
    Answer

    It is equidistant from everything at the extremes and so has no more reason to move in one direction than another (Aristotle, On the Heavens 295b), the first argument from sufficient reason.

  7. What did Xenophanes infer from sea-shells found inland?
    Answer

    That the earth had once been mud (Hippolytus, Refutation 1.14).

  8. Why do Kirk and Marcovich doubt that Heraclitus taught universal flux?
    Answer

    Because the genuine fragments (B12, B30, B90) stress measure, exchange and identity through change, while the slogan panta rhei and the "cannot step twice" formulation come from Plato (Cratylus 402a) and Plutarch rather than from Heraclitus' own words.

  9. State Parmenides' central inference in two steps.
    Answer

    "It is not" cannot be thought or said, since nothing cannot be thought (B2, B3); therefore what-is cannot have come from or pass into what-is-not, and so is ungenerated, imperishable, unmoving and complete (B8).

  10. How does Democritus explain the difference between things made of the same atoms?
    Answer

    By shape, arrangement and position of atoms, which Aristotle illustrates with letters: A differs from N in shape, AN from NA in order, N from Z in position (Metaphysics 985b).

10Carry forward

  1. Archaic lyric and Ionian philosophy are products of the same world: the polis, the symposium and the Aegean trade routes gave a named individual the standing to speak in the present tense and to argue in public, which is why Xenophanes is both a sympotic poet and a critic of Homer's gods.
  2. Parmenides is the hinge of Greek thought. Every later physics (Empedocles, Anaxagoras, the atomists) is an attempt to allow change while obeying his ban on coming-to-be from nothing, and Plato's Forms and Aristotle's analysis of change both start from him.
  3. Almost everything here survives in fragments quoted by later writers with their own agendas, Aristotle and Plato above all; always ask who is quoting, why, and what frame they have imposed before treating a Presocratic "doctrine" or a lyric "confession" as the author's own.
