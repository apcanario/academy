Three lost poems and how we read them

The Iliad ends with Hector's funeral; the Odyssey begins ten years later. The gap was filled by three archaic hexameter poems later grouped into the Epic Cycle: the Aethiopis in five books, attributed to Arctinus of Miletus; the Little Iliad in four, attributed to Lesches of Mytilene; and the Iliou Persis ("Sack of Ilion") in two, again given to Arctinus. None survives. We have roughly a page of plot summary for each, a few dozen quoted lines in all, and a vast penumbra of retellings.

The summaries come from the Chrestomathy of a certain Proclus, described by Photius (Bibliotheca cod. 239) and preserved for the Trojan poems in manuscripts of the Iliad, above all the tenth-century Venetus A. Proclus gives author, book count and a bare narrative, trimmed so that the poems run on from one another without overlap. Fragments quoted elsewhere show that the Little Iliad originally narrated the sack as well, with details that contradict the Iliou Persis. Proclus' text is a harmonised storyline, not a faithful abstract of each poem.

Where sources disagree. Proclus' identity is unsettled: some take him for the fifth-century Neoplatonist; most editors (Severyns, Davies, West) prefer the second-century grammarian Eutychius Proclus. The poems' dates are equally uncertain. Ancient chronographers placed Arctinus in the eighth century; West dates the surviving forms of all three to the seventh or sixth.

The other witnesses are of four kinds. Quoted fragments and testimonia in scholia, Pausanias and Tzetzes, collected by Bernabé, Davies and West (Loeb Greek Epic Fragments, 2003). Vase painting from the seventh century on, often older than any surviving text of an episode: the Mykonos relief pithos (c. 670 BC) already shows the Wooden Horse with warriors visible through portholes and, below, the killing of women and children. Fifth-century tragedy, which mined the poems systematically: Aristotle, Poetics 1459b, lists ten plays drawn from the Little Iliad alone, including Judgement of the Arms, Philoctetes, Neoptolemus, Sinon, Sack of Troy and Trojan Women. And late continuous narratives: Virgil's Aeneid 2, told from the Trojan side; Quintus of Smyrna's Posthomerica (probably third century AD), fourteen books covering exactly the ground of the three poems; Tryphiodorus' Sack of Troy; and the mythographers Apollodorus (Epitome 5) and Hyginus.

Where sources disagree. Whether Quintus read the cyclic poems or worked from summaries, scholia and the tragedians is disputed. His sequence matches Proclus closely; but he never contradicts the mythographic consensus where fragments show the archaic poems did, which suggests they were already lost to him.

The Aethiopis: Penthesilea and Memnon

Proclus opens with the arrival of Penthesilea, daughter of Ares, "Thracian by birth", with her Amazons. Later sources (Apollodorus, Epitome 5.1; Quintus 1.18–32) explain she sought purification after accidentally killing her sister Hippolyta. She fights with distinction; Achilles kills her; the Trojans bury her. Thersites, the railer of Iliad 2, mocks Achilles for his "alleged love" for the dead Amazon, and Achilles kills him. A quarrel follows; Achilles sails to Lesbos, sacrifices to Apollo, Artemis and Leto, and is purified by Odysseus. The love motif is thus already in the poem as rumour. Art made it the centre: Exekias' amphora in London (c. 530 BC) shows Achilles' spear in the throat of a fallen Penthesilea who looks up at him; the Penthesilea Painter's cup in Munich (c. 460 BC) makes that exchanged look the whole subject; Quintus 1.657–674 and Propertius 3.11.13–16 say outright that she conquered her conqueror once the helmet came off. Note the structure: the killing of Thersites is a stain, and Odysseus removes it, foreshadowing his inheritance of Achilles' arms.

Memnon, son of Eos and Tithonus, king of the Ethiopians (Hesiod, Theogony 984–985), arrives in armour made by Hephaestus. He kills Antilochus, Nestor's son, who had thrown himself between his father and the enemy (Pindar, Pythian 6.28–42; cf. Odyssey 4.187–188). Achilles kills Memnon; Eos obtains immortality for her son from Zeus. The episode deliberately doubles the Iliad: a friend's death drives Achilles to kill an enemy champion mourned by a divine mother, and the victor's own death is the price.

The weighing of souls (psychostasia) is the best-known element of the Memnon story and is absent from Proclus. Its model is Iliad 22.209–213, where Zeus weighs the death-fates of Achilles and Hector. Aeschylus wrote a Psychostasia in which Zeus weighed the souls of Achilles and Memnon with Thetis and Eos pleading on either side (Plutarch, Moralia 17a; Pollux 4.130). Attic vases of c. 520–470 BC show Hermes holding a balance with two small winged figures in the pans while the mothers watch.

Where sources disagree. Whether the Aethiopis contained a weighing is unknown; Proclus omits much. Some scholars think the vases reflect the epic, others Aeschylus, others a free-floating motif with Egyptian antecedents. Homer weighs keres, death-fates; the vases and Aeschylus weigh souls, and the pleading mothers add a courtroom logic foreign to the Iliad.

The death and funeral of Achilles

Achilles routs the Trojans, bursts into the city, and is killed "by Paris and Apollo". The double agency is Homeric: dying Hector foretells it at Iliad 22.359–360, and Xanthus the horse had said he would fall "to a god and a man" (19.416–417). Pindar, Paean 6, has Apollo take Paris' likeness; Hyginus, Fabulae 107, keeps the disguise and puts the wound in the ankle. In the Aethiopis a battle rages over the body: Ajax carries it to the ships while Odysseus holds off the Trojans, the composition on the handles of the François Vase (c. 570 BC). This partnership is the seed of the later quarrel.

The heel is late. The earliest evidence for an arrow in the lower leg is a Chalcidian amphora of c. 540 BC (lost, known from drawings); Apollodorus, Epitome 5.3, also says the ankle. But nothing archaic or classical makes it his only vulnerable point; Asteropaeus wounds his arm at Iliad 21.166–167. Invulnerability appears first in Statius, Achilleid 1.133–134 and 1.269–270, where Thetis dipped the infant in the Styx; that she held him by the heel is spelled out only by Servius on Aeneid 6.57. Apollodorus, Library 3.13.6, knows an older-looking story: Thetis laid the baby in the fire by night to burn away his mortality and Peleus interrupted her.

Where sources disagree. A different death circulates late: Achilles lured to the sanctuary of Thymbraean Apollo by a promise of marriage to Polyxena and ambushed by Paris and Deiphobus (Dictys 4.11; Dares 34; Hyginus 110). It has no archaic authority but became the standard medieval version.

The funeral in the Aethiopis: Thetis arrives with the Muses and Nereids and laments; then she snatches her son from the pyre and carries him to the White Island (Leuke) in the Black Sea; the Achaeans heap a mound and hold games. Odyssey 24.36–94 gives seventeen days of mourning, the Muses singing antiphonally, the bones placed with those of Patroclus in a golden amphora, the mound on the headland above the Hellespont, and games with prizes from Thetis.

Where sources disagree. The two accounts cannot both be right about where Achilles ended. In Odyssey 11.467–540 his shade is in Hades and resents it; in the Aethiopis he is translated bodily to Leuke, where he received historical cult; Pindar, Olympian 2.79–80, sends him to the Isle of the Blest. Monro observed that the Odyssey never narrates an event told in the Cycle; whether this means Homer knew and avoided the poems, or that the poems were composed to fill his gaps, remains the central question of Cycle scholarship (Burgess 2001 argues the tradition is older than Homer; West 2013 that the poems as we have them are later).

The Judgement of the Arms and the madness of Ajax

The Aethiopis ends and the Little Iliad begins with the quarrel of Ajax and Odysseus over the arms. In the Little Iliad, on Nestor's advice, spies listened under the Trojan walls and overheard two girls: one said Ajax was the braver because he carried the body; the other, prompted by Athena, replied that any woman can carry a load but would not stand and fight (fr. 2 West, in the scholia to Aristophanes, Knights 1056). Homer differs: at Odyssey 11.543–567 "the sons of the Trojans and Pallas Athena" judged, and Ajax's shade will not speak to Odysseus even in Hades. Pindar makes it a secret ballot corrupted by eloquence (Nemean 7.20–27, 8.23–34).

Ajax, driven mad, slaughters the Achaean herds and kills himself at dawn. Exekias' amphora in Boulogne (c. 540 BC) shows him alone, fixing the sword hilt in the earth. Sophocles' Ajax (probably 440s) is the fullest treatment: Athena displays the deluded hero to Odysseus; Ajax recovers and delivers the deception speech (646–692); alone on stage he plants the sword Hector gave him (Iliad 7.303–305) and falls on it (815–865), apparently in view of the audience. The second half is the fight over burial, which Odysseus, the enemy, secures against the Atreidae. That has epic backing: Little Iliad fr. 3 West says that because of Agamemnon's anger Ajax was laid in a coffin, not cremated, the only hero at Troy denied the pyre. Ovid adds the hyacinth lettered AI AI (Metamorphoses 13.394–398).

The Little Iliad: Philoctetes, Paris, Neoptolemus, the Palladium

With Achilles and Ajax dead the war can only be ended by oracle. Odysseus captures the Trojan seer Helenus, who reveals the conditions. Diomedes fetches Philoctetes from Lemnos, where he had been abandoned with his festering snake-bite; Machaon heals him; he kills Paris with the bow of Heracles. Menelaus mutilates the corpse; the Trojans bury it. Deiphobus marries Helen, which the Odyssey presupposes when Menelaus and Odysseus go to "the house of Deiphobus" on the night of the sack (8.517–520).

Where sources disagree. The Little Iliad has Diomedes alone fetch Philoctetes. Aeschylus made it Odysseus; Euripides (431 BC) Odysseus and Diomedes; Sophocles (409 BC) Odysseus and Neoptolemus, which puts Neoptolemus at Troy before Philoctetes, reversing Proclus' order (Dio Chrysostom, Oration 52 compares the plays). Paris' death also grows: Apollodorus, Library 3.12.6, Quintus 10.259–489 and Ovid, Heroides 5 have the wounded Paris carried to the nymph Oenone, who refuses to heal him and then kills herself. None of this is in Proclus.

Odysseus brings Neoptolemus from Scyros, gives him his father's arms, and Achilles' ghost appears to him. Eurypylus, son of Telephus, arrives with a Mysian army bought by Priam's gifts and is killed by Neoptolemus (Odyssey 11.519–521). Epeius, by Athena's design, builds the Wooden Horse. Odysseus disfigures himself, enters Troy as a beggar, is recognised by Helen and plans the capture with her (cf. Odyssey 4.242–264). Then Odysseus and Diomedes steal the Palladium, the image of Athena on which Troy's safety depended. The leaders enter the horse; the rest burn the camp and sail to Tenedos; the Trojans breach their own wall to bring the horse in.

The Palladium's afterlife is long. Later Greek versions make the theft a test: Diomedes carried the image, Odysseus tried to kill him from behind, Diomedes saw the sword's shadow in the moonlight and drove him back at sword-point, the "Diomedean compulsion" of the proverb collectors (Conon, Narratives 34). Argos and Athens both claimed the real image. Rome claimed most: Aeneas brought it, and it lay in the temple of Vesta as the pledge of empire (Dionysius of Halicarnassus 1.68–69; Ovid, Fasti 6.421–436). Virgil's Sinon makes the theft the Greeks' sacrilege against Athena (Aeneid 2.162–170).

The Wooden Horse: Laocoön, Sinon, Cassandra

The Iliou Persis opens with the Trojans debating the horse: throw it from the cliff, burn it, or dedicate it to Athena. The third view prevails and they feast. Then "two serpents appear and destroy Laocoön and one of his two sons"; Aeneas and his people, alarmed by the portent, withdraw to Mount Ida. Sinon, who had entered the city under false pretences, raises the fire signal; the fleet returns, the men climb out, and the slaughter begins. In Arctinus the serpents strike after the decision is made. They do not warn; they punish.

Virgil reverses the function. In Aeneid 2.13–249 Laocoön alone sees through the gift, hurls a spear into the horse's flank (2.50–53), and is destroyed with both sons by serpents swimming from Tenedos (2.199–233) while the horse still stands outside the walls; the Trojans read the death as punishment for the spear and drag the horse in. Between the two scenes stands Sinon's speech (2.57–198), the pretended deserter who claims to have escaped sacrifice at Odysseus' hands. Sophocles wrote both a Laocoön and a Sinon; Dionysius 1.48 cites the Laocoön for Aeneas' withdrawal to Ida, so Sophocles followed Arctinus there.

Where sources disagree. Arctinus: one son dies, and no offence is stated. Servius on Aeneid 2.201 reports that in Bacchylides and Euphorion the serpents came for a prior fault, Laocoön having lain with his wife before Apollo's image; Hyginus 135 makes him a priest of Apollo who married against the god's will. Virgil: two sons, and the moral ambiguity is the point. The Vatican marble group (Pliny, Natural History 36.37) shows both sons, as in Virgil; whether it depends on Virgil or both on a Hellenistic model is unresolved.

Cassandra is the unheeded prophet. The Iliad knows her only as Priam's most beautiful daughter (13.365–366); her prophetic gift and Apollo's curse that she would never be believed appear first in Aeschylus, Agamemnon 1202–1212. Virgil has her open her lips in vain "by the god's command" (2.246–247); Quintus 12.525–585 gives her a full speech and has the Trojans drive her off with stones; Lycophron's Alexandra is one enormous riddling prophecy in her mouth. In Proclus she appears only at her violation.

The sack

Proclus' sequence is terse. Neoptolemus kills Priam at the altar of Zeus Herkeios in his own courtyard. Menelaus finds Helen, kills Deiphobus, and leads her to the ships. Ajax son of Oileus drags Cassandra away by force, pulling down the image of Athena she clings to; the Greeks mean to stone him, he escapes to Athena's altar, and the goddess's anger falls on the fleet. Odysseus kills Astyanax; Neoptolemus takes Andromache; the spoils are divided. Demophon and Acamas find their grandmother Aethra among Helen's servants. The Greeks burn the city and slaughter Polyxena at Achilles' tomb.

Priam and Astyanax

Priam had foreseen his death at Iliad 22.60–71. The altar scene is in Pindar, Paean 6.112–117, and Euripides, Trojan Women 16–17, and reaches its height in Aeneid 2.506–558: Pyrrhus kills Polites before his father's eyes, drags Priam by the hair through his son's blood, and leaves "a huge trunk on the shore, a nameless body". The Little Iliad told it otherwise: Neoptolemus dragged Priam from the altar and killed him at the door of his house (fr. 25 West, from Pausanias 10.27.2, who notes the discrepancy because Polygnotus' painting at Delphi followed Lesches). Attic vases of c. 500–470 BC, above all the Kleophrades Painter's hydria in Naples, merge two deaths: Priam sits on the altar with the bleeding body of Astyanax in his lap as Neoptolemus strikes.

Where sources disagree. Astyanax's killer is a clean split. The Iliou Persis names Odysseus. The Little Iliad (fr. 29 West, in Tzetzes) has Neoptolemus seize the child from his nurse and hurl him from the tower. Andromache already fears a Greek will throw him from the wall at Iliad 24.734–738. Euripides, Trojan Women 709–739, keeps both: the army votes on Odysseus' motion and Talthybius carries out the sentence; the body returns on Hector's shield (1123–1250).

Cassandra, Polyxena, Helen and the women

Cassandra's violation becomes a sexual assault only gradually. Alcaeus fr. 298 (c. 600 BC) already treats Ajax's crime as the cause of Athena's wrath against the Greeks; Proclus says he "dragged her away by force"; Apollodorus, Epitome 5.22, Lycophron, Alexandra 348–364, and Quintus 13.420–429 make the rape explicit, Quintus adding that the statue turned its eyes to the roof. Locris was believed to have sent maidens to serve Athena at Troy for a thousand years in expiation (Lycophron 1141–1173). Cassandra falls to Agamemnon (Odyssey 11.421–423) and dies with him.

Polyxena's sacrifice closes the Iliou Persis. Ibycus (fr. 307) is the earliest poet named for it, and a Tyrrhenian amphora in London (c. 570–560 BC) shows three warriors holding her horizontal while Neoptolemus cuts her throat over the mound. Euripides' Hecuba (c. 424 BC) makes the demand come from Achilles' ghost and has Talthybius describe how she refused to be held and knelt to the blade (518–582). A fragment of the Cypria (fr. 34 West) knew a different end: Polyxena wounded by Odysseus and Diomedes during the sack and buried by Neoptolemus.

Euripides' Trojan Women (415 BC) is built on the division. Talthybius announces the lots: Cassandra to Agamemnon, Polyxena to "serve at Achilles' tomb" (a euphemism Hecuba does not yet understand), Andromache to Neoptolemus, Hecuba to Odysseus. Helen alone is argued over (860–1059) and, as the audience knows from Homer, never punished; Little Iliad fr. 28 West already had Menelaus catch sight of her breasts and drop the sword. Hecuba's end varies: Hecuba 1259–1273 prophesies her transformation into a dog and burial at Cynossema; Apollodorus, Epitome 5.23, gives her to Odysseus or to Helenus.

Where sources disagree. Whether Trojan Women comments on the Athenian destruction of Melos in winter 416/5 is an old controversy. The play was in preparation before the massacre, and many scholars now read it as a general meditation on victors and victims rather than topical protest; the question is not closed.

Aeneas and the Roman claim

The Greek tradition gives Aeneas survival but no destiny. Poseidon at Iliad 20.302–308 says he is fated to escape so that the line of Dardanus shall not die and his descendants will rule the Trojans. In the Iliou Persis he reads the Laocoön portent and leaves for Ida; the Little Iliad, in Tzetzes' fragment, has Neoptolemus take him captive. Attic black-figure vases found in Etruria (c. 520–470 BC) show him carrying Anchises out of the city, so the escape was popular in Italy well before Roman literature. Hellanicus of Lesbos (fifth century BC), reported by Dionysius 1.45–48 and 1.72, is the first Greek to bring him to Italy and found Rome. Rome then annexed the sequence: Naevius and Ennius told it in Latin; the Julii descended themselves from his son Iulus; Virgil built Aeneid 2 around Aeneas' own narrative, with Hector's ghost, the loss of Creusa and the escape with Anchises and Ascanius, and sent Troy's gods and the Palladium to the Tiber.

Where sources disagree. The Tabula Iliaca Capitolina (Augustan) shows Aeneas departing "for Hesperia" and claims to follow Stesichorus' Iliou Persis; most scholars doubt that a sixth-century poet sent him west and read the label as Augustan flattery. A hostile tradition also existed: Menecrates of Xanthus (in Dionysius 1.48) said Aeneas betrayed Troy; Livy 1.1 preserves the softer form, that he and Antenor were spared for having urged peace; Dares and Dictys make them open the gates. The Roman claim was asserted against as well as from the Greek material.

Self-test

  1. Name the three poems of the Epic Cycle that cover the end of the war, their attributed authors and book counts.
    Answer

    Aethiopis, Arctinus of Miletus, five books; Little Iliad, Lesches of Mytilene, four books; Iliou Persis, Arctinus, two books.

  2. Through what text do we know their plots, and what is the main distortion of that text?
    Answer

    The summaries in Proclus' Chrestomathy, preserved by Photius (Bibliotheca cod. 239) and in Iliad manuscripts. They were trimmed to remove overlap, so the Little Iliad's own account of the sack is omitted.

  3. Why does Achilles need purification in the Aethiopis, and who purifies him?
    Answer

    He killed Thersites for mocking his supposed love for the dead Penthesilea; Odysseus purifies him on Lesbos after sacrifices to Apollo, Artemis and Leto.

  4. What is the Homeric model for the weighing of souls, and what does Aeschylus change?
    Answer

    Iliad 22.209–213, where Zeus weighs the death-fates of Achilles and Hector. Aeschylus' Psychostasia weighs the souls of Achilles and Memnon, with Thetis and Eos pleading.

  5. Which is the earliest source to make Achilles invulnerable except at the heel?
    Answer

    Statius, Achilleid 1.133–134 (dipping in the Styx), with the heel spelled out in Servius on Aeneid 6.57. Archaic art shows an ankle wound but no invulnerability.

  6. How does the Little Iliad decide the Judgement of the Arms?
    Answer

    Spies overhear two Trojan girls; one says Ajax was braver for carrying the body, the other, prompted by Athena, that even a woman can carry but would not fight. Odysseus wins.

  7. What is unusual about Ajax's burial in the epic and in Sophocles?
    Answer

    He is buried in a coffin, not cremated, because of Agamemnon's anger (Little Iliad fr. 3 West); in Sophocles his enemy Odysseus secures the burial against the Atreidae.

  8. How does the function of the Laocoön episode differ between Arctinus and Virgil?
    Answer

    In Arctinus the serpents strike after the horse is inside and the decision made: a punishment, prompting Aeneas to leave. In Virgil Laocoön attacks the horse outside the walls and his death persuades the Trojans to bring it in.

  9. Who kills Astyanax in each of the two poems, and how does Euripides handle the discrepancy?
    Answer

    Odysseus in the Iliou Persis; Neoptolemus in the Little Iliad. In Trojan Women the army votes on Odysseus' motion and Talthybius executes it.

  10. What is the earliest evidence that Aeneas' escape was known in Italy?
    Answer

    Attic black-figure vases found in Etruria, c. 520–470 BC, showing Aeneas carrying Anchises; the literary link to Rome begins with Hellanicus in the fifth century.

Carry forward

  1. Proclus' summaries are a harmonised storyline, not the poems. Where a quoted fragment contradicts the summary (Priam's death, Astyanax, Aeneas' capture), the fragment shows what the poem actually said.
  2. Vase painting is often the oldest witness to an episode and must be weighed against the texts, not read as illustration of them; the Mykonos pithos, the Chalcidian amphora and Exekias predate every surviving literary version of what they show.
  3. Famous details are frequently late accretions: the heel, the explicit rape of Cassandra, Oenone, Laocoön's two sons, Aeneas' voyage west. Ask for each episode what the archaic poems, Homer, the tragedians and the Roman poets contribute, because the Odyssey and the Nostoi depend on which version of the sack and of Athena's anger one accepts.
