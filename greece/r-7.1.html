01Babylon, June 323: the settlement that settled nothing

Alexander died in Babylon on 10 or 11 June 323 without a competent heir: his half-brother Arrhidaeus was mentally impaired and Roxane was pregnant. The narrative is Diodorus 18.2–4, with Arrian's Events after Alexander (in Photius' summary), Curtius 10.6–10 and Justin 13.2–4. The cavalry under Perdiccas, to whom the dying king had supposedly handed his ring, wanted to await Roxane's child; the infantry under Meleager proclaimed Arrhidaeus as Philip III. The compromise made both kings, with Perdiccas as chiliarch and effective regent; Meleager was soon executed. Ptolemy took Egypt, Antigonus Monophthalmus kept Greater Phrygia, Lysimachus got Thrace, Eumenes of Cardia the unconquered Cappadocia; Antipater stayed as general in Europe with Craterus, the army's favourite, awkwardly named his colleague. Seleucus received only the Companion cavalry. Roxane's son became Alexander IV.

Where sources disagree. Diodorus 17.117 and Curtius 10.5 have Alexander leave the kingdom "to the strongest" (tōi kratistōi), a pun on Craterus that ancient readers already noticed; Arrian, Anabasis 7.26, says he was speechless. Whether the ring was ever given to Perdiccas is unverifiable. The "last plans" he read out to be voted down (Diodorus 18.4) were rejected as forgery by Tarn; Badian and most recent scholars accept a genuine core.

02Perdiccas, Antipater, Craterus and Eumenes, 323–316

Greece rose at once. In the Lamian War Athens trapped Antipater in Lamia until Craterus arrived with the veterans and crushed the Greeks at Crannon in 322 (Diodorus 18.9–18). Demosthenes took poison; Athens received a garrison and an oligarchic franchise, the pattern of Macedonian control for a century.

Perdiccas overreached. Married to Antipater's daughter, he began negotiating for Alexander's sister Cleopatra, a bid for the throne itself. Antigonus fled to Antipater; Ptolemy hijacked Alexander's funeral cortège to Egypt (Diodorus 18.26–28). Perdiccas invaded Egypt in 320, failed to force the Nile and was killed in his tent by his own officers, Seleucus among them (Diodorus 18.33–36). Two days later came news that Eumenes, the Greek secretary turned general, had defeated and killed Craterus in Asia Minor (Diodorus 18.30–32; Plutarch, Eumenes 5–7). At Triparadeisus (Diodorus 18.39) Antipater became regent, Antigonus general in Asia against Eumenes, and Seleucus satrap of Babylonia. Dying in 319, Antipater passed the regency to Polyperchon rather than his son Cassander; Cassander went to Antigonus and Ptolemy, and Polyperchon answered by proclaiming the freedom of the Greeks and recalling Olympias (Diodorus 18.55–56).

Eumenes led the royal cause in Asia with the Silver Shields, Alexander's veterans now in their sixties. He drew with Antigonus at Paraetacene (317); at Gabiene (winter 317/316) he won the infantry fight but lost the baggage with the veterans' wives and loot, and the Silver Shields handed him over to be killed (Diodorus 19.26–44; Plutarch, Eumenes 16–19). To hold officers who despised a Greek, he had held council before Alexander's empty throne and diadem in a tent (Diodorus 18.60–61; Plutarch, Eumenes 13): the dead king as political cult object.

Where sources disagree. Diodorus 18–20 derives largely from Hieronymus of Cardia, Eumenes' compatriot and later an Antigonid courtier, so Eumenes the loyalist and Antigonus the magnanimous are partisan portraits. The Babylonian Diadochi Chronicle (BCHP 3) is the independent check and has shifted the chronology of 323–319 towards the "low" scheme (Perdiccas' death 320, not 321); the question is not closed.

03Antigonus and Demetrius, Cassander, and the end of the Argeads

Antigonus now held Asia from the Hellespont to Iran and in 316 drove Seleucus from Babylon; Seleucus fled to Ptolemy, who allied with Cassander and Lysimachus (Diodorus 19.55–57). In Macedon Olympias killed Philip III and Eurydice in 317; Cassander besieged her at Pydna, had her executed in 316 (Diodorus 19.11, 19.35–36, 19.49–51), and kept Alexander IV and Roxane under guard at Amphipolis.

At Gaza in 312 Ptolemy and Seleucus beat the young Demetrius (Diodorus 19.80–85). Seleucus rode back to Babylon with a thousand men and retook it; the Seleucid era was counted from autumn 312 or spring 311. The peace of 311 (Diodorus 19.105) recognised the dynasts as governors until Alexander IV came of age, which was the boy's death warrant. Cassander had him and Roxane murdered in 310 or 309; Polyperchon killed Heracles, Alexander's son by Barsine, in 309; Antigonus had Cleopatra killed at Sardis in 308 (Diodorus 20.20, 20.28, 20.37). No Argead with a claim survived. Seleucus fought Antigonus in a Babylonian War (311–309) known only from cuneiform sources, then around 305–303 ceded the Indian satrapies to Chandragupta Maurya for five hundred elephants (Strabo 15.2.9; Appian, Syriaca 55; Justin 15.4).

Demetrius freed Athens in 307 and destroyed Ptolemy's fleet off Cypriot Salamis in 306 (Diodorus 20.47–52); Antigonus took the diadem and gave his son one (Plutarch, Demetrius 17–18); Ptolemy, Seleucus, Lysimachus and Cassander followed (Diodorus 20.53). His invasion of Egypt failed on the Nile as Perdiccas' had. The siege of Rhodes (305–304; Diodorus 20.81–99) earned Demetrius the name Poliorcetes and ended in a draw; the Rhodians sold his abandoned engines to pay for the Colossus (Pliny, Natural History 34.41).

04Ipsus, 301, and Corupedium, 281

Antigonus revived Philip II's Hellenic League in 302 to legitimise a drive on Macedon; Lysimachus crossed to Asia while Seleucus came from the east with his elephants. At Ipsus in Phrygia Demetrius' cavalry charge succeeded too well: Seleucus screened the gap with elephants, the Antigonid phalanx was surrounded, and the eighty-year-old king died under javelins still expecting his son's return (Plutarch, Demetrius 28–29). Seleucus took Syria, Lysimachus Asia Minor; Ptolemy, who had not fought, kept Coele-Syria, and the Seleucid claim to it as spear-won land (Polybius 5.67) poisoned the two dynasties for a century. Demetrius was left, in Plutarch's image, a wounded beast with a fleet and a few cities.

Cassander died in 297; in 294 Demetrius murdered his son Alexander V and took Macedon (Plutarch, Demetrius 36–37). Seven years building a fleet to reconquer Asia ended when Pyrrhus and Lysimachus invaded in 288 and his army deserted; he surrendered to Seleucus in 285 and drank himself to death in captivity (Plutarch, Demetrius 44–52). Lysimachus took Macedon, then at his wife Arsinoe's urging killed his heir Agathocles; Seleucus invaded, and at Corupedium in February 281 Lysimachus fell (Appian, Syriaca 62–64; Justin 17.1–2; Memnon, FGrH 434 F 5–8). Seleucus, last of Alexander's companions, crossed to claim Macedon and was stabbed near Lysimacheia by Ptolemy Ceraunus, his guest. Ceraunus died fighting the Galatians in 279; Demetrius' son Antigonus Gonatas beat them at Lysimacheia in 277 and secured Macedon for his line. Pyrrhus, after Italy (280–275), died in a street fight in Argos in 272 (Plutarch, Pyrrhus 34).

Where sources disagree. Plutarch, Demetrius 28, gives the allies 480 elephants at Ipsus against Antigonus' 75, implying Seleucus brought nearly all Chandragupta's gift across Iran intact; Bar-Kochva accepts it, others do not. Diodorus' account of the battle is lost with book 21.

05Ptolemaic Egypt: Alexandria, the pharaoh-king and the Greek elite

Ptolemy I Soter (satrap 323, king 305/304, died 283/282) took the most defensible and richest prize and never seriously left it. Alexandria became the capital, with the Soma holding Alexander's body, the Museum and Library (Strabo 17.1.8; the Letter of Aristeas is unreliable on their founding) and the Pharos. Euclid, Callimachus and Eratosthenes worked under royal patronage, and Sarapis, Osiris-Apis in Greek dress, was promoted as a shared cult (Plutarch, On Isis and Osiris 28).

The kings ruled two ways at once. To Greeks they were basileis with a Macedonian court and Greek as the language of administration; the Zenon archive from the Fayum shows the finance minister Apollonius' estate run by Greek and Carian managers over Egyptian labour. To Egyptians they were pharaohs. The Satrap Stele of 311 already shows Ptolemy in pharaonic style before he took the title; kings were crowned at Memphis by the priests of Ptah, and the Rosetta Stone (196) records a priestly synod decreeing honours for Ptolemy V in hieroglyphic, Demotic and Greek. Ptolemy II married his full sister Arsinoe II around 275, a pharaonic rather than Greek precedent praised in Theocritus, Idyll 17. Ptolemy IV's enrolment of twenty thousand Egyptian phalangites for Raphia in 217 (Polybius 5.65, 5.107) was followed by the Great Revolt of the Thebaid (206–186) under native pharaohs. Polybius divided Alexandria's population into Egyptians, mercenaries and mixed "Alexandrians", and liked none of them (34.14, via Strabo 17.1.12).

Where sources disagree. Préaux and Rostovtzeff saw a colonial state in which Greeks ruled and Egyptians were taxed. Manning and Thompson argue from Demotic papyri that the Ptolemies ruled through the temples and Egyptian notables from the start and that the ethnic line blurred quickly in the countryside. Both agree few Egyptians reached the top before the second century.

06The Seleucid empire: cities, eastern losses, Antiochus III and IV, the Maccabees

Seleucus I ruled from the Aegean to the Hindu Kush and answered the size by founding: Seleucia-on-Tigris (c. 305), Antioch, Apamea and Laodicea (300), and military colonies along the roads to Iran (Appian, Syriaca 57). Cuneiform archives show Babylonian satrapal and temple structures carrying on beneath. The founding story, Seleucus ceding his wife Stratonice to his lovesick son Antiochus I (Plutarch, Demetrius 38), is a parable of orderly succession in a world that rarely had one.

The east came loose in the 240s: Diodotus of Bactria struck his own coins around 250, and Arsaces' Parni took Parthia, conventionally in 247 (Justin 41.4; Strabo 11.9.2–3). Antiochus III (223–187) rebuilt. He lost Raphia in 217, then marched east from 212 to 205: Armenia submitted, Arsaces II accepted alliance, Euthydemus of Bactria withstood a two-year siege and kept his crown by treaty (Polybius 10.27–31, 10.49, 11.34). He took the title Great King and won Coele-Syria at Panium in 200. Then he crossed to Greece, lost Thermopylae in 191 and Magnesia in 190/189, and by the Peace of Apamea in 188 gave up everything north of the Taurus, his fleet, his elephants and 15,000 talents (Polybius 21.42; Livy 38.38). He died in 187 plundering a temple in Elymais to pay for it.

Antiochus IV Epiphanes (175–164), "Epimanes" to Polybius (26.1), had all but conquered Egypt when the Roman envoy Popilius Laenas drew a circle in the sand at Eleusis in 168 and demanded an answer before he stepped out (Polybius 29.27); the parade at Daphne in 166 (Polybius 30.25) was his reply to Roman triumphs. In Judaea he sold the high priesthood to Jason and then Menelaus, garrisoned the Akra, and in December 167 banned Jewish observance and set a pagan altar in the Temple (1 Maccabees 1; 2 Maccabees 4–6; Josephus, Antiquities 12.237–264; Daniel 11.31). Mattathias' revolt became Judas Maccabaeus' war: the rededication of the Temple in 164, death at Elasa in 160. His brothers exploited Seleucid civil wars; Jonathan became high priest in 152, Simon won independence in 142 (1 Maccabees 13.41), and the Hasmonean state outlived the dynasty.

Where sources disagree. Why Antiochus IV persecuted a religion, which no Hellenistic king had done, has no agreed answer. Tacitus, Histories 5.8, says he meant to civilise; Bickerman blamed Jewish Hellenising reformers around Menelaus; Tcherikover made the persecution a response to a rising already under way; Gruen sees a king punishing a rebellious temple-state in the usual way, with the religious ban as Maccabean framing. The dates of Arsaces' secession (247 or 238) and Diodotus' (256 or 246) are also uncertain.

07Antigonid Macedon

Antigonus Gonatas (277–239) ruled a poor land and a Macedonian people, answered to an army assembly that acclaimed him and tried capital cases, and was never a god to his own subjects. A pupil of Zeno the Stoic, he called kingship "a noble servitude" (Aelian, Varia Historia 2.20). He held Greece by the "fetters", garrisons at Demetrias, Chalcis and Corinth (Polybius 18.11), and survived the Chremonidean War (268–261) against Athens, Sparta and Ptolemy II. Antigonus Doson (229–221) returned at Aratus' invitation, refounded a Hellenic League under Macedonian presidency in 224 and broke Cleomenes at Sellasia in 222.

Philip V (221–179) is Polybius' tragic figure, the gifted young king who turned tyrant. He made peace with Aetolia at Naupactus in 217 after Agelaus warned of "the cloud in the west" (Polybius 5.104), then allied with Hannibal in 215 (Polybius 7.9). Cynoscephalae in 197 showed the legion beating the phalanx on broken ground (Polybius 18.18–27); his son Perseus lost at Pydna in 168 (Livy 44.40–42; Plutarch, Aemilius Paullus 16–22), the monarchy was replaced by four republics, and in 148 Macedon became Rome's first eastern province.

Where sources disagree. Whether the Macedonian assembly had constitutional powers (Granier) or was acclamatory and summoned at the king's convenience (Errington; Hatzopoulos with reservations) turns on a few passages in Curtius and Polybius 5.27–29 and remains open.

08The smaller powers

Pergamon. Philetaerus, Lysimachus' eunuch treasurer, took fortress and treasury over to Seleucus in 282 and kept both. Attalus I took the royal title after a Galatian victory around 238 and allied with Rome; Eumenes II fought at Magnesia, received most of Seleucid Asia Minor at Apamea and built the Great Altar. Attalus III willed the kingdom to Rome in 133 (Strabo 13.4.2).

Rhodes. The republic that defied Demetrius became arbiter of the Aegean, policing pirates and setting commercial law; when an earthquake around 227 toppled the Colossus every king sent aid (Polybius 5.88–90). Neutrality in the Third Macedonian War cost it Rome's favour, and the free port at Delos in 167 drained its harbour dues (Polybius 30.31).

The Leagues. The Aetolian League controlled Delphi, lived partly by licensed plunder and became Rome's first Greek ally in 211; Polybius, an Achaean, loathes it. The Achaean League, refounded in 280, grew when Aratus of Sicyon brought his city in (251) and seized Acrocorinth in 243 (Plutarch, Aratus 18–24). Polybius 2.37–44 describes a federal state with common citizenship, laws, coinage and an annual strategos, the nearest Greece came to one polis. Fear of Sparta drove Aratus into Doson's arms; quarrels with Rome ended in the sack of Corinth in 146.

Sparta. By 244 Sparta had perhaps seven hundred citizens. Agis IV (244–241) proposed debt cancellation and 4,500 citizen lots; the ephors and his co-king had him executed (Plutarch, Agis 5–20). Cleomenes III (235–222) used the army: after a coup in 227 he abolished the ephorate, cancelled debts, enfranchised perioikoi to make four thousand hoplites and restored the agoge, with the Stoic Sphaerus as adviser (Plutarch, Cleomenes 7–11). He beat the Achaeans repeatedly until Sellasia, fled to Alexandria and died in a failed rising there in 219 (Polybius 5.35–39).

Where sources disagree. Plutarch's Agis and Cleomenes follow Phylarchus, who admired the kings; Polybius 2.56–63 attacks Phylarchus by name and paints Cleomenes as a tyrant who nearly destroyed Achaea. Social revolution or a bid for a bigger army: the sources themselves pose the question.

09Ruler cult and the new idea of kingship

Greeks had honoured a living man as a god once before, when Samos gave Lysander altars and paeans in 404 (Duris in Plutarch, Lysander 18); Alexander's request for divine honours in 324 still drew mockery (Hypereides, Against Demosthenes 31). After 323 cult became ordinary. Athens received Antigonus and Demetrius in 307 as Saviour Gods and named two new tribes after them (Plutarch, Demetrius 10–13; Diodorus 20.46); Rhodes founded a cult of Ptolemy Soter after the siege (Diodorus 20.100; Pausanias 1.8.6). The hymn sung for Demetrius in 290 gives the logic:

the other gods are far away, or have no ears, or do not exist (Athenaeus 6.253e)

Dynastic cult followed: Ptolemy II deified his parents as Saviour Gods and himself and Arsinoe as Sibling Gods; Antiochus III ordered a kingdom-wide cult of Queen Laodice in 193, attested on stone from Iran to Caria. Macedon alone did not follow.

The kingship itself was new. It was personal, not territorial: Antigonus was king of wherever his army stood. The Suda's entry on basileia preserves the doctrine that neither descent nor legitimacy gives men kingdoms but the ability to lead an army and manage affairs. Legitimacy came from victory (Nicator, Callinicus), spear-won land, benefaction (Euergetes, Soter) and a court of Friends (philoi) chosen by the king, not by birth. Plutarch, Demetrius 42, sets Demetrius, who threw petitions in the river, against Philip II, who read them; the kings who lasted learned that cities must be courted. Treatises On Kingship and Theocritus 17 describe the same ideal: the king as living law, generous, victorious, accessible.

Where sources disagree. Tarn and Taylor saw ruler cult as Oriental servility or cynical manipulation. Habicht showed it was a city's initiative, granted for concrete benefits; Price and Chaniotis treat it as the language of reciprocity, honours in a divine register because nothing less fitted the power. Whether anyone "believed" becomes the wrong question.

10The far edge: the Greco-Bactrian and Indo-Greek kingdoms

When Diodotus I broke away around 250, Bactria had been Greek-ruled for eighty years; Strabo 11.11.1 calls it the land of a thousand cities, and Justin 41.1–6 gives the thin narrative. Euthydemus survived Antiochus III; his son Demetrius I crossed the Hindu Kush around 185 as the Mauryas collapsed and took Gandhara and the Punjab; Eucratides (c. 170–145) seized Bactria in civil war. Menander I (c. 155–130) debates a Buddhist monk in the Pali Milindapanha, and Plutarch, Precepts of Statecraft 821d, says his cities divided his ashes as relics. Coins became bilingual, Greek and Kharosthi, with Buddhist wheels beside Zeus; Heliodorus, envoy of King Antialcidas, raised a pillar to Vasudeva at Besnagar around 110. Ai Khanoum had a gymnasium, a theatre and Delphic maxims copied by Clearchus of Soli, beside a palace in Achaemenid style. Bactria fell to the Yuezhi between about 145 and 130; the last Indo-Greek, Strato III, held a sliver of the Punjab until about AD 10.

Where sources disagree. Almost all this chronology rests on coins. Tarn's Greeks in Bactria and India (1938) made an epic of Greek civilisation in the east; Narain's The Indo-Greeks (1957) replied that their history "is part of the history of India". The number of kings called Demetrius and whether Menander ruled Bactria at all remain uncertain.

11Self-test

  1. Who were the two kings recognised at Babylon in 323, and who held real power?
    Answer

    Philip III Arrhidaeus and the infant Alexander IV; Perdiccas as chiliarch and regent held power until his murder in Egypt in 320.

  2. What did Eumenes set up in his camp to command Macedonian loyalty, and where is it reported?
    Answer

    Alexander's empty throne and diadem, before which the council met; Diodorus 18.60–61 and Plutarch, Eumenes 13.

  3. Which battle in 312 allowed Seleucus to return to Babylon, and from when is the Seleucid era counted?
    Answer

    Gaza, where Ptolemy and Seleucus defeated Demetrius; the era runs from autumn 312 or spring 311.

  4. Name the Argead victims of Cassander, Polyperchon and Antigonus between 317 and 308.
    Answer

    Olympias (Cassander, 316); Alexander IV and Roxane (Cassander, c. 310/309); Heracles (Polyperchon, 309); Cleopatra (Antigonus, 308). Olympias herself had killed Philip III and Eurydice in 317.

  5. How did Demetrius lose Ipsus despite winning the cavalry fight?
    Answer

    He chased the enemy horse off the field; Seleucus placed elephants across his return path and the isolated Antigonid phalanx was surrounded (Plutarch, Demetrius 29).

  6. Why did Coele-Syria become a permanent quarrel after Ipsus?
    Answer

    Ptolemy, who had not fought, kept it, but the Seleucids claimed it as spear-won land (Polybius 5.67), and the claim poisoned the two dynasties for a century.

  7. Who killed Seleucus I, where, and what had happened just before?
    Answer

    Ptolemy Ceraunus, near Lysimacheia, after Lysimachus had fallen against Seleucus at Corupedium in February 281 and Seleucus had crossed into Europe to claim Macedon.

  8. What does the Satrap Stele of 311 show about Ptolemy's rule, and what does the Rosetta Stone add?
    Answer

    Ptolemy was presented in pharaonic style to Egyptians before taking the royal title; the Rosetta Stone (196) shows a priestly synod decreeing honours for Ptolemy V in three scripts, a king working through the temples.

  9. What were the terms of the Peace of Apamea (188), and what did they mean for Pergamon?
    Answer

    Antiochus III gave up Asia Minor north of the Taurus, his fleet and elephants, and paid 15,000 talents; most of the ceded land went to Eumenes II of Pergamon, Rome's ally.

  10. Why does Polybius' account of Cleomenes III differ so sharply from Plutarch's?
    Answer

    Plutarch follows Phylarchus, who admired the Spartan reformers; Polybius, an Achaean whose league Cleomenes nearly destroyed, attacks Phylarchus (2.56–63) and casts Cleomenes as a tyrant.

12Carry forward

  1. Hellenistic kingship was personal and won by the spear: legitimacy came from victory, benefaction and the loyalty of an army and Friends, not from territory or descent, and ruler cult was the cities' way of bargaining with a power too large for ordinary honours.
  2. The three kingdoms ruled differently because they ruled different peoples: the Ptolemies as pharaohs and Greek kings in one body, the Seleucids through founded cities over inherited satrapies, the Antigonids as national kings answerable to a Macedonian assembly. Rome met each in turn.
  3. The source problem is structural: Diodorus 18–20 is Hieronymus' Antigonid-leaning narrative, Polybius is an Achaean writing for Romans, Plutarch moralises from both; cuneiform chronicles, papyri, inscriptions and coins are the independent checks, and for Bactria almost the only evidence.
