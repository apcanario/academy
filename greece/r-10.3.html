01The house of Priam

Priam tells Achilles that he fathered fifty sons, nineteen "from one womb" and the rest by women of the palace (Homer, Iliad 24.495–497). That sentence fixes what the Greeks found most striking about the Trojan royal house: its size and its polygamy. Hecuba, daughter of the Phrygian Dymas (Iliad 16.718–719), is the matriarch; Laothoe is the named secondary wife, mother of Lycaon and Polydorus (21.84–91). The family descends from Dardanus through Ilus, the senior line, while the junior line of Assaracus runs to Anchises and Aeneas (20.215–240):

Where sources disagree. The Iliad is inconsistent about which daughter is "fairest": Laodice in Books 3 and 6, Cassandra in Book 13. Polydorus has two incompatible mothers and deaths. Hecuba's father is Dymas in Homer but Cisseus in Euripides (Hecuba 3); Apollodorus (Library 3.12.5) lists both. Cassandra's prophetic gift is post-Homeric; the Iliad gives prophecy to Helenus, her twin only in later sources.

Two stories about this house were told as omens. Hecuba, pregnant with Paris, dreamed she bore a firebrand that burned the city (Pindar, Paean 8a; Euripides, Trojan Women 919–922). And Priam began as the thing ransomed: when Heracles killed Laomedon, the boy Podarces was bought back by his sister Hesione and renamed Priamos, read by Greeks as "the purchased one" (Apollodorus, Library 2.6.4).

02The senior Trojans

Antenor is the elder statesman and the peace party. He hosted Menelaus and Odysseus when they came to demand Helen (Iliad 3.203–224) and tells the assembly of Book 7 that she and her goods should go back (7.347–353); Paris refuses. His wife Theano, priestess of Athena, leads the women's failed prayer (6.297–311). Later tradition turns his reasonableness into treason: the Greeks spare his house, marked with a leopard skin (Pausanias 10.27.3 on Polygnotus' painting), and he founds Patavium (Livy 1.1.1–3).

Anchises is in the Iliad only a horse-breeder (5.265–272); his seduction by Aphrodite on Ida belongs to the Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite. Aeneas is treated with an oddly protective hand. Aphrodite and Apollo pull him away from Diomedes (5.311–346), Poseidon from Achilles (20.288–339), and Poseidon states why: Aeneas is fated to survive so that the line of Dardanus does not perish and "his children's children" will rule (20.302–308). Aeneas also resents Priam, who "did not honour him" (13.459–461). The poem knows a rival branch.

Where sources disagree. Whom did Poseidon mean? Demetrius of Scepsis, cited by Strabo (13.1.52–53), read the prophecy as referring to the Aeneadae who ruled at Scepsis in the Troad, which would make the lines local dynastic propaganda inside the poem. Rome's reading comes much later. The text names no place.

Polydamas, son of Panthous and born the same night as Hector (18.251), is the counsellor whose advice is sound and almost always rejected. He reads the omen of the eagle and the snake as a warning to withdraw (12.210–229), answered by Hector's "one omen is best: to fight for one's country" (12.243), and after Achilles reappears he urges retreat behind the walls (18.254–283), rejected by acclamation. He exists to make Hector's final error legible as a choice.

03Hector

The aristeia and Book 6

Hector's great day runs from Book 8 to Book 17 with Zeus's explicit backing (11.186–194). Its peak is the smashing of the Achaean gate with a boulder "no two men could lift" (12.445–471), the firing of Protesilaus' ship (15.704–746) and the killing of Patroclus (16.818–867). Yet the poem frames the aristeia so that it never reads as pure triumph. Sarpedon tells Hector he leaves the allies to do his fighting (5.471–492); Glaucus calls him a coward for abandoning Sarpedon's body (17.142–168); Zeus, watching him put on Achilles' armour, pities a doomed man who will never return it to Andromache (17.198–208).

Book 6 is the poem's one domestic interlude and it is given to the Trojans. Hector refuses Hecuba's wine (6.264–268), rebukes Paris in his bedroom (6.325–331), and meets Andromache at the Scaean gate. She reminds us who she is: daughter of Eetion of Thebe-under-Placus, whose father and seven brothers Achilles killed in one day (6.414–428); Hector is "father and lady mother and brother" to her (6.429–430). He refuses her plea to fight defensively, foresees the city's fall and her slavery, and turns to the baby: Astyanax screams at the helmet's crest, both parents laugh, and Hector prays the child may be a better man than his father (6.466–481). No epic before the Iliad and few after it put a soldier's marriage at the centre.

Paris

Hector's dealings with Paris are a study in fraternal contempt without hatred. He calls him "Dyspari", evil Paris, "best in looks, woman-mad, seducer" (3.39; 13.769), and wishes he had never been born. Paris's answer is always the same: you are right, let me fight (3.59–75; 6.333–341). The wider tradition makes Paris the seducer and the archer who kills Achilles with Apollo's help (Aethiopis); the Iliad makes him something less comfortable, a brother who is charming, unreliable and not actually a coward.

Death and judgement

Does the poem judge Hector? Yes, and it has him do it first. Alone outside the wall in Book 22 he admits that Polydamas was right and that his own recklessness (atasthaliai, 22.104) destroyed the army. He considers surrendering Helen and half the city's treasure, dismisses it, resolves to fight, and then runs. Three circuits of the walls, Zeus's golden scales (22.209–213), Athena in the shape of Deiphobus promising support and vanishing (22.226–247, 294–299): the gods cheat, and the poem does not hide it. Dying, Hector asks for his body to be returned; Achilles says he wishes he could eat him raw (22.345–348).

Where sources disagree. James Redfield (Nature and Culture in the Iliad: The Tragedy of Hector, 1975) reads Hector as the poem's true tragic figure, culpable through error, not vice. Others stress the darker Hector: the boast over the dying Patroclus (16.830–842), the attempt to behead the corpse and feed it to dogs (17.125–127), the over-reading of Zeus's favour. Both camps read the same lines. The poem gives Hector the self-knowledge Achilles never quite reaches, and also the mistakes.

The funeral

The Iliad ends not with Troy's fall but with Hector's funeral (24.719–804): three laments, by Andromache, Hecuba and, last and most surprisingly, Helen (24.762–775), who says Hector alone never spoke a harsh word to her. Nine days gathering wood, the tenth burning, the eleventh building the tomb. The last line, "Thus they held the funeral of Hector, tamer of horses" (24.804), is a Trojan line. A variant preserved in the scholia continued, "and the Amazon came, daughter of great-hearted Ares", the splice to the Aethiopis; the tradition that fixed the text declined it.

04Priam

Priam moves from pure passivity to action. He watches from the wall in Book 3 and asks Helen to name the Greek leaders (3.161–242); he leaves before the duel because he cannot bear to see his son fight (3.304–309); he pleads from the gate, tearing his hair, as Achilles approaches (22.33–78). In Book 24, after Zeus sends Iris and Hermes, he drives a mule-cart into the enemy camp at night and kisses the hands of the man who killed his sons (24.477–479). His speech asks Achilles to remember his own father and ends: "I have done what no mortal has done before" (24.505–506). The two weep together, then eat. The poem's last reconciliation is between a Greek and a Trojan, and it is private.

The death belongs to the Iliou Persis: in Proclus' summary Neoptolemus kills Priam at the altar of Zeus Herkeios in his own courtyard. Virgil (Aeneid 2.506–558) has the old man arm himself, see Polites killed in front of him, throw a useless spear, and be dragged through his son's blood to the altar.

Where sources disagree. Pausanias (10.27.2) records that the Little Iliad had Neoptolemus drag Priam from the altar and kill him at the doors, away from the sanctuary, whether to lessen the sacrilege or as a different local version is unknown. Pindar (Paean 6.112–117) keeps the altar and makes it the reason Apollo later kills Neoptolemus at Delphi.

05Hecuba and Andromache after Troy

In the Iliad Hecuba is formidable within limits: she tries to hold Hector back by baring the breast that fed him (22.79–89), calls Priam's night journey madness (24.200–216), and says she would gladly "eat the liver" of Achilles (24.212–213). Euripides builds a play on that sentence. In Hecuba (c. 424 BC) she loses Polyxena to sacrifice and finds Polydorus' body washed up from Thrace; she lures the murderer Polymestor into the women's tent, blinds him and kills his sons. He prophesies that she will become a dog and name the headland Cynossema (1259–1273). Trojan Women (415 BC) gives her the other ending, grief without revenge, as Astyanax's body is brought in on his father's shield.

Andromache's later life is the cruellest symmetry in the cycle. She is allotted to Neoptolemus, who in the Little Iliad threw Astyanax from the wall. She bears him Molossus (three sons in Pausanias 1.11.1) and lives in Epirus under the jealousy of his Spartan wife Hermione. When Neoptolemus dies at Delphi, Thetis decrees that she marry Helenus and that Molossus found the Molossian dynasty (Euripides, Andromache 1243–1252). Virgil's Aeneas finds her at Buthrotum, in a replica Troy with a sham Simois, pouring libations at an empty tomb for Hector (Aeneid 3.294–355). Pausanias (1.11.2) adds that she ended in Asia with Pergamus, founder of Pergamon, where she had a shrine.

Where sources disagree. Who killed Astyanax? Neoptolemus in the Little Iliad; Odysseus in the Iliou Persis; the whole army on Odysseus' advice in Euripides (Trojan Women 721–725). Who killed Neoptolemus is equally unstable: Orestes in Euripides, the Delphians in Pindar (Nemean 7.40–47), Apollo in Paean 6.

06The allies

The Trojan catalogue (2.816–877) lists a west Anatolian coalition with Balkan support, but only the Lycians get full characters. Sarpedon, son of Zeus and Laodameia (6.198–199), delivers the clearest statement of aristocratic duty in the poem: Lycian kings enjoy their lands and wine precisely so that they will fight in the front line (12.310–328). Glaucus, grandson of Bellerophon, discovers an inherited guest-friendship with Diomedes and the two agree not to fight (6.119–236). The Trojan side contains men bound to Greeks by the same codes.

Sarpedon's death (16.419–507) matters for three reasons. Zeus weeps blood and wants to save him; Hera's answer, that every god would then rescue a favourite son and the order of things would fail, is the poem's statement of the limit on divine power (16.431–461). His body is carried by Sleep and Death to Lycia for burial (16.666–683), the only honour of its kind in the poem, painted by Euphronios on the krater of about 515 BC now at Cerveteri. And it is the template for Hector: Zeus weighs the same choice in Book 22 (22.168–181) and reaches the same answer.

Where sources disagree. Hesiod and Apollodorus (Library 3.1.2) make Sarpedon son of Zeus and Europa and brother of Minos, three generations too early; Diodorus (5.79.3) has Zeus grant him three lifetimes. Homer knows nothing of this.

Three allies stand outside the poem's main action. Rhesus, the Thracian with silver-white horses, is killed asleep by Diomedes on the night he arrives (10.469–525); scholia add that had his horses once drunk from the Scamander, Troy would never have fallen. The tragedy Rhesus under Euripides' name makes him son of the Strymon and a Muse; both its authorship and Book 10 itself, which scholia call a Pisistratean insertion, are disputed. Memnon, son of Eos and Priam's brother Tithonus, kills Antilochus and is killed by Achilles in the Aethiopis, where Eos wins him immortality; Aeschylus staged the weighing of the two souls in the lost Psychostasia. Penthesilea, the Amazon daughter of Ares, opens the same poem; Achilles kills her and then kills Thersites for mocking his grief over her body (Proclus; Quintus Smyrnaeus, Posthomerica 1). Exekias' amphora in the British Museum (c. 530 BC) shows the spear going in as their eyes meet.

07Are the Trojans foreign?

Hector and Achilles converse without an interpreter. Trojans swear by Zeus, Athena has a temple on the citadel (6.297–311), Apollo defends the walls, and Hector's name is a Greek word, "the holder". Iris (2.803–804) and the narrator (4.437–438) remark that the allies are of "many tongues", but the Trojans themselves are never marked as speaking anything else. Edith Hall (Inventing the Barbarian, 1989) made the standard case that Homer has no concept of the barbarian: barbarophonos occurs once, of the Carians (2.867), and means only "odd-sounding".

What the poem marks is subtler. Trojans advance in a clamour like bleating sheep, Achaeans in silence (3.1–9; 4.428–438). Priam is polygamous; Paris fights with the bow, wears a leopard skin (3.17) and goes to bed with Helen in the afternoon; Hecuba is Phrygian and Priam once fought beside the Phrygians (3.184–189). Hilary Mackie (Talking Trojan, 1996) argued that Trojan speech is more lyrical and less given to blame. These are differences of texture, not of kind; whether they amount to an "orientalised" Troy is argued both ways.

The Trojans become barbarians after 480 BC. Aeschylus' Clytemnestra asks whether Cassandra speaks "an unknown barbarian tongue, like a swallow" (Agamemnon 1050–1051), and his lost Phrygians staged the ransom of Hector with a chorus in eastern dress. Euripides calls Trojans Phryges throughout, gives Orestes a cowardly Phrygian eunuch who sings his terror (1369–1502), and has Hermione sneer that barbarians sleep with their relatives (Andromache 173–176). Athenian painters, who in the sixth century drew Trojans as hoplites identical to Greeks, begin after the Persian Wars to give Paris a Phrygian cap, trousers and a Scythian bow; by the fourth century the cap is a Trojan uniform. Herodotus supplies the political logic: Xerxes sacrifices a thousand oxen to Athena at Ilion on his way to Greece (7.43), and the Persians count Asia and its peoples as their own (1.4.4). Troy had been re-filed under Asia.

08Wilusa and the Luwian hypothesis

Hittite texts of the fourteenth and thirteenth centuries BC know a kingdom in north-western Anatolia called Wilusa, once Wilusiya, among the Assuwa coalition defeated by Tudhaliya I/II around 1400 BC. A treaty of Muwatalli II (c. 1280 BC) binds its king Alaksandu as vassal and names an earlier king, Kukkunni. The Manapa-Tarhunta letter reports an attack on Wilusa by the renegade Piyamaradu; the Tawagalawa letter recalls a dispute over Wilusa between the Hittite king and the king of Ahhiyawa; the Milawata letter shows Tudhaliya IV restoring a deposed Wilusan king, Walmu.

The equations are phonologically attractive. Wilusa matches Greek Wilios, the form of Ilios with its original w-sound, still preserved in the metre of many Homeric lines; the neighbouring Taruisa matches Troia. Alaksandu looks like Alexandros. A god in the treaty's witness list, Appaliunas, has been read as Apollo. J. D. Hawkins' decipherment of the Karabel inscription (1998) fixed the kingdom of Mira and pushed Wilusa into the Troad, where most Hittitologists now place it.

The Luwian hypothesis goes further. Calvert Watkins (1986) drew attention to a Luwian song fragment embedded in a Hittite ritual text (KUB 35.102+103), ahha-ta-ta alati awienta Wilusati, "when they came from steep Wilusa", and suggested it might be the ghost of a Luwian Troy epic. In 1995 Korfmann's excavators found a Luwian hieroglyphic seal in Troy VIIb, still the only writing found on the site. Watkins also derived Priam from Luwian Pariya-muwa, "exceptionally brave".

Where sources disagree. None of this is settled. Alaksandu may be a Luwian name folk-etymologised by Greeks, or a Greek name borne by an Anatolian king with Aegean connections (Joachim Latacz argues the latter). Appaliunas is a damaged reading. One seal does not establish a city's language. Kolb's attack on Korfmann over the scale of Troy VI belongs to module 10.10; the Wilusa equation, while the majority view, has not persuaded everyone. What is secure: a kingdom with a name like Ilios existed where Troy stands, was a Hittite vassal, and was fought over in the thirteenth century.

09Troy to Rome

Aeneas' westward journey is older than Rome's interest in it. Sixth-century Etruscan vases and fifth-century terracottas from Veii show him carrying Anchises; Hellanicus of Lesbos made him a founder of Rome with Odysseus (Dionysius of Halicarnassus 1.72.2); Thucydides (6.2.3) knows the Elymians of Sicily as Trojan refugees. Rome adopted the genealogy through Naevius and Ennius and used it in diplomacy: Ilion received Roman favour in the second century BC because the Romans were, formally, its colonists.

The Julii made it dynastic. Caesar's funeral oration for his aunt Julia derived the family from Venus (Suetonius, Divus Julius 6.1); he dedicated the temple of Venus Genetrix in 46 BC. Virgil's Jupiter spells out the line Aeneas–Iulus–Alba–Romulus–"Julius, a name descended from great Iulus" (Aeneid 1.267–288), and the Forum of Augustus set Aeneas with Anchises and Ascanius opposite Romulus. Lucretius had already called the Romans Aeneadae (1.1). To be Trojan was to be Roman, and the city the Greeks had barbarised became the ancestor of the people who ruled the Greeks.

10The poem's refusal

The Iliad is a Greek poem about a Greek victory, and it never once asks us to hate the Trojans. The first death in the fighting is Simoeisios, a Trojan youth who never repaid his parents for rearing him, compared to a felled poplar (4.473–489). The only family we enter is Hector's. The city's women, children and old men are on the walls; the Greek camp has no women except captives. Priam gets the closing book. Simone Weil's observation in "The Iliad, or the Poem of Force" (1940) remains the sharpest: a reader who did not know would find it hard to tell whether the poet was Greek or Trojan.

This is not neutrality about the war's cause. The poem knows that Paris broke the laws of hospitality and that Troy, by keeping Helen, is complicit; Antenor says so. But it declines the step every later tradition took, in which an enemy's wrongdoing licenses contempt. The barbarian of Euripides, the treacherous Antenor, the effeminate Paris in his cap are answers to a question the Iliad refused to ask. Its Trojans are men who fight well, love their families, make wrong choices under pressure and die. That is also its description of the Greeks. The remarkable thing is not that the poet sympathised with the losers but that he saw no reason to write them differently.

11Self-test

  1. How many sons does Priam claim, and how many by Hecuba? Cite the passage.
    Answer

    Fifty, nineteen "from one womb"; Iliad 24.495–497.

  2. Give the two incompatible accounts of Polydorus' parentage and death.
    Answer

    Iliad 20.407–418 and 21.84–91: son of Laothoe, killed by Achilles. Euripides, Hecuba: son of Hecuba, murdered by Polymestor in Thrace.

  3. Who in the Iliad is the prophet of Priam's house, and who takes over the role later?
    Answer

    Helenus, "best of augurs" (6.76). Cassandra acquires prophecy only post-Homerically, notably in Aeschylus' Agamemnon.

  4. What advice of Polydamas does Hector reject in Book 18, and where does Hector later admit the error?
    Answer

    To retreat inside the walls before Achilles returns (18.254–283). Hector admits it alone outside the gate, calling it his own atasthaliai (22.104).

  5. Which three women lament Hector, in what order, and why is the third surprising?
    Answer

    Andromache, Hecuba, Helen (24.719–804). Helen is a Greek and the cause of the war; she says Hector alone never reproached her.

  6. At whose altar does Priam die according to Proclus' Iliou Persis, and how does the Little Iliad differ?
    Answer

    Zeus Herkeios, in his courtyard. Pausanias 10.27.2 reports that the Little Iliad had Neoptolemus drag him from the altar and kill him at the doors.

  7. Why does Hera stop Zeus saving Sarpedon, and where is the argument re-run?
    Answer

    Because every god would then rescue a favourite son and the order of things would fail (16.431–461). Zeus faces the same choice over Hector at 22.168–181.

  8. Name two markers by which Attic art and drama turned Trojans into barbarians after 480 BC.
    Answer

    The Phrygian cap and eastern dress on vases; the term Phryges and the cowardly Phrygian eunuch in Euripides' Orestes; Clytemnestra's "swallow" line in Agamemnon 1050–1051.

  9. What are the two Hittite place-names matched with Ilios and Troia, and which treaty names King Alaksandu?
    Answer

    Wilusa (Wilios) and Taruisa (Troia). The treaty of Muwatalli II, c. 1280 BC.

  10. How did the Julii connect themselves to Troy?
    Answer

    Through Aeneas' son Iulus, and so to Venus (Virgil, Aeneid 1.267–288); Caesar's funeral speech for Julia claimed descent from Venus (Suetonius, Divus Julius 6.1).

12Carry forward

  1. The Iliad's Trojans speak Greek, worship Greek gods and live by Greek codes; the barbarian Trojan in the Phrygian cap is an Athenian invention of the decades after the Persian Wars, and every later text that uses it must be read with that date in mind.
  2. Hector is judged by the poem through his own mouth, not the narrator's: good counsel rejected (Polydamas), then self-knowledge too late, is the shape Greek tragedy will take from epic.
  3. The Hittite Wilusa dossier and the Aeneas-to-Rome line are the two directions in which Troy escapes Greek ownership, backwards into Anatolian history and forwards into Roman ideology; both grow from the small protected space the Iliad gave to Aeneas and to the idea that Troy was not simply the enemy.

The prompt this answers

Give me an in-depth report on the Trojans as the Iliad and the wider tradition present them. Cover: the house of Priam (Hecuba, the fifty sons, Hector, Paris, Helenus, Deiphobus, Troilus, Polydorus, Cassandra, Polyxena, Laodice) and the senior Trojans (Antenor, Anchises, Aeneas, Polydamas); Hector as the poem's second hero: his aristeia, Book 6 with Andromache and Astyanax, his relationship with Paris, his death and the question of whether the Iliad judges him, his funeral as the poem's last word; Priam: the ransom in Book 24 and the death at the altar; Hecuba from matriarch to Euripides' avenger; Andromache's afterlife with Neoptolemus and Helenus; the Trojan allies (Sarpedon and Glaucus of Lycia, Rhesus, Memnon, Penthesilea) and why Sarpedon's death matters; the language question: the Trojans speak Greek and worship Greek gods, so in what sense are they foreign, and when do Trojans become Phrygian barbarians (Aeschylus, Euripides, Athenian vase painting after the Persian Wars); the Hittite material: Wilusa, Alaksandu and the Luwian hypothesis for Troy's real identity; Troy as ancestor city for Rome (Aeneas) and for the Julii. End with the Iliad's refusal to make the Trojans villains and why that is the poem's most remarkable feature. Format: about 2500 words, headed sections, name sources, flag disagreements, end with a 10-question self-test with answers and the three ideas I must carry forward.
