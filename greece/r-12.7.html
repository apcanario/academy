Zeno of Citium and the Painted Stoa

Module 12.5 tells how Zeno came to philosophy: the shipwreck, the bookshop, Crates and the lentil soup (Diogenes Laertius 7.2–3). Diogenes adds Stilpo the Megarian and, in the Academy, Xenocrates and Polemo (7.2). From Crates came the sufficiency of virtue, from the Megarians compressed argument: what uses reason is better than what does not, nothing is better than the cosmos, so the cosmos uses reason (Cicero, On the Nature of the Gods 2.21). Cicero's On Ends 4, arguing Antiochus' case, presses that Zeno changed the Academy's words and kept its things.

He taught in the Stoa Poikile on the north side of the Agora, a public portico of the 460s hung with panels of Oinoe, the Amazons, fallen Troy and Marathon (Pausanias 1.15); his followers, first called Zenonians, became Stoics from the building (Diogenes 7.5). A public building gave the school no property to bequeath, unlike the Academy and the Garden. Athens voted Zeno a gold crown and a public tomb because his life matched his teaching (decree in Diogenes 7.10–12).

His most notorious book, the Republic, answered Plato's. Its hostile reporters describe a city of the wise without temples, law courts or coinage, where men and women dress alike and the wise hold wives in common (Diogenes 7.32–33, 7.131), bound by Eros as a god of friendship and concord (Athenaeus 13.561c). His pupils already disagreed: Aristo of Chios denied that any indifferent is preferred (7.160), and Herillus made knowledge the end (7.165).

Where sources disagree. Some later Stoics, Philodemus reports (On the Stoics), disowned the Republic as spurious or juvenile, and Athenodorus, keeper of the library at Pergamum, cut its offending passages from the school's books (Diogenes Laertius 7.34); Chrysippus went further in his own On the Republic (7.188). Plutarch read it as a dream of one world-city (module 7.2); Malcolm Schofield (The Stoic Idea of the City, 1991) argues for a community of the wise bound by concord and Eros, a rival to Plato's city rather than a world government.

Cleanthes, the Hymn to Zeus and Heraclitus' fire

Cleanthes came to Athens a boxer, drew water for gardeners by night to study by day, and was called the Ass for his slowness and endurance (Diogenes Laertius 7.168–170). His physics is where the Stoics took Heraclitus' fire (module 12.1). Zeno had defined nature as a craftsmanlike fire proceeding methodically to generation (Cicero, On the Nature of the Gods 2.57); Cleanthes wrote an Interpretation of Heraclitus in four books (7.174), made the sun the ruling part of the cosmos (Cicero, Academica 2.126), and called tension a stroke of fire (Plutarch, On Stoic Self-Contradictions 1034d).

The Hymn to Zeus, thirty-nine hexameters preserved by Stobaeus (Anthology 1.1.12; SVF 1.537), is the one early Stoic text that survives whole. Zeus steers everything by law, and the cosmos obeys his ever-living thunderbolt, Heraclitus' thunderbolt that steers all things (DK 22 B 64). Nothing happens without him except what the bad do in their folly; yet he fits good and bad together, so that one logos of all things exists for ever (lines 15–21). The bad flee this common logos, like Heraclitus' many who live as though they had a private understanding (B 2), and the poem prays Zeus to rescue them from ignorance. Evil is human, and the whole absorbs it. Cleanthes' short prayer asking Zeus and Destiny to lead him survives in Epictetus (Handbook 53; SVF 1.527); Seneca's Latin of it belongs to Rome module 13.2.

Where sources disagree. Did the Stoics find the conflagration in Heraclitus or put it there? Aristotle, before the Stoa, says Heraclitus has all things become fire at some time (Physics 205a3–4), and Zeller accepted a Heraclitean world-fire. Burnet (Early Greek Philosophy) and Kirk (Heraclitus: The Cosmic Fragments, 1954) argued that a fire kindled and quenched in measures (B 30) excludes a total conflagration, and that Clement and Hippolytus read Heraclitus through Stoic eyes.

Chrysippus: propositions and arguments

Chrysippus of Soli heard Cleanthes and, in the Academy, Arcesilaus and Lacydes (Diogenes Laertius 7.183). He told Cleanthes to give him the doctrines and he would find the proofs (7.179); of his 705 books (7.180) we have quotations and a charred roll of his Logical Questions from Herculaneum.

Aristotle's syllogistic (module 12.4) relates terms; Chrysippus' logic relates whole propositions. What is true or false is a sayable (lekton), the incorporeal content of a sentence; a complete sayable that is true or false is a proposition (axiōma: 7.65). Connectives make conditionals, conjunctions and exclusive disjunctions. Five indemonstrable forms need no proof (7.79–81; Sextus, Outlines of Pyrrhonism 2.157–158):

  1. If the first, the second; the first; therefore the second.
  2. If the first, the second; not the second; therefore not the first.
  3. Not both the first and the second; the first; therefore not the second.
  4. Either the first or the second; the first; therefore not the second.
  5. Either the first or the second; not the first; therefore the second.

Rules called themata reduce other valid arguments to these. Module 12.5 gives Philo's and Diodorus' conditionals; a stricter view, usually credited to Chrysippus, required that the contradictory of the consequent conflict with the antecedent (Outlines 2.111).

Chrysippus' possible, in Diogenes' wording, is what admits of being true and is not prevented by external circumstances (7.75). Against the Master Argument, which module 12.5 hands on, he denied that the impossible cannot follow from the possible. "Dion has died" is possible, but "this man has died", said pointing at Dion, can never be true, since at Dion's death the "this" loses its object; yet it follows (Alexander of Aphrodisias, On Aristotle's Prior Analytics 177–178). Cleanthes instead gave up the necessity of every past truth (Epictetus, Discourses 2.19.2–4). The criterion was Zeno's cognitive impression: from what is, stamped in accordance with it, and such as could not come from what is not, the last clause added against Arcesilaus (Cicero, Academica 2.77), whose attack, and Carneades', is module 12.8's.

Where sources disagree. Carl Prantl's Geschichte der Logik im Abendlande (1855) dismissed Stoic logic as barren formalism. Jan Łukasiewicz (1935) recognised in the indemonstrables a logic of propositions two millennia before Frege, and Benson Mates (Stoic Logic, 1953) set it out in modern notation. How far it is a propositional calculus in the modern sense is still argued, since Chrysippus' conditional was not the truth-functional one.

Chrysippus: pneuma, mixture, fate and the conflagration

There are two principles, active (god, reason) and passive (unqualified matter), both bodies (Diogenes Laertius 7.134), since only body acts or is acted on; void, place, time and sayables are incorporeals that subsist without existing (Sextus, Against the Mathematicians 10.218). Chrysippus supplied the mechanism for module 7.2's outline. God is pneuma, fire and air, whose tensile motion runs inward and outward at once, giving each body its unity and qualities (Nemesius, On the Nature of Man 2). Grades of tension make the scale of nature: cohesion (hexis) in stones, growth (physis) in plants, soul in animals, reason in humans (Philo, Allegorical Interpretation 2.22–23).

Pneuma pervading matter means two bodies in one place. Chrysippus distinguished juxtaposition, as of grains; fusion, in which the ingredients perish into a new stuff; and total blending, in which each extends through the whole of the other and keeps its qualities, as wine in water or soul in body (Alexander of Aphrodisias, On Mixture 216–218). A drop of wine, he said, will blend with the whole sea (Diogenes 7.151); Alexander and Plutarch found this absurd.

Fate is the chain of causes, and divination its evidence (Cicero, On Divination 1.125–127). The Lazy Argument objected that if you are fated to recover you will recover whether or not you call the doctor. Chrysippus answered that recovery is co-fated with calling the doctor, as Oedipus' birth with Laius lying with a woman (Cicero, On Fate 28–30). He divided causes into perfect and principal, and auxiliary and proximate (41–43): an impression is only the proximate cause of assent, the principal cause being the mind's own character, as the cylinder's shape, not the push, makes it roll (module 7.2). Action is fated and still ours, because it runs through us.

Against evil, Chrysippus argued that opposites need each other, so there is no justice without injustice, and that some harms are by-products of good design, as the thin bones that suit the skull make it fragile (Gellius 7.1); even bedbugs serve, by waking us (Plutarch, On Stoic Self-Contradictions 1044d). Periodically the whole returns to fire, god alone remaining, and the same world is reborn with the same Socrates (Nemesius, On the Nature of Man 38).

Where sources disagree. Cicero presents Chrysippus as an honorary arbiter between those who put everything under fate and those who freed the mind's motions from it (On Fate 39); Alexander of Aphrodisias (On Fate) replies that responsibility without the power to do otherwise is responsibility in name only. Susanne Bobzien (Determinism and Freedom in Stoic Philosophy, 1998) argues that Chrysippus was not answering that question: "up to us" meant action that comes about through our own assent.

Chrysippus: oikeiosis and the end

Stoic ethics begins with the newborn. An animal's first impulse, Chrysippus wrote in book 1 of On Ends, is to preserve itself, since nature makes it from the start appropriated (oikeion) to itself and aware of its own constitution; pleasure, the Epicurean first object (module 12.6), is a by-product that supervenes once it has what suits it (Diogenes Laertius 7.85–86; SVF 3.178; LS 57A). Cicero's Cato traces the development (On Ends 3.16–22): from self-preservation to selecting what accords with nature, then to seeing order and agreement in conduct, which the agent values above everything that first attracted him (3.21), as one may value a person more than the friend who introduced him (3.23). Extended through love of offspring, the impulse grounds justice and the community of rational beings (3.62–64); Hierocles' circles are Rome module 13.2's.

The end was restated generation by generation. Diogenes Laertius (7.87) gives Zeno "living in agreement with nature"; Stobaeus (2.75–76) says Zeno said only "living in agreement" and Cleanthes added "with nature". Chrysippus glossed it as living according to experience of what happens by nature (7.87). Zeno had coined kathēkon, appropriate action, an act with a reasonable justification (7.107–108); done perfectly, from virtue, it is a katorthōma, open only to the sage. Chrysippus' passions, with his runner who cannot stop at will (Galen, On the Doctrines of Hippocrates and Plato 4.2), are what Posidonius attacked.

Diogenes of Babylon and Antipater of Tarsus

After Chrysippus and Zeno of Tarsus came Diogenes of Seleucia, called the Babylonian. His Art of Voice supplied Diogenes Laertius' Stoic grammar (7.55–58), and his books on music and rhetoric survive through Philodemus' replies at Herculaneum; his embassy to Rome in 155 with Carneades and Critolaus belongs to Rome modules 4.5 and 13.1. He defined the end as reasoning well in selecting what accords with nature (7.88). His pupil Antipater, who wrote so much against Carneades and debated him so little that he was called the pen-shouter (Plutarch, On Talkativeness), made it living continuously selecting what accords with nature and rejecting what does not (Stobaeus 2.76). Cicero's archer, who does all he can to hit the mark while the aiming, not the hit, is what counts (On Ends 3.22), states the position; A. A. Long ("Carneades and the Stoic Telos", 1967) reads both restatements as answers to Carneades.

The two argue in Cicero, On Duties 3.50–57. A merchant brings grain to famine-struck Rhodes knowing more ships are coming: must he say so? Diogenes says keeping silent is not concealing; Antipater that a man bound into the human community may not profit from his neighbours' ignorance. Cicero sides with Antipater.

The sources

Hans von Arnim's Stoicorum Veterum Fragmenta (1903–1905; index volume by Maximilian Adler, 1924) gathers Zeno and his pupils, Chrysippus' logic and physics, and his ethics with the successors through Antipater; Panaetius and Posidonius have editions of their own. Long and Sedley's The Hellenistic Philosophers (1987) translates the core texts by topic.

Diogenes Laertius book 7 gives Zeno's life, a long doxography (7.38–160), partly quoted word for word from Diocles of Magnesia (7.48), and lives of Cleanthes and Chrysippus; it breaks off in Chrysippus' book list. Stobaeus keeps the Hymn and an epitome of Stoic ethics usually credited to Arius Didymus (Anthology 2.7; its passages are cited here by the volume and page of Wachsmuth's edition, as 2.75–76). Cicero expounds the system through spokesmen, Cato in On Ends 3 and Balbus in On the Nature of the Gods 2, and attacks it in his own voice in On Ends 4. The longest verbatim quotations come from enemies: Plutarch's On Stoic Self-Contradictions and On Common Conceptions quote Chrysippus to convict him of inconsistency, and Galen's On the Doctrines of Hippocrates and Plato quotes pages of his On Passions to refute them, with Posidonius as witness. Doctrine and hostile frame must be separated every time.

Panaetius and Posidonius: the hinge to Rome

Panaetius of Rhodes, pupil of Diogenes and Antipater, lived for years in Scipio Aemilianus' house (module 7.3) before heading the school at Athens. He dropped what critics found hardest to believe. He held the cosmos everlasting and doubted the conflagration (Diogenes Laertius 7.142; Cicero, On the Nature of the Gods 2.118), as Boethus of Sidon had (Philo, On the Eternity of the World 76); he alone of the Stoics rejected astrology, and he doubted divination (Cicero, On Divination 2.88, 1.6); and he argued that the soul, being born, must die, though he called Plato the Homer of philosophers (Tusculan Disputations 1.79). Cicero says he always had Plato, Aristotle, Xenocrates, Theophrastus and Dicaearchus on his lips (On Ends 4.79). His three books On Proper Functions were the model of Cicero's On Duties (Letters to Atticus 16.11.4); the four personae are Rome module 13.2's.

Posidonius of Apamea studied with Panaetius, settled at Rhodes and went as its envoy to Rome in 87/86 (Plutarch, Marius 45; EK F255). He travelled to Gades and Gaul, where he grew used to heads nailed over doorways (Strabo 4.4.5; EK F274). His Histories, fifty-two books continuing Polybius from 146 (Suda; EK T1), included the satire on Athenion, the Peripatetic who took Athens over to Mithridates in 88 (Athenaeus 5.211d–215b; EK F253), the prelude to Sulla's siege in module 7.3; whether he is the tyrant Aristion of Plutarch and Appian, as module 7.3 names him, or Aristion's predecessor is disputed. He measured the earth from the height of Canopus: 240,000 stades in Cleomedes (On the Heavens; EK F202), 180,000 in Strabo (2.2.2). Strabo found in him much hunting of causes and much Aristotelising, which Stoics avoid (2.3.8).

On the passions he turned Chrysippus' admissions against him, as Galen reports (On the Doctrines of Hippocrates and Plato 4–5; module 7.2). Why does grief fade with time while the belief that one has suffered an evil remains, as Chrysippus conceded (4.7; EK F165)? Why do children and animals, without reason, have passions? His answer was that the soul has irrational powers, spirited and appetitive, whose affective movements must be trained by habit, rhythm and music rather than refuted; he claimed Cleanthes, and behind him Pythagoras and Plato, for the view (5.6). His end was to live contemplating the truth and order of the whole, in no way led by the irrational part of the soul (Clement, Stromateis 2.21). What Romans heard from the two is Rome modules 13.1 and 13.2.

Where sources disagree. Galen makes Posidonius a Stoic converted to Plato on the soul, while conceding that he spoke of powers of one soul, not of parts. Karl Reinhardt (Poseidonios, 1921) built an original system-maker from passages in Cicero, Seneca and others that do not name him; Ludwig Edelstein (1936) admitted named fragments only, the rule of the Edelstein–Kidd edition (1972), and Kidd's commentary (1988) reads the result as a Stoic who widened his school's search for causes without leaving it. Willy Theiler's edition (1982) admits much unnamed material and keeps the larger figure.
Where sources disagree. "Middle Stoa" is a modern period, fixed by August Schmekel's Die Philosophie der mittleren Stoa (1892) for a softened, Platonising phase between Chrysippus and the Roman Stoics. Brad Inwood (Later Stoicism 155 BC to AD 200, 2022) rejects any middle period and begins a later Stoicism with Antipater; his reviewer Jacob Klein (Bryn Mawr Classical Review 2025.01.21) answers that Diogenes Laertius has Panaetius and Posidonius deny that virtue suffices for happiness (7.128), which looks like a real departure under Academic pressure.

Self-test

  1. What did Zeno take from Crates and from the Megarians, and what charge does Cicero's On Ends 4 bring against his borrowing from the Academy?
    Answer

    From Crates the sufficiency of virtue, from the Megarians compressed argument. Cicero, arguing Antiochus' case, says Zeno changed the Academy's words and kept its substance.

  2. Why did some Stoics want to be rid of Zeno's Republic, and how do Plutarch and Schofield read its city differently?
    Answer

    It abolished temples, courts and coinage and held wives in common among the wise (Diogenes Laertius 7.32–33, 7.131), so Athenodorus cut such passages at Pergamum (7.34). Plutarch saw a single world-city; Schofield a community of sages bound by concord and Eros.

  3. How does Cleanthes' Hymn to Zeus square providence with evil, and where does Heraclitus show through?
    Answer

    Nothing happens without Zeus except what the bad do in their folly, and Zeus fits good and bad into one logos of the whole (lines 15–21). The steering thunderbolt (DK 22 B 64) and the common logos the many ignore (B 2) are Heraclitean.

  4. Why is Chrysippus' logic called a logic of propositions, and what is the form of the second indemonstrable?
    Answer

    Its units are whole propositions joined by connectives, where Aristotle's syllogistic relates terms. The second: if the first, the second; not the second; therefore not the first.

  5. Which premise of the Master Argument did Chrysippus deny, and how does the Dion example support him?
    Answer

    That the impossible cannot follow from the possible. "Dion has died" is possible, but "this man has died", pointing at Dion, can never be true, yet follows from it (Alexander, On Aristotle's Prior Analytics 177–178).

  6. Why did Stoic physics need total blending, and how does it differ from fusion?
    Answer

    Pneuma, a body, must pervade matter, another body, so two bodies must occupy one place. In total blending each ingredient keeps its qualities through the whole (wine in water); in fusion the ingredients perish into a new stuff.

  7. How did Chrysippus answer the Lazy Argument, and how do principal and proximate causes keep action "up to us"?
    Answer

    Recovery is co-fated with calling the doctor, so effort is not idle (Cicero, On Fate 28–30). The impression is only the proximate cause of assent; the principal cause is the agent's own character, as the cylinder's shape makes it roll (41–43).

  8. What is the first impulse of an animal for Chrysippus, and why does that matter against Epicurus?
    Answer

    Self-preservation, from being appropriated to one's own constitution (Diogenes Laertius 7.85–86). Pleasure, which Epicurus made the first object, is only a by-product that supervenes once the animal has what suits it.

  9. Why must Plutarch's and Galen's quotations of Chrysippus be handled with care, and why are they indispensable?
    Answer

    Both quote him to refute him, choosing and framing passages to show inconsistency or error. Yet they give the longest verbatim extracts we have, from works such as On Passions.

  10. Name two doctrines Panaetius gave up or doubted, and one argument Posidonius used against Chrysippus on the passions.
    Answer

    Any two of the conflagration, divination and astrology, and the soul's survival. Posidonius: grief fades with time though the judgement of evil remains, as Chrysippus admitted (Galen 4.7); or children and animals have passions without reason.

Carry forward

  1. Stoicism is one system: fate needs the physics of pneuma, responsibility needs the theory of assent, and the end needs a providential cosmos. Romans who kept the ethics and left the rest, as Rome module 13.1 asks why, inherited conclusions without their supports.
  2. The early Stoa survives almost wholly in reporters, many hostile; name the reporter for every doctrine (Diogenes, Stobaeus, Cicero's spokesmen, Plutarch, Galen) and strip the frame before judging the claim.
  3. The school changed under attack: Antipater's restated end, Panaetius' doubts on conflagration and divination, Posidonius' irrational powers. Whether or not that makes a "Middle Stoa", the Stoicism Rome met at Rhodes and in Scipio's house was the revised one.
