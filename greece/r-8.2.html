Delphi: the Pythia and the procedure

Delphi sits on the southern slope of Parnassus above the Pleistos gorge, in Phocis. Apollo's sanctuary there was active from the eighth century BC and functioned, with fluctuations, until the fourth century AD. The mouthpiece was the Pythia, a local woman of mature age who, after Delphi's prestige grew, was supplemented by two more so that consultations could run in shifts (Plutarch, De defectu oraculorum 414b). Our best witness is Plutarch himself, who held a priesthood at Delphi around AD 100 and wrote three dialogues set there; the price of his reliability is that he describes a diminished Roman-era oracle, not the archaic one.

Procedure, reconstructed from Plutarch, Euripides' Ion, Diodorus 16.26 and inscriptions: consultation originally fell once a year on the seventh of the month Bysios, Apollo's birthday, later on the seventh of every month outside the three winter months when Apollo was away and Dionysus held the sanctuary. The enquirer paid the pelanos (a cake-fee, with cities such as Phaselis and Sciathus negotiating fixed rates), sacrificed a goat, and waited in the order of promanteia, the right of priority that Delphi granted to favoured states such as Chios. Before the session the goat was sprinkled with cold water; if it failed to tremble, the day was unpropitious (Plutarch, De defectu 435b, 437a–b). The Pythia bathed at the Castalian spring, burned laurel and barley on the altar, and sat on the tripod in the adyton, the sunken inner room of the temple. The prophetai and the five hosioi, drawn from Delphian aristocratic families, managed the enquirer and transmitted the response.

Where sources disagree. Whether the Pythia raved or spoke lucidly is unresolved. Plato (Phaedrus 244b) and Plutarch treat her inspiration as a kind of divine madness, and Plutarch records a Pythia who was forced to prophesy on a bad day, shrieked, fled the adyton and died within days (De defectu 438a–b). Fontenrose (1978) argued from the catalogue of historically attested responses that she answered plainly and that the frenzied Pythia is a literary construct, largely Lucan's; Maurizio (1995) replied that spirit possession can be coherent and that the priests' alleged hexameter "versifying" of her words is a modern assumption with little ancient support. Uncertain, and probably varied over nine centuries.

Delphi: the gas theory, colonisation and politics

Strabo 9.3.5 and Diodorus 16.26 speak of a chasm beneath the tripod exhaling a pneuma enthousiastikon, and Plutarch's companions discuss a sweet fragrance that sometimes filled the room (De defectu 437c). The French excavators of the 1890s found no chasm, and Oppé (1904) dismissed the vapour as fiction. In 2001 de Boer, Hale and Chanton published geological work showing that two faults, the Delphi and the Kerna, intersect beneath the temple, that the bituminous limestone below is capable of releasing light hydrocarbons, and that spring water nearby contained ethylene, methane and ethane. Ethylene has a sweet smell and in low concentration produces a dissociated, euphoric state, which matched Plutarch suspiciously well.

Where sources disagree. The gas theory was widely reported as settled; it is not. Lehoux (2007) and Foster and Lehoux (2007) pointed out that the measured ethylene was far below any pharmacological threshold and that the adyton was not a sealed chamber. Etiope and colleagues (2006) proposed that methane and carbon dioxide, not ethylene, were the active agents, with oxygen depletion rather than intoxication at work. The ancient tradition itself is not unanimous: Plutarch's own speakers are divided over whether the pneuma matters at all, and no classical author before the Hellenistic period mentions a chasm. A fault zone and seeping gas are now established; what the Pythia breathed, and whether it mattered to the responses, remains uncertain.

Herodotus is the great storehouse of Delphic responses, and he uses them as plot. Croesus tests the oracles by asking what he is doing on a given day; only Delphi and Amphiaraus answer correctly (1.46–49). Delphi then tells him that attacking Persia will "destroy a great empire" (1.53) and that he need not fear until "a mule becomes king of the Medes" (1.55); after his fall the Pythia defends herself by explaining that the empire was his own and the mule was the half-Median Cyrus (1.91). The Spartans ask about conquering Arcadia and are told they may "dance on Tegea" in chains, which they do, as prisoners (1.66). Battus of Thera is ordered to found Cyrene and, after resisting, is punished with seven years' drought (4.150–157). In 481/480 the Athenians receive first a response telling them to flee to the ends of the earth and then, after supplication, the famous "wooden wall" and "divine Salamis" response that Themistocles reads as a reference to the fleet (7.140–143). Herodotus also records the oracle's corruptibility: the Alcmaeonids bribe the Pythia to urge Sparta to free Athens (5.63), and Cleomenes bribes her to declare Demaratus illegitimate (6.66).

Delphi's authority in colonisation is beyond doubt for the archaic period; Cyrene, Croton, Rhegium, Taras and Gela all carried foundation oracles, and Pindar's Pythian 4 and 5 celebrate Battus's commission. Malkin (1987) saw Apollo Archegetes as a genuine clearing-house for geographical knowledge gathered from enquirers; Londey (1990) and others treat many foundation oracles as later legitimation. Delphi's political alignments are easier to see: the Spartan constitution was attributed to Delphic sanction (Tyrtaeus fr. 4 West; Herodotus 1.65), the Spartan kings kept standing envoys, the Pythioi (Herodotus 6.57), and the oracle's discouraging responses to Greeks resisting Xerxes (7.140, 7.148 to Argos) left it open to the charge of medising.

The Amphictyony and the Sacred Wars

Delphi was not governed by Delphi. The Pylaean-Delphic Amphictyony was a league of twelve ethne (Thessalians, Boeotians, Dorians, Ionians, Perrhaebians, Magnesians, Locrians, Oetaeans, Phthiotic Achaeans, Malians, Phocians, Dolopians), each with two hieromnemones, meeting twice a year at Anthela near Thermopylae and at Delphi. Aeschines 2.115 quotes its oath: not to destroy any Amphictyonic city and not to cut off its running water. The league administered the sanctuary's funds, rebuilt the temple after the fire of 548 and the earthquake of 373, and ran the Pythian Games.

Dodona, Siwa, Trophonius and the Asclepieia

Dodona in Epirus belonged to Zeus Naios and Dione. Homer already knows the Selloi, interpreters "with unwashed feet, sleeping on the ground" (Iliad 16.233–235), and Odysseus claims to have gone "to hear the will of Zeus from the high-leaved oak" (Odyssey 14.327–328). Herodotus 2.54–57 calls it the oldest oracle in Greece and tells of the prophetic doves, rationalising them as priestesses nicknamed peleiades; Plato pairs the Dodonaean priestesses with the Pythia as inspired madwomen (Phaedrus 244b). The excavations of Evangelidis and Dakaris, and the 2013 corpus of Dakaris, Vokotopoulou and Christidis, have produced over four thousand lead tablets inscribed with the enquirers' questions: whether to marry, whether a child is one's own, which god to sacrifice to, who stole the blankets. They are the single best evidence for what ordinary Greeks actually asked an oracle. The sanctuary was sacked by the Aetolians in 219 BC and never fully recovered.

Siwa, the oracle of Ammon in the Libyan desert, was Egyptian Amun identified with Zeus; Herodotus 1.46 lists it among the oracles Croesus tested, and Cyrene and Sparta both consulted it (Pausanias 3.18.3 on Lysander). The god answered through the movements of a statue carried in a boat by priests (Diodorus 17.50). Alexander's visit in 331 made it famous; Callisthenes (in Strabo 17.1.43), Arrian 3.3–4 and Plutarch Alexander 26–27 disagree about what he was told, Plutarch preserving the story that the priest's Greek slipped from o paidion to o pai Dios, "son of Zeus".

Trophonius at Lebadeia in Boeotia is the only oracle whose procedure we have from an initiate: Pausanias 9.39.5–14 describes his own consultation. The enquirer lodged for days in the house of Good Fortune and the Good Daimon, bathed in the river Hercyna, sacrificed repeatedly, drank from the springs of Forgetfulness and Memory, and was lowered feet first through a narrow hole into a chamber, carrying honey-cakes, to be swept away and shown or told the future. He returned stupefied and was seated on the Chair of Memory to dictate what he had experienced. The proverb "he has consulted Trophonius", said of a man who never laughs, was already current for Aristophanes (Clouds 508). Herodotus 8.134 has Mardonius's agent Mys consult it before Plataea.

Epidaurus and the other Asclepieia (Cos, Pergamon, Athens from 420/419, Rome's Tiber island from 293) were healing oracles working by enkoimesis, incubation: the patient slept in the abaton and the god, or his snakes and dogs, visited in dreams. The fourth-century iamata stelae from Epidaurus (IG IV² 1, 121–124; LiDonnici 1995) record some seventy cures: Ambrosia of Athens, blind in one eye, laughs at the cures and is healed anyway; Cleo, pregnant for five years, gives birth to a boy who walks at once. Aristophanes' Wealth 653–747 parodies an incubation at the Piraeus with a priest stealing the offerings, which shows the practice was familiar enough to mock by 388. Aelius Aristides' Sacred Tales are the second-century patient's view from Pergamon.

Eleusis

The Mysteries of Demeter and Kore at Eleusis, twenty kilometres west of Athens, were open to any Greek speaker of either sex and any status, slaves included, who was free of blood-guilt. The foundation charter is the Homeric Hymn to Demeter (seventh or sixth century), which ends with Demeter teaching her rites to the Eleusinian princes and the promise that whoever has seen them is olbios, blessed, while the uninitiated "has no share in such things" below (473–482). Pindar fr. 137 and Sophocles fr. 837 repeat the promise; Isocrates (Panegyricus 28) makes it the Athenian gift to mankind.

The calendar, reconstructed from inscriptions and scholia, is reasonably secure. The Lesser Mysteries at Agrai on the Ilissos, in Anthesterion (February–March), were a preliminary purification; the Greater fell in Boedromion (September–October). On the 14th the sacred objects (hiera) were carried from Eleusis to the Eleusinion in Athens. On the 15th, the agyrmos, the hierophant proclaimed the exclusion of the impure and of those "unintelligible of speech". On the 16th, "to the sea, initiates!", each candidate washed a piglet and himself. Sacrifices and a day of rest followed; on the 19th the great procession walked the Sacred Way with the statue of Iacchus, enduring ritual abuse at the Cephisus bridge (the gephyrismos), arriving by torchlight. The initiates then fasted and broke the fast with the kykeon, barley water flavoured with pennyroyal, as Demeter did in the Hymn (208–209). What happened in the Telesterion on the nights of the 20th and 21st was never to be spoken.

The Telesterion was a square hall, enlarged under Peisistratus and rebuilt under Pericles by Ictinus and others (Strabo 9.1.12; Plutarch, Pericles 13), seating thousands on stepped benches around a small inner chamber, the Anaktoron, which only the hierophant entered. He came from the Eumolpidae; the torch-bearer (dadouchos) and sacred herald from the Kerykes. The ancient grammarians divide the rite into things done, things said and things shown. Of the first, the Christian Clement (Protrepticus 2.21) quotes the password: "I fasted, I drank the kykeon, I took from the chest". Of the third, Hippolytus (Refutatio 5.8.39) says the hierophant displayed in silence a reaped ear of grain. Aristotle (fr. 15 Rose) said initiates were "not to learn but to experience"; Plutarch (fr. 178 Sandbach) compares dying to the initiate's passage from terror in the dark to light, meadows and voices. A second grade, epopteia, followed a year later.

Where sources disagree. Almost everything about the central night rests on hostile Christian writers of the second and third centuries AD, who may be reporting hearsay or conflating cults. Mylonas (1961) refused to use them; Clinton (1992) and Burkert use them cautiously. The suggestion of Wasson, Hofmann and Ruck (1978) that the kykeon contained ergot is unsupported by any ancient text and is rejected by most specialists.

In summer 415, on the eve of the Sicilian expedition and amid the mutilation of the Herms, informers accused Alcibiades and others of parodying the Mysteries in private houses (Thucydides 6.27–29, 6.53, 6.60–61; Andocides 1 On the Mysteries; Plutarch, Alcibiades 19–22). Alcibiades sailed, was condemned in absentia, had his property confiscated and his name cursed by the Eumolpidae and Kerykes; the stelae recording the sale of the condemned men's goods survive. When he returned in 408/7 he made amends by leading the procession overland under armed escort for the first time since the Spartan occupation of Decelea (Xenophon, Hellenica 1.4.20; Plutarch, Alcibiades 34). The cult survived Christian legislation until Alaric's Goths destroyed the sanctuary in 395/6 (Eunapius, Lives of the Sophists 7.3).

Orphism, the gold tablets and the Pythagoreans

"Orphism" names not a church but a body of poems attributed to Orpheus, a set of ritual experts who used them (Plato's orpheotelestai, Republic 364e), and a cluster of ideas about the soul: the body as a prison or tomb (Cratylus 400c), transmigration, purification by abstinence, and a judgement below. The Derveni papyrus, carbonised on a pyre near Thessaloniki around 330 BC and finally published in 2006, is an allegorical commentary on an Orphic theogony in which Zeus swallows the phallus of the sky-god and recreates the world; it proves that Orphic poetry and its exegesis were current in the fifth century.

The anthropogony most readers call Orphic runs: the Titans lure the infant Dionysus with toys, dismember and cook him; Zeus blasts them with lightning, and from the smoking remains humankind is born, carrying both a Titanic guilt and a Dionysiac spark. This explains Plato's reference to a "Titanic nature" (Laws 701c) and Pindar's "penalty for ancient grief" (fr. 133) that Persephone accepts before releasing souls.

Where sources disagree. The full myth, with humans made from the Titans' ashes, is first stated by Olympiodorus in the sixth century AD (On the Phaedo 1.3). Edmonds (1999, 2013) argues it is a Neoplatonic construction and that Pindar and Plato are being read backwards; Bernabé, and Graf and Johnston (2007), hold that the Pelinna tablets and Pindar fr. 133 presuppose it. Genuinely open.

The gold tablets, some forty thin inscribed leaves placed in graves from the late fifth century BC to the second century AD, are the hard evidence. The Hipponion leaf (c. 400 BC) tells the dead to avoid the first spring, to say to the guardians "I am the child of Earth and starry Heaven", to drink from the lake of Memory, and to walk the road that "other mystai and bacchoi" walk. The Pelinna ivy leaves (late fourth century) say "tell Persephone that Bacchios himself has freed you" and "a bull you leapt into milk". Zuntz (1971) denied the tablets were Orphic at all; the Pelinna finds, naming Bacchus, and the Olbia bone plaques of the fifth century, which scratch "life death life" and "Dio(nysus) Orphikoi", have turned most scholars towards a Bacchic-Orphic milieu.

The Pythagorean overlap was noticed early. Herodotus 2.81 says that the rule against burial in wool is shared by "the so-called Orphic and Bacchic" rites, "which are really Egyptian and Pythagorean", and Ion of Chios claimed Pythagoras wrote poems under Orpheus's name. Both traditions taught transmigration, vegetarianism and ritual purity; Burkert (1972) treated Pythagoras as a shamanic figure in an Orphic world, while the Pythagorean political brotherhoods of Croton and the mathematical tradition belong elsewhere.

Dionysiac mysteries and maenadism

Ritual maenadism was a real institution, but a regulated one. Women's thiasoi left the city in alternate winters for oreibasia, dancing on the mountain, at Thebes, Delphi (the Thyiades; Plutarch, Mulierum Virtutes 249e–f) and elsewhere. A Milesian inscription of 276/5 BC (LSAM 48) has the public priestess of Dionysus licensing private initiations and reserving the right to throw in the omophagion, the raw-meat offering; a Magnesian inscription (I.Magnesia 215) records the city importing three maenads from Thebes, "of the race of Ino", on Delphic instruction to found three thiasoi. Henrichs (1978) used such texts to show that historical maenads were respectable citizen women performing a periodic rite, not hysterics; Dodds (1951) had taken ecstasy more literally.

Euripides' Bacchae (405 BC) gives the fullest literary picture and the greatest risk of confusion. Its chorus of Asian Bacchants describes genuine ritual: fawnskins, thyrsus, ivy, the mountain dances, "the joy of raw-eaten flesh" (138–139), and the blessedness of one who "knows the rites of the gods" (73). The messengers report miracles of milk, wine and honey and the tearing of cattle (677–774, 1043–1152). But the Theban women on Cithaeron are not initiates; they are driven mad as punishment for denying the god's birth, and Agave's killing of Pentheus is the myth of resistance, not the content of a mystery. The play tells us what Athenians found plausible about Bacchic worship and nothing certain about any initiation.

Private Dionysiac mysteries with a promise for the afterlife are better attested than the maenadic rites. Plato already quotes "many carry the thyrsus, few are bacchoi" (Phaedo 69c). Ptolemy IV ordered everyone initiating into Dionysus in Egypt to register and deposit a sealed copy of their sacred text (BGU VI 1211). The Roman senate's suppression of the Bacchanalia in 186 BC (Livy 39.8–19; CIL I² 581) shows initiation groups spreading through Italy; the Villa of the Mysteries frieze at Pompeii and the Torre Nova inscription of Agrippinilla's four-hundred-member thiasos (IGUR 160) show the cult as a graded, prosperous, imperial-age association.

Samothrace and the Cabiri

The Great Gods of Samothrace were never securely named in public; Greeks called them Cabiri by analogy with the Cabirion of Thebes (Pausanias 9.25.5–10) and Lemnos, and a scholiast on Apollonius (1.917, citing Mnaseas) gives the secret names Axieros, Axiokersa, Axiokersos and Kasmilos, with Kasmilos equated to Hermes. Herodotus 2.51 says the Pelasgians taught the Samothracians the ithyphallic Hermes and that "whoever has been initiated knows what I mean". Diodorus 5.47 reports that the ritual retained a non-Greek language, confirmed by inscriptions in an unidentified pre-Greek tongue. Unlike Eleusis, both grades could be taken on one visit at any time of year, by anyone; initiates wore a purple sash and an iron ring, and were promised safety at sea (Aristophanes, Peace 277–278; Diodorus 4.43). A confession of one's worst deed to a priest is recorded in the story of the Spartan who answered that if he had done anything bad the gods already knew (Plutarch, Apophthegmata Laconica 217c–d). Philip II is said to have met Olympias at the initiation (Plutarch, Alexander 2.2), and the Macedonian and Ptolemaic kings endowed the sanctuary's great buildings. Cole (1984) and Dimitrova's 2008 edition of the initiate lists are the standard studies.

Isis, Cybele, Mithras: imports through a Greek filter

The three great cults of the Roman Empire were not "oriental religions" imported wholesale, as Cumont framed them; Burkert (1987) showed that each was remodelled on the Greek mystery pattern, voluntary, individual, graded and promising personal benefit, before it reached Rome.

Isis: Herodotus 2.59 already equated her with Demeter. Under the Ptolemies she acquired a Greek consort, Sarapis, a hellenised Osiris-Apis whose cult statue came from Sinope (Plutarch, De Iside 28; Tacitus, Histories 4.83–84), and Greek aretalogies in which she speaks in the first person: "I am Isis, mistress of every land" (the Cyme text). Egyptians were allowed a temple in Piraeus by 333/2 BC (IG II² 337), and the Delian Sarapeia date from the third century. Her mysteries proper, with an initiation modelled on death and rebirth, are known from Apuleius, Metamorphoses 11, where Lucius says he "approached the boundary of death" and returned.

Cybele, the Phrygian Mountain Mother, was in Greek cities from the sixth century, with the Metroon in the Athenian Agora housing the state archive; Herodotus 4.76 has Anacharsis killed for celebrating her rites learned at Cyzicus. The castrated Galli and the Attis myth (Catullus 63) were a later, Hellenistic and Roman development; Rome formally imported her from Pessinus in 204 BC through Pergamene mediation (Livy 29.10–14). Roller (1999) traces how a Greek iconography of the enthroned goddess with lions preceded Roman reception.

Mithras carries an Iranian name and nothing securely Iranian in cult. The earliest notice is Plutarch's Cilician pirates performing his rites (Pompey 24); the mithraea, the bull-slaying image and the seven grades (Corax, Nymphus, Miles, Leo, Perses, Heliodromus, Pater: Jerome, Epistles 107.2; the Felicissimus mosaic at Ostia) appear from the late first century AD. The Greek intermediaries are visible in Commagene, where Antiochus I worshipped Apollo-Mithras-Helios-Hermes, in the Greek treatises of Eubulus and Pallas that Porphyry used (De abstinentia 4.16; De antro nympharum 6), and in the cult's Hellenistic astrology (Beck 2006).

Where sources disagree. Cumont's Iranian derivation of Mithraism was abandoned after the 1970s, but no replacement commands consent: Gordon and Clauss see a new Roman creation under a Persian label; Beck reads the tauroctony as a star map; Merkelbach proposed a single founder in Rome. The dependence on Greek astrology and Greek mystery form is the one point agreed.

Self-test

  1. On which day of the month did Delphic consultations take place, and why were three months unavailable?
    Answer

    The seventh: at first once a year, on the seventh of Bysios, Apollo's birthday, later the seventh of every month; in the three winter months Apollo was held to be away and Dionysus occupied the sanctuary.

  2. What was the purpose of sprinkling the sacrificial goat with cold water?
    Answer

    If it trembled, the day was propitious; if not, the day was unpropitious (Plutarch, De defectu 435b, 437a–b).

  3. State the two main objections to the ethylene version of the gas theory.
    Answer

    Measured concentrations were far below any pharmacological threshold, and the adyton was not a sealed space; alternative proposals favour methane and carbon dioxide or doubt the pneuma mattered at all.

  4. Which two Herodotean responses did Croesus misread, and how did the Pythia defend herself?
    Answer

    The "great empire" he would destroy and the "mule" king of the Medes; after his fall she explained that the empire was his own and the mule was the half-Median Cyrus (1.91).

  5. What did the Amphictyonic oath forbid, and who quotes it?
    Answer

    Destroying any Amphictyonic city or cutting off its running water; Aeschines 2.115.

  6. Why is Robertson's view of the First Sacred War significant?
    Answer

    He argued it was invented in the fourth century as a precedent for the Third, since no fifth-century author mentions it.

  7. What makes the Dodona lead tablets uniquely valuable?
    Answer

    They preserve thousands of actual questions from ordinary enquirers, showing the everyday business of an oracle rather than the literary set-pieces.

  8. Name the two Eleusinian priestly families and the offices each supplied.
    Answer

    The Eumolpidae supplied the hierophant; the Kerykes supplied the dadouchos (torch-bearer) and the sacred herald.

  9. Why does the Hipponion tablet matter for the "Orphic or Bacchic" debate?
    Answer

    It names the fellow travellers on the sacred road as mystai and bacchoi, tying the afterlife instructions to Dionysiac initiation.

  10. In what sense is Mithraism "Greek-mediated"?
    Answer

    Its form is that of a Greek mystery cult, its astrology is Hellenistic, its earliest notice is Plutarch's Cilician pirates, its Greek intermediaries are visible in Commagene, and its theology survives through Greek writers used by Porphyry; nothing securely Iranian survives in the cult.

Carry forward

  1. Greek oracles were institutions embedded in politics: Delphi's authority depended on the Amphictyony, on priority rights granted to favoured cities, and on a reputation that Herodotus shows could be bought.
  2. Mystery cults were optional additions to civic religion, not rival religions: the same person could be an Eleusinian initiate and a Samothracian ring-wearer, and the promise was personal benefit, not doctrine.
  3. Most of what we "know" about secret rites comes from hostile, late or literary witnesses; the inscriptions, tablets and papyri are the control, and where they are silent the honest answer is "uncertain".
