The Prometheus cycle: Mekone, fire, the eagle, Heracles

Greek myth has no single story of human creation. Hesiod, the oldest surviving source, never says where men came from; they are simply there, living alongside the gods, when the trouble starts. The story that explains the condition of humanity rather than its origin is the quarrel between Zeus and Prometheus, told twice by Hesiod (Theogony 507–616; Works and Days 42–105), and the two tellings differ in emphasis.

The sequence in the Theogony runs as follows. At Mekone (later Sicyon), gods and mortals meet to settle the division of the sacrificial animal. Prometheus, son of the Titan Iapetus and Clymene, carves an ox and lays two portions before Zeus: the meat and entrails hidden inside the stomach, and the bare bones wrapped attractively in glistening fat. Zeus chooses the fat-covered bones. Hesiod insists that Zeus "knew the trick and was not deceived" (Theogony 551), and chose the worse portion deliberately while already planning evil for mortals. This is why, Hesiod says, Greeks burn white bones on the altar for the gods and keep the meat (556–557). The myth is an aetiology of sacrifice, and at its heart is a separation: from Mekone onward gods and men no longer share a table.

Zeus then withholds fire from the ash trees, meaning from mortals (563). Prometheus steals it, carrying the "far-seen gleam of unwearying fire" in a hollow fennel stalk (narthex, 566–567). Zeus's response is twofold. For Prometheus personally, binding: Hesiod has him fastened in inescapable chains, a shaft driven through his middle, and a long-winged eagle sent to eat his liver, which grows back each night (521–525). Hesiod places this punishment before the Mekone narrative, as a flashback, and resolves it at once: Heracles kills the eagle and frees the Titan, not against Zeus's will but with it, "so that the fame of Theban-born Heracles should be greater than before" (526–534). Apollodorus (Library 1.7.1) sets the binding on the Caucasus and (2.5.11) ties the release to the Labours, on the journey for the apples of the Hesperides. Apollodorus also makes Prometheus the maker of men "from water and earth" (1.7.1), a tradition unknown to Hesiod but attested locally: Pausanias (10.4.4) says the clay Prometheus used could still be seen at Panopeus in Phocis.

Where sources disagree. Ancient commentators already asked why an all-knowing Zeus would choose the worse portion. Hesiod's line 551 looks like a defence of Zeus's omniscience bolted onto an older trickster tale in which the god was simply fooled. Modern readers (West's 1966 commentary among them) generally take it that way; others argue the "knowing" Zeus is integral, because the whole Theogony is about his unassailable sovereignty. The question cannot be settled from the text alone.

Prometheus Bound: the rebel reinterpreted

In the Prometheus Bound transmitted under Aeschylus's name, the trickster becomes a culture hero. Prometheus tells the chorus of Oceanids that he stopped mortals from foreseeing their deaths and "planted blind hopes in them" (250), and that "all the arts (technai) came to mortals from Prometheus" (506): house-building, number, writing, the yoking of animals, ships, medicine, divination, metallurgy. Fire is now the symbol of civilisation rather than a stolen cooking tool. Zeus is a new tyrant, barely secure on his throne, who "wished to wipe out the whole race" of men (231–233) and was prevented only by Prometheus; Io's wanderings show his cruelty, Hermes is his thuggish messenger. Prometheus holds a secret, the marriage that would produce a son to overthrow Zeus, and the play ends with him refusing to reveal it. The lost sequel, Prometheus Unbound, evidently brought Heracles and some settlement with Zeus, so the trilogy may have moved toward reconciliation rather than ending in defiance.

The reinterpretation lies in the moral alignment. Hesiod's Prometheus is a clever subordinate whose cleverness rebounds on the humans he claims to help; in the play his suffering is unjust and his gifts are the content of human life. That inversion made him the patron of Romantic rebellion (Goethe, Shelley), but it is already present in the fifth-century text.

Where sources disagree. Whether Aeschylus wrote the play is seriously doubted. Mark Griffith (The Authenticity of Prometheus Bound, 1977) and M. L. West (1990) argued on metrical, lexical and theological grounds that it is by a later hand, possibly Aeschylus's son Euphorion, and dates after 456 BC. Others defend the attribution, noting that no ancient writer doubted it. The portrait of Zeus as tyrant is central to the argument on both sides.

Pandora: Theogony against Works and Days, and the problem of the jar

Zeus's second response to the theft is the woman. The two Hesiodic versions should be read side by side.

FeatureTheogony 570–612Works and Days 57–105

Two things are commonly misunderstood. First, the container is a pithos, a large storage jar for grain, oil or wine, not a box. The "box" is a Renaissance error: Erasmus, retelling the story around 1508, apparently confused pithos with pyxis (a small box), as Dora and Erwin Panofsky showed in Pandora's Box (1956). Second, Hesiod does not say where the jar came from or why Pandora opened it; the text moves from her arrival to the lid coming off in two lines.

Where sources disagree. The jar poses a logical problem. If it held evils, then Hope remaining inside means hope is withheld from humanity, which is grim but consistent with the evils being loose. If Hope is a good, why was it in a jar of evils? Some scholars (following Verdenius) read Elpis as a deceptive expectation, itself an evil, kept back so men would not be "saved" from toil by despair. West (1978 commentary) treats the inconsistency as Hesiod adapting a folktale in which the jar originally held goods: Babrius (Fable 58) preserves exactly such a version, where a man opens a jar of blessings and all fly off to the gods except Hope. The text is genuinely ambiguous and probably was so already for Hesiod.

The Five Ages and their parallels

Immediately after Pandora, Hesiod offers Perses "another account" (Works and Days 106–201): a sequence of human races (genea, not strictly "ages") made by the gods.

Iron174–201Hesiod's own; toil and grief by day, death by night, mixed with some goodProphecy: children born grey-templed, oaths and kin ties fail, Aidos and Nemesis leave the earth

Hesiod's own position is explicit: "Would that I were not among the fifth men, but had died before or been born after" (174–175). The last clause matters, because it hints that the sequence is not a straight line to the bottom; something may come after.

The parallels are real but their direction is contested. The metallic sequence recurs in the Book of Daniel (2:31–45), where a statue of gold, silver, bronze and iron symbolises successive kingdoms; in the Zoroastrian Zand-i Wahman Yasn, where Zarathustra sees a tree with branches of gold, silver, steel and "mixed iron"; and, without metals, in the four Indian yugas (Krita, Treta, Dvapara, Kali), named after dice throws and declining in virtue and lifespan. M. L. West (The East Face of Helicon, 1997) argued that Hesiod's scheme is ultimately Iranian or Near Eastern. The problem is that Daniel and the Zoroastrian text in their surviving forms are centuries younger than Hesiod (c. 700 BC), so the common source, if any, is a reconstruction.

Where sources disagree. The race of heroes breaks the metallic pattern and is not worse than the bronze race. Some older scholars treated it as Hesiod's clumsy insertion of epic tradition into a borrowed four-metal scheme. Jean-Pierre Vernant ("Le mythe hésiodique des races", 1960) rejected the idea of linear decline altogether, reading the five races as paired contrasts of dike and hybris (gold/silver, bronze/heroes) with the iron race split between a present still mixed with good and a future of pure injustice. Vernant's structural reading has been influential but criticised for imposing more symmetry than the text shows.

Deucalion, Pyrrha and the flood

Hesiod does not tell the flood story, and Homer does not know it. It appears in the fragmentary Hesiodic Catalogue of Women and then in Pindar (Olympian 9.41–56), who has Deucalion and Pyrrha come down from Parnassus and found a city, making "a stone race" of people. The fullest Greek prose account is Apollodorus (Library 1.7.2): Deucalion, son of Prometheus, married to Pyrrha, daughter of Epimetheus and Pandora, is warned by his father to build a chest (larnax); Zeus floods Greece to destroy the bronze race; the chest floats for nine days and nights and lands on Parnassus; Deucalion sacrifices to Zeus Phyxios ("of escape") and is granted a wish, which is for people; both throw stones over their heads, and his become men, hers women. The aetiology is a pun: laos, "people", from laas, "stone". Ovid (Metamorphoses 1.313–415) gives the best-known version, adding Themis's oracle to throw "the bones of your great mother", meaning the stones of the earth.

The genealogy is pointed: the survivors are children of both Titan brothers and of Pandora, so post-flood humanity descends from the whole Promethean complex. Greeks also knew other floods and other survivors (Ogyges in Boeotia, Dardanus in the Troad); Plato (Timaeus 22a–b) has an Egyptian priest tell Solon that the Greeks remember only one deluge and are therefore children in history. Most scholars take the flood to be a Near Eastern import, given the close parallels in Atrahasis, Gilgamesh tablet XI and Genesis 6–9; when it entered Greek tradition is uncertain.

The sons of Hellen: genealogy as politics

Deucalion's son is Hellen, eponym of the Hellenes. The stemma, attested in the Catalogue of Women (fr. 9 Merkelbach–West) and set out in Apollodorus (1.7.3), runs:

Deucalion + Pyrrha
    |
  Hellen (some say son of Zeus)
    |
    +-- Dorus  ----------------> Dorians
    +-- Xuthus (+ Creusa of Athens)
    |     |
    |     +-- Achaeus --------> Achaeans
    |     +-- Ion ------------> Ionians
    +-- Aeolus ----------------> Aeolians (Thessaly, Boeotia, Lesbos)

What was this for? In Homer, "Hellenes" are a small people in Thessaly (Iliad 2.684); Thucydides (1.3) noticed this and inferred that the common name spread only later. The genealogy is a charter: it makes the dialect and cult groups of historical Greece into a single family, and it ranks them. Dorus and Aeolus are sons; Achaeus and Ion are grandsons, through a father, Xuthus, who married the Athenian Creusa. Jonathan Hall (Ethnic Identity in Greek Antiquity, 1997) argues the stemma was assembled in the Archaic period, around the Thessalian and Delphic networks, and that the Dorian claim to Peloponnesian lands (the "return of the Heracleidae", already in Tyrtaeus fr. 2) was woven into it. Herodotus (1.56) treats Dorians and Ionians as the two great stocks and traces the Dorians' wanderings before they reached the Peloponnese.

Athens answered. In Euripides' Ion (1571–1594) Ion is not Xuthus's son at all but Apollo's, by the Athenian princess Creusa; Dorus and Achaeus are demoted to Xuthus's later, lesser children. The Ionians, ancestors of Athens' allies, thereby become the purest Hellenic line and the Dorians, ancestors of Sparta, a side branch. An ethnic genealogy is an argument about who belongs and who ranks, and it was revised whenever the argument changed.

Where sources disagree. Hellen's father is Deucalion in the Catalogue and in most authors, but Apollodorus notes that "some say" Zeus. Which son married Creusa, and whether Xuthus is a late addition designed to attach Athens to the tree, are also disputed; Hall thinks Xuthus and Ion were grafted on to an older three-way scheme.

Plato's Protagoras: the myth rewritten as philosophy

In Plato's Protagoras (320c–322d) the sophist, asked whether virtue can be taught, offers to answer "by a myth or by an argument" and chooses the myth. The gods mould mortal creatures from earth and fire and hand them to the brothers Prometheus and Epimetheus to equip. Epimetheus insists on doing it alone and distributes claws, fur, speed and fertility so that no species dies out, but, being Afterthought, he runs out of gifts before he reaches man, who is left "naked, unshod, unbedded, unarmed". Prometheus, at a loss, steals from Athena and Hephaestus the "wisdom of the crafts together with fire" (entechnos sophia, 321d) and gives it to men. They acquire religion, speech, houses, clothing and agriculture, but not the political art, which is kept by Zeus. Scattered, they are destroyed by beasts; gathering into cities, they wrong one another and scatter again. Zeus then sends Hermes with aidos and dike, shame and justice, and orders that they be given not to a few specialists like medicine but to all, "for cities could not exist if only a few shared in them", and that anyone unable to share in them be killed "as a disease of the city" (322d).

The rewrite reverses Hesiod at every joint. Fire is a gift of pure benefit, not the cause of Zeus's revenge; Prometheus is punished almost as an afterthought ("later, as the story goes", 322a), and there is no Pandora. Zeus is not the jealous withholder of livelihood but the donor of justice. Hesiod's Works and Days 276–280 had said that Zeus gave dike to men alone, while fish and beasts devour each other; Protagoras keeps that but makes dike universally distributed, which is the premise of Athenian democracy, where every citizen sits in the assembly and the courts. Hesiod's iron-age prophecy that Aidos and Nemesis will abandon mankind is answered by a Zeus who hands Aidos and Dike down to everyone.

Where sources disagree. How much of this is Protagoras and how much Plato is unknowable; the historical Protagoras's own works are lost. Readers also divide over whether Plato presents the "Great Speech" admiringly or ironically, since Socrates proceeds to dismantle its claim that virtue is both innate in all and teachable.

Hesiod's pessimism and dike as a worldview

The Works and Days is addressed to a brother, Perses, who has cheated Hesiod in an inheritance dispute and bribed the local "kings". Out of that quarrel the poem builds a theory of the human condition. "The gods keep livelihood hidden from men" (42): without the Prometheus affair a day's work would feed a man for a year, but now toil is the law. The fable of the hawk and nightingale (202–212) states the tyrant's view that might makes right; the poet replies that hybris is bad for a poor man and even the great cannot carry it long (213–218). Dike, personified as Zeus's daughter (256; cf. Theogony 901–903), is dragged through the streets by crooked judges but reports to her father, whose eye "sees all and notices all" (267). The just city flourishes (225–237); the unjust city suffers plague and famine, and the whole community pays for one man's wrong (238–247).

This is pessimism of a specific kind. The present is the worst age so far and will get worse; women, gods and kings are each a tax on men's labour; the beasts' world has no justice. But justice does exist for men, as gift and burden, and the farmer who works, sacrifices, keeps his oaths and avoids the courts can prosper. The two Strifes (11–26), the good one that sets neighbour competing with neighbour and the bad one that makes war and lawsuits, show Hesiod channelling competition rather than condemning it. Friedrich Solmsen (Hesiod and Aeschylus, 1949) saw here the beginning of Greek moral theology that Aeschylus would develop; others read Hesiod as a hard-headed peasant whose "justice" is a prudential calculus about what Zeus will punish. Both are in the text. What is distinctive is that the human condition is explained by a divine quarrel in which men were bystanders, and answered not by appeasement but by work and straight dealing.

Where sources disagree. Whether the Theogony and Works and Days are by one poet has been questioned since antiquity (Pausanias 9.31.4 reports Boeotians who accepted only the Works and Days). West's commentaries defend common authorship for these two while rejecting it for the Catalogue and the Shield. The biographical details about Perses may be a poetic persona rather than fact.

Self-test

  1. What happened at Mekone, and what Greek religious practice did the story explain?
    Answer

    Prometheus divided a sacrificial ox so that the bones wrapped in fat looked better than the meat hidden in the stomach; Zeus chose the bones. The story explains why Greeks burned white bones for the gods and kept the meat for themselves (Hesiod, Theogony 551–557).

  2. In Hesiod, who frees Prometheus, and does he do so against Zeus's will?
    Answer

    Heracles kills the eagle and releases him, and Hesiod says this was with Zeus's consent, to increase Heracles' fame (Theogony 526–534).

  3. Name two ways Prometheus Bound reinterprets the Hesiodic story.
    Answer

    Prometheus becomes the giver of all the arts of civilisation rather than a trickster; Zeus becomes an insecure tyrant who wanted to destroy mankind rather than the just sovereign of the Theogony.

  4. Why is the authorship of Prometheus Bound disputed?
    Answer

    Griffith (1977) and West (1990) argued on grounds of metre, vocabulary and the hostile portrait of Zeus that it is not by Aeschylus and may date after 456 BC; no ancient author doubted the attribution.

  5. How does Pandora differ between the Theogony and the Works and Days?
    Answer

    In the Theogony she is unnamed, a "beautiful evil", and the harm she brings is the race of women who consume men's labour; in the Works and Days she is named Pandora, given to Epimetheus, and opens the jar that releases toil and disease while Hope stays inside.

  6. Why do we speak of "Pandora's box" when Hesiod says jar?
    Answer

    Erasmus, retelling the myth in the early sixteenth century, confused pithos (storage jar) with pyxis (box); the Panofskys traced the error in Pandora's Box (1956).

  7. Which of Hesiod's five races breaks the metallic pattern, and what did Vernant make of that?
    Answer

    The race of heroes, which has no metal and is not worse than the bronze race. Vernant argued the sequence is not linear decline but a structure of paired contrasts between justice and hybris.

  8. What is the pun behind the repopulation of the earth after the flood?
    Answer

    Deucalion and Pyrrha throw stones (laas) over their heads, which become people (laos); so Apollodorus, Library 1.7.2.

  9. In the standard genealogy, why are Ion and Achaeus grandsons rather than sons of Hellen, and how did Athens respond?
    Answer

    They are sons of Xuthus, a son of Hellen, which ranks Ionians and Achaeans below Dorians and Aeolians. Euripides' Ion made Ion the son of Apollo and the Athenian Creusa and demoted Dorus and Achaeus to Xuthus's younger children.

  10. What does Zeus give humanity in Protagoras's myth that he withholds in Hesiod, and why does it matter politically?
    Answer

    Zeus sends Hermes with aidos and dike to be shared by all, not by a few experts. This grounds the democratic claim that every citizen is competent to judge political matters, and inverts Hesiod's prophecy that Aidos and Nemesis will abandon the iron race.

Carry forward

  1. The Greek account of the human condition is a story of separation from the gods, caused by a quarrel between Zeus and Prometheus in which humans were bystanders; sacrifice, fire, toil, women and death are its terms, and later writers (Aeschylus, Protagoras) argue with Hesiod by changing the moral alignment of Zeus and Prometheus rather than the plot.
  2. Genealogies are arguments. The stemma from Deucalion through Hellen to Dorus, Aeolus, Xuthus, Achaeus and Ion is a charter for who counts as Greek and who ranks, revised by Athens in the fifth century; expect the same logic in the Heracleidae, the Trojan War and city foundation legends.
  3. Hesiodic dike, justice as Zeus's daughter who reports crooked judgements to her father and brings collective reward or ruin, is the seed of Greek moral theology; Solon, Aeschylus and Plato all build on it, and the "ages" scheme and the flood show Greece in conversation with Near Eastern tradition without a settled account of who borrowed from whom.
