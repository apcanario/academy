The killing of Agamemnon and Cassandra

Agamemnon came home from Troy with three debts hanging over him. He had cut his daughter's throat at Aulis so the fleet could sail; he brought back Priam's daughter Cassandra as a concubine; and his wife had meanwhile taken his cousin Aegisthus, son of Thyestes, whose children Atreus had served to him at table. Every version of what happened next mixes these three motives differently, and the mixture tells you what the story is for.

The oldest account is Homer's, told in pieces. Zeus opens the Odyssey (1.29–43) complaining that Aegisthus took Agamemnon's wife and killed him despite a warning from Hermes. Nestor (Od. 3.254–312) describes the seduction: Clytemnestra at first resisted "for her mind was good", until Aegisthus removed the bard set to guard her. Proteus (Od. 4.512–537) supplies an ambush: a paid watchman, twenty men hidden, a feast, Agamemnon slaughtered "like an ox at the manger". Agamemnon's ghost (Od. 11.405–434) adds that he heard the cry of Cassandra, "whom treacherous Clytemnestra killed beside me", and that his wife would not close his eyes. In Homer, then, Aegisthus is the killer and Clytemnestra the accomplice who kills Cassandra; the venue is a banquet, not a bath; Iphigenia is never mentioned.

Between Homer and Aeschylus the woman moves to the centre. Stesichorus' Oresteia (fr. 210–219 PMG) survives in scraps: Clytemnestra's dream of a snake with a bloodied head, the nurse who saves the child, Apollo's gift of a bow to Orestes against the Erinyes. Pindar, Pythian 11.17–37, makes Clytemnestra kill both victims and asks what Homer never asked: was it Iphigenia's slaughter that stung her, or "nightly beddings" with a lover? Aeschylus' Agamemnon (458 BC) answers "both, and more": Clytemnestra alone kills, in the bath, netting her husband in a robe and striking three times (Ag. 1372–1398), then kills Cassandra, and claims the deed as justice for Iphigenia and as the work of the house's old alastor. Aegisthus arrives only afterwards to boast.

Where sources disagree. Who actually struck? Homer gives the blow to Aegisthus; Pindar and Aeschylus to Clytemnestra; Sophocles (El. 97–99) and Euripides (El. 86–87) have them act together. The weapon is also contested. Aeschylus' Agamemnon speaks of the sword (1528) but Clytemnestra calls for "a man-killing axe" at Choephori 889, and vase-painters show an axe; Fraenkel argued for a sword in the murder itself, Davies (1987) for an axe. Sophocles' Electra is explicit that it was an axe (El. 99). The question matters because the axe is a sacrificial tool, and the sacrificial colouring of the killing is central to Aeschylus.

Electra, the exile of Orestes, and the matricide

Homer knows no Electra. The Iliad names Agamemnon's daughters as Chrysothemis, Laodice and Iphianassa (9.144–145), and Aelian (Varia Historia 4.26) records that the lyric poet Xanthus renamed Laodice "Electra", the unmarried one. She is a sixth-century invention who grows: minor for Aeschylus, the whole play for Sophocles and Euripides. In the Odyssey Orestes simply returns "from Athens" in the eighth year and kills Aegisthus (3.306–310); Zenodotus read Phocis. The lyric poets supply the rescue: a nurse, Arsinoe in Pindar (Pyth. 11.17), Laodameia in Stesichorus, Cilissa in Aeschylus (Cho. 734–765), smuggles the boy to Strophius of Phocis, where he grows up with Pylades.

The matricide is already in Homer if you read 3.309–310 closely: Orestes holds a funeral feast for "his hateful mother and the coward Aegisthus", so she is dead the same day, though Homer will not say who killed her. The Hesiodic Catalogue of Women (fr. 23a Merkelbach–West) is blunt: Orestes "killed his mother". From Stesichorus onward Apollo commands the act, and Aeschylus makes the oracle's threats central (Cho. 269–296): if Orestes fails, his father's Erinyes will bring leprosy, madness and exclusion from every altar. Aeschylus gives the canonical scenes. Electra matches a lock of hair on the tomb to her own, then footprints, then a piece of weaving (Cho. 164–234). Orestes enters the palace as a Phocian stranger bearing news of his own death; Aegisthus dies first, then Clytemnestra, who bares her breast and pleads with the son she nursed (Cho. 896–898), while Pylades speaks his only three lines to recall Apollo's oracles. The moment the deed is done Orestes sees what the chorus cannot: women in black, wreathed in snakes (Cho. 1048–1062).

Where sources disagree. Where does the house of Atreus live? Homer has Agamemnon rule Mycenae; Aeschylus moves the action to Argos, probably because Athens had just made an alliance with Argos (and the Eumenides advertises it, 762–774); Stesichorus and Simonides, according to the scholia on Euripides' Orestes, placed the palace in Lacedaemon, and Sparta later claimed Orestes' bones (Herodotus 1.67–68). The mythic geography is political, not antiquarian.

The Erinyes, Delphi and the trial at Athens

What happens to a matricide? Stesichorus' answer was a bow from Apollo to drive the Erinyes off: self-help, not law. Aeschylus' Eumenides converts the pursuit into a legal process. Orestes is purified at Delphi with pig's blood (Eum. 280–283) and sent to embrace the ancient image of Athena at Athens, where the Erinyes catch him. Athena refuses to judge alone a case too great for one person and summons "the best of my citizens" to form a homicide court for all time (Eum. 470–489, 681–710). Apollo testifies for the defence; the Erinyes prosecute; the vote is equal and Orestes is acquitted (752–753). Athena then does what Homeric gods never do: she persuades. The Erinyes accept a cult beneath the hill and a share in the city's prosperity, becoming the Semnai Theai, escorted to their new home in torchlight.

The hill is the Areopagus, and the council that met there was Athens' homicide court. In 462/1 BC, three years before the play, Ephialtes stripped it of its wider political powers (Aristotle, Constitution of the Athenians 25) and left it the trials for deliberate killing. Aeschylus gives this reduced body a divine charter and the loftiest praise, as a court "untouched by gain, reverent, swift to anger" (Eum. 704–706). Others told the aetiology differently. Euripides, Iphigenia among the Taurians 940–986, has Orestes tried at the Areopagus but also explains the Choes festival by his visit: Athenians would neither refuse hospitality nor share a table with a polluted man, so each drank from his own pitcher. Pausanias (1.28.5) and Demosthenes (23.66) record an older tradition that the hill was named from the trial of Ares for killing Halirrhothius. Apollodorus (Epitome 6.25) names Orestes' accusers variously as Tyndareus, Erigone (daughter of Aegisthus and Clytemnestra) or Perilaus; in Hyginus (Fabulae 122) Erigone hangs herself at the acquittal.

Where sources disagree. Two cruxes. First, Athena's vote. At Eum. 734–741 she says she will add her pebble to Orestes' side and that an equal count acquits. Either the human jurors split evenly and she breaks the tie (so her vote decides), or they are one short for Orestes and her pebble creates the tie that acquits (so the play founds the Athenian rule that an equal vote favours the defendant, attested at Antiphon 5.51 and Aristotle, Problems 29.13). Gagarin (1975), Hester (1981) and Sommerstein (1989) favour the tie-creating reading; the staging cannot be recovered. Second, politics. Dover (1957) and Dodds (1960) read the trilogy as a conservative plea for restraint after Ephialtes' reforms and his murder; Podlecki (1966) and Macleod (1982) see Aeschylus endorsing the democratic settlement while warning against civil strife. Athena's words cut both ways: "neither anarchy nor despotism" (Eum. 696). The disagreement is genuine and unlikely to be settled.

The later wanderings: Iphigenia, Hermione, Neoptolemus

Iphigenia among the Taurians

The Cypria, as summarised by Proclus, already had Artemis snatch Iphigenia from the altar at Aulis, substitute a deer, and make her immortal among the Taurians; Herodotus (4.103) reports that the Tauri of the Crimea sacrificed shipwrecked strangers to a goddess they called Iphigenia. Euripides' Iphigenia among the Taurians (c. 414 BC) ties these threads to Orestes. Acquitted at Athens but still hunted by Erinyes who refused the verdict (IT 970–978), he is sent by Apollo to steal the Tauric image of Artemis. He and Pylades are captured and brought to the priestess who sacrifices Greeks; she is Iphigenia, and the letter she asks Pylades to carry to Argos triggers the recognition. They escape Thoas by a feigned purification at sea, and Athena directs the image to Halae Araphenides in Attica, where a token drop of blood from a man's throat replaces human sacrifice, and installs Iphigenia as priestess at Brauron (IT 1446–1467). Both were real Attic cults. Sparta claimed the Tauric image as its own Artemis Orthia (Pausanias 3.16.7–11); Megara showed Iphigenia's tomb (Pausanias 1.43.1).

Where sources disagree. Did Euripides invent Orestes' voyage to the Taurians? No earlier source connects Orestes with the Crimea or with Iphigenia's return, and the Brauron and Halae aetiologies look like an Athenian appropriation of a Peloponnesian heroine. Most scholars treat the plot as Euripidean invention built on Herodotus' notice; a minority posit a lost epic or local Attic tradition. Uncertain, but the Athenian motive is clear: the cult of Iphigenia at Brauron predates the play, and the play explains why it is there.

Hermione and the death of Neoptolemus at Delphi

In the Odyssey (4.3–9) Menelaus gives Hermione to Achilles' son Neoptolemus, fulfilling a promise made at Troy. Tragedy inserts a prior claim: Tyndareus had betrothed Hermione to Orestes before the war. Pindar twice tells how Neoptolemus died at Delphi: in Nemean 7.34–47 in a quarrel over sacrificial meat; in Paean 6.117–120 at Apollo's own hand, in revenge for Priam. Euripides' Andromache (c. 425 BC) gives the murder to Orestes, who incites the Delphians and carries Hermione off (Andr. 993–1008, 1085–1165). Apollodorus (Epit. 6.14) names the killer Machaereus. Euripides' Orestes (408 BC) ends with Apollo arranging the marriage and announcing Neoptolemus' death as still to come (Or. 1653–1659). Thereafter Orestes rules Argos and, through Hermione, Sparta; his son Tisamenus leads the Achaeans against the returning Heraclids. He dies of a snakebite in Arcadia (Apollodorus, Epit. 6.28), and the Spartans later carry his giant bones from Tegea (Herodotus 1.67–68).

Aeschylus' Oresteia: from vendetta to law

The Oresteia (458 BC) is the only complete trilogy we possess, and its argument is cumulative. Agamemnon shows a world where justice is retaliation and every act of justice is also a crime: the war punishes Helen but costs Iphigenia; Clytemnestra's killing avenges her daughter and Thyestes' children, and is also adultery and husband-murder. Imagery of nets, hunting and sacrifice binds killer and killed, and Cassandra, who sees the whole chain from Thyestes' feast to her own death, cannot be believed. Choephori narrows the frame to the family and asks whether a son can be commanded to do a thing that damns him; he can, and the Erinyes come anyway. Eumenides widens it to the city. The movement is from house to polis, from blood-right to a jury, from the Erinyes as hunters to the Erinyes as guardians of civic fear. Athena's founding speech is the thesis: fear must not be expelled from the city, but housed in an institution rather than loose in the bloodline.

Aeschylus also makes gender carry the weight. The Erinyes argue that a wife's killing of a husband is not kin-murder; Apollo retorts that the mother is merely the nurse of the father's seed (Eum. 657–666); Athena, motherless, votes for the male. Zeitlin (1978) read the trilogy as "the dynamics of misogyny", founding law on the subordination of the female; others reply that the Erinyes end honoured, not defeated, and that the point is integration rather than suppression.

Sophocles' Electra

Sophocles' Electra (date unknown; usually placed 420–410) takes the middle play of Aeschylus and removes its frame. There is no Delphic prologue, no Erinys, no madness, no trial. The oracle Orestes consulted asked only how to kill, not whether (El. 32–37). The play is about Electra: grief as a way of life, isolation, a sister Chrysothemis who has made terms with the usurpers, two set-piece confrontations with Clytemnestra. The old tutor delivers a brilliant false report of Orestes' death in a chariot crash at the Pythian games (El. 680–763), so that Electra receives the urn she believes holds his ashes before he reveals himself (1098–1231). Clytemnestra is killed first, offstage, while Electra shouts "strike twice, if you have the strength" (1415), and the play ends with Aegisthus led in to die where Agamemnon fell. There is no divine epilogue.

Where sources disagree. Is this a clean revenge or an indictment? Jebb, Bowra and recently March (2001) read the play as Sophocles restoring the Homeric view that Orestes' act was simply right, the ending as triumph. Sheppard (1927), Kells (1973) and Winnington-Ingram (1980) point to the ominous silence about the Erinyes, Electra's near-derangement in the final scenes, and the ambiguous last words of the chorus, and read an irony Sophocles leaves unspoken. The relative dating of Sophocles' and Euripides' Electra plays is also unresolved: the once-standard 413 date for Euripides, based on the Dioscuri's reference to Sicily (El. 1347–1348), was undermined by Zuntz on metrical grounds, who argued for c. 420, so either poet may be answering the other.

Euripides: Electra, Orestes and the Iphigenia plays

Euripides lowers the social register and raises the moral temperature. In his Electra Aegisthus has married Electra to a poor but decent farmer so that her children cannot threaten him; a hut replaces the palace. The recognition parodies Aeschylus: Electra scoffs that hair, a footprint and weaving could prove anything (El. 524–544), and an old man identifies Orestes by a scar. Aegisthus is killed while hospitably sacrificing to the Nymphs; Clytemnestra is lured by the lie that Electra has given birth. The killers are instantly broken by remorse, and the Dioscuri deliver the judgment Aeschylus withheld: "she has justice, but you have not acted justly" (El. 1244); Phoebus is wise, "but his oracle was not". Orestes must go to Athens for trial, Electra marries Pylades, and the siblings part for ever.

Orestes (408 BC) picks up six days after the matricide. Orestes lies on a sickbed, intermittently mad; the Argive assembly is to vote on stoning the pair; Menelaus promises help and gives none. Tyndareus makes the play's most striking speech (Or. 491–525): Orestes should have prosecuted his mother and driven her out, because law exists precisely so that blood need not answer blood. Condemned, Orestes, Pylades and Electra plot to murder Helen and hold Hermione hostage, and are about to burn the palace when Apollo announces that Helen is a star and Orestes will be acquitted at Athens, marry Hermione and rule Argos. Iphigenia at Aulis (405 BC, posthumous) returns to the source of the chain, with Agamemnon writing and unwriting the letter that summons his daughter, and Iphigenia choosing to die for Greece.

Where sources disagree. Verrall and the rationalist school read Euripides' Apollo as a fraud exposed, and the epilogues as deliberately unconvincing. Later critics (Zuntz, Burnett, Kovacs) see tragic irony without atheism: the gods are real and their commands bind, which is exactly what makes them intolerable. The ending of the Orestes is the test case, and there is no consensus. The authenticity of the final scene of Iphigenia at Aulis is also doubted on linguistic grounds.

Athens' myth about its own courts

Athens had five homicide courts: the Areopagus for deliberate killing, the Palladion for involuntary killing, the Delphinion for killing admitted but claimed as lawful, Phreatto for exiles accused of a second killing, and the Prytaneion for killings by unknown hands or objects. Orestes attached himself to this system because his case fitted it: a killing admitted, commanded by a god, done to avenge a father, and therefore a dispute about justification. The Choes story shows a second use of the myth, turning a ritual oddity at the Anthesteria into a memory of hospitality to the polluted. The cult of the Semnai Theai in a cave at the foot of the Areopagus, where acquitted defendants sacrificed, sealed the identification of court and myth.

The payload is larger than any single court. Fifth-century Athens told itself that it was the city where other cities' crises were resolved: Oedipus dies at Colonus, the Heraclids find refuge, Orestes is judged. The Orestes story made that self-image specifically about law. Aeschylus claims that Athens invented trial by jury, with a goddess presiding, as the replacement for vendetta; the tie-vote rule, the jurors' oath and the Areopagus' primacy all receive divine warrant. Tyndareus' speech in Euripides shows the same assumption from the other side: an Argive elder lectures Orestes on a legal culture that is recognisably Athenian. Whether or not Aeschylus meant to defend Ephialtes, Athenians afterwards could not think about homicide law without seeing a matricide standing by Athena's statue. The myth did what foundation myths do: it made a recent, contested, human institution look older than the gods who sanctioned it.

Self-test

  1. In the Odyssey, who kills Agamemnon, who kills Cassandra, and where does the killing take place?
    Answer

    Aegisthus kills Agamemnon in an ambush at a feast (Od. 4.512–537, 11.405–434); Clytemnestra kills Cassandra beside him (11.421–423). There is no bath and no mention of Iphigenia.

  2. Which source first asks whether Iphigenia or adultery motivated Clytemnestra, and roughly when?
    Answer

    Pindar, Pythian 11.22–25, in the 470s or 450s BC, before Aeschylus' trilogy of 458.

  3. Why is Electra absent from Homer, and who named her?
    Answer

    Homer's daughters of Agamemnon are Chrysothemis, Laodice and Iphianassa (Il. 9.144–145). According to Aelian, the lyric poet Xanthus renamed Laodice "Electra" because she was unmarried.

  4. What three tokens does Aeschylus' Electra use to recognise Orestes, and how does Euripides treat them?
    Answer

    A lock of hair, footprints and a piece of weaving (Cho. 164–234). Euripides' Electra dismisses all three as absurd (El. 524–544); Orestes is recognised by a scar instead.

  5. What political event immediately preceded the Oresteia, and how does the Eumenides relate to it?
    Answer

    Ephialtes' reforms of 462/1 BC stripped the Areopagus of its political powers, leaving it homicide jurisdiction. Aeschylus gives this homicide court a divine foundation by Athena; whether he thereby endorses or regrets the reform is disputed.

  6. State the two readings of Athena's vote in the Eumenides.
    Answer

    Either the jurors tie and Athena's vote breaks it for Orestes, or the jurors are one short and her pebble creates the tie, which under the Athenian rule acquits (Antiphon 5.51). The second founds the tie-acquittal rule; the text (734–741) allows both.

  7. How does Euripides' Iphigenia among the Taurians account for the Attic cults at Halae and Brauron?
    Answer

    Athena orders Orestes to install the stolen Tauric image of Artemis at Halae Araphenides, with a token blood-letting replacing human sacrifice, and makes Iphigenia priestess at Brauron (IT 1446–1467).

  8. Give two incompatible versions of how Neoptolemus died at Delphi.
    Answer

    Pindar, Nemean 7: killed in a quarrel over sacrificial meat; Paean 6: killed by Apollo avenging Priam; Euripides, Andromache: murdered by Delphians incited by Orestes; Apollodorus names the killer Machaereus.

  9. What structural change does Sophocles make to the order of the killings, and what is its effect?
    Answer

    Clytemnestra is killed first, Aegisthus last, so the play ends not with the Erinyes but with Aegisthus led in to die where Agamemnon died, and no divine epilogue follows. Whether this is triumph or unspoken irony is disputed.

  10. What is Tyndareus' argument in Euripides' Orestes, and why does it matter for the myth's Athenian meaning?
    Answer

    Orestes should have used the law to prosecute and exile his mother instead of killing her, since law exists to end the chain of blood for blood (Or. 491–525). It shows the Athenian assumption that legal process, not vendetta, is the default even in the heroic age.

Carry forward

  1. The Orestes story is the Greek test case for justified killing: the same act is both commanded and polluting, and every version from Homer onward shifts blame among Aegisthus, Clytemnestra, Apollo and Orestes to make a different point. Watch for the same pattern of "justice that is also crime" in the Theban and Heraclid material.
  2. Aeschylus turns a vendetta into a court case and uses the trilogy to charter the Areopagus; Athens thereafter read its homicide courts, its Anthesteria rituals and its asylum-city self-image through this myth. Expect the technique of mythic aetiology for recent institutions to recur in Athenian tragedy and local history.
  3. Sophocles and Euripides work by subtraction and relocation: removing the divine frame (Sophocles), or lowering the social setting and letting a god condemn his own oracle (Euripides). The three Electras are the clearest demonstration in surviving literature of how tragedians argued with one another through the same plot.
