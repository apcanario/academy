01Land and dynasty

Two Macedonias

Lower Macedonia was the alluvial plain at the head of the Thermaic Gulf, made up of Pieria, Bottiaea and Emathia and watered by the Haliacmon, Loudias and Axios. It had grain and horse pasture, but the good harbours belonged to Greeks for most of the period. Aegae, the old royal seat, sat where the Haliacmon leaves the Pierian hills. Pella was already the largest city by 382 (Xenophon, Hellenica 5.2.13). Archelaus is usually credited with moving the court there, but that is a modern inference, and Amyntas III is the alternative candidate.

Upper Macedonia was the ring of highland basins to the west: Elimeia, Orestis, Lyncestis, Eordaea, Tymphaea and Pelagonia. Thucydides (2.99.2) calls the Lyncestae and Elimiotae allies and subjects of the lowland kings who nevertheless "have kings of their own". Their position between the Illyrians and Paeonians made their loyalty negotiable. Binding them to the crown was Philip's first structural achievement. This is why Alexander's marshals include so many highlanders (Perdiccas and Craterus of Orestis, Coenus of Elimeia, Polyperchon of Tymphaea), and why phalanx battalions were still raised by region (Diodorus 17.57.2).

The Argead claim

The ruling house claimed descent from Temenus of Argos, and so from Heracles. In Herodotus' founding story (8.137–139), three exiled Temenid brothers serve a local king, and the youngest, Perdiccas, founds the line. Thucydides (2.99.3) accepts this. Justin (7.1) names Caranus as founder instead, and Euripides' lost Archelaus gave the founder the name of its royal patron (Hyginus, Fabulae 219). The genealogy was evidently flexible.

The key text is Herodotus 5.22. When Alexander I entered the stadion at Olympia, rivals objected that barbarians could not compete. He proved his Argive descent, the Hellanodikai admitted him, and he "tied for first". Herodotus endorses the claim:

That these descendants of Perdiccas are Greeks, as they say, I myself know.

Three points matter. The claim covers the dynasty, not the people. It had to be argued, so other Greeks did not take it for granted. And Alexander I had been a Persian vassal: his sister married the Persian Bubares (5.21), and he served as Mardonius' envoy in 479 (8.136). Badian ("Herodotus on Alexander I of Macedon", 1994) read Herodotus' flattering stories, the murdered Persian envoys (5.17–21) and the warning before Plataea (9.44–45), as the king's own post-war apologetics. The Olympic episode cannot be dated, and no victor list records him.

Where sources disagree. Appian (Syrian Wars 63) derives the Argeadae from Argos Oresticum in Upper Macedonia, not from Peloponnesian Argos. Hesiod's Catalogue (fr. 7) makes Makedon a son of Zeus, a cousin of Hellen's line rather than a member of it. The Peloponnesian pedigree may be fifth-century diplomacy built on a convenient name.

02Kingship and court

The throne was hereditary within the Argead house, without primogeniture. Any adult Argead could claim it. The candidate who held the army and the great men at the moment of vacancy was acclaimed by the Macedonians under arms. Hammond treated this as quasi-constitutional and gave the army assembly real rights to acclaim kings and to try capital cases, citing Curtius 6.8.25, where treason was judged by the army "by ancient Macedonian custom". Errington ("The Nature of the Macedonian State", Chiron 1978) and Borza answered that these were customs a strong king used and a weak one could not avoid, not entitlements. Most recent scholars side with Errington.

The king was warlord: he fought in the front rank and carried the scars. Demosthenes (18.67) lists Philip's lost eye, broken collarbone and maimed hand and leg. He was priest: he made the state sacrifices daily, and Alexander kept them up even when dying (Arrian 7.25). He presided at rites such as the Xandika, an army purification (Livy 40.6, describing the Antigonid version). He was judge: in an anecdote also told of other kings, an old woman refused a hearing tells Philip "then stop being king" (Plutarch, Demetrius 42).

The hetairoi were the king's personal following. They hunted, drank and fought beside him and were paid in land. Theopompus, hostile and moralising, claims Philip's 800 Companions held as much land as 10,000 of the richest Greeks (FGrH 115 F225). Under Philip the name also passed to the heavy cavalry. The royal pages, sons of the nobility, guarded the king's sleep and hunted with him. Arrian (4.13.1) dates them "from Philip's time", which may mean Philip founded the institution or only that it existed under him. They were a school for officers and hostages for their fathers' loyalty.

The hunt conferred rank. Hegesander (in Athenaeus 1.18a) says no Macedonian reclined at dinner until he had killed a boar without a net. Aristotle (Politics 7.1324b) says a man who had killed no enemy wore a halter for a belt. The symposium was the arena of free speech among Companions, and the drinking was notorious. Theopompus calls Philip a drunkard, and Ephippus (FGrH 126) says the same of Alexander. Southern Greek sources exaggerate, but two decisive ruptures happened over wine: Attalus' wedding toast and the killing of Cleitus (Arrian 4.8).

03Gods and graves at Aegae

The pantheon was Greek with a northern emphasis. There was Zeus Olympios at Dion, where Archelaus founded a festival with dramatic contests (Diodorus 17.16.3–4). Heracles Patroos was worshipped as the dynasty's ancestor. Dionysiac cult was strong: Plutarch (Alexander 2) has Olympias handling snakes in orgiastic rites. Philip's mother Eurydice made dedications to Eukleia at Aegae.

In 1977 Manolis Andronikos opened the Great Tumulus at Vergina, which Hammond had identified with Aegae in 1968. Tomb I was a looted cist with a painting of Hades seizing Persephone. Tomb III held a youth's remains, usually taken as Alexander IV. Tomb II, barrel-vaulted with a hunting frieze, was intact. In its chamber a gold larnax held a man's cremated bones with armour, an oak wreath and a gilded silver diadem. In the antechamber lay a woman with a myrtle wreath and a Scythian-style gorytos.

Where sources disagree. Andronikos and Hammond named the man in Tomb II as Philip II, and Musgrave, Neave and Prag (JHS 1984) reported damage to the eye socket. Lehmann (AJA 1980), Borza and Palagia argued for Philip III Arrhidaeus and Adea-Eurydice, buried at Aegae by Cassander in 316. They pointed to the vault (thought to be a post-Alexander eastern borrowing), the diadem (adopted after 330), the pottery and the Asian gear. Bartsiokas (Science 2000) found no eye wound. He and colleagues (PNAS 2015) then proposed that a leg in Tomb I, with a fused knee, belongs to Philip, who was lamed in 339. Each step is contested. The identification is uncertain and should carry no argument.

04From Archelaus to the crisis of the 360s

Archelaus (c. 413–399) took the throne by murder. Plato makes him the model of the happy criminal (Gorgias 470d–471d). Thucydides (2.100.2) credits him with roads, forts, and more horses and arms than his eight predecessors combined. He built a Greek court deliberately. Zeuxis painted the palace (Aelian, Varia Historia 14.17), and Agathon and Timotheus came to Pella. Euripides, by tradition, wrote the Archelaus and perhaps the Bacchae there. Socrates declined an invitation (Aristotle, Rhetoric 2.23, 1398a). Archelaus was murdered by his lover Crataeus, a case Aristotle (Politics 5.1311b) files with other killings provoked by sexual insult, the same category in which he places Philip.

Where sources disagree. Scullion ("Euripides and Macedon, or the Silence of the Frogs", CQ 2003) argued that Euripides never went to Macedon, since the Frogs of 405 does not mention it, and that the stories are biographical fiction. Most still accept the visit, but its basis is thin.

Forty years of weak reigns followed. Amyntas III (393–370/69) lost his kingdom at least once. Then:

Where sources disagree. Justin (7.5.9–10) says Philip first ruled as guardian for his infant nephew Amyntas. Diodorus (16.1–2) has him king from the start. Whether there was a regency is uncertain.

05Philip II: building the instrument

The hostage years. Philip lived in Thebes from about 368 to 365, in the house of Pammenes, when Thebes was at its peak after Leuctra. Plutarch (Pelopidas 26.5) calls him a zealous imitator of Epaminondas. Whether he learned specific tactics or a broader lesson, that a drilled infantry state could overturn the Greek order, is inference. Diodorus (16.2.2) and Justin (7.5.1–3) give confused sequences involving Illyrian hostage-taking as well.

The army. Diodorus (16.3.1–2) describes constant drill and new equipment from 359. Its parts:

  • Sarissa: a cornel-wood pike of up to 12 cubits, about 5.5 m (Theophrastus, Historia Plantarum 3.12.2). Polybius (18.29) gives 14 cubits for the later phalanx. It was held two-handed, so the shield was small. It was cheap, which meant a poor peasantry could be armed in mass.
  • Pezhetairoi, "Foot Companions", extending the Companion bond to the infantry. Demosthenes mentions them by 349 (2.17).
  • Hypaspists, mobile elite infantry hinging phalanx and cavalry, with the royal agema.
  • Companion cavalry in regional squadrons using the wedge, which Arrian (Tactica 16.6) attributes to Philip. Thessalian horse was added after 352.
  • Siege train under Polyidus of Thessaly, whose pupils Diades and Charias served at Tyre. The failures at Perinthus and Byzantium (340) show its limits.
  • Year-round war: Demosthenes (9.48–50) contrasts Philip, fighting summer and winter with light troops, cavalry and mercenaries, with old Spartan four-month seasons. Frontinus (Strategemata 4.1.6) says he banned wagons.
Where sources disagree. Anaximenes (FGrH 72 F4) credits an unspecified "Alexander" (I, II or III) with creating the Companions and pezhetairoi. Theopompus (F348) uses pezetairoi for a picked guard. Some therefore think the name began with the guard, spread to the whole phalanx, and the guard was then renamed hypaspists.

Gold. In 356 Philip took Crenides under Mount Pangaion, refounded it as Philippi and worked the mines to over 1,000 talents a year (Diodorus 16.8.6–7). The figure may be inflated, but the gold staters he struck circulated as far as Gaul. The money paid mercenaries, bought politicians and rewarded Companions. The milestones were Amphipolis (357), Methone and the lost eye (354), the Crocus Field and the Thessalian archonship (352), Olynthus (348), the Peace of Philocrates (346), Chaeronea (338) and the League of Corinth (337).

There was no fixed rank of queen. Carney (Women and Monarchy in Macedonia, 2000) shows that a wife's standing rested on her son, his prospects and her kin, so rivalry between wives was succession politics. Olympias, a Molossian princess claiming descent from Achilles, had the strongest son, but Alexander was never formally designated.

At Philip's wedding to Cleopatra in 337, Attalus prayed for a "legitimate" heir. Alexander threw his cup. Philip drew on him and fell, and Alexander jeered that the man who would cross into Asia could not cross from couch to couch (Plutarch, Alexander 9.6–11). Alexander took Olympias to Epirus and went to the Illyrians himself until Demaratus of Corinth reconciled them. Arrian (3.6.5) calls the bride Eurydice. Whether she was formally renamed is unknown. In the Pixodarus affair (Plutarch, Alexander 10.1–4), the satrap of Caria offered his daughter to Arrhidaeus. Alexander secretly offered himself instead through the actor Thessalus. Philip rebuked him and exiled Harpalus, Nearchus, Erigyius, Laomedon and Ptolemy.

Where sources disagree. The Pixodarus story is told only by Plutarch. Some scholars think it is misdated or apologetic invention. Others take it as proof that Alexander's position in 337/6 was genuinely insecure.

In 336, with Parmenion and Attalus already in Asia, Philip married his daughter Cleopatra to Alexander of Epirus at Aegae. His statue was carried into the theatre as a thirteenth beside the twelve Olympians (Diodorus 16.92.5). He entered without guards and was stabbed by his bodyguard Pausanias of Orestis (Diodorus 16.93–94). According to Diodorus, Attalus had made Pausanias drunk and handed him to muleteers to be raped, and Philip had refused redress. Aristotle (Politics 5.1311b), a contemporary, gives the same grievance. The theories:

  1. Pausanias alone: the only version with contemporary support.
  2. Olympias: Justin (9.7) has her ready horses, crown the corpse and force Cleopatra to suicide after killing her infant. This is likely hostile embroidery.
  3. Alexander: Plutarch (Alexander 10.6–7) says suspicion "touched" him. Badian ("The Death of Philip II", Phoenix 1963) made the classic case. Bosworth and Carney are sceptical.
  4. Persia: Alexander's letter to Darius blames him (Arrian 2.14.5). Convenient and unprovable.
  5. The Lyncestians: Arrhabaeus and Heromenes were executed. Their brother Alexander Lyncestes, first to hail the new king, was spared (Arrian 1.25.1–2).

The purge that followed is certain. Attalus was killed in Asia (Diodorus 17.2.4–6), and Amyntas son of Perdiccas was killed, as were Cleopatra and her child.

07Greek views and the Hellenicity question

For Demosthenes Philip was not Greek, not kin to Greeks, not even a respectable barbarian, but (9.31)

a pestilent Macedonian, from where you could not once buy a decent slave.

It is polemic, but it worked in front of an Athenian assembly. Isocrates' Philippus (346) urges Philip, as a Heraclid, to unite the Greeks against Persia. Yet even he says the founder left Greece because Greeks would not bear monarchy, and ruled a people "not of the same stock" (5.105–108). Friend and enemy agree that the kings could be Greek while the Macedonians were something else.

The modern debate concerns the people:

  • Language. Hesychius' glosses and the use of b for ph (Bilippos; Plutarch, Moralia 292e) suggest a distinct speech. The Pella curse tablet is in north-west Doric Greek, and Hatzopoulos treats Macedonian as a Greek dialect. But Alexander shouted Makedonisti to his guards in the Cleitus crisis (Plutarch, Alexander 51.6), and Philotas' use of the "native tongue" became an issue at his trial (Curtius 6.9.34–36).
  • Hammond (A History of Macedonia, 1972–88) held that they were Greek speakers, northern cousins of the Dorians. Herodotus 1.56 calls the proto-Dorians the Makednon ethnos.
  • Borza (In the Shadow of Olympus, 1990) saw a distinct Balkan people with a Hellenised elite, and thought the question unanswerable on present evidence.
  • Badian ("Greeks and Macedonians", 1982) moved the question to perception: whatever they spoke, Greeks and Macedonians each treated the other as outsiders.
Where sources disagree. The dispute over the name of North Macedonia, settled at Prespa in 2018, charged this debate beyond what the evidence can bear. A fair summary has three parts. Macedonian was probably a Greek dialect or a close relative. The court was Greek in culture by 400. Many southern Greeks still did not count Macedonians as Greek. These statements do not contradict each other.

08What Alexander inherited

At Opis Alexander reminded his army that Philip found them

wanderers without means, most of you dressed in skins

and brought them down from the hills to cities and laws (Arrian 7.9.2). Alexander did not have to invent:

  • A professional, year-round, combined-arms army of over 30,000 foot and 5,000 horse at the crossing (Arrian 1.11.3), with engineers.
  • Upper Macedonia bound to the crown, and a nobility trained as pages and paid as Companions.
  • Hegemony through the League of Corinth, a declared Panhellenic war of revenge (Isocrates' programme), and a bridgehead in Asia.
  • Heraclid and Aeacid ancestry, and the beginnings of divine honours: the thirteenth statue, and the Philippeion at Olympia with gold-and-ivory family statues by Leochares (Pausanias 5.20.9–10).
  • Aristotle's tutelage at Mieza (343–340), and the generals Antipater and Parmenion.

He did not inherit solvency or security. At Opis he says he found under sixty talents and debts of five hundred (Arrian 7.9.6). He took over a throne secured by killing rivals, and a Greece that revolted at once, so that Thebes was destroyed in 335. Philip left him the instrument but not the settlement.

09Self-test

  1. What does Herodotus 5.22 actually claim about Macedonian Greekness, and why is its scope narrower than it first appears?
    Answer

    It claims that the Argead dynasty, the descendants of Perdiccas, were Greeks of Argive origin, as shown by Alexander I's admission to Olympia. It says nothing about the Macedonian people. It also shows that the claim had to be argued.

  2. How does Thucydides (2.99) describe the relationship between the lowland kings and Upper Macedonia?
    Answer

    The Lyncestae, Elimiotae and other upland peoples were allies and subjects of the lowland kings but had kings of their own. Until Philip II bound them to the crown, their loyalty was negotiable.

  3. Summarise the Hammond–Errington debate on the Macedonian army assembly.
    Answer

    Hammond saw quasi-constitutional rights to acclaim kings and to try capital cases, citing Curtius 6.8.25. Errington and Borza saw customs that depended on the king's strength, so the monarchy was personal, not constitutional.

  4. Give two pieces of evidence that the hunt and killing conferred status in Macedonian society.
    Answer

    Hegesander (Athenaeus 1.18a): no reclining at dinner until a boar had been killed without a net. Aristotle (Politics 7.1324b): a man who had killed no enemy wore a halter for a belt.

  5. What are the main arguments for identifying the man in Vergina Tomb II as Philip III Arrhidaeus rather than Philip II?
    Answer

    The barrel vault, thought to be a post-Alexander borrowing; the diadem, adopted after 330; the pottery dates; the Asian-style gear; and Bartsiokas' finding of no eye wound. Arrhidaeus and Adea-Eurydice were buried at Aegae in 316. The question remains unresolved.

  6. What did Thucydides credit Archelaus with, and what cultural episode of his reign is now doubted?
    Answer

    Roads, forts, and more horses and arms than his eight predecessors combined (2.100.2). Euripides' residence at Pella, which Scullion (2003) argued never happened.

  7. Name four components of Philip's military reform and the ancient evidence for one of them.
    Answer

    Any four of: the sarissa phalanx, the pezhetairoi, the hypaspists, the Companion cavalry in the wedge, the siege train, and year-round campaigning. For example: Theophrastus, Historia Plantarum 3.12.2 on the sarissa; Demosthenes 9.48–50 on year-round war; Demosthenes 2.17 on the pezhetairoi; Diodorus 16.3 on the drill.

  8. According to Satyrus, what principle governed Philip's marriages, and why did the seventh threaten Alexander?
    Answer

    He "always married in connection with a war", so each marriage sealed an alliance. Cleopatra was a Macedonian noblewoman, niece of Attalus. A son by her could be presented as the "legitimate" heir Attalus prayed for, which undercut the son of the Molossian Olympias.

  9. What motive do Aristotle and Diodorus give for Pausanias' assassination of Philip, and which modern scholar argued most strongly for Alexander's involvement?
    Answer

    Personal outrage: Attalus had Pausanias raped, and Philip refused redress (Aristotle, Politics 5.1311b; Diodorus 16.93). E. Badian, "The Death of Philip II" (Phoenix 1963).

  10. How does Isocrates' Philippus, though pro-Macedonian, support the view that the Macedonian people were not considered Greek?
    Answer

    It treats Philip as Greek through his Heraclid ancestry but says the founder ruled a people "not of the same stock" (5.105–108). The dynasty is distinguished from the people.

10Carry forward

  1. The Macedonian monarchy was personal and was won by force. Every succession, Alexander's included, went to whoever held the army and the Companions and removed his rivals first. This explains the purges, the conspiracies, and the break-up after 323.
  2. Philip built almost everything Alexander used: the army, the engineers, the officer class, a unified Upper Macedonia, hegemony over Greece, the Persian war and the first steps towards divine honours. Measure Alexander against an instrument already made.
  3. Whether the Macedonians were Greek depended on who was asking. The dynasty claimed Argive descent, the court was Greek in culture, and the language was probably Greek, yet southern Greeks often counted Macedonians as outsiders. That tension runs from the League of Corinth to Opis.

Cover: the geography of Upper and Lower Macedonia and the Argead claim to Heraclid descent from Argos (Alexander I at Olympia and the Hellenicity question, Herodotus 5.22); the kingship: hereditary but acclaimed by the army, the king as warlord, priest and judge, the Companions and the royal pages, the royal hunt, the symposium culture and the heavy drinking; Macedonian religion and the royal tombs at Aegae (Vergina), including the debate about Tomb II; Archelaus and the Hellenisation of the court (Euripides at Pella); the chaos of the 360s; Philip II in detail: the hostage years at Thebes, the army reforms (sarissa, pezhetairoi, hypaspists, Companion cavalry, siege train, the end of seasonal campaigning), the gold of Mount Pangaion, the seven marriages and the harem politics, Olympias, the Pixodarus affair and the quarrel with Alexander, Attalus and Cleopatra Eurydice, the assassination by Pausanias and the theories about who was behind it; Demosthenes' and Isocrates' opposite views of Macedon; the modern debate about whether the Macedonians were Greeks (language, Hammond, Borza, Badian). End with what Alexander inherited and what he did not have to invent. Format: about 2500 words, headed sections, name sources, flag disagreements, end with a 10-question self-test with answers and the three ideas I must carry forward.
