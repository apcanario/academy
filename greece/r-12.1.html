Fragments, testimonia and the making of a canon

Module 5.6 names the evidence base: Aristotle, Theophrastus, Simplicius and the DK numbers. No Presocratic book survives. What survives is quotation embedded in later authors, each chosen for the quoter's purpose, and summaries of doctrine further removed still.

The first layer is Aristotle. Metaphysics A (983a24–993a27) reads his predecessors as partial discoverers of his own four causes; early philosophy, he says, is like someone lisping (993a15–16). His pupil Theophrastus arranged earlier opinions by topic in the Physical Opinions, lost but for the extract On the Senses and passages in Simplicius. Hermann Diels' Doxographi Graeci (1879) derived the later handbooks from it through Aëtius, probably of the first century AD, whom he reconstructed from the pseudo-Plutarchan Placita and Stobaeus.

The quoters form a second layer. Simplicius, in the sixth century AD, copies out Parmenides at length because, he says, the book has become rare (Commentary on Aristotle's Physics 144.25–28); much of Parmenides, Melissus, Anaxagoras and Empedocles exists only because he did. Hippolytus quotes Heraclitus to convict a Christian heretic of pagan borrowing; Sextus Empiricus quotes Xenophanes and Democritus as ancestors of doubt.

Diels gathered both layers in Die Fragmente der Vorsokratiker (1903); Walther Kranz revised it from the fifth edition (1934–37), and the sixth (1951–52) is cited. Each thinker has a chapter (Heraclitus 22, Parmenides 28, Democritus 68) divided into A, testimonia about life and doctrine; B, fragments held to be his own words; and C, imitations. Diels ordered Heraclitus' fragments alphabetically by citing author, so the numbers say nothing about the book's shape. André Laks and Glenn Most's Loeb Early Greek Philosophy (2016) replaces A and B with P (person), D (doctrine) and R (reception), conceding that words and report often blur; DK numbers remain the common currency.

Where sources disagree. Harold Cherniss (Aristotle's Criticism of Presocratic Philosophy, 1935) argued that Aristotle's reports are reconstructions shaped by his own problems, not history; W. K. C. Guthrie ("Aristotle as a Historian of Philosophy", 1957) replied that Aristotle declares his framework openly and his quotations can often be checked. Jaap Mansfeld and David Runia (Aëtiana, from 1997) have also qualified Diels' single Theophrastean stream: the tradition was reworked by many hands, and a report's Theophrastean origin must be argued case by case.

The Milesians: arguing for a principle

Module 5.6 asks why philosophy began in Ionia and gives the core claims: water, the boundless, air, the floating earth, equidistance, condensation and rarefaction.

For Thales there is only Aristotle's guess at an argument. Aristotle says Thales held that water is the principle, perhaps from seeing that the nourishment of all things is moist and that seeds are moist by nature (Metaphysics 983b20–27); the reasons are flagged as conjecture. He also reports that Thales' earth floats on water like wood, and objects that the water then needs a support of its own (On the Heavens 294a28–b1). Anaximander's earth, needing no support, removes the difficulty.

Anaximander's boundless is argued for. Aristotle reports, without naming him, a reason to make the principle something other than the elements: they are opposed, so a boundless one would long since have destroyed the rest (Physics 204b22–29). Elsewhere he says the boundless is ungenerated and imperishable and surrounds and steers all things, as Anaximander and most natural philosophers say (203b6–15). Simplicius, drawing on Theophrastus, preserves the one sentence in Anaximander's words (DK 12 B 1), in which things pay one another penalty for injustice: change is encroachment kept in balance by time, and the source must be neutral among the opposites. Aristotle called the equidistance argument clever but untrue, likening it to the claim that a man equally hungry and thirsty between food and drink must stay put (On the Heavens 295b10–34).

Anaximenes returned to a named stuff for the sake of mechanism. Theophrastus, through Simplicius (Commentary on the Physics 24.26–25.1), reports that air differs by rarity and density: rarefied it is fire; condensed, wind, cloud, water, earth, stone. Plutarch preserves the evidence: breath through pursed lips is cold, from an open mouth warm (On the Principle of Cold 947f; DK 13 B 1). One stuff and one process make every difference quantitative. The cost is Anaximander's worry: what is air when it is stone? Diogenes of Apollonia revived air in the fifth century, arguing that unlike things could neither mix nor act on one another unless all were forms of one substance (DK 64 B 2).

Xenophanes and the Pythagoreans

Module 5.6 gives Xenophanes' attack on Homer's gods (B 11, 15, 16), his one unmoving god (B 23–26), the sea-shells and B 34. The argument beneath B 14–16 is general: if the gods people picture always resemble the picturers, the picture is explained by the believer and is no evidence about its object. That removes the anthropomorphic gods but leaves Xenophanes' own god resting on what is fitting (B 26). B 18 makes inquiry progressive: the gods did not show mortals everything from the beginning, but by searching they find out better in time. B 38 makes perception relative: without honey, men would think figs far sweeter. Plato's Eleatic visitor traces the Eleatics back to Xenophanes (Sophist 242d), and Aristotle says Parmenides was said to be his pupil (Metaphysics 986b21–22): the undivided god became a template for what-is. Sextus quotes B 34 as an early statement of sceptical doubt (Against the Mathematicians 7.49), a line module 12.8 follows.

For Pythagoras module 5.6 gives transmigration, number and the dispute between Burkert and Zhmud. Plato names him once, as founder of a way of life (Republic 600a–b); Heraclitus says he practised inquiry beyond all men and made a wisdom of his own, much learning and bad craft (B 129); Aristotle's treatises speak almost always of the so-called Pythagoreans. The lives by Porphyry and Iamblichus are Neoplatonic hagiography. Iamblichus (On the General Mathematical Science 76) records a split between akousmatikoi, who kept the oral rules, and mathematikoi, who claimed the science and whose teaching, the former said, came from Hippasus.

Philolaus of Croton (c. 470–c. 385), whom Cebes and Simmias had heard at Thebes (Phaedo 61d–e), is the first Pythagorean whose words survive, and he argues in Eleatic terms. Nature was fitted together from unlimiteds and limiters (DK 44 B 1). Things are all limiting, all unlimited, or both (B 2); were all unlimited, nothing could be known (B 3); so both. Being unlike, they could not be ordered unless a harmonia came upon them (B 6), given by the octave ratios 2:1, 3:2, 4:3. Everything known has number, since without it nothing could be thought or known (B 4). Aristotle, who names Philolaus only once, reports of the Pythagoreans a central fire (On the Heavens 293a20–b1) circled by ten bodies, the tenth a counter-earth invented to reach the perfect ten (Metaphysics 986a8–12), and objects that heavy bodies cannot be built from weightless numbers (1090a30–35); later doxography (Aëtius 2.7.7) gives the system to Philolaus. Plato's Philebus (16c) takes over limit and unlimited (module 12.3).

Where sources disagree. Ingram Bywater ("On the Fragments Attributed to Philolaus the Pythagorean", 1868) showed that some fragments under Philolaus' name were spurious and inferred that all were. Burkert (Weisheit und Wissenschaft, 1962; English 1972) argued for a genuine core, now generally put at about eleven fragments (B 1–7, 6a, 13, 16, 17) and defended by Carl Huffman (Philolaus of Croton, 1993); the two still differ over B 6b and some testimonia.

Heraclitus: the argument behind the riddles

Module 5.6 gives the logos, the road, sea water, fire in measures, strife, the rivers and the flux dispute. The unity of opposites is several claims. Some opposites belong to one thing for different subjects: sea water is life to fish, death to men (B 61); donkeys prefer rubbish to gold (B 9). Some belong to one thing under different aspects: the road (B 60), the carding-comb's path straight and crooked (B 59). Some succeed one another and so are the same: living and dead, waking and sleeping, young and old (B 88). Some give each other value: disease makes health sweet (B 111); men would not know the name of Justice without injustice (B 23).

The conclusion is structural: a thing at variance agrees with itself, a back-turning harmony as of bow and lyre (B 51), whose opposed pulls make the instrument. Fire is the paradigm because it lives by consuming, and its turnings (B 31) and the soul's death into water (B 36) make cosmic and personal economy one. He prefers what is seen and heard (B 55), but eyes and ears are bad witnesses for those with barbarian souls (B 107), who cannot read the language they report.

Aristotle reports that some think Heraclitus says the same thing is and is not, and replies that nobody can believe it (Metaphysics 1005b23–26). His follower Cratylus, Aristotle adds, rebuked him for saying one cannot step twice into the same river, since one cannot do so once, and ended by only moving his finger (1010a10–15); Plato learned the Heraclitean view of sensible things from Cratylus (987a32–b1). The Stoics took his fire for their physics (module 12.7).

Where sources disagree. Parmenides scorns two-headed mortals for whom being and not-being are the same and not the same, and whose path is back-turning (DK 28 B 6.4–9). Jacob Bernays (1850) read this as an answer to Heraclitus' back-turning harmony, as Daniel Graham (2002) still does; Karl Reinhardt (Parmenides und die Geschichte der griechischen Philosophie, 1916) made Heraclitus the later thinker, and Eduard Zeller and Jaap Mansfeld (1964) held that neither knew the other. The echo hangs on a reading: Hippolytus (Refutation 9.9.2) gives B 51 as palintropos, Plutarch (On Isis and Osiris 369b) as palintonos.

Parmenides and Zeno

Module 5.6 gives the central inference, the Doxa and the monism dispute. B 8 shows how the deductions run. Against generation there are two arguments: coming-to-be from what is not cannot be said or thought (B 8.7–9); and what need would have roused it to grow later rather than sooner, starting from nothing (B 8.9–10)? The second is the principle of sufficient reason module 5.6 finds in Anaximander. So it must be wholly or not at all (B 8.11). It is indivisible because all alike (B 8.22–25), unmoving because Necessity holds it in bonds (B 8.26–31), complete like a well-rounded sphere (B 8.42–49). B 7 bids the hearer judge by reasoning (logos), not by aimless eye and echoing ear: reason against sense begins here.

What "is" means is the crux: existence, predication, or truth, the last Charles Kahn's reading (The Verb "Be" in Ancient Greek, 1973). On any reading every Milesian principle, becoming what it was not, is ruled out. Plato's Eleatic visitor answers with parricide, showing that what is not, as what is different, in a way is (Sophist 241d–259b; module 12.3). Aristotle answered that Parmenides took "is" to be said in one way when it is said in many (Physics 186a24–25), and that things come to be from a privation in a persisting subject, not from what is not without qualification (191a23–b34).

Zeno's purpose is stated in Plato's Parmenides (128c–e): his youthful book answered those who mocked Parmenides by showing that plurality led to worse absurdities. Module 5.6 gives the motion paradoxes and B 1–2. Two more show the method: if everything is in a place, place too will be in a place, without end (Aristotle, Physics 209a23–25); a bushel of millet makes a sound when poured, a single grain none (250a19–22). Aristotle first answered the Dichotomy by making time divisible like distance (233a21–31), then conceded that this answers the questioner, not the problem, and distinguished potential from actual division (263a4–b9). Convergent series show that the halves sum to a finite distance; whether that explains how a runner completes infinitely many tasks is still argued. The Arrow led Bertrand Russell (The Principles of Mathematics, 1903) to define motion as being at different places at different times, and nothing more.

Where sources disagree. Paul Tannery (1885) argued that Zeno's target was a Pythagorean number-atomism that built bodies from units with magnitude, and F. M. Cornford and, in Pythagoreans and Eleatics (1948), J. E. Raven built on it. G. E. L. Owen ("Zeno and the Mathematicians", 1958) showed that no early evidence attests such a doctrine and read Zeno as attacking plurality as everyone assumed it; most now follow Owen.

Melissus and the road to atomism

Melissus of Samos commanded the fleet that beat an Athenian squadron in the Samian revolt of 441/0 (Plutarch, Pericles 26). His prose, preserved by Simplicius, restates Parmenides as a plain chain, and changes it. Whatever is always was, since nothing comes from nothing (DK 30 B 1); without beginning or end it is boundless in magnitude (B 2–3), not a bounded sphere; if boundless, it is one, since two would limit each other (B 6). There is no void, for void is nothing; without void nothing moves, having nowhere to give way (B 7.7–10). And if there were many things, each would have to be as the One is; but we see hot become cold and iron worn away, so we did not see rightly (B 8).

Aristotle thought him crude (Metaphysics 986b26–27) and caught a fallacy: from "what came to be has a beginning" he infers "what did not come to be has none" (Physics 186a10–16). His clarity mattered more. B 8 states the condition a pluralist must meet, B 7 ties motion to void, and Aristotle's account of Leucippus (On Generation and Corruption 325a2–b5) takes both premises and reverses the conclusion: motion is evident, so there is void, and the many are uncuttable ones moving in it. The pseudo-Aristotelian treatise that preserves one version of Gorgias' On Not Being sets him beside Melissus and Xenophanes (module 12.2).

Empedocles and the Strasbourg papyrus

Module 5.6 gives the roots, Love and Strife, the wandering limbs, the pores, the exiled daimon and the ban on flesh. The answer to Parmenides is explicit: there is no birth of any mortal thing nor end in death, only mixing and exchange, and birth is a name men give (DK 31 B 8); nothing of the whole is empty or overfull (B 13). Kinds are fixed by ratio: bone is two parts earth, two water, four fire (B 96), which Aristotle read as a glimpse of form (Parts of Animals 642a17–24). Like knows like: by earth we see earth, by water water (B 109, quoted at On the Soul 404b11–15).

Aristotle objected that Love often separates and Strife combines (Metaphysics 985a21–29), and that a zoogony in which chance combinations survived if fit, like the man-faced ox-progeny (B 61), cannot explain why nature produces its results always or for the most part (Physics 198b16–199a8); hence final causes. Lucretius (Rome module 5.6) praised Empedocles as a poet (1.716–733) before dismantling his roots.

The Strasbourg papyrus is a roll copied in the late first century AD, whose strips were folded to back the bronze leaves of a funerary crown and bought at Akhmim, ancient Panopolis, in 1904. Alain Martin recognised Empedocles in 1990; he and Oliver Primavesi published L'Empédocle de Strasbourg in 1999. One group overlaps the end of B 17 and continues it, a marginal sign placing the quoted passage's end at line 300 of the first book. Another, ensemble d, sets the lament of B 139 for dreadful deeds done for food inside the physical narrative. Module 5.6 gives what that does to the two-poem question; the cycle's shape is a separate fight.

Where sources disagree. Denis O'Brien (Empedocles' Cosmic Cycle, 1969) and M. R. Wright (Empedocles: The Extant Fragments, 1981) reconstruct a symmetrical cycle with two cosmogonies and two zoogonies, one as Love advances and one as Strife does; Jean Bollack (Empédocle, 1965–69) and A. A. Long ("Empedocles' Cosmic Cycle in the 'Sixties'", 1974) allow one world and one zoogony. Simon Trépanier ("Empedocles on the Ultimate Symmetry of the World", 2003) reads ensemble d as a zoogony under Strife distinct from the one under Love; André Laks (2001) doubts that the fragments can bear that weight.

Anaxagoras and Mind

Module 5.6 gives no coming-to-be (B 17), everything in everything (B 11), Mind (B 12), the trial and the Phaedo. The argument for B 11 starts from nutrition: how could hair come from what is not hair, or flesh from what is not flesh (B 10)? If bread becomes flesh and nothing comes from nothing, flesh was in the bread. Infinite divisibility, Zeno's weapon against plurality, becomes a premise: there is no smallest, always a smaller (B 3), so the portions never run out, and each thing is most manifestly what it has most of (B 12). Mind alone is unmixed, by argument: mixed with anything, it would share in everything and be hindered from controlling anything (B 12).

Aristotle praised him as a sober man among random talkers for making Mind a cause (Metaphysics 984b15–18), then complained that he wheels Mind in only when at a loss (985a18–21), and objected that infinitely many ingredients make everything unknowable (Physics 1.4, 187a26–188a18). Aristotle's term for the stuffs, homoiomerē, like-parted (On Generation and Corruption 314a18–20), is not in the fragments. Diogenes Laertius says Anaxagoras foretold the stone that fell at Aegospotami (2.10), which Pliny dates to 467 (Natural History 2.149).

Leucippus and Democritus

Module 5.6 gives atoms and void, convention against reality (B 9), the senses' reply (B 125) and the indivisibility dispute. Aristotle makes atomism an Eleatic settlement (On Generation and Corruption 325a2–b5): Leucippus granted that motion needs void and that void is what-is-not, but held motion evident, and concluded that what-is-not exists no less than what-is. Democritus coined a word for it: "den" is no more than nothing (Plutarch, Against Colotes 1108f–1109a; DK 68 B 156). Each atom has the properties of Melissus' One, and there are infinitely many. Aristotle reports an argument for indivisibility (316a14–b16): divide a body through and through; what remains is either magnitudes, still divisible, or points or nothing, from which no body can be composed; so there are uncuttable bodies.

Leucippus' one fragment says nothing happens at random, but everything for a reason and by necessity (DK 67 B 2). Aristotle objected that the atomists lazily set aside where motion comes from (Metaphysics 985b19–20); Theophrastus examined point by point Democritus' theory of perception, in which tastes follow atomic shapes (On the Senses 49–83). The ethical sayings, mostly from Stobaeus (B 169–297), make the goal euthymia, settled cheerfulness through measure (B 191); whether they connect to the physics, as Gregory Vlastos argued (1945–46), is uncertain. Epicurus revised the system (module 12.6).

Where sources disagree. Sextus (Against the Mathematicians 7.135–139) quotes B 9 and its kin to make Democritus a forerunner of doubt; Diogenes Laertius (9.72) gives B 117, that in reality we know nothing, for truth is in the depth; and Metrodorus of Chios said none of us knows anything (DK 70 B 1). But Sextus also quotes B 11, which ranks a legitimate knowledge, reached by reasoning finer than the senses, above their bastard kind, and Aristotle took Democritus to equate thought with perception and so make appearance true (Metaphysics 1009b11–17).

The Derveni papyrus as a reader of philosophy

Modules 2.1 and 8.2 give the Derveni papyrus' Orphic theogony, the aidoion dispute and the roll's survival on a pyre near Thessaloniki. Here the point is the commentator, writing about 400 BC or a little earlier, as the oldest surviving reader of philosophy. He treats the poem as riddling and decodes it into physics: Zeus becomes air and Mind, the swallowing a gathering of matter, Moira the god's thought. The physics is Anaxagorean, Mind arranging particles separated from a prior mixture, and in places close to Diogenes of Apollonia's intelligent air. Column IV quotes Heraclitus on the sun, the width of a human foot, kept within its measures by the Erinyes, overlapping DK 22 B 3 and B 94: since the roll is the oldest surviving Greek book, this is the oldest manuscript witness to any Presocratic, and proof that Heraclitus was already a text cited in argument.

Where sources disagree. Walter Burkert (1986) proposed Stesimbrotus of Thasos as the author, Charles Kahn (1997) Euthyphro, the seer of Plato's dialogue, and Richard Janko ("The Physicist as Hierophant", 1997) Diagoras of Melos, the notorious atheist, reading the allegory as a physicist's demythologising. Gábor Betegh (The Derveni Papyrus: Cosmology, Theology and Interpretation, 2004) argues instead for an Orphic priest who used physics to explain the rites he performed.

Self-test

  1. Why must Aristotle's account of Thales at Metaphysics 983b20–27 be read in two layers?
    Answer

    Aristotle reports that Thales held water to be the principle, but the reasons (moist nourishment, moist seeds) are his own conjecture, marked "perhaps", and the frame of a material cause is his too. Thales left no words of his own.

  2. What do A and B mean in a DK reference, and why did Laks and Most abandon the division?
    Answer

    A marks testimonia, reports about a thinker's life and doctrine; B marks fragments held to be his own words. Laks and Most (2016) use P, D and R instead because the line between quotation and paraphrase is often unclear.

  3. Reconstruct the argument Aristotle reports for a principle distinct from the elements, and say why its attribution needs care.
    Answer

    The elements are opposed, so a boundless one would destroy the others; the source must therefore be something neutral (Physics 204b22–29). Aristotle does not name Anaximander there; the attribution is inferred.

  4. How does Philolaus argue that the cosmos is made of both limiters and unlimiteds, and what binds them?
    Answer

    Things are all limiting, all unlimited, or both (B 2); were all unlimited, nothing could be known (B 3); so both. Being unlike, they need a harmonia to be ordered, given by the octave ratios (B 6).

  5. Sort Heraclitus B 61, B 59, B 88 and B 111 by the kind of unity of opposites each shows.
    Answer

    B 61: one thing, opposite effects for different subjects (sea water). B 59: one thing under different aspects (the comb's straight and crooked path). B 88: opposites that succeed one another (living and dead). B 111: opposites that give each other value (disease and health).

  6. State Parmenides' two arguments against generation in B 8 and name the principle behind the second.
    Answer

    Coming-to-be from what is not cannot be said or thought (B 8.7–9); and nothing explains why what-is should start from nothing at one time rather than another (B 8.9–10). The second is a principle of sufficient reason.

  7. What was Aristotle's first answer to the Dichotomy, and why did he later call it inadequate?
    Answer

    That time is infinitely divisible like distance, so infinitely many stages are crossed in infinitely many moments (Physics 233a21–31). He later said this answers the questioner, not the problem, and appealed to the difference between potential and actual division (263a4–b9).

  8. Which two Melissan premises did the atomists take over, and how did they reverse his conclusion?
    Answer

    That motion requires void (B 7) and that each of many things would have to be like the Eleatic One (B 8). Holding motion evident, Leucippus inferred that void exists and made the many into uncuttable, unchanging atoms (On Generation and Corruption 325a2–b5).

  9. What do Aristotle's objections to Empedocles' zoogony and to Anaxagoras' Mind have in common?
    Answer

    Both fault the absence of a working final cause: chance survival cannot explain why nature achieves its results always or for the most part (Physics 198b16–199a8), and Anaxagoras introduces Mind but uses it only as a device when at a loss (Metaphysics 985a18–21).

  10. Why does the Derveni papyrus matter for the history of philosophy apart from its theogony?
    Answer

    Its commentator, writing about 400 BC, decodes the poem into Anaxagorean physics and quotes Heraclitus (column IV, overlapping B 3 and B 94), so it shows Presocratic texts being read and cited within the fifth century, in the oldest manuscript witness to any of them.

Carry forward

  1. Every Presocratic sentence reaches us through a reporter with a purpose: Aristotle's four causes, Simplicius' commentary, Hippolytus' heresy-hunting, Sextus' scepticism. Name the reporter and separate B words from A reports before arguing about doctrine.
  2. The Presocratics form a chain of arguments, not of guesses: each accepts most of a predecessor's premises and denies one, as the atomists took Melissus' void premise and reversed it. To understand a position, find the premise it rejects.
  3. Parmenides' rules (nothing from nothing, reason over the senses, no change without sufficient reason) set the terms for Plato's Sophist, Aristotle's Physics and the Hellenistic schools, and the Derveni commentator's habit of reading old poems as coded physics runs on into Stoic allegory.
