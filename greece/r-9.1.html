The three orders

An order is a grammar: a fixed set of parts whose proportions and ornament are agreed in advance, so that a trained eye reads a building as a reader scans a line of verse. Vitruvius (De architectura 4.1) gives the ancient account; his numbers are a Roman systematisation of looser Greek practice.

Doric is the mainland and western order, canonical by about 600 BC. Identify it by what is missing: the column has no base and rises straight from the stylobate. The shaft carries twenty shallow flutes meeting in sharp edges (arrises). The capital is a plain cushion (echinus) under a square slab (abacus). Above the architrave the frieze alternates triglyphs (three vertical bars) and metopes (square panels, often carved). Beneath the triglyphs hang guttae, the fossilised pegs of a timber ancestor. Early shafts at Paestum and Corinth are barely four lower diameters high.

Ionic comes from the eastern Aegean and the islands in the sixth century. The column stands on a moulded base; the shaft carries twenty-four flutes separated by flat fillets; the capital rolls into two volutes like a scroll laid over the shaft. The frieze is either continuous and sculpted (Attic practice) or replaced by a row of dentils (Asia Minor). Proportions are slender, around nine diameters, and the mouldings (egg-and-dart, bead-and-reel) are carved.

Corinthian is an Ionic variant distinguished only by its capital, a bell wrapped in two rows of acanthus leaves with small volutes at the corners. Vitruvius (4.1.9–10) credits its invention to the sculptor Kallimachos, moved by a basket left on a girl's grave through which acanthus had grown. The earliest surviving example is a single interior column at Bassae (late fifth century); the first exterior use is the Choregic Monument of Lysikrates in Athens (334 BC). Rome preferred it because the capital reads the same from every angle, which the Ionic volute does not.

Where sources disagree. Vitruvius (4.2) says Doric forms translate carpentry into stone: triglyphs are beam ends, guttae pegs. Barletta (The Origins of the Greek Architectural Orders, 2001) argues they were devised in stone, since no excavated wooden temple shows the prototype. Uncertain.

Anatomy of a temple

A temple is a house for a cult statue; sacrifice happened at the altar outside. The building is a box inside a colonnade on a platform.

The standard Doric plan by 500 BC is hexastyle with thirteen columns on the flank; the Parthenon is octastyle with seventeen. The Doric corner is the order's permanent headache: a triglyph should sit over each column and also close each end of the frieze, which at the corner it cannot, so architects narrowed the end intercolumniations (angle contraction).

The Parthenon's refinements

The Parthenon (447–432 BC; Iktinos and Kallikrates architects, Phidias overseeing according to Plutarch, Pericles 13) contains almost no straight line. Penrose measured this in the 1840s (Principles of Athenian Architecture, 1851), confirming hints in Vitruvius 3.3.13 and 3.4.5:

  • The stylobate rises in a convex curve, about 6 cm on the short sides and 11 cm on the long, carried up through the entablature.
  • Every column leans inward by about 7 cm; the corner columns lean on the diagonal.
  • The corner columns are some 4 cm thicker than the rest, and the end intercolumniations are narrower.
  • Each shaft swells slightly below the midpoint (entasis), so the profile is a curve rather than a cone.
  • A 4:9 ratio governs width to length, column diameter to interval, and façade height to width.
Where sources disagree. Vitruvius says the refinements correct illusions: a straight stylobate would appear to sag, a corner column against the sky would look thin. Since Goodyear (Greek Refinements, 1912) many reject this, because the curves are visible, and argue they give the building a muscular tension; others invoke drainage. The intention is uncertain; the precision is not.

Sculpture by period

Daedalic and Archaic, c. 680–480 BC

The earliest monumental style is called Daedalic after the mythical craftsman: frontal, flat figures with triangular faces and wig-like hair, such as the Auxerre Goddess (Louvre, c. 640) and the Nikandre from Delos (c. 650). Egyptian influence shapes the kouros, the standing nude youth with left leg advanced and arms at his sides, which appears about 600 BC (New York Kouros) and evolves over a century from diagram to anatomy: Kleobis and Biton at Delphi (c. 580; Herodotus 1.31), the Anavysos Kouros (c. 530, the tomb of Kroisos), the Aristodikos (c. 500). The kore, the draped maiden, shows the same trajectory through dress: the Peplos Kore (c. 530) and the Phrasikleia of Aristion of Paros (c. 540), whose epitaph says the gods gave her the name maiden in place of marriage.

The Severe style, c. 480–450 BC

After the Persian Wars the archaic smile vanishes. The Kritios Boy (Acropolis, c. 480) is the first surviving figure whose weight shifts onto one leg, tilting the hips: the beginning of contrapposto. The Tyrannicides by Kritios and Nesiotes (477/6; copies in Naples) replaced Antenor's group that Xerxes carried off (Pausanias 1.8.5; Arrian, Anabasis 3.16.7). The Charioteer of Delphi (c. 475) and the Artemision god (c. 460) are rare bronze originals. Faces are heavy-lidded and grave; drapery falls in columnar folds.

Polykleitos and the canon

Polykleitos of Argos (active c. 460–410) wrote a treatise, the Kanon, and made a statue to demonstrate it, almost certainly the Doryphoros (best copy in Naples). The treatise is lost; Galen (De placitis Hippocratis et Platonis 5) says it located beauty in the commensurability (symmetria) of finger to finger, hand to forearm and so on, and Philo Mechanicus quotes him:

Perfection comes about little by little through many numbers.

The Doryphoros systematises contrapposto into chiasmus: tensed leg under relaxed arm, relaxed leg under tensed arm. Pliny (Natural History 34.55–56) also lists his Diadoumenos.

Phidias

Phidias of Athens made the two most famous statues of antiquity, both lost: the gold-and-ivory Athena Parthenos (dedicated 438; Pausanias 1.24.5–7, Pliny 36.18) and the seated Zeus at Olympia (Pausanias 5.11; Strabo 8.3.30 says that standing he would unroof the temple). His Olympia workshop, excavated in 1954–58, yielded drapery moulds and a cup scratched I belong to Phidias. Whether he carved any Parthenon marble is unknown; the style is nonetheless called Phidian: wet-looking drapery revealing the body beneath, faces of serene detachment.

Praxiteles

The fourth century softens the body into an S-curve and gives it a mood. Praxiteles (active c. 370–330) made the Aphrodite of Knidos, the first monumental female nude, which the Knidians refused to sell even for cancellation of their public debt (Pliny 36.20–21), and the Apollo Sauroktonos, a boy teasing a lizard (Pliny 34.70). Asked which marbles he valued most, he named those the painter Nikias had coloured (Pliny 35.133).

Where sources disagree. Pausanias (5.17.3) saw a Hermes with the infant Dionysus by Praxiteles in the Heraion at Olympia; a matching statue was found there in 1877. Whether it is the original, a later Praxiteles, or a Roman copy has been argued since Blümel (1927) from tool marks and the strut. Divided.

Lysippos

Lysippos of Sikyon (active c. 370–310), Alexander's portraitist (Plutarch, Alexander 4), made heads smaller and bodies slimmer than Polykleitos and represented men as they appeared rather than as they were (Pliny 34.65). His Apoxyomenos, an athlete scraping himself, reaches an arm into the viewer's space: the first statue that demands to be walked around.

Hellenistic baroque

Under the kings, sculpture turns theatrical: extremes of age, pain, sleep and movement. The Great Altar of Pergamon (c. 180–160 BC, under Eumenes II; Berlin) wraps its podium in a Gigantomachy over two metres high, gods treading on serpent-legged giants whose faces contort; the inner Telephos frieze tells the city's founding myth. The Laocoön (Vatican) shows the Trojan priest and his sons strangled by serpents; Pliny (36.37) names Hagesandros, Polydoros and Athenodoros of Rhodes. The Nike of Samothrace and the Dying Gaul share the sensibility.

Where sources disagree. The Laocoön, found in 1506, has been dated from c. 200 BC to the reign of Tiberius. Andreae sees a Roman marble version of a Pergamene bronze; others an original Rhodian work of the first century BC, when the same sculptors signed at Sperlonga. Unresolved.

Lost bronzes, Roman copies, painted marble

Most celebrated Greek statues were bronze, hollow-cast by the lost-wax method, with inlaid eyes, copper lips and silver teeth (all visible on the Riace warriors, c. 460–450). Bronze is money; nearly all was melted. What survives was lost at sea (Artemision, Antikythera) or buried (Delphi, Riace, Piraeus). Our image of Polykleitos, Praxiteles and Lysippos rests on Roman marble versions, made from plaster casts (a cache was found at Baiae in 1954) and burdened with tree-trunk supports and struts that bronze never needed. Furtwängler's Meisterwerke (1893) founded Kopienkritik, reconstructing lost originals by comparing copies.

Where sources disagree. Ridgway (Roman Copies of Greek Sculpture, 1984) and Marvin (The Language of the Muses, 2008) argue that many copies are Roman creations in a Greek manner, and that hunting a lost original behind each type misreads Roman taste. The Doryphoros is secure; many others are not.

Marble, too, was coloured. Plato (Republic 420c) speaks of statue-painters putting the finest colours on the finest parts; Euripides (Helen 262–3) has Helen wish her beauty wiped away like paint from a statue. Hair, lips, eyes, borders and often whole garments were painted; metal supplied spears, wreaths and jewellery. Brinkmann's reconstructions (Gods in Color, 2003 onwards), from ultraviolet and raking-light traces, restore the Peplos Kore and the Aegina archer to a brightness that still shocks viewers raised on white marble.

Vase painting

Pots survive because they are worthless to melt; they are the largest body of Greek narrative art.

  • Geometric (c. 900–700). Meanders, zigzags and concentric circles cover the surface in bands; late in the period silhouette figures appear. The Dipylon Amphora (Athens, c. 750) shows a laying-out of the dead with mourners tearing their hair: the owner's funeral, not myth.
  • Corinthian (c. 720–550). Corinth invents the black-figure technique about 700: figures painted in glossy slip, details incised with a point, red and white added. Protocorinthian miniaturists reach a peak on the Chigi Vase (c. 640), with its hoplite phalanx and Judgement of Paris; later Corinthian fills every space with animal friezes and rosettes.
  • Attic black-figure (c. 630–500). Athens takes over the technique and the market. The François Vase of Kleitias and Ergotimos (c. 570, Florence) carries over 200 named figures in six friezes. Exekias (c. 545–530), potter and painter, is the master: Achilles and Ajax absorbed over a board game (Vatican), Dionysus sailing alone across a cup (Munich), Ajax planting the sword on which he will fall (Boulogne). He paints the moment before the event.
  • Red-figure (c. 530 onwards). The Andokides Painter reverses the scheme, reserving figures in clay colour against black and drawing inner lines with a brush, which allows anatomy, foreshortening and expression. Euphronios and Euthymides, the Pioneers, compete openly; Euthymides writes on one amphora, as never Euphronios. The Berlin Painter (c. 505–460, named by Beazley after an amphora in Berlin) isolates one or two figures against unbroken black, with no ground line or frame: a kitharode mid-song, Hermes with a satyr, the pot itself the stage.
  • White-ground lekythoi (c. 470–400). Oil flasks for the grave, painted in matt colours on a white slip too fragile for use. The Achilles Painter shows a woman handing a soldier his helmet, a mourner at a stele, Charon waiting in his boat, in a register unlike symposium ware.
Where sources disagree. The painter-names rest on Beazley's connoisseurship (ABV 1956, ARV 1963), attributing unsigned pots by Morellian comparison of ears, knees and folds. Whitley (1997) questions its assumptions about individual genius; Vickers and Gill (Artful Crafts, 1994) argued painted pottery merely imitated silver and gold and was cheap. Most specialists accept the attributions and reject the metalware thesis.

How to read a mythological scene

Painters had no captions beyond occasional name-labels, so they built a vocabulary of attributes. Herodotus (2.53) says Homer and Hesiod gave the gods their forms; painters gave them their luggage.

Then ask what the action is, who is present who need not be, and what the pot is for. A hoplite leaving home is generic; the same figure with a woman labelled Andromache is Hector. Gods standing by signal the weight of a duel. Heracles feasting on a drinking cup is a joke shared with the drinker.

Vases routinely show what no surviving text describes. Achilles and Ajax at their game appears on over 150 vases and in no literary source; Achilles' ambush of Troilos comes from the lost Cypria, Ajax carrying Achilles' corpse from the lost Aethiopis, both known through Proclus's summaries. Painters drew on oral tradition, local cult, lost epic and lyric, the stage, and their own invention, for an audience that knew the stories in versions Homer and Hesiod never fixed. Snodgrass (Homer and the Artists, 1998) showed how rarely archaic painters follow Homer's text even on Homeric subjects; Small (The Parallel Worlds of Classical Art and Text, 2003) treats image and text as largely separate traditions. A vase is evidence for a myth, not an illustration of a book.

The great sculptural programmes

The Siphnian Treasury at Delphi, c. 525 BC

Herodotus (3.57–58) says the Siphnians, rich from gold and silver mines, built their treasury from a tithe before Samian raiders humbled them. It is a small Ionic building with caryatid porch columns and a continuous frieze on all four sides, cut by two masters of different style. North: Gigantomachy, Apollo and Artemis shooting side by side. East: the gods in council while Greeks and Trojans fight over a body below. South: a chariot abduction. West: the Judgement of Paris. The east pediment shows Heracles trying to carry off the Delphic tripod while Apollo holds on and Zeus steps between. It is a statement of piety at a sanctuary that polices the boundary between hero and god: the greatest of men does not get to take Apollo's oracle.

The Temple of Zeus at Olympia, c. 470–456 BC

Built from the spoils of Elis's war against Pisa for control of the games. East pediment (Pausanias 5.10.6–8): the moment before the chariot race between Pelops and Oinomaos for Hippodameia, Zeus unseen between them, an old seer at the edge who already knows the outcome. West: centaurs assaulting the Lapith women at Peirithoos's wedding, Apollo calm at the centre. Twelve metopes over the porches show the labours of Heracles, the first appearance of the canonical twelve, with Athena assisting. The message is local and Panhellenic at once: Pelops is the founder-hero buried in the sanctuary, Heracles the founder of the games, and a race won through the perjury of Myrtilos warns athletes swearing before the altar of Zeus Horkios.

Where sources disagree. Pausanias attributes the pediments to Paionios (east) and Alkamenes (west). Both worked a generation too late; modern scholars speak instead of an anonymous Olympia Master, and argue from style whether one workshop or two carved the ends.

The Parthenon, 447–432 BC

Ninety-two metopes carry four battles: east, gods against giants; west, Greeks against Amazons; south, Lapiths against centaurs; north, the sack of Troy. One thought stated four ways: order defeats disorder, Greek defeats barbarian, and the Persian who burned the Acropolis in 480 is written into myth without being named. The east pediment showed the birth of Athena before the gods, framed by the chariots of Helios and Selene; the west, the contest of Athena and Poseidon for Attica (Pausanias 1.24.5). The Athena Parthenos inside repeated the programme: Amazonomachy on her shield, Centauromachy on her sandals, Pandora on the base. The frieze, 160 metres of horsemen, chariots, elders, musicians and sacrificial animals converging on seated gods and a folded cloth, has since Stuart and Revett (1787) been read as the Panathenaic procession, living Athenians on a temple for the first time.

Where sources disagree. Boardman (1977) counted 192 horsemen and read them as the Marathon dead heroised. Connelly (1996) argued the central scene shows Erechtheus preparing his daughter for sacrifice, the cloth her shroud, making the frieze mythological like every other. Most retain the procession reading; no ancient source mentions the frieze.

Self-test

  1. Name three features that distinguish a Doric from an Ionic column without looking at the frieze.
    Answer

    Doric has no base, twenty flutes meeting in sharp arrises, and a plain echinus-and-abacus capital; Ionic has a moulded base, twenty-four flutes separated by fillets, and a volute capital.

  2. What is the Doric corner problem and how was it solved?
    Answer

    A triglyph must sit over each column and also close the frieze at the corner, which is geometrically impossible; architects narrowed the end intercolumniations (angle contraction) so the last triglyph could reach the corner.

  3. Which part of a temple is the opisthodomos, and how does it differ from the pronaos?
    Answer

    The rear porch, mirroring the front porch (pronaos) but with no door into the cella; at the Parthenon it served as a treasury.

  4. List four optical refinements of the Parthenon.
    Answer

    Convex curvature of the stylobate and entablature, inward inclination of the columns, thickening of the corner columns with angle contraction, and entasis of the shafts.

  5. What was Polykleitos's Kanon, and how do we know anything about it?
    Answer

    A lost treatise on proportion demonstrated in a statue, almost certainly the Doryphoros; known from Galen's account of its doctrine and a quotation in Philo Mechanicus.

  6. Why is almost every famous Greek statue known only through Roman marbles?
    Answer

    The originals were bronze and were melted for the metal; Roman workshops made marble versions from plaster casts, adding struts and tree-trunk supports that bronze did not need.

  7. What is the technical difference between black-figure and red-figure, and what did red-figure make possible?
    Answer

    Black-figure paints figures in slip and incises details; red-figure reserves figures in clay colour and paints the background black, drawing details with a brush, which permitted fluid anatomy, foreshortening and expression.

  8. What attributes identify Hermes, Heracles and Perseus?
    Answer

    Hermes: kerykeion, petasos, winged boots. Heracles: lion skin, club, bow. Perseus: winged cap and boots, kibisis, sickle sword, averted gaze.

  9. Give an example of a myth scene common on vases but absent from surviving literature, and explain why this happens.
    Answer

    Achilles and Ajax playing a board game (over 150 vases, no text). Painters drew on lost epic, oral and local tradition, and their own invention; image and text were largely parallel traditions.

  10. What single idea unites the four metope battles of the Parthenon?
    Answer

    Order defeating disorder and Greek defeating barbarian, read as a mythological statement of the Persian defeat; gods over giants, Greeks over Amazons, Lapiths over centaurs, Greeks over Trojans.

Carry forward

  1. Greek art is a grammar of fixed parts and attributes: the orders, the temple plan and the gods' identifiers are conventions learnt by the whole culture, so a building or a pot could be read at a glance.
  2. What survives is a distorted sample: marble and clay, not bronze and paint. Reconstruct the lost originals and the lost colour in your mind before judging any Classical work.
  3. Images are independent evidence for myth. Vase scenes and temple programmes preserve versions and emphases the texts do not, and their choice of subject is always a political and religious statement by whoever paid.
