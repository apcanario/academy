What a secondary saga is

The great cycles absorbed whole epic poems. The sagas here survive as digressions inside other works, as lost tragedies known from fragments, as paragraphs in Apollodorus' Library, and as episodes Ovid reworked. That mode of survival matters. Each story was carried by a city or region with a stake in it, and the version we read usually served that stake: Corinth's Bellerophon, Olympia's Pelops, Argos' Io, Athens' Cephalus, Procne and Erichthonius. Read them as local charters stitched into the Panhellenic fabric.

Bellerophon

The oldest account is a speech. In Iliad 6.152–205 Glaucus the Lycian tells Diomedes that his grandfather Bellerophon, from Corinthian Ephyra, was desired by Anteia, wife of King Proetus of Argos. Refused, she accused him of attempted rape. Proetus would not kill a guest, so he sent him to his father-in-law in Lycia with a folded tablet bearing "many baneful signs" (6.168–169) that told the recipient to kill the bearer. He survived the Chimaera, the Solymi, the Amazons and an ambush, and won the king's daughter and half the kingdom. Yet Homer ends darkly: Bellerophon "became hated by all the gods" and wandered alone on the Aleian plain (6.200–202).

Homer never mentions Pegasus. The winged horse enters with Hesiod, Theogony 319–325, where the Chimaera is killed by "Pegasus and noble Bellerophon"; the horse's birth from Medusa's neck is at 280–286. The Corinthian form is Pindar's, Olympian 13.60–92: Athena appears in a dream by her altar, hands Bellerophon a golden bridle and tells him to sacrifice to his father Poseidon. The fall, in Isthmian 7.44–47, is the attempt to ride Pegasus to heaven; Zeus threw him, and Pegasus went on to carry the thunderbolt. Euripides' lost Stheneboea and Bellerophon dramatised the queen and the crippled hero; Apollodorus 2.3.1–2 gives the compact version.

Where sources disagree. Homer calls Proetus' wife Anteia; tragedy and Apollodorus call her Stheneboea. Homer makes Bellerophon son of Glaucus; Hesiod's Catalogue (fr. 43a) and Pindar make Poseidon his father. Whether Homer's "baneful signs" are writing is disputed: they are the only possible reference to a written message in the poems. The fall is hubris in Pindar, a gadfly sent by Zeus in the scholiasts, and absent from Homer, where the hero simply loses divine favour.

The interest is Corinthian and Lycian. Corinth stamped Pegasus on its coinage from the sixth century, located the horse's capture at the Peirene spring, and received Pindar's ode. The Lycian frame in Homer gives a Trojan ally a Greek pedigree.

The Calydonian boar hunt

In Iliad 9.529–599 Phoenix urges Achilles with the example of Meleager. Oeneus of Calydon in Aetolia forgot Artemis at the harvest sacrifice; she sent a boar that wrecked his orchards. Meleager killed it with many hunters, but Artemis stirred war between the Aetolians and the Curetes of Pleuron over the head and hide. His mother Althaea, whose brother he had killed, cursed him; he withdrew in anger until his wife Cleopatra persuaded him to fight, and got none of the promised gifts. There is no brand, no Atalanta, no death on stage: Homer's point is the cost of resentment held too long.

The brand appears in the fifth century. Phrynichus' Pleuroniae alluded to it, and Bacchylides 5.93–154 gives it in full: Meleager's ghost tells Heracles that when he killed his uncles over the hide, Althaea burned the log on which the Fates had made his life depend, and he felt his strength ebb in the field. Apollodorus 1.8.2–3 is the synthesis: the Fates' prophecy at his birth, the catalogue of hunters, Atalanta drawing first blood, Meleager killing the boar and giving her the hide, his uncles Plexippus and Toxeus taking it from her because a woman had no right to it, Meleager killing them, Althaea burning the brand and hanging herself. Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.260–546, dramatises Althaea's wavering. Pausanias 10.31.3–4 records that Hesiod's Catalogue had Meleager killed by Apollo.

Where sources disagree. Homer's Meleager seems to die by the curse working through Apollo; Bacchylides and Apollodorus make the brand the mechanism, and scholars argue whether the brand is an archaic folk-motif Homer suppressed to preserve his parallel with Achilles, or a later invention. Apollodorus 1.8.2 reports that some denied Atalanta was at the hunt at all. Her parentage splits: daughter of Iasus of Arcadia, or of Schoeneus of Boeotia (Hesiod, Ovid); Apollodorus 3.9.2 records both.

The saga served Aetolia, otherwise marginal to the heroic map: it made Oeneus' house host of a Panhellenic gathering a generation before the Argonauts and Troy, and gave Diomedes (Tydeus' son, Oeneus' grandson) a glorious ancestry. The Arcadians claimed Atalanta: Pausanias saw the boar's tusks at Tegea in the temple of Athena Alea, whose pediment showed the hunt (8.45.6–7, 8.46.1).

Atalanta's race and Hippomenes

Atalanta refused marriage and set a test: a suitor must outrun her, and losers died. In the Boeotian version, Hesiod's Catalogue (frr. 72–76) followed by Ovid, Metamorphoses 10.560–707, she is Schoeneus' daughter and the winner is Hippomenes, who drops three golden apples from Aphrodite; Atalanta stoops for each and loses. In the Arcadian version, Apollodorus 3.9.2, she is Iasus' daughter, exposed at birth and suckled by a bear, and the suitor is Melanion. Both agree on the end: the couple made love in a sacred precinct and were turned into lions, which ancient lore held could not mate with each other. Her son Parthenopaeus, one of the Seven against Thebes (Aeschylus, Seven 526–549), links into the next cycle.

Where sources disagree. The winner (Hippomenes or Melanion), the father (Schoeneus or Iasus), the apples' source, and whether the suitor ran ahead with Atalanta armed in pursuit (the Hesiodic scheme) or side by side. Whether the huntress of Calydon and the runner were originally one heroine is uncertain; Apollodorus clearly felt the two needed reconciling.

The house of Tantalus

This saga must be held in full, because the Oresteia and the Trojan War presuppose it.

Tantalus. Son of Zeus, king at Sipylus in Lydia, admitted to the gods' table. His crime is told three ways: he served his son Pelops to the gods to test them (Apollodorus, Epitome 2.3; Ovid, Metamorphoses 6.403–411); he stole nectar and ambrosia for mortals (Pindar, Olympian 1.60–64); he repeated the gods' secrets (Euripides, Orestes 10). In Odyssey 11.582–592 water recedes from his lips and fruit lifts away; in Pindar a stone hangs over his head.

Pelops and the shoulder. The gods detected the dish; only Demeter, grieving for Persephone, ate a shoulder. Clotho restored the boy in a cauldron and Demeter replaced the shoulder with ivory. Pindar knows this and rejects it: at Olympian 1.25–53 he calls Pelops "ivory-shouldered" yet refuses to call the gods gluttons, blames an envious neighbour, and has Poseidon carry the boy to Olympus as Zeus did Ganymede. Pausanias 5.13.4–6 says the shoulder-blade was later taken to Troy and lost at sea.

Hippodamia and the chariot race. Oenomaus of Pisa, warned he would die at a son-in-law's hands, raced every suitor to the Isthmus and speared the losers. Pelops prayed to Poseidon for a golden chariot and winged horses (Olympian 1.71–88) and in Pindar wins cleanly. In Apollodorus, Epitome 2.4–8, and Pausanias 6.20.17, he or Hippodamia bribes the charioteer Myrtilus, son of Hermes, who fits wax linchpins; Oenomaus is dragged to death. Myrtilus had been promised a night with Hippodamia; when he claimed it, Pelops threw him into the sea off Geraestus, and as he fell he cursed the race of Pelops. At Olympia the Taraxippus that frightened horses was said to be his ghost (Pausanias 6.20.15–19).

Atreus and Thyestes. Exiled from Pisa after killing Chrysippus, the brothers came to Mycenae, where an oracle had told the people to choose a Pelopid king. Atreus kept a golden lamb he had vowed to Artemis; his wife Aerope, Thyestes' lover, gave it to Thyestes, who proposed that the lamb's owner should rule. Zeus had Atreus counter-propose that the sun reverse its course, and it did (Euripides, Electra 699–746, where the chorus doubts it). Atreus then recalled Thyestes under pretence of reconciliation and served him his own sons, showing him the hands and feet afterwards. Aeschylus, Agamemnon 1583–1602, has Aegisthus tell it.

Aegisthus' birth. An oracle told Thyestes that only a son begotten on his own daughter could avenge him. He raped Pelopia at Sicyon by night, unrecognised; she took his sword. Atreus married her; the child was exposed, suckled by a goat (aix, hence the name) and raised as Atreus' own. Sent to kill the captured Thyestes, Aegisthus was recognised by the sword, learned the truth and killed Atreus (Apollodorus, Epitome 2.14; Hyginus, Fabulae 87–88).

Where sources disagree. Pindar's rejection of the feast is the first explicit case of a Greek poet correcting a myth on moral grounds; most scholars take the feast as traditional and Pindar's abduction as the revision. Whether Pelops cheated divides Pindar from the mythographers; the east pediment of the temple of Zeus at Olympia (Pausanias 5.10.6–7) showed the moment before the race and left it open. Hesiod's Catalogue fr. 194 and Aeschylus, Agamemnon 1569 and 1602, insert a Pleisthenes between Atreus and Agamemnon. How often the brothers exchanged the throne, and whether Aerope was drowned or banished, vary with every source.

The stake was Olympia's. The Pelopion stood inside the Altis, with black-ram sacrifices to Pelops as a hero (Pausanias 5.13.1–3), and the race against Oenomaus competed with Heracles as the foundation legend of the games. The Lydian origin gave the Peloponnese its name and an eastern pedigree. Above all the saga gave tragedy an inherited curse, Myrtilus' and Thyestes' both, that made the deaths of Agamemnon and Clytemnestra seem fated.

Cephalus and Procris

Procris, daughter of Erechtheus of Athens, married Cephalus of Thoricus. Odysseus sees her in Hades (Odyssey 11.321) without a story. The narrative comes from the Athenian genealogist Pherecydes (FGrH 3 F 34): Cephalus, returning disguised after a long absence, tested his wife and seduced her with gold. Apollodorus 3.15.1 and Antoninus Liberalis 41 continue: shamed, Procris fled to Minos in Crete, cured him of Pasiphae's curse, and was rewarded with the hound Laelaps and a spear that never missed. Returning disguised as a boy, she beat Cephalus at the hunt and offered the gifts for his favours; when he agreed, she revealed herself and they were reconciled. Then, hunting with the spear, Cephalus threw at a rustle in the undergrowth and killed Procris, who had followed suspecting a lover. Ovid, Metamorphoses 7.690–865, makes the suspicion arise from Cephalus calling on the breeze, aura, which an eavesdropper took for a name. Tried on the Areopagus, Cephalus was exiled and later gave his name to Cephallenia (Strabo 10.2.14).

Where sources disagree. The order and motivation of the mutual tests differ in every source; Ovid drops the Minos episode and the sale for gold to keep Procris sympathetic. Whether the Cephalus loved by Eos is Procris' husband or a separate son of Hermes and Herse was already disputed in antiquity.

The interest is Attic. Thoricus' deme calendar lists offerings to Cephalus and Procris; the Areopagus trial made the story a precedent for jurisdiction over involuntary homicide; Cephallenia's claim shows the saga travelling west with colonists.

Procne, Philomela and Tereus

Pandion of Athens, at war with Labdacus of Thebes, won the alliance of Tereus the Thracian and gave him his daughter Procne (Apollodorus 3.14.8). She bore Itys. Years later she asked to see her sister Philomela; Tereus fetched her, raped her on the way, cut out her tongue and hid her, telling Procne she was dead. Philomela wove the story into a robe. Procne found her and in revenge killed Itys and served him to Tereus. He pursued the sisters with an axe; all three became birds: Procne the nightingale, Philomela the swallow, Tereus the crested hoopoe. The canonical shape is Sophocles' lost Tereus, known from fragments and a papyrus hypothesis, and Ovid, Metamorphoses 6.412–674. An older relative exists: in Odyssey 19.518–523 the nightingale is Pandareus' daughter who killed her son Itylus by mistake. Hesiod, Works and Days 568, already calls the swallow "daughter of Pandion".

Where sources disagree. Thucydides 2.29.3 denies that Tereus was a Thracian king: he lived at Daulis in Phocis, where "the women did the deed about Itys", and poets call the nightingale Daulian. Pausanias 10.4.8–9 agrees; the Megarians showed Tereus' tomb at Megara (Pausanias 1.41.8–9). Greek sources make Procne the nightingale and Philomela the swallow; Latin poets reversed the pair, which is why English "Philomel" means the nightingale. Aeschylus, Suppliants 60–62, and Hyginus 45 make Tereus a hawk.

The interest was Athenian in two senses. The alliance with Tereus mirrors fifth-century courtship of Thracian kings, notably Sitalces, whose connection Thucydides is anxious to dissociate from the barbaric Tereus. And the tale made Pandion's daughters a warning about giving Athenian women to foreign husbands, an anxiety that also shapes Euripides' Ion.

Io, Danaus and the Danaids

Io, priestess of Hera at Argos and daughter of the river Inachus, was loved by Zeus and turned into a cow. Hermes killed her guard Argus, and Hera's gadfly drove Io across the world. Aeschylus, Prometheus Bound 561–886, stages her arrival in the Caucasus and Prometheus' prophecy: in Egypt Zeus will restore her by a touch, she will bear Epaphus, and in the thirteenth generation her line will produce Heracles. Suppliants 291–324 retells it from the Danaids' side. Apollodorus 2.1.3–5 runs the genealogy: Epaphus begets Libya; Libya and Poseidon beget Agenor (father of Cadmus and Europa) and Belus; Belus begets Aegyptus and Danaus, with fifty sons and fifty daughters. Fearing his nephews, Danaus built the first ship, sailed to Argos and claimed the kingdom as Io's descendant; a wolf killing a bull at the walls decided the Argives for him (Pausanias 2.19.3–4). His daughters found the springs of Lerna (Pausanias 2.37.1). When Aegyptus' sons came demanding marriage, Danaus consented and gave each daughter a dagger; all killed their husbands but Hypermnestra, who spared Lynceus. From them descend Abas, Acrisius, Danae, Perseus, and so Heracles.

Where sources disagree. Io's father is Inachus in Aeschylus, Iasus in Hesiod and Acusilaus, Peiren in others (Apollodorus 2.1.3). Aeschylus has Hera transform her (Suppliants 299); Apollodorus has Zeus do it to hide her. Herodotus 1.1–2 reports a rationalised version in which Phoenician traders carried her off, and at 2.41 identifies her with Isis. The Danaids' punishment in Hades, filling leaking jars, is not attested before the Hellenistic period (pseudo-Plato, Axiochus 371e); classical Argos knew them as founders of springs and of the Thesmophoria (Herodotus 2.171), not as sinners.

The saga is Argive throughout. Hera's great sanctuary lay between Argos and Mycenae, and Io as her priestess binds the goddess to the royal line; Danaus' arrival explains the Homeric name Danaoi and claims the first ship and the Lernaean springs; Epaphus and Libya gave Argos kinship with Egypt and Phoenicia, which Herodotus took seriously enough to trace the Spartan kings to Egypt via Perseus (6.53–54).

Cecrops, Erichthonius and Athenian autochthony

Athens claimed its people had never migrated but were born from Attic soil. Cecrops was the first king, "autochthonous, with a body compounded of man and serpent" (Apollodorus 3.14.1). Under him Athena and Poseidon contested Attica: Poseidon's trident made a salt spring, Athena planted the olive, with Cecrops as witness, and the gods judged for Athena (Herodotus 8.55, who saw both in the Erechtheum; Pausanias 1.26.5). Herodotus 8.44 lists the stages of the Athenian name: Cranai, Cecropidae, Athenians under Erechtheus, Ionians.

Erichthonius is the autochthon proper. Hephaestus tried to rape Athena; his semen fell on her thigh; she wiped it with wool (erion) and threw it to the earth (chthon), which bore the child. Athena put the infant in a chest with a snake and gave it to Cecrops' daughters Pandrosus, Herse and Aglaurus with orders not to look. Two did, saw the serpent-child, went mad and leapt from the Acropolis. Athena raised the boy; as king he set up the olive-wood image of Athena Polias and founded the Panathenaea; his son was Pandion. Sources: Euripides, Ion 20–26, 265–274; Apollodorus 3.14.6; Pausanias 1.18.2 and 1.24.7. In Euripides' lost Erechtheus (fr. 360) Praxithea boasts that the Athenians "are not immigrants brought in from elsewhere".

Where sources disagree. Homer, Iliad 2.546–551, knows only Erechtheus, "whom Athena reared and the grain-giving earth bore"; Erichthonius does not appear before the fifth century, and most scholars hold the two were originally one figure, split so that the earth-born child could become grandfather of the warrior king. Pausanias 1.18.2 names only Aglaurus and Herse as the disobedient sisters; Pandrosus, with her own precinct beside the Erechtheum, is usually exonerated. The political reading, that autochthony underwrote both democratic equality and exclusion of foreigners (Loraux, Rosivach), dominates modern scholarship; Thucydides 1.2.5 gives it a rational form: Attica's poor soil deterred invaders, so the population never changed.

The interest is Athens' self-image and its monuments. The Erechtheum housed Athena Polias, the olive, Poseidon's sea and Cecrops' tomb; the Arrhephoroi, girls who carried a covered basket by night from the Acropolis (Pausanias 1.27.3), re-enacted the chest and the sisters. The funeral orations (Lysias 2.17; Plato, Menexenus 237b–c; Isocrates, Panegyricus 24–25) made autochthony the first item in praise of the city: alone among Greeks, the Athenians had driven no one out and held their land by birth.

Self-test

  1. What does Homer's Bellerophon carry to Lycia, and why does it matter for the history of writing?
    Answer

    A folded tablet with "many baneful signs" (Iliad 6.168–169) ordering the bearer's death. It is the only possible reference to a written message in Homer, and scholars dispute whether the signs are script.

  2. Which hero does Phoenix use Meleager to warn, and what elements of the later saga are missing from his telling?
    Answer

    Achilles (Iliad 9). Homer has no fatal brand and no Atalanta; Meleager is undone by his mother's curse and his own withdrawal from battle.

  3. Name the two versions of Atalanta's parentage and the two names of the suitor who won her race.
    Answer

    Daughter of Iasus of Arcadia (suitor Melanion) or of Schoeneus of Boeotia (suitor Hippomenes).

  4. How does Pindar alter the story of Pelops in Olympian 1, and why?
    Answer

    He denies that Tantalus served Pelops to the gods, blaming the tale on slander, and substitutes Poseidon's abduction of the boy to Olympus; he will not attribute gluttony to the gods. Tantalus' crime becomes theft of nectar and ambrosia.

  5. Who is Myrtilus and what is his lasting effect on the family?
    Answer

    Oenomaus' charioteer, son of Hermes, who sabotaged the chariot with wax linchpins. Pelops threw him into the sea, and as he fell he cursed the Pelopids; the curse is one explanation for the crimes of Atreus and Thyestes and their descendants.

  6. Explain the derivation of Aegisthus' name and the circumstances of his birth.
    Answer

    From aix, goat: exposed as an infant and suckled by a goat. Thyestes begot him by raping his daughter Pelopia, following an oracle that only such a son could avenge the feast.

  7. What two gifts did Procris bring back from Crete, and how did one of them kill her?
    Answer

    The hound Laelaps and a spear that never missed, rewards from Minos. Cephalus, hunting, threw the spear at a rustle in the bushes and killed Procris, who had followed him suspecting a lover.

  8. Where does Thucydides locate Tereus, and why does he insist on it?
    Answer

    At Daulis in Phocis, not Thrace (2.29.3). He wants to deny any link between the mythical Tereus and Teres, father of Athens' Thracian ally Sitalces.

  9. Trace the line from Io to Perseus.
    Answer

    Io and Zeus beget Epaphus; Epaphus begets Libya; Libya and Poseidon beget Belus; Belus begets Danaus; Danaus' daughter Hypermnestra and Lynceus beget Abas, then Acrisius, Danae and Perseus.

  10. What etymology did Athenians give for Erichthonius, and how was his birth re-enacted in cult?
    Answer

    From erion (wool) and chthon (earth): Athena wiped Hephaestus' semen with wool and threw it to the ground. The Arrhephoroi carried a covered basket by night from the Acropolis, re-enacting the chest entrusted to Cecrops' daughters.

Carry forward

  1. Myth was local property before it was Panhellenic. Each saga here is a city's charter (Corinth, Olympia, Argos, Athens), and the surviving version served the stronger interest; counter-claims from Daulis, Megara, Arcadia or Boeotia show the competition in action.
  2. The house of Tantalus supplies the inherited curse that the Trojan generation and the Oresteia presuppose: Tantalus' feast, Myrtilus' curse, Thyestes' feast and Aegisthus' birth are the backstory to Agamemnon's murder and Orestes' revenge.
  3. Genealogy is argument. Io's line makes Argos the parent of Perseus, Heracles, Cadmus and Europa and kin to Egypt; Erichthonius makes Athenians born of their own soil. When a later module presents a hero's pedigree, ask which city gained from it.
