How to read it

five rules
  1. Parts I to IV in order. Each report assumes the ones before it. Part I is the foundation; skipping it is how the gaps got there. Part IV ends with a capstone exam across the whole course.
  2. Deep dives when you want them. Unit 10 (Troy) extends unit 4, unit 11 (Alexander) extends module 6.7, and unit 12 (Greek philosophy) extends modules 5.6, 6.5, 7.2 and 7.4. Take each straight after the material it extends, or after the capstone.
  3. One or two a sitting. A report is about 3,000 words, fifteen to twenty minutes. More than two a day and the self-tests stop meaning anything.
  4. Self-test before moving on. Under 7 of 10, re-read. The carry-forward ideas at the end of each report are the ones later reports lean on.
  5. A study text, not scripture. Citations are given so you can check them against the primary sources. Where sources disagree, the report says so in a red-edged box.

The reading spine

primary and secondary

The reports keep naming these. Read the primary ones at least once each, in this order, any decent translation: Hesiod (Theogony, Works and Days) · Homer (Iliad, then Odyssey) · Apollodorus, The Library (Robin Hard's Oxford translation; keep it open through Part II) · Aeschylus' Oresteia, Sophocles' Theban plays, Euripides' Medea and Bacchae · Herodotus · Thucydides · Plato (Apology, Symposium, Republic; with unit 6 and deep dive 12) · Diogenes Laertius, books 6, 7 and 10 (with deep dive 12) · Plutarch's Lives as each name comes up · Pausanias, book 1.

Secondary, three only: Robin Hard, The Routledge Handbook of Greek Mythology; Walter Burkert, Greek Religion; Robin Osborne, Greece in the Making, with Simon Hornblower, The Greek World 479 to 323.
