Causes: the truest cause and the stated grievances

Thucydides distinguishes two kinds of explanation. The grievances openly argued about, the aitiai, were Corcyra, Potidaea and the Megarian Decree. Behind them lay the alēthestatē prophasis, the truest cause, "least spoken of openly": the growth of Athenian power and the fear it produced in Sparta (Thucydides 1.23.6; restated at 1.88 and 1.118.2). The Pentecontaetia (1.89–118) exists to make that claim visible.

The grievances are told with care. Epidamnus, a Corcyraean colony with a Corinthian founder, appealed to Corinth; Corcyra sought alliance with Athens; Athens granted a defensive alliance only, and at Sybota in 433 its ships fought Corinthians (1.24–55). Potidaea, a Corinthian colony paying tribute to Athens, was ordered to pull down its seaward wall and expel its Corinthian magistrates; it revolted and Athens besieged it (1.56–65). The Megarian Decree, excluding Megarians from Attic markets and imperial harbours, is mentioned almost in passing (1.67.4, 1.139.1–2), though Sparta made its repeal the final condition for peace and Pericles refused to yield to an ultimatum dressed as a small request (1.140–144).

At Sparta the Corinthians urge war (1.68–71), Archidamus counsels delay (1.80–85), and the ephor Sthenelaidas carries the vote (1.86).

Where sources disagree. Aristophanes (Acharnians 515–539; Peace 605–611) makes the Megarian Decree and Pericles' personal position the cause, as many Athenians evidently believed. De Ste. Croix (The Origins of the Peloponnesian War, 1972) accepts Thucydides' verdict and blames Spartan aggression; Kagan (The Outbreak of the Peloponnesian War, 1969) argues the war was avoidable and that Thucydides overstates structural causation; Badian (From Plataea to Potidaea, 1993) reads Thucydides as a partisan concealing Athenian provocation. The weight of the Megarian Decree remains uncertain.

The Archidamian War: Pericles' strategy, the Funeral Oration, the plague

The war began in spring 431 with a Theban night attack on Plataea (2.2–6), followed by the first invasion of Attica. Pericles' strategy (1.140–144, 2.13) was deliberately unheroic: abandon the countryside, bring the population inside the walls, refuse pitched battle against superior Peloponnesian hoplites, use the fleet to raid the Peloponnese and keep tribute flowing, and make no new conquests while the war lasted. His verdict at 2.65 is that the plan would have worked had Pericles' successors kept to it.

The Funeral Oration for the dead of the first year (2.35–46) is an idealised portrait of what the city claimed to be: equal laws, public honour by merit (2.37), citizens who are "lovers of the city" (2.43.1), the whole earth the tomb of famous men (2.43.3). It is followed, by evident design, with the plague of 430 (2.47–54), described clinically from Thucydides' own illness, refusing to speculate on causes and recording instead the collapse of law and piety when the pious and impious died alike. The toll he gives later is 4,400 hoplites and 300 cavalry (3.87). Pericles, deposed and fined, defended the empire as a tyranny now unsafe to lay down (2.60–64), was re-elected, and died in 429.

Where sources disagree. Delbrück classed Periclean strategy as exhaustion that could not produce victory; Kagan argues it assumed a Spartan readiness to tire that never appeared, and that Athens had no plan for ending the war. The plague's identity is contested: Papagrigorakis and colleagues (2006) reported typhoid DNA from a Kerameikos mass grave, but the method has been challenged, and smallpox, measles and an extinct pathogen all have defenders.

Mytilene and the Mytilenean Debate

In 428 Mytilene, one of the few allies still contributing ships rather than tribute, revolted and appealed to Sparta (3.2–6). Spartan help came too late; starving, and with the demos refusing to fight once armed, Mytilene surrendered in 427 (3.27–28). The assembly voted to execute all adult males and enslave the rest, then regretted it and reconvened.

In the paired speeches (3.37–48) Cleon, "the most violent of the citizens" (3.36.6), argues that democracy cannot rule an empire, that pity and fine speaking are the vices of empire, and that exemplary terror is justice. Diodotus, otherwise unknown, answers from interest rather than mercy: the death penalty does not deter because hope and desire drive men to risk everything (3.45), and killing the demos along with the oligarchs will teach every subject city that revolt has no exit. Diodotus wins narrowly; a second trireme, its crew eating as they row, overtakes the first as Paches reads the original decree (3.49). Thucydides then records that the Mytileneans Paches had sent to Athens as chief culprits, "a little more than a thousand", were executed (3.50).

The same summer the Plataeans were executed after a sham Spartan trial (3.52–68), and the Corcyraean civil war produced the analysis of stasis at 3.82–83.

Where sources disagree. The "more than a thousand" at 3.50.1 has troubled editors, since Paches had sent only ringleaders; some propose corruption (thirty for a thousand), though the manuscripts agree. Whether Diodotus is a real speaker or a construct for the argument Thucydides wanted heard is uncertain.

Pylos and Sphacteria, Cleon and Brasidas, Amphipolis

In 425 Demosthenes fortified Pylos on the Messenian coast (4.2–6). The Spartans, alarmed at a base in helot country, landed 420 hoplites on the island of Sphacteria; the Athenian fleet then defeated the Peloponnesian ships and cut the island off (4.8–23). Sparta offered peace to recover the men; Cleon had the assembly demand more (4.17–22). When the blockade dragged on and Cleon attacked the generals, Nicias offered him the command; trapped by his own rhetoric, Cleon accepted and promised to bring the Spartans back within twenty days, "a mad promise" that came true (4.27–28). Demosthenes' light-armed troops wore the hoplites down, and 292 survivors, about 120 of them Spartiates, surrendered: the first time Spartans had done so, a shock to Greece (4.29–41). Athens held them as hostages against invasion.

Fortune then turned. In the north Brasidas, the one Spartan in Thucydides with initiative and charm, marched through Thessaly and in winter 424/3 took Amphipolis, the colony on the Strymon guarding timber and the Hellespont route (4.78–88, 102–108). Thucydides himself, commanding at Thasos, arrived too late and held only Eion; he says at 5.26 that he was exiled for twenty years after Amphipolis. In 422 Cleon led an expedition to recover the city; caught in a disordered withdrawal, his army was routed and he was killed fleeing, while Brasidas died of his wounds in victory and was buried in the agora as the city's second founder (5.6–11).

Where sources disagree. Thucydides' hostility to Cleon is unmistakable, and Woodhead ("Thucydides' Portrait of Cleon", 1960) argued it distorts the record: Pylos and the raised tribute of 425 both served Athens, and the ancient life by Marcellinus claims Cleon secured Thucydides' exile. Whether Thucydides was negligent at Amphipolis cannot be settled from his own brief account.

The Peace of Nicias and the rise of Alcibiades

With Cleon and Brasidas, the two men Thucydides says most wanted war, dead (5.16), Nicias and King Pleistoanax negotiated a fifty-year peace in spring 421 (5.18–19) and an Athenian-Spartan alliance (5.23). Thucydides insists this was a suspect truce, not peace, and counts the war as one twenty-seven-year whole (5.26). Sparta could not deliver Amphipolis; Athens kept Pylos; Corinth, Boeotia, Megara and Elis rejected the treaty; Argos became the centre of a rival alliance (5.27–48).

Into this stepped Alcibiades, Pericles' ward, young and personally offended that Sparta had negotiated through Nicias (5.43). He built an alliance of Athens, Argos, Mantinea and Elis in 420, tricking the Spartan envoys into denying their own full powers before the assembly (5.44–46). The confrontation came at Mantinea in 418, the largest hoplite battle of the war, where King Agis won decisively and restored Spartan prestige (5.64–74). His extravagance made the Athenians suspect him of aiming at tyranny, a suspicion that, Thucydides says, "not long after brought the city down" (6.15).

The Melian Dialogue

In 416 Athens attacked Melos, a Dorian island outside the empire (5.84–116). Before the siege the Athenian envoys and Melian councillors talk in private, and Thucydides casts it as dialogue rather than paired speeches. The Athenians refuse to discuss justice, which applies only between equals in power, while "the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must" (5.89); their rule follows a law of nature the Melians themselves would obey (5.105). The Melians answer with hope, the gods and Spartan kinship; the Athenians dismiss all three (5.103–105). Melos resisted, was betrayed and taken; the men were killed, the women and children enslaved, and 500 colonists planted (5.116.4).

The placement is deliberate: the Dialogue closes Book 5 and Sicily opens Book 6, so the logic of power without limit expounded at Melos is tested at Syracuse. Thucydides offers no comment of his own.

Where sources disagree. No one supposes Thucydides had a transcript of a closed meeting; the Dialogue is treated as his own composition. De Romilly (Thucydides and Athenian Imperialism, 1947) reads it as the climax of a critique of imperial reasoning; others as a realistic record of how such negotiations went. Xenophon (Hellenica 2.2.3) shows Athenians in 405 remembered Melos as a crime they now feared to pay for.

The Sicilian Expedition

In 415 Segesta appealed to Athens against Selinus and Syracuse with promises of money that proved fraudulent (6.6, 6.46). Nicias argued against the venture and tried to deter the assembly by demanding an enormous force; the Athenians voted him everything (6.9–14, 6.20–26). Alcibiades argued that empire cannot stand still (6.16–18). The command was shared between Nicias, Alcibiades and Lamachus.

Before sailing, the Herms were mutilated in a single night, and informers added charges that Alcibiades had parodied the Mysteries (6.27–29). The fleet left anyway: 134 triremes and about 5,100 hoplites (6.43). In Sicily the generals disagreed (6.47–49); then the Salaminia arrived to recall him, he slipped away at Thurii, and at Sparta advised sending a commander to Syracuse and fortifying Decelea (6.53, 6.61, 6.88–93).

In 414 the Athenians seized Epipolae and began a wall of circumvallation; Lamachus was killed, and the city was near surrender when the Spartan Gylippus arrived, slipped past the blockade, and built a counter-wall that cut the Athenian line (6.96–103, 7.1–7). Nicias, ill, asked to be relieved or massively reinforced; the assembly sent Demosthenes and Eurymedon with 73 ships and some 5,000 hoplites (7.8–16, 7.42). Demosthenes' night assault on Epipolae collapsed in confusion as Dorians on both sides sang the same paean (7.43–45). He urged immediate withdrawal; Nicias refused, fearing the assembly (7.47–49). When he agreed, a lunar eclipse on 27 August 413 led the soothsayers to prescribe a delay of thrice nine days (7.50). The Syracusans closed the Great Harbour and destroyed the fleet in a battle narrated as watched from the shore like a theatre (7.69–71). The army retreated overland; Demosthenes' division surrendered, Nicias' was cut down at the Assinarus (7.75–85). Both generals were executed, the prisoners penned in the quarries, and Thucydides calls it "the greatest action of this war", a total destruction from which "few out of many returned home" (7.86–87).

Where sources disagree. Thucydides judges at 2.65.11 that the fault lay less with the expedition than with those at home who failed to support it, which sits uneasily with 6.1 and Nicias' warnings; Kagan thinks the venture unwinnable, Lazenby that it was lost by command decisions in 414. Plutarch (Nicias 19–28), using the Syracusan historians Philistus and Timaeus, has Nicias and Demosthenes take their own lives; Diodorus 13.19 gives a long Syracusan debate on their fate.

The Decelean War and the oligarchic coup of 411

Athens did not collapse. Sparta fortified Decelea in 413, Attica was occupied all year, slaves deserted, and Athens replaced tribute with a five-per-cent harbour tax (7.19, 7.27–28). Ionia revolted, and Sparta built a fleet with Persian money under three treaties with Tissaphernes that conceded the King's right to the cities of Asia (8.18, 8.37, 8.58). Alcibiades, now distrusted at Sparta, crossed to Tissaphernes and played each side against the other while negotiating his return (8.45–56).

That return was to come through a change of constitution. Officers at Samos led by Peisander and Phrynichus were told Alcibiades could bring Persian support if democracy were abolished (8.47–54). In 411 the assembly at Colonus was induced to appoint Four Hundred to govern on behalf of Five Thousand who were never listed (8.63–70); Thucydides shows how a small conspiracy paralyses a city by making each man suspect his neighbour (8.66). The fleet at Samos refused to follow, made Thrasybulus and Thrasyllus its leaders and recalled Alcibiades, whose one indisputable service was to stop it sailing against Athens (8.73–82, 8.86). At Athens the extremists began fortifying Eetioneia to admit a Spartan fleet; Theramenes broke with them, Phrynichus was assassinated, and when Euboea revolted the Four Hundred fell (8.89–96). Power passed to the Five Thousand, those who could furnish arms, under which, Thucydides says, "the Athenians appear to have been better governed than ever in my time" (8.97.2). The History breaks off in autumn 411 after Cynossema (8.104–109).

Where sources disagree. Aristotle's Constitution of the Athenians 29–33 preserves constitutional documents for 411 that cannot be fully reconciled with Thucydides, and scholars dispute whether the Five Thousand ever functioned or whether democracy simply returned by stages in 410. Thucydides' praise of the interim regime is read both as moderate-oligarchic sympathy and as a verdict on performance alone.

Arginusae, Aegospotami, the siege and the Thirty

Xenophon's Hellenica begins where Thucydides stops. Alcibiades and Thrasybulus destroyed the Peloponnesian fleet at Cyzicus in 410; Xenophon preserves the dispatch, "Ships gone; Mindarus dead; men starving; don't know what to do" (1.1.23). Alcibiades returned home in 407 (1.4.8–20), but after Lysander, funded by Cyrus the Younger, beat his deputy at Notium in 406 he withdrew to Thrace (1.5.11–17).

In 406 Callicratidas blockaded Conon at Mytilene; Athens melted temple dedications, enrolled slaves as rowers, and at the Arginusae islands won its last great victory, losing 25 ships to the enemy's 70 (1.6.24–38). A storm prevented rescue of the crews from the wrecks, and at Athens the generals were deposed; six returned. Theramenes, one of the trierarchs assigned to the rescue, blamed them; Euryptolemus argued for separate trials under the law, but the assembly condemned all six by a single vote, against the decree of Cannonus, and executed them, Pericles the younger among them. The Athenians later repented and prosecuted Callixenus, the proposer (1.7). Plato (Apology 32b) and Xenophon (Memorabilia 1.1.18) say Socrates, presiding, refused to put the motion.

In 405 Lysander took Lampsacus; the Athenian fleet of 180 ships beached opposite at Aegospotami, an open shore. For four days Lysander refused battle while the crews scattered for food; on the fifth he struck the empty ships and took almost the whole fleet. Conon escaped with eight ships; Lysander executed the Athenian prisoners, some 3,000 according to Plutarch (Lysander 13), after Xenophon's account of the capture (2.1.20–32). "That night no one slept" in Athens (2.2.3). After months of blockade Theramenes negotiated, and in spring 404 Athens accepted terms: Long Walls and Piraeus fortifications demolished, the fleet cut to twelve ships, exiles recalled, Athens to follow Sparta's lead. The walls came down to flute music, the Peloponnesians believing "this day was the beginning of freedom for Greece" (2.2.10–23).

Under Lysander's pressure thirty men were appointed to draft laws; they ruled instead, with a Spartan garrison on the Acropolis and a reign of judicial murder that Aristotle sets at not fewer than 1,500 dead (Constitution of the Athenians 35.4). When Theramenes objected to killing rich men for their property, Critias struck his name off the list of Three Thousand and had him dragged from the altar to drink hemlock (2.3.11–56). Thrasybulus seized Phyle with seventy men in winter 404/3, grew to a thousand, took the Piraeus, and killed Critias at Munychia (2.4.2–19). Lysander moved to crush the democrats, but King Pausanias, jealous of him, brokered reconciliation instead. In autumn 403 the democracy was restored under an amnesty forbidding anyone except the Thirty, the Ten and the Eleven to "remember past wrongs" (2.4.28–43).

Where sources disagree. Diodorus (13.101–103), following Ephorus, orders the Arginusae accusations differently and makes the generals strike first against Theramenes. On Aegospotami, Diodorus 13.105–106 has Philocles sail out with thirty ships and be caught in disorder, not a simple surprise on the beach; Plutarch (Lysander 11) follows Xenophon. On the Thirty, Constitution of the Athenians 34–35 says the peace terms required the "ancestral constitution" and that Theramenes brokered the appointment, which Xenophon omits and Lysias 12.71–76 uses against him. Xenophon's chronology of 404/3 is unreliable in detail.

Thucydides as historian

Thucydides states his method at 1.20–22. He distrusts tradition and poets, checks reports against one another, and writes for use, not a prize composition for one hearing but "a possession for all time" (1.22.4), because what happened will, human nature being constant, happen again in similar form. For events he claims autopsy or cross-examined testimony, admitting that eyewitnesses differ through partiality and memory. For speeches he claims to keep "as closely as possible to the general sense of what was actually said" while making speakers say what the situation seemed to demand (1.22.1). Herodotus is never named, but 1.20.3 corrects him and the whole opening answers him.

The speeches carry the analysis, placed in antithetical pairs whose arguments recur across the work. Three propositions hold it together: states are driven by fear, honour and interest (1.76.2); the strong rule wherever they can (5.105); and under plague, siege and civil war the restraints of law and language give way "as long as human nature remains the same" (3.82.2). This does not make him an amoralist: his Athens is undone less by Sparta than by its own excess and by factional ambition after Pericles (2.65).

His composition is itself a problem. He says he began writing at the outbreak, but 5.26 shows he knew the end. Ullrich's thesis of 1846, that an Archidamian War history was later reworked, began the "Thucydidean question"; most now read the work as composed and revised over decades, accepting that Book 8 and much of Book 5 are unfinished, with documents not yet turned into narrative and no speeches.

He matters partly through reception: Hobbes translated him in 1629 as the most politic of historians, and international relations theory adopted the Melian Dialogue as a founding text of realism, usually without its context; the "Thucydides trap" is a modern coinage. More durably, he set a standard of evidence and causation the discipline still measures itself by, he refused consolation, and he showed that a narrative can argue. He is also a difficult witness: exiled, aristocratic, admiring of Pericles, hostile to Cleon. Reading him well means reading him against himself.

Where sources disagree. Cornford (Thucydides Mythistoricus, 1907) saw a tragedian shaping events into hubris and nemesis; Hunter (Thucydides the Artful Reporter, 1973) argued the narrative was built to fit conclusions already drawn; Connor (Thucydides, 1984) and Rood (Thucydides: Narrative and Explanation, 1998) treat the shaping as the method itself. Loraux's "Thucydides is not a colleague" (1980) warns against reading him as a modern positivist. The reliability of the speeches remains the most contested question in the field.

Self-test

  1. What does Thucydides identify as the "truest cause" of the war, and where does he say it?
    Answer

    The growth of Athenian power and the fear this caused in Sparta; stated at 1.23.6 and restated at 1.88 and 1.118.2, with the Pentecontaetia (1.89–118) as the supporting narrative.

  2. Name the three stated grievances and the Athenian action in each.
    Answer

    Corcyra: a defensive alliance and ships that fought Corinth at Sybota (433). Potidaea: an ultimatum to demolish walls and expel Corinthian magistrates, then a siege. Megara: the Megarian Decree excluding Megarians from Attic markets and imperial harbours, whose repeal Pericles refused.

  3. What were the main elements of Pericles' war strategy, and what did Thucydides say at 2.65 about why it failed?
    Answer

    Abandon Attica, stay behind the walls, refuse land battle, use the fleet and reserve to outlast Sparta, and make no new conquests. Thucydides says his successors did the opposite, pursuing private ambition and gain, above all in Sicily.

  4. Who opposed Cleon in the Mytilenean Debate, and on what grounds did he argue against the massacre?
    Answer

    Diodotus (3.42–48). He argued from Athenian interest, not pity: the death penalty does not deter because hope and desire drive men to risk; killing the demos would deprive Athens of the party inside every city that might open its gates.

  5. Why was the surrender on Sphacteria in 425 so shocking to Greece?
    Answer

    Some 292 Lacedaemonian hoplites, about 120 of them Spartiates, surrendered rather than die (4.38–40), contradicting the Thermopylae image of Spartans who never yield; Athens then held them as hostages against invasion.

  6. Where and when did both Cleon and Brasidas die, and what was the consequence?
    Answer

    At Amphipolis in 422 (5.6–11). With the two chief proponents of war gone, Nicias and Pleistoanax negotiated the Peace of Nicias in 421 (5.16–19).

  7. Give the central proposition of the Athenians in the Melian Dialogue and the fate of Melos.
    Answer

    Justice applies only between equals in power; the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must (5.89), by a law of nature (5.105). Melos was besieged, betrayed and taken; the men were killed, women and children enslaved, and 500 Athenian colonists settled (5.116).

  8. List three decisions or events that turned the Sicilian Expedition from near-success into catastrophe.
    Answer

    Any three of: the recall of Alcibiades; Gylippus' arrival and counter-wall in 414; the failed night attack on Epipolae; Nicias' refusal to withdraw when Demosthenes urged it; the lunar eclipse delay of 27 August 413; the defeat in the Great Harbour and the retreat to the Assinarus.

  9. What was legally wrong with the Arginusae trial, and who recorded that Socrates objected?
    Answer

    The six generals were tried and condemned collectively by a single vote, contrary to the decree of Cannonus requiring individual trials; Euryptolemus argued against it (Xenophon, Hellenica 1.7). Plato (Apology 32b) and Xenophon (Memorabilia 1.1.18) record that Socrates, as a presiding prytanis, refused to put the motion.

  10. What terms did Athens accept in 404, and how did the democracy return in 403?
    Answer

    Demolition of the Long Walls and Piraeus fortifications, a fleet of twelve ships, recall of exiles, and following Spartan leadership (Xenophon, Hellenica 2.2.20). Thrasybulus' democrats from Phyle took the Piraeus and killed Critias at Munychia; King Pausanias, outflanking Lysander, brokered a reconciliation and amnesty (2.4).

Carry forward

  1. Thucydides separates stated grievances from structural cause, and the whole History is built to argue that distinction; later Greek historians, and modern readers, inherit both the method and its blind spots.
  2. The war's turning points were political as much as military: the plague and Pericles' death, the rejection of Spartan peace in 425, the recall of Alcibiades, and the Arginusae trial each show the Athenian assembly destroying its own instruments.
  3. The Melian principle, that the strong rule where they can, is tested at Syracuse and answered at Aegospotami; the fourth century begins with Sparta holding the hegemony Athens lost, Persia paying for it, and the Athenian amnesty of 403 as the model for ending civil war.
