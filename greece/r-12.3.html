01Life, Sicily and the Academy

Module 6.5 gives the outline, from the birth about 428 to the founding of the Academy. The details rest on Diogenes Laertius book 3, compiled from Hellenistic biographers, and on the Letters, whose authority is disputed. Diogenes says Plato was named Aristocles after his grandfather and called Plato for his breadth, of build or of style (3.4), that he burned his tragedies after meeting Socrates (3.5), and that after the execution he withdrew with other Socratics to Euclides at Megara (3.6).

Sicily is told from Plato's side. On the first visit, about 388/7, he won Dion, brother-in-law of Dionysius I, to philosophy (Seventh Letter 327a); Diogenes adds that the tyrant had him sold at Aegina (3.18–20), which the Letter omits. In 367 Dion summoned him to make Dionysius II a philosopher-king, and Plato went for fear of seeming to himself a man of words only (328c); within four months Dion was exiled (329c). In 361 Plato returned on the promise of Dion's recall, was held in virtual custody, and got away when Archytas of Tarentum sent a ship (350a–b). Dion took Syracuse in 357 and was murdered in 354 by the Athenian Callippus (Plutarch, Dion 54–57); the Letter insists that Dion's Athenian killers were his friends through hospitality and the Mysteries, not philosophy (333d–e). The Letter explains why Plato wrote no treatise: the thing itself is grasped only after long shared inquiry, suddenly, like light kindled from a leaping spark (341c–d); its digression (342a–344d) adds that name, account, image and even knowledge show what a thing is like rather than what it is.

Where sources disagree. Morrow (Studies in the Platonic Epistles, 1935) accepted the Seventh Letter, and Ledger's computer analysis (Re-counting Plato, 1989) found its style compatible with Plato's. Edelstein (Plato's Seventh Letter, 1966) read it as a later apologia for the Sicilian failure, and Burnyeat and Frede (The Pseudo-Platonic Seventh Letter, 2015) argued that its theory of knowledge is not Plato's. The claim that Plato withheld his deepest thought rests largely on this letter.

The Academy, a circle in module 6.5's phrase, did mathematics: Theaetetus worked on irrationals, as the dialogue named after him shows (147d–148b), and on the regular solids, and Eudoxus' concentric spheres answered the problem, which Simplicius reports Plato set, of what uniform circular motions would save the planets' appearances (Commentary on Aristotle's On the Heavens). It practised definition by division, which the comic poet Epicrates mocked with young Academics classifying a pumpkin while Plato mildly told them to start again (fr. 10, in Athenaeus 2.59c–f). It lent lawgivers to Arcadia, Elis and Pyrrha (Plutarch, Against Colotes 1126c). It had no orthodoxy: Aristotle reports that Speusippus posited a different series of substances beginning from the One (Metaphysics 1028b21–24) and that Eudoxus made pleasure the good (Nicomachean Ethics 1172b9–15). The sceptical Academy belongs to module 12.8.

02Canon and chronology

Every work antiquity cited as Plato's survives, with some that are not his. Thrasyllus arranged thirty-six works, the Letters counted as one, in nine tetralogies (Diogenes Laertius 3.56–61). Modern doubt falls on the Alcibiades I (rejected by Schleiermacher, defended by Denyer, 2001), the Hippias Major (defended by Woodruff, 1982), the Epinomis, which some gave to Philip of Opus (3.37), several minor pieces and most of the Letters.

No ancient source dates the dialogues, but Diogenes says Philip transcribed the Laws from wax tablets (3.37), and Aristotle that it was written after the Republic (Politics 1264b26–27). Lewis Campbell, introducing his edition of the Sophist and Statesman (1867), took the Laws as fixed point and found its peculiar vocabulary in those two and the Philebus, Timaeus and Critias. Dittenberger (1881) tracked particle combinations such as ti mēn, and Lutosławski (The Origin and Growth of Plato's Logic, 1897) some five hundred features, claiming a full order. What survives scrutiny, in Brandwood's survey (The Chronology of Plato's Dialogues, 1990), is three groups, not a sequence: a large first group whose internal order style cannot settle; the Republic, Parmenides, Theaetetus and Phaedrus; and the late six, which also avoid hiatus. That the short Socratic dialogues come first is inferred from content, not style.

Schleiermacher, introducing his translation from 1804, made the form the method: a planned course, opening with the Phaedrus, that leaves readers to find the answers themselves; K. F. Hermann (1839) saw the record of a mind developing from Socratic beginnings.

Where sources disagree. Unitarians hold that Plato had one philosophy and chose what to show: Shorey (The Unity of Plato's Thought, 1903), and Kahn (1996), who reads the Socratic dialogues as proleptic, preparing readers for the Phaedo and Republic. Developmentalists hold that the doctrines changed, Vlastos (1991) most sharply; module 6.5 gives his exchange with Kahn. When Owen ("The Place of the Timaeus in Plato's Dialogues", 1953) moved the Timaeus among the middle dialogues on philosophical grounds, Cherniss (1957) replied, and most still place it late.

03The Forms under attack: Parmenides and Sophist

Module 6.5 gives what a Form is, the Phaedo's sticks, the Sun and the Third Man. The dialogues never expound a theory; they use Forms as premises, as in the safe answer that beautiful things are beautiful by the Beautiful (Phaedo 100b–e). Aristotle supplies a genealogy they lack: Plato, familiar from youth with Cratylus and the Heraclitean flux of sensibles, accepted Socrates' search for definitions and concluded that definitions must be of other things, which he called Ideas (Metaphysics 987a32–b10).

The Parmenides puts the objections in the mouth of Parmenides, addressing a very young Socrates (127b–c). Are there Forms of hair, mud and dirt? Socrates denies it, uneasily (130b–e). A thing sharing a Form gets the whole or a part; Socrates' day present in many places becomes a sail spread over many people, each under a part (131a–e). After the Third Man (132a–b), Forms as thoughts fail, since a thought is of something (132b–c), and Forms as patterns regress through resemblance (132c–133a). The greatest difficulty is that Forms relate only to Forms, so knowledge itself is beyond us and the gods cannot know us (133b–134e). Yet without Forms thought has nothing to fix on and dialectic is destroyed (135b–c). Vlastos ("The Third Man Argument in the Parmenides", 1954) showed that the regress needs two premises, that the Large is itself large and that what makes things large is none of them, which are jointly inconsistent. The second part's deductions on the One (137c–166c) became Neoplatonist theology (module 12.8).

The Sophist answers the problem module 12.1 hands on. To say what is false is to say what is not, which Parmenides forbade (237a, quoting DK 28 B 7). The Eleatic visitor commits parricide (241d): complete being must include motion, life and mind (248e–249a); five greatest kinds blend selectively; and not-being is difference, not the contrary of being (257b), so "Theaetetus flies" says what differs from what is true of him (263a–b).

Where sources disagree. Ryle ("Plato's Parmenides", 1939) and Owen (1953) took the objections as real flaws Plato had found, after which the late dialogues work with kinds, not separated paradigms. Cornford (Plato and Parmenides, 1939) and Cherniss (1957) held that he never gave the Forms up, citing the Timaeus' fresh argument that if intelligence differs from true opinion there are Forms (51b–e).

04Recollection and the immortal soul

The Meno poses the paradox of inquiry: one cannot search for what one knows, nor for what one does not, since one would not recognise it (80d–e). Socrates answers from priests and Pindar (81a–c; the fragment is in module 8.4): the immortal soul has learned everything, so learning is recollection. Meno's untaught slave, asked to double a square, doubles the side, is refuted, reaches perplexity, and by questions alone sees that the square on the diagonal is double (82b–85b). The true opinions were in him, stirred up as in a dream (85c–d). Knowledge is true opinion tied down by reasoning about the cause, like Daedalus' runaway statues tethered, and that tying is recollection (97d–98a).

The Phaedo gives the proofs module 12.2 hands on. Opposites come from opposites, so the living from the dead (70c–72e); recollection (72e–77a) proves only pre-existence; the soul resembles the invisible, unchanging things it knows (78b–80b). Simmias objects that the soul may be an attunement of the body (85e–86d), Cebes that it may outlast several bodies as a weaver outlasts several cloaks and still die (87b–88b). An attunement cannot rule its instrument, as soul rules body (92a–95a). For Cebes, Socrates recounts his disappointment with Anaxagoras' Mind (97b–99c) and his second sailing, taking Forms as hypotheses (99c–100b). Then the final argument: soul brings life wherever it is, as fire brings heat, so it cannot admit death, as three cannot admit the even (102a–107b). The weak step is from deathless to imperishable, asserted at 106b–e; Socrates grants that the first hypotheses need examination (107b).

The Phaedrus argues that what always moves itself cannot cease (245c–246a), a definition of soul the Laws reuses (896a); Republic 10, that injustice, the soul's own evil, does not destroy it (608d–611a). The Phaedo's soul is intellect hindered by the body; the Republic's has parts.

05The Republic: the challenge and the answer

Module 6.5 outlines the Republic, and module 12.2 shows why Thrasymachus' defeat settles nothing. Glaucon sharpens the challenge (357b–358a): some goods are welcomed for themselves, some also for their results, like health, some only for results, like medicine. The many put justice in the third class; Socrates must show it belongs in the second. Justice, on the many's theory, is a compact of the weak (358e–359b); parents and poets, Adeimantus adds, praise only its wages (362e–367e).

The central books add the Divided Line to the Sun and Cave (509d–511e). Above the cut lie mathematical thought, which reasons from hypotheses down to conclusions with the help of diagrams, and understanding, which treats hypotheses as steps up to an unhypothetical principle and descends without images (510b–511c). Socrates will not say what that principle, the Good, is (506d–e). Philosophers must be compelled to return to the Cave, since law aims at the whole city's happiness (519c–520a), and they are just men receiving just orders (520e).

Book 9 proves the just life happier three times: the tyrannical soul is enslaved to its worst part (576c–580c); only the philosopher has tasted the pleasures of all three parts, so his verdict counts (580d–583a); and his pleasures are truer and purer, the king's life 729 times more pleasant than the tyrant's (583b–588a). Only then are the wages restored (612b–614a).

Where sources disagree. Sachs ("A Fallacy in Plato's Republic", 1963) objected that Plato proves the worth of psychic harmony, not of justice in Glaucon's sense, and never shows that the harmonious soul will refrain from theft, betrayal and oath-breaking. Vlastos ("Justice and Happiness in the Republic", 1971) answered that the text makes the link itself (442e–443b): the motives for such acts are disorders the just soul lacks. Whether that suffices, or Plato simply redefined justice, is still argued.

06Love in the Symposium and Phaedrus

Module 6.5 gives the Symposium's setting, Aristophanes, Diotima's daimon and the ascent. An argument leads to it: love desires beauty, so lacks it (199c–201c). Diotima, probably Plato's invention, defines love as desire for the good to be one's own for ever (206a), working by begetting in the beautiful, in body or soul (206b–e): lovers of honour beget laws and poems, the children of Homer, Lycurgus and Solon (208c–209e). The ascent is the purest begetting, issuing in true virtue (212a).

The Phaedrus' palinode recants the case, Lysias' and Socrates' own, for the non-lover. Divine madness gives the greatest goods: prophecy, purification, poetry and love (244a–245c). Beauty seen here recalls the Beautiful seen before birth, and the soul's wings regrow (249d–252b). Unlike the ascent, the palinode keeps the beloved: the pair grow their wings together in philosophy (256a–b). The dialogue's critique of writing is in module 6.5.

Where sources disagree. Vlastos ("The Individual as an Object of Love in Plato", 1973) charged that Platonic love loves a person only as a bearer of beauty, never as a whole individual. Nussbaum (The Fragility of Goodness, 1986) replied that Plato knew it: Alcibiades' speech of love for one irreplaceable Socrates shows what the ascent leaves out, and the Phaedrus recants in favour of the particular beloved.

07The Timaeus' cosmos

The Timaeus, read in the Latin West through Calcidius' partial translation, is Timaeus of Locri's monologue. What becomes is grasped by opinion and has a cause; the cosmos is visible, so it has become (27d–28c), and an account of a copy of an eternal model can only be a likely story (eikōs mythos, 29d). The maker, good and without envy, wished all things to be like himself (29e–30a). He made the world one living creature with soul and intellect (30b), its soul blended from Being, Sameness and Difference and divided by harmonic ratios into circles carrying stars and planets (35a–36d). Time is a moving image of eternity (37d).

Midway Timaeus starts again: the world is the work of Reason and Necessity, Reason ruling by persuasion (47e–48a). Beside model and copy stands the receptacle, nurse of becoming (49a), later called space (chōra) and grasped by a bastard reasoning (52a–b). The elements are geometry: the half-equilateral triangle builds the tetrahedron of fire, the octahedron of air and the icosahedron of water, which can change into one another; the isosceles right triangle builds earth's cube, which cannot (53c–57c).

Where sources disagree. Aristotle took the generation literally and objected that what came to be cannot be everlasting, rejecting the defence, which the commentators assign to Xenocrates, that it is told for teaching, as geometers draw figures (On the Heavens 279b32–280a1). Plutarch and Atticus read it literally, most later Platonists not. Vlastos ("The Disorderly Motion in the Timaios", 1939) defended a real pre-cosmic chaos; Cornford (Plato's Cosmology, 1937) and Tarán (1971) read a didactic myth.

08The Laws

Module 6.5 calls Magnesia a second-best city under law. The Laws, Plato's longest work and the only one without Socrates, has an Athenian Stranger, the Cretan Cleinias and the Spartan Megillus talk on the road from Cnossus to the cave of Zeus (625a–b). The best city remains the one where friends hold all in common (739a–e); Magnesia, built for human beings, has 5,040 lots, a number divisible by every number from one to ten (737e–738b), and a constitution mixing monarchy and democracy (693d–e).

Law persuades before it compels: as a free doctor explains to free patients (720a–e), each law has a preamble (722d–723b). God, not man, is the measure of all things (716c), against Protagoras (module 12.2). Book 10 legislates theology. The beliefs that gods do not exist, do not care, or can be bribed (885b) are answered, the first by the argument that soul, self-moving motion, is older than body (891e–899d); the honest unbeliever gets at least five years in a house of correction, death if he relapses (908e–909a). A Nocturnal Council guards the laws (961a–968e).

Where sources disagree. Popper (The Open Society and Its Enemies, 1945) read the Republic and Laws as a programme for a closed society, citing the rule that no one should ever be without a commander (942a–c). Levinson (In Defense of Plato, 1953) answered him point by point. Book 10's penalties remain the hardest text for Plato's defenders.

09The unwritten doctrines

Here Aristotle is the witness, and his reports must not be read into the dialogues. He mentions Plato's so-called unwritten teachings (agrapha dogmata, Physics 209b11–16) and reports what no dialogue states: the elements of the Forms are the One and the Great-and-Small; the Forms are numbers; and the objects of mathematics stand between Forms and sensibles (Metaphysics 987b14–988a17). Aristoxenus says Aristotle used to tell how hearers came to Plato's lecture On the Good expecting wealth or health, heard mathematics and at last that the Good is one, and left scornful (Elements of Harmonics 2.30–31). Simplicius says Aristotle, Speusippus and Xenocrates wrote the lecture up (Commentary on Aristotle's Physics 151.6).

The Tübingen school made this the key to Plato. Hans Joachim Krämer (Arete bei Platon und Aristoteles, 1959) and Konrad Gaiser (Platons ungeschriebene Lehre, 1963) reconstructed a system deriving Forms, numbers and world from the One, identified with the Good, and the indefinite dyad. Thomas Szlezák (Platon und die Schriftlichkeit der Philosophie, 1985) read the dialogues' deferrals, Republic 506d–e above all, as deliberate passages of reserve.

The Philebus, which module 12.1 hands on, offers a middle way. Its god-given method holds that whatever is said to be consists of one and many and contains limit and unlimitedness (16c–17a), Philolaus' pair (module 12.1); all things fall under the unlimited, limit, their mixture and the cause of mixing (23c–27c). Sayre (Plato's Late Ontology, 1983) argued that the theses of Metaphysics A are found here and in the Parmenides, the Great-and-Small being the unlimited.

Where sources disagree. Cherniss (The Riddle of the Early Academy, 1945) held that no oral system existed: Aristotle misread the dialogues through his own polemic, and the riddle is how his reports arose. Vlastos, reviewing Krämer (Gnomon, 1963), allowed some oral teaching but denied it could override the dialogues. The Tübingen side answers that Aristotle sat in the Academy for twenty years and that Aristoxenus had the lecture story from him.

10Plato's myths as method

Module 8.4 reads the judgement myths of the Gorgias, Phaedo and Republic for the afterlife. As method they share three features. Each follows argument that has done what it can: Callicles is silenced but unpersuaded, the Phaedo's hypotheses await examination (107b), and justice has been proved good in itself. Each carries a disclaimer: the Gorgias tale is a logos Callicles will take for a mythos (523a); no sensible man would insist on the Phaedo's details, but believing them is a noble risk (114d); Er's tale can save us if we are persuaded (621b–c). Each turns the afterlife into a lesson about the present soul, above all the choice of lives: aitia helomenou, theos anaitios, the responsibility is the chooser's, god is blameless (617e). Edelstein ("The Function of the Myth in Plato's Philosophy", 1949) read the myths as addressed to the parts of the soul argument does not reach. The Laws states the premise: the tale of Cadmus' sown men shows that young souls can be persuaded of anything (663e–664a).

The Phaedrus' charioteer states the principle: to say what the soul is would take a divine account, what it is like a human one (246a). It is like a winged charioteer and team; ours has one horse upright, white and led by word alone, the other crooked, dark and insolent, barely yielding to the goad (253c–254e). In the gods' procession souls glimpse the colourless, shapeless being seen only by intellect (247c); those dragged down lose their wings and fall into bodies, ranked in nine lives from philosopher to tyrant (248c–e), and a philosopher who chooses that life three times running regains his wings after three thousand years, not ten thousand (248e–249a). The Republic's three parts become a picture, and the fall explains why love can recall the Forms.

Atlantis inverts the method. Critias says Solon heard at Saïs that nine thousand years earlier an island beyond the Pillars of Heracles, larger than Libya and Asia together, attacked Europe and Asia, that ancient Athens alone defeated it, and that in one day and night the island sank (Timaeus 20d–25d). Socrates welcomes it as no fabricated myth but a true account (26e). The Critias describes the island and breaks off as Zeus summons the gods to punish its decline (108e–121c).

Where sources disagree. Proclus reports that Crantor, the first commentator on the Timaeus, took the story as history (Commentary on the Timaeus), and Strabo (2.3.6) that Posidonius thought it might not be fiction. Vidal-Naquet ("Athènes et l'Atlantide", 1964) read Atlantis as a mirror of imperial, naval Athens beaten by the land-bound city of the Republic, and Gill (1977) as fiction given the form of history.

11Self-test

  1. What fixes the late group of dialogues, and why can stylometry not order the first group?
    Answer

    Campbell (1867) took the Laws, transcribed from wax by Philip of Opus (Diogenes Laertius 3.37) and later than the Republic (Politics 1264b26–27), as fixed point and found its vocabulary in the Sophist, Statesman, Philebus, Timaeus and Critias. Style cannot settle the first group's internal order; that the short Socratic dialogues come first is inferred from content.

  2. Why does the authenticity of the Seventh Letter matter for the unwritten doctrines?
    Answer

    Its claim that Plato wrote no treatise on what matters most (341c–d) is the main written warrant for a withheld teaching. If Edelstein and Burnyeat and Frede are right that it is not Plato's, the Tübingen case rests on Aristotle's reports and on reading the dialogues' deferrals as reserve.

  3. Which two premises does Vlastos find in the Third Man, and why does the Parmenides not abandon the Forms anyway?
    Answer

    Self-predication (the Large is large) and non-identity (what makes things large is none of them), which are jointly inconsistent. Parmenides himself says that without Forms thought has nothing to fix on and dialectic is destroyed (135b–c).

  4. What does the slave-boy episode show, and what does the Meno later add about knowledge?
    Answer

    Untaught, the boy reaches the square on the diagonal by questioning alone, so the true opinions were already in him (82b–85d). Knowledge is true opinion tied down by reasoning about the cause, and that tying is recollection (97d–98a).

  5. State Cebes' objection in the Phaedo and the step in the final argument most open to challenge.
    Answer

    A soul might outlast several bodies, as a weaver outlasts several cloaks, and still perish (87b–88b). The final argument shows soul cannot admit death; that the deathless is therefore imperishable is asserted (106b–e), and Socrates grants that the hypotheses need examination (107b).

  6. Where does Socrates place justice among Glaucon's goods, and why does Sachs think the Republic's answer misses its target?
    Answer

    Among goods welcomed both for themselves and for their results (357b–358a). Sachs (1963) argued that Plato proves psychic harmony good but not that the harmonious person will refrain from ordinary injustice; Vlastos (1971) replied that 442e–443b makes the link.

  7. How does the Phaedrus treat the beloved differently from Diotima's ascent, and why does that matter to Vlastos' charge?
    Answer

    The ascent leaves the individual beloved behind; the palinode keeps lover and beloved together, growing their wings in a shared philosophical life (256a–b). Nussbaum reads this, with Alcibiades' speech, as Plato's own answer to the charge that he loves qualities, not persons.

  8. Why can fire, air and water change into one another in the Timaeus while earth cannot?
    Answer

    Their solids (tetrahedron, octahedron, icosahedron) are all built from the half-equilateral triangle, so they can change into one another; earth's cube is built from the isosceles right triangle, so it cannot become the others (53c–57c).

  9. How does the Laws combine persuasion and compulsion, and how does Book 10 apply the principle to the gods?
    Answer

    Each law has a preamble arguing for it, as a free doctor explains his treatment (720a–e, 722d–723b). Book 10 first argues that soul is older than body (891e–899d), then penalises the unpersuaded (908e–909a).

  10. What do the three judgement myths share as method, and how does Atlantis invert it?
    Answer

    Each follows an argument that has gone as far as it can, disclaims literal truth while affirming its lesson (Gorgias 523a, Phaedo 114d, Republic 621b–c), and turns the afterlife into a lesson about choosing now (617e). Atlantis is presented as a true account, not a myth (Timaeus 26e).

12Carry forward

  1. Keep two Platos apart: the Plato of the dialogues, who argues from hypotheses he leaves exposed, and the Plato Aristotle reports, with Forms as numbers derived from the One and the Great-and-Small. Every claim about "Plato's doctrine" should name the witness it rests on, a discipline module 12.4 needs for Aristotle's criticisms.
  2. The dialogues end in admitted difficulties (the Parmenides' objections, the Phaedo's unexamined hypotheses, the Good left unsaid), and later Platonists split over what that means: a system withheld, as the Tübingen school and the Neoplatonists read it, or inquiry left open, as the sceptical Academy did (module 12.8).
  3. Plato's myths are a method: placed after argument, disclaimed in detail and affirmed in lesson. The charioteer, the maker, the receptacle and the judgement of souls passed into later Platonism and Christian theology as doctrine, so ask of any Platonic image whether Plato offered it as a likeness or an argument.
