Book 1: the quarrel

The movement is a chain of refused supplications. Chryses comes with ransom for his daughter and is driven off (1.11–32); Apollo's plague follows; Calchas speaks only under Achilles' protection (1.74–100); Agamemnon, forced to give up Chryseis, takes Briseis; Achilles withdraws, and Thetis goes to Zeus. The book closes on Olympus with Hephaestus pouring wine and the gods laughing (1.595–611), a deliberate tonal drop after the human bitterness.

Achilles' first attack on Agamemnon (1.149–171) is forensic: he lists what he does and what he receives; the grievance is distributive. The second (1.225–244), with the sceptre oath, is where he stops arguing within the system and steps outside it. Nestor's mediation (1.254–284) fails because it treats the dispute as a seniority problem, which it no longer is. The epithets dios Achilleus and anax andrōn Agamemnōn set godlike excellence against institutional rank, which is exactly the quarrel. The one simile is Apollo's descent "like the night" (1.47); the sceptre "which will never again grow leaves" (1.234–237) brings in the cut tree that recurs in Book 6 and in Sarpedon's fall.

Rage. Sing, goddess, the rage of Peleus' son Achilles (1.1)
and the plan of Zeus was moving towards its end (1.5)
Wine-sack, with a dog's eyes and a deer's heart (1.225)
Mother, since you bore me to be short-lived (1.352)
Zeus nodded with his dark brows, and great Olympus shook (1.528–530)
Where sources disagree. Whether Achilles is right in Book 1 divides readers. Redfield (Nature and Culture in the Iliad) and Schein (The Mortal Hero) read his withdrawal as a legitimate protest against a broken honour economy; others, following Nestor's own verdict at 9.108–111 and Achilles' later self-reproach at 18.98–111, stress that both men are guilty. The poem itself gives no narrator's verdict.

Book 6: Hector in the city

Book 6 leaves the battlefield twice. First Diomedes and Glaucus meet, discover a hereditary guest-friendship, and exchange armour instead of blows (6.119–236). Then Hector enters Troy and meets, in order, his mother Hecuba (6.251–285), Paris and Helen (6.313–368), and Andromache with the child at the Scaean gate (6.390–502). The three encounters are graded: duty, contempt, love. Hector refuses wine from his mother because he is bloodied and must not pour to Zeus unclean; refuses to sit with Paris; refuses Andromache's plea to fight from the wall.

Glaucus's speech is the poem's great statement of mortality: the generation of leaves (6.146–149), which deliberately recalls the dead sceptre of Book 1. The homilia between Hector and Andromache is a ring: her plea, his reply, the child, her tears, his departure. Andromache's "you are father to me, and honoured mother, and brother" (6.429–430) is a woman who has already lost every other male relative to Achilles (6.414–428), a detail that makes Book 22 inevitable. Hector's reply moves from shame (aidōs) before the Trojans to a vision of Troy's fall and her enslavement, and ends in a prayer for his son that the audience knows is futile.

Hector is koruthaiolos, of the glancing helmet, and that helmet frightens Astyanax (6.467–470), so he takes it off: the formula briefly becomes a prop. Andromache is leukōlenos, white-armed, the formula for a protected woman, used again at 24.723 as she holds Hector's head.

As is the generation of leaves, so is that of men (6.146)
I would feel deep shame before the Trojans and the Trojan women with trailing robes (6.441–442)
The day will come when sacred Ilios will perish (6.448)
May men say of him one day, "He is far better than his father" (6.479)
she took him to her fragrant breast, laughing through her tears (6.483–484)
They mourned Hector in his house while he yet lived (6.500)

Book 9: the embassy

The movement is from the Achaean assembly at night (9.9–181), where Agamemnon offers gifts but no apology, through the embassy's three speeches and Achilles' three replies, back to the assembly where Diomedes dismisses the whole exercise (9.697–709). Odysseus speaks first (9.225–306) and repeats Agamemnon's catalogue of gifts word for word but silently drops Agamemnon's closing demand that Achilles submit to him as the greater man (9.160–161). Achilles' reply (9.308–429) is the longest and most disordered speech in the poem: he rejects the gifts, rejects the honour system that makes gifts meaningful (the coward and the brave man are held in one honour, 9.318–320), announces his departure, and reveals Thetis's prophecy of the two fates.

Phoenix (9.434–605) argues by paradigm: the allegory of the Prayers who follow Ruin (9.502–512) and the long story of Meleager, who withdrew in anger, refused gifts, and was finally persuaded by his wife Cleopatra when the enemy fired the city, but by then the gifts were gone. Achilles softens: he will decide at dawn whether to sail (9.618–619). Ajax (9.624–642) speaks shortest and in the plain idiom of the warrior: a man accepts blood-price even for a brother; Achilles has forgotten his philoi. Achilles yields furthest: he will stay, but not fight until Hector reaches the Myrmidons' ships (9.650–655).

Achilles' one simile, the mother bird feeding her chicks while she goes without (9.323–327), has the greatest warrior figure himself as a female bird; the image of care points forward to Patroclus.

Hateful to me as the gates of Hades is the man who hides one thing and says another (9.312–313)
My homecoming is lost, but my fame will be imperishable (9.412–413)
I count him at the worth of a splinter (9.378)
Not worth my life are all the possessions of Ilios (9.401)
as if I were some vagabond without honour (9.648)
Where sources disagree. At 9.182–198 the Greek uses dual forms ("the two of them went") although three ambassadors go. Page and the Analyst school read this as proof that Phoenix was added later to an embassy of two; Unitarians reply that Phoenix went ahead, or that the duals cover the two heralds, or that the poet counts Phoenix as one of Achilles' own household rather than an envoy. The question remains open. Separately, Nagy holds that kleos aphthiton (9.413) is an inherited Indo-European formula; Finkelberg argued it is a one-off phrase, which would make Achilles' choice of words his own.

Book 16: the Patrocleia

Patroclus comes weeping to Achilles (16.2–4, likened to a girl tugging at her mother's skirt, 16.7–11) and asks to borrow the armour. Achilles consents with a condition he repeats twice: drive them from the ships, then turn back (16.87–96). He prays to Zeus, who grants half (16.249–252). The Myrmidons go out like wasps (16.259–267). Patroclus kills Sarpedon, Zeus's son, whom Zeus considers saving and does not; the god weeps blood onto the earth (16.459–461). Patroclus forgets the condition, charges the wall three times, and is struck by Apollo, speared by Euphorbus, and finished by Hector (16.786–863).

The key speech is Patroclus's rebuke (16.21–45): Achilles is nēlees, pitiless; the grey sea and the cliffs bore him, not Peleus and Thetis. The narrator's comment is unlike anything else in the poem: "so he spoke, begging, the great fool; for it was his own death he was begging for" (16.46–47). Achilles' reply (16.49–100) is the moment where he half-chooses: he will not fight, he will not sail, he will send a proxy. His closing wish that all Achaeans and Trojans perish and only he and Patroclus survive to take Troy (16.97–100) is the ethical low point of his withdrawal.

Sarpedon falls like an oak, poplar or pine cut for ship-timber (16.482–484), the shipwright's world replacing the warrior's; the wrangle over his corpse rehearses Book 17. Patroclus's death is narrated in the second person (16.787), the narrator's only direct address to a dying character.

Peleus was not your father nor Thetis your mother; the grey sea bore you (16.33–34)
the father granted one part and refused the other (16.250)
he wept tears of blood down onto the earth (16.459)
Then, Patroclus, the end of life appeared to you (16.787)
You yourself will not live long; already death stands beside you (16.852–853)
his soul flew from his limbs to Hades, lamenting its fate, leaving manhood and youth (16.856–857)

Book 18: grief and the Shield

Antilochus brings the news; Achilles pours dust on his head and lies in it, "mighty in his mightiness" (18.26), the posture of a corpse. Thetis hears from the sea depths and comes with the Nereids, a catalogue of thirty-three names (18.39–49) that functions as a dirge. Achilles chooses death (18.90–126). Iris sends him unarmed to the trench, where Athena sets a flame on his head like a beacon from a besieged island city (18.207–214); he shouts three times and the Trojans break. The Trojan assembly follows: Polydamas urges retreat inside the walls, Hector refuses (18.243–313), the error that kills him. Then Hephaestus's forge, the Shield, and dawn.

Achilles' speech to Thetis is the pivot of the poem. He wishes Briseis had died rather than cause the quarrel (18.86–87); he calls himself a useless weight on the earth; he accepts the fate revealed in Book 9 as already chosen. The Meleager paradigm has come true: he returns, and the gifts no longer matter. Thetis's dysaristotokeia (18.54) is a word coined for the line.

The Shield (18.478–608) is the poem's largest simile in effect though not in form. Its rings are: cosmos (earth, sea, sky, sun, moon, constellations, 18.483–489); the city at peace with a wedding and a lawsuit over a blood-price (18.490–508); the city at war, besieged, with an ambush and a cattle-raid (18.509–540); ploughing, reaping, vintage (18.541–572); cattle attacked by lions (18.573–586); a sheepfold (18.587–589); a dance-floor like the one Daedalus made for Ariadne (18.590–606); Ocean around the rim (18.607–608).

unhappy mother of the best of men (18.54)
Let me die at once, since I was not to help my companion as he was killed (18.98–99)
I sit by the ships, a useless burden on the earth (18.104)
Not even mighty Heracles escaped death (18.117)
On it he made the earth, the sky, the sea, the untiring sun (18.483–484)
Where sources disagree. Lines 18.535–538, personifying Strife, Tumult and Death on the Shield, are nearly identical to Hesiod, Shield of Heracles 156–159, and were athetised by Alexandrian critics as an interpolation; most modern editors print them with brackets or a note. The lawsuit at 18.497–508 is also unclear: whether the two talents go to the judge who speaks straightest or to the winning litigant, and whether the defendant is offering a blood-price or refusing one, cannot be settled from the Greek.

Book 22: the death of Hector

Hector alone remains outside the wall. Priam (22.38–76) and Hecuba (22.79–89) plead; he deliberates (22.99–130), considers surrender, rejects it, and runs when Achilles arrives. Three circuits of the city, Zeus's scales (22.209–213), Athena disguised as Deiphobus, the duel, the prophecy, the mutilation, and three laments from the wall by Priam, Hecuba and Andromache.

Hector's monologue is the Trojan counterpart to Achilles' speech in Book 9: a hero reasoning his way through alternatives and ending up where honour requires. He imagines Polydamas's reproach (22.100), repeats his Book 6 shame formula word for word (22.105 = 6.442), fantasises surrendering Helen and half the city's wealth, and knows Achilles would kill him anyway. His last proposal to Achilles, a pact over the corpses (22.254–259), is refused in the poem's most savage speech: no oaths between lions and men (22.262–267). Hector dying asks only for burial; Achilles wishes he could eat him raw (22.346–348).

The similes are the richest in the poem and all drawn from peace or sport. Achilles approaching is the Dog Star, brightest and most baneful (22.26–32); his spear-point the evening star (22.317–319). The chase is a hawk and a dove (22.139–142), then horses racing round turning posts for a tripod or a woman as prize, "but they ran for the life of Hector" (22.162–166), then the dream in which pursuer never catches pursued (22.199–201). Andromache, hearing the cry, drops her shuttle: she had been weaving and heating Hector's bath (22.440–446). She runs out "like a maenad" (22.460) and throws off the headdress Aphrodite gave her on her wedding day (22.468–472).

No way now to talk sweetly with him, as a young man and a girl talk (22.126–128)
Let me not die without a struggle and without glory (22.304–305)
There are no trustworthy oaths between lions and men (22.262)
Paris and Phoebus Apollo will destroy you at the Scaean gates (22.359–360)
I will accept my death whenever Zeus and the other gods wish to accomplish it (22.365–366)
as if all Troy were burning from its summit (22.410–411)

Book 24: the ransom

Achilles cannot sleep and drags Hector's corpse round Patroclus's tomb each dawn; Apollo preserves the body. On the twelfth day Apollo protests to the gods (24.33–54): Achilles has destroyed pity and has no shame. Zeus sends Thetis to Achilles and Iris to Priam. Priam loads the ransom, quarrels with his sons, is escorted by Hermes disguised as a young Myrmidon, and enters Achilles' hut unannounced. He clasps Achilles' knees and kisses his hands (24.477–479). They weep together; Achilles speaks of the two jars of Zeus; a flash of the old anger (24.559–570); they eat; they sleep. Hector's body goes home and three women lament him: Andromache, Hecuba, Helen.

Priam's speech (24.486–506) is built on one word: remember your father. It is Phoenix's technique, argument by substitution, but the paradigm is now a living old man in the room. Achilles' reply (24.518–551) is the only place where he generalises about the human condition, with his own father's grief beside Priam's. With Niobe (24.602–617) Achilles uses the mythological paradigm himself, as Phoenix did to him, to persuade Priam to eat. Helen's lament (24.762–775), that Hector alone never spoke a harsh word to her, ends the human voices on gentleness. The one great simile is Priam's entrance: like a killer who comes as a suppliant to a rich man's house, so that amazement seizes those who see him (24.480–484); the roles are inverted, the killer is the host.

Achilles has destroyed pity, and has no shame (24.44–45)
Remember your father, godlike Achilles (24.486)
I have reached out my hand to the face of the man who killed my sons (24.506)
The gods have spun it so that wretched mortals live in grief, while they are free of care (24.525–526)
Now let us think of food. Even Niobe remembered to eat (24.601–602)
Thus they held the funeral of Hector, tamer of horses (24.804)
Where sources disagree. Nineteenth-century Analysts argued Book 24 was a late addition, pointing to its Odyssean vocabulary and to Hermes' escort. Whitman's Homer and the Heroic Tradition (1958) answered with the geometric argument: 24 answers 1 so precisely that the poem is unthinkable without it, and almost all scholars now treat it as integral. Whether its vision of shared grief amounts to a reconciliation or only a truce is still debated; the final line looks past the funeral to Troy's fall.

How the seven books speak to each other

Book 24 answers Book 1

Both open with an old father bringing ransom for a child; Chryses is refused and Priam accepted. Apollo opens both books (1.9, 24.33). Nine days of plague (1.53) answer nine days of mourning (24.664, 24.784); twelve days of divine absence (1.425) answer twelve days of Hector unburied (24.31). Thetis goes up to Zeus in 1 and comes down from him in 24. The quarrel over a prize of honour becomes a shared meal. Whitman called this geometric symmetry; Taplin (Homeric Soundings) prefers to see three movements, 1–9, 11–18, 19–24, each ending at night.

Book 6 sets up Book 22

Everything Andromache loses in 22 was shown in 6: the shame formula (6.442 = 22.105), the Scaean gate, the child, the vision of her slavery (6.454–465) which her lament repeats as fact (22.484–507). The maids who mourned Hector alive at 6.500 mourn him dead. In 6 Hector removes the helmet to comfort the child; in 22 the helmet flashes as he runs. He never comes home between the two books.

The three speeches of Book 9 and Achilles' three choices

Odysseus offers compensation; Achilles rejects it and declares he will sail. Phoenix offers the Meleager paradigm; Achilles wavers and in effect becomes Meleager, returning too late, in 18, when the gifts are worthless. Ajax appeals to the companions; Achilles concedes the condition that is fulfilled to the letter in 16, Hector at the Myrmidons' ships. The three answers map onto the three choices still ahead: send Patroclus (16), return for Patroclus (18), release Hector (24). Several scholars, Nagy among them, have noted that Cleopatra, the wife who moves Meleager, inverts the name Patroclus.

Book 16 mirrors Book 22

Both deaths begin with a warning ignored (Achilles to Patroclus, Priam to Hector). Both are killed by a god's intervention first, Apollo's blow and Athena's deceit, then by a man. Both victims prophesy their killer's death. The two lines for the departing soul are identical (16.856–857 = 22.362–363). Each killer answers the corpse: Hector refuses the prophecy (16.859–861), Achilles accepts it (22.365–366), and that difference is the difference between them. Hector dies wearing Achilles' own armour, which is why Achilles knows where the gap at the throat is (22.322–325).

The Shield and the world outside

The Shield gives the Iliad its missing half: harvest, law, marriage, dance, the whole seasonal and civic order that war interrupts. The lawsuit over a blood-price anticipates Ajax's argument and Priam's ransom; the besieged city is Troy seen from outside; the dancing-floor recalls Andromache's wedding veil. Taplin's reading is that the similes do piecemeal what the Shield does whole, keeping peace present in a poem of killing.

The translators

Lattimore (1951) uses a loose six-beat line, keeps every epithet and repetition, and translates mēnis as "anger"; he is closest to the Greek word order and still the standard for study, at the cost of some stiffness. Fagles (1990) opens "Rage—Goddess, sing the rage", expands freely, varies formulas, and gives the speeches theatrical energy; line numbers do not match the Greek. Lombardo (1997) is the most colloquial, sets each simile off in italics as a separate stanza, and was built for performance; purists object to its slang. Alexander (2015) is line-for-line and as faithful as Lattimore in modern diction, with a preference for "wrath"; the best choice for close reading alongside the Greek line numbers given here. Wilson (2023) writes strict iambic pentameter, which forces a longer poem than the original, flattens archaism, renders epithets variably by context, and translates the opening as "cataclysmic wrath". The disagreement is not about accuracy but about which Homeric quality to keep: formulaic repetition (Lattimore, Alexander), speed and voice (Fagles, Lombardo), or English metre (Wilson).

Where sources disagree. Translators split on whether the repeated formulas are meaning-bearing. Parry's oral-formulaic theory implies many epithets are metrical filler, which licenses Fagles, Lombardo and Wilson to vary or drop them. Lattimore and Alexander hold that the repetitions are heard as echoes, and the mirrored lines of Books 16 and 22 are strong evidence for that view.

Reading the remaining seventeen books

Read Books 2 to 5 fast: the Catalogue (2.484–877) only for names; Helen on the wall (3.121–244); Diomedes' aristeia (5) as the model Achilles will exceed. Skim 7 and 8. Book 10, the night raid, is probably a later insertion and can be skipped. In 11 read Nestor to Patroclus (11.655–803), which sets the proxy plan in motion; in 12 Sarpedon on why princes fight (12.310–328). In the long middle of 13 to 15 read the Deception of Zeus (14.153–351) and Zeus's plot summary at 15.59–71. In 17 read Zeus pitying the immortal horses (17.426–447); in 19 Briseis's lament (19.282–300) and the horse Xanthus speaking (19.404–417); skim 20; in 21 Achilles and Lycaon (21.34–135), the suppliant he kills, which 24 reverses; in 23 Patroclus's ghost (23.65–107) and Achilles' grace at the games, the first sign of the man Priam will meet. With the seven held, each passage locks in as a rung between them.

Self-test

  1. Which two refused or accepted ransoms frame the poem, and in which books?
    Answer

    Chryses' ransom for his daughter is refused by Agamemnon in Book 1; Priam's ransom for Hector is accepted by Achilles in Book 24.

  2. What formula does Hector repeat word for word in Books 6 and 22, and what does it express?
    Answer

    "I would feel shame before the Trojans and the Trojan women with trailing robes" (6.442 = 22.105); it expresses aidōs, the shame before one's community that overrides family pleas.

  3. What does Odysseus omit from Agamemnon's offer when he repeats it to Achilles?
    Answer

    Agamemnon's demand that Achilles submit to him as the greater and elder man (9.160–161).

  4. In what way does Achilles become Meleager?
    Answer

    Like Meleager he withdraws in anger, refuses gifts, and returns only when his own people are desperate, so the gifts no longer matter; Patroclus plays the role of Cleopatra.

  5. Which lines are identical in Books 16 and 22, and what do they describe?
    Answer

    16.856–857 and 22.362–363, the soul leaving the limbs and flying to Hades, lamenting its fate and leaving manhood and youth.

  6. How do Hector and Achilles respond differently to the dying prophecies they receive?
    Answer

    Hector denies that he need die (16.859–861); Achilles accepts his death whenever the gods will it (22.365–366).

  7. Name the main scenes on the Shield of Achilles in order.
    Answer

    Cosmos; city at peace (wedding, lawsuit); city at war (siege, ambush); ploughing, reaping, vintage; cattle and lions; sheepfold; dance; Ocean round the rim (18.483–608).

  8. What is the sceptre oath of Book 1 and what later image does it anticipate?
    Answer

    Achilles swears by the sceptre that will never again grow leaves (1.234–244); it anticipates Glaucus's generation of leaves (6.146–149) and the felled-tree simile for Sarpedon (16.482–484).

  9. Why can Achilles find the vulnerable spot on Hector so precisely in Book 22?
    Answer

    Hector is wearing the armour stripped from Patroclus, which is Achilles' own, so Achilles knows the gap at the collarbone (22.322–325).

  10. What was Andromache doing when she heard the cry from the wall?
    Answer

    Weaving a purple cloak and ordering a hot bath for Hector's return (22.440–446).

Carry forward

  1. The Iliad is built on ring composition and verbal echo: identical lines across books (6.442 and 22.105; 16.856 and 22.362) are deliberate signals, and any translation that varies formulas for elegance hides the structure.
  2. Achilles' arc is a sequence of choices about compensation, from refusing gifts in Book 9 to accepting Priam's in Book 24; the poem's ethics are worked out through what can and cannot be exchanged for a life, which Greek tragedy and Plato's Socrates will both take up.
  3. The similes and the Shield keep the world of peace present inside a poem of war; this Homeric habit of placing the heroic against the ordinary is the model for later Greek thinking about the limits of glory.
