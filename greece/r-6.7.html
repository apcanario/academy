01The sources problem

Everything we read about Alexander was written more than two and a half centuries after his death. The contemporary accounts are lost: Callisthenes of Olynthus, the official historian who died in the army's custody in 327 or, by Chares' account, in 326/325; Ptolemy son of Lagus, writing as king of Egypt late in life; Aristobulus of Cassandreia, an engineer who began his history after 301 aged eighty-four (Lucian, Macrobii 22); Cleitarchus of Alexandria, whose colourful narrative became the most popular; Nearchus the admiral; Chares the chamberlain; and the Ephemerides, or Royal Diaries, whose authenticity is contested. What survives quarried these.

Arrian names his method: where Ptolemy and Aristobulus agree he follows them; where they differ he chooses the more plausible. Diodorus, Curtius and Justin draw mainly on Cleitarchus, hence "the vulgate tradition". The only strictly contemporary evidence is a handful of inscriptions, coins, and the Babylonian astronomical diaries, which fix the eclipse before Gaugamela and the day of his death.

Where sources disagree. Cleitarchus was long dated to c. 310–300 BC, almost a contemporary. A papyrus published in 2007 (P.Oxy. LXXI 4808) calls him tutor to Ptolemy IV, which would push him into the mid-third century and weaken the vulgate's independent authority. Curtius's date is equally open; both questions are argued in full in module 11.7.

02Upbringing: Olympias, Philip, Aristotle

Alexander was born at Pella in July 356, the son of Philip II and Olympias, a Molossian princess of Epirus who claimed descent from Achilles (Plutarch, Alexander 2–3). Philip was turning Macedon into the dominant power in Greece, and his many marriages, seven by Satyrus's count in Athenaeus (13.557b–e), bred court factions in which succession was never automatic.

His tutors were Leonidas, an austere kinsman of Olympias, and Lysimachus of Acarnania, who cast the boy as Achilles (Plutarch, Alexander 5). From 343 to about 340 Aristotle taught him and a group of noble sons at Mieza (Plutarch, Alexander 7). What he taught is barely recoverable: Plutarch says Alexander loved medicine and kept Aristotle's recension of the Iliad under his pillow; the two later cooled, perhaps over Callisthenes, Aristotle's kinsman. Aristotle's advice to treat barbarians as a master treats slaves (Strabo 1.4.9) is quoted mainly to show Alexander ignoring it.

The rivalry with Philip shaped him. Alexander was regent at sixteen and led the Companion cavalry at Chaeronea in 338 (Diodorus 16.86). In 337 Philip married Cleopatra, niece of the general Attalus. At the wedding Attalus prayed for a legitimate heir; Alexander threw a cup at him, and Philip, drawing his sword, stumbled drunk, earning the jibe that the man who would cross to Asia could not cross from one couch to another (Plutarch, Alexander 9; Justin 9.7). Alexander and Olympias withdrew to Epirus and Illyria; a return was brokered, but the Pixodarus affair of 336, when Alexander secretly offered himself to the Carian dynast's daughter, shows he feared being set aside (Plutarch, Alexander 10). The sources paint Olympias as a scheming queen and her real influence is hard to measure; but Alexander's only recorded family quarrel was over Philip's new wife.

03Accession and the destruction of Thebes

In October 336 at Aegae Philip was stabbed by Pausanias of Orestis, a bodyguard with a grievance against Attalus (Diodorus 16.93–94; Aristotle, Politics 1311b). Alexander, twenty, was acclaimed by the army. He then removed Amyntas IV, two Lyncestian princes and Attalus; Olympias is said to have killed Cleopatra and her infant (Justin 9.7; Pausanias 8.7.7).

Where sources disagree. Diodorus gives Pausanias a private motive. Plutarch (Alexander 10) says suspicion fell on Olympias and even on Alexander; Justin (9.7) states that Olympias arranged the murder and crowned the assassin's corpse. Alexander himself blamed Persia (Arrian 2.14.5). Badian and Bosworth find complicity plausible but unprovable; Hammond rejects it. Uncertain.

A rapid tour of Greece renewed the League of Corinth and Alexander's position as hegemon. In 335, while he campaigned in Thrace and Illyria, a rumour of his death set Thebes against the Macedonian garrison on the Cadmea. He appeared before Thebes in under a fortnight, before the city knew he was alive (Arrian 1.7). The city was stormed, allegedly after Perdiccas attacked without orders (Arrian 1.8, from Ptolemy; Diodorus 17.12 says Alexander planned it). About 6,000 Thebans were killed and 30,000 sold; the city was razed except for the temples and Pindar's house (Arrian 1.9; Diodorus 17.14). Arrian stresses that the formal decision was taken by the League allies, Plataeans, Orchomenians, Phocians and Thespians, with old scores to settle. Screen or not, no Greek city rose again until he was dead.

04From the Granicus to Siwa, 334–331

Alexander crossed the Hellespont in spring 334 with about 32,000 foot and 5,000 horse, leaving Antipater as regent (Arrian 1.11; Diodorus 17.17). He sacrificed at Troy and took a shield from Athena's temple. The satraps of Asia Minor met him at the Granicus in May. Arrian (1.13–16) has him attack across the river against Parmenion's advice; Spithridates struck his helmet with an axe and Cleitus the Black severed the Persian's arm (1.15.8), a debt with a long shadow. The Greek mercenaries in Persian service were massacred as traitors to the League.

Where sources disagree. Diodorus (17.19) has Alexander cross at dawn unopposed and fight on the far bank; Arrian, from Ptolemy, has the Companions fighting their way up a muddy bank in the afternoon. Some modern scholars prefer Diodorus as tactically more credible. The two cannot be reconciled.

Sardis, Miletus and Halicarnassus fell; the fleet was disbanded on the gamble that the Persian navy could be beaten by taking its harbours. At Gordium in 333 he cut, or according to Aristobulus unpinned, the knot of Midas's cart (Arrian 2.3). In November 333 Darius III brought the royal army behind him to the narrow plain of Issus. Alexander broke through on the right to the royal chariot; Darius fled, and his mother, wife and children were taken in the camp (Arrian 2.6–11; Curtius 3.8–12). Darius's offer of the lands west of the Euphrates was refused; Alexander's reply demanded to be addressed as king of Asia (Arrian 2.14).

Tyre, on its island, refused him entry to sacrifice to Melqart-Heracles. The siege lasted seven months of 332: a mole from the mainland, Phoenician and Cypriot fleets that came over after Issus, siege towers on ships, a breach on the south wall (Arrian 2.18–24; Diodorus 17.40–46). About 8,000 Tyrians died and 30,000 were sold; Curtius (4.4.17) adds 2,000 crucified along the shore. Gaza followed, and Curtius (4.6.29) alone says its governor Batis was dragged behind a chariot in imitation of Achilles.

Egypt submitted without a fight in late 332. Alexander sacrificed to Apis at Memphis and in early 331 founded Alexandria. The journey to the oracle of Ammon at Siwa is the campaign's most argued episode. Arrian (3.3–4) says he went to rival Perseus and Heracles and received the answer he wanted, unspecified. Plutarch (Alexander 27) reports that the priest greeted him as son of Zeus, or, in a rationalising variant, mispronounced paidion as pai Dios. Callisthenes, quoted by Strabo (17.1.43), gave the official line: the god acknowledged him as his son. From Siwa onward divine paternity is part of his repertoire.

05Gaugamela, Babylon, Persepolis and the death of Darius, 331–330

A lunar eclipse on 20 September 331, recorded in the Babylonian diary and by Arrian (3.7.6), fixes Gaugamela, near Arbela, to 1 October. Darius had levelled the ground for scythed chariots and had a large numerical advantage. Alexander advanced obliquely to the right, drew the Persian left after him and charged the gap that opened when Darius's cavalry followed; Darius fled a second time (Arrian 3.11–15). On the left Parmenion was hard pressed, and Arrian has a message from him recall Alexander from the pursuit, an incident Callisthenes used against Parmenion (Plutarch, Alexander 33).

Babylon opened its gates under the satrap Mazaeus, whom Alexander kept in office with Macedonian officers beside him, the first clear instance of retaining Iranians (Arrian 3.16). Susa and its treasury followed. In January 330 he forced the Persian Gates and reached Persepolis, which was given to the troops to plunder (Diodorus 17.70). Some months later the palace of Xerxes burned.

Where sources disagree. Arrian (3.18.11–12) makes the fire deliberate policy, revenge for Xerxes' burning of Athens, taken over Parmenion's objection. Diodorus (17.72), Curtius (5.7) and Plutarch (Alexander 38) describe a drunken revel in which the Athenian courtesan Thais urged him on. The treasury had been emptied first. Badian saw a political signal to Greece that the war of revenge was over; Bosworth thought a sober decision could hide behind the party; Hammond followed Arrian. Plutarch says Alexander soon regretted it.

Darius retreated east from Ecbatana. Near Hecatompylos in July 330 the Bactrian satrap Bessus arrested him and, as the Macedonians closed in, stabbed him and left him by the road (Arrian 3.21; Curtius 5.8–13). Alexander sent the body to Persepolis for royal burial and thereafter behaved as Darius's successor, adopting the diadem and the striped tunic while refusing the upright tiara and trousers (Diodorus 17.77; Plutarch, Alexander 45). The League allies were sent home with bonuses (at Ecbatana in Arrian 3.19.5, after Darius's death in Diodorus 17.74.3): the Hellenic war of revenge was formally over, and what followed was conquest on his own account.

06Bactria and Sogdiana: Philotas, Cleitus, proskynesis, the pages, 330–327

The three north-eastern years were the hardest of the war and the most corrosive for the high command. In autumn 330 at Phrada a minor plot by one Dimnus was reported to Philotas, commander of the Companion cavalry and son of Parmenion, who sat on it for two days. Arrian (3.26) gives the bare account; Curtius (6.7–11) and Plutarch (Alexander 48–49) give the long version, with torture, a trial before the army and death by javelins. Alexander then sent riders to Ecbatana with orders to kill Parmenion, who controlled the treasury and the communications (Arrian 3.26.3–4). Philotas's guilt beyond arrogance and silence is unknowable; the effect is clear. The affair destroyed the most powerful family in the army, ended the collegial command inherited from Philip, and split the Companion cavalry between Hephaestion and Cleitus.

In 329 Alexander crossed the Hindu Kush and had Bessus delivered to him; Arrian (3.29–30) notes that Ptolemy claimed the capture and Aristobulus credited Spitamenes. A massacre at Cyropolis and the founding of Alexandria Eschate on the Jaxartes provoked a Sogdian revolt under Spitamenes that destroyed a Macedonian column at the Polytimetus (Arrian 4.5–6). Two years of garrisons and reprisals followed; Spitamenes was killed in 328, by the Massagetae in Arrian (4.17), by his own wife in Curtius (8.3).

At Maracanda in autumn 328, at a drinking party, Cleitus the Black, provoked by flattery of Alexander at Philip's expense, taunted the king with the hand that had saved him at the Granicus and was run through by Alexander with a guard's spear (Arrian 4.8–9; Plutarch, Alexander 50–51; Curtius 8.1–2). All record three days of remorse; Arrian adds that the philosopher Anaxarchus consoled him with the doctrine that whatever a king does is just. Cleitus had just been named satrap of Bactria, which is to say removed from the centre.

At Bactra in spring 327 Alexander tried to introduce proskynesis, the Persian obeisance before the king, among Macedonians and Greeks; to Greeks it implied worship. Arrian (4.10–12) gives a staged debate in which Anaxarchus proposed it and Callisthenes, who had himself written that Alexander was son of Zeus, refused on the grounds that gods and men are honoured differently; Chares, in Plutarch (Alexander 54), has guests bow and receive a kiss, and Callisthenes skip the bow and lose the kiss. The experiment was dropped for Macedonians. Soon afterwards the page Hermolaus, flogged for killing a boar before the king, plotted with other royal pages to kill Alexander in his sleep; it failed because he drank until dawn (Arrian 4.13–14; Curtius 8.6–8). The pages were stoned, and Callisthenes was arrested as their teacher.

Where sources disagree. Arrian (4.14.3) records that Ptolemy said Callisthenes was tortured and hanged, while Aristobulus said he was carried about in chains and died of disease. Curtius (8.8.21) and Chares in Plutarch (Alexander 55) favour imprisonment. If the two best-placed eyewitnesses could not agree on this, the limits of the whole tradition are plain; module 11.7 weighs the versions.

In the same season he took the Sogdian Rock and married Roxane, daughter of the Bactrian noble Oxyartes. Arrian (4.19) and Plutarch (Alexander 47) call it love; Curtius (8.4) stages a banquet. The political reading is obvious: the marriage ended the war by binding the Bactrian aristocracy. That Alexander's first marriage, at twenty-eight, was to an Iranian was not lost on the army.

07India, the Hyphasis and the Gedrosian desert, 327–325

Alexander entered India in 327 with an army swollen by Iranian cavalry. Taxiles submitted and asked for help against Porus across the Hydaspes (Jhelum). In May 326, in the monsoon, Alexander crossed upstream at night by a wooded island, leaving Craterus opposite the Indian camp, and defeated Porus's elephants in the only set battle of the campaign (Arrian 5.8–19; Curtius 8.13–14; Plutarch, Alexander 60). Porus asked to be treated "as a king" and was restored and enlarged. Bucephalas died after the battle.

At the Hyphasis (Beas) in late summer the army refused to go on. Arrian (5.25–29) gives Alexander a speech on glory and the end of Asia, answered by Coenus for men who wanted to see their homes; Alexander sulked for three days, took unfavourable omens and turned back, building twelve altars on the bank (Diodorus 17.93–95; Curtius 9.2–3). Some scholars, notably Spann, argue that Alexander, knowing of the Nanda power on the Ganges, wanted the retreat and let the army take the blame; most accept a genuine refusal. It was the first time anything had stopped him, and it was his own army.

The voyage down the Indus became a war of extermination against the Malli. At a Mallian town Alexander mounted the wall almost alone and took an arrow in the chest (Arrian 6.9–11). Arrian (6.11.8) notes that Cleitarchus placed Ptolemy at his side, and that Ptolemy himself wrote that he was elsewhere, a neat illustration of how the vulgate improved on the record. At Patala in summer 325 the force divided: Nearchus took the fleet along the coast, Craterus led the veterans by the Bolan pass, and Alexander marched with the rest through Gedrosia (the Makran) to dig wells for the fleet.

The sixty-day desert crossing in autumn 325 killed more of his people than any battle: sand, heat, night marches, a flash flood that swept away the camp followers (Arrian 6.22–26). Strabo (15.2.5) says Alexander undertook it to outdo Semiramis and Cyrus, who had failed there. Plutarch (Alexander 66) says only a quarter survived, which cannot be right since most of the army was not with him; the losses are uncertain. Badian read the executions of satraps and generals in 325–324, including Cleander who had killed Parmenion, as a "reign of terror" against men who had assumed he would not return; Bosworth saw genuine misconduct under a king restoring control. The treasurer Harpalus fled with 5,000 talents and 6,000 mercenaries (Diodorus 17.108.6) and reached Athens with about 700 of them.

08Susa, Opis, Hephaestion and Babylon, 324–323

At Susa in spring 324 Alexander held the mass marriage. He took Stateira, elder daughter of Darius, and Parysatis, daughter of Artaxerxes III, marrying into both branches of the Achaemenid house, while Hephaestion married Stateira's sister (Arrian 7.4). Some eighty Companions received Iranian noblewomen in a five-day Persian ceremony described by Chares (Athenaeus 12.538b–539a); about ten thousand soldiers' unions with Asian women were registered and dowered. He also paid the army's debts and received the 30,000 Epigoni, Iranian youths trained in Macedonian arms (Arrian 7.6; Diodorus 17.108).

The Macedonian response came at Opis on the Tigris that summer. When Alexander announced that the old and unfit would go home with Craterus, the whole army took it as dismissal in favour of Persians and jeered at the son of Ammon. He had thirteen ringleaders executed, delivered the speech Arrian (7.9–10) wrote for him, shut himself away, and began giving Persians Macedonian commands and the title of kinsmen. The men broke. At the reconciliation banquet he prayed for "concord and partnership in rule between Macedonians and Persians" (Arrian 7.11.9). Ten thousand veterans left with Craterus, who was to replace Antipater. In the same year the Exiles' Decree ordered Greek cities to readmit their exiles, breaching the League's autonomy clause (Diodorus 18.8), and cities debated divine honours; Demosthenes said Alexander could be son of Zeus and Poseidon too, if he liked (Hyperides, Against Demosthenes col. 31).

At Ecbatana in October 324 Hephaestion died after a week of fever (Arrian 7.14; Plutarch, Alexander 72). Alexander's grief is drawn in Achillean terms: he lay on the body, cut his hair, crucified the doctor, ordered a pyre at Babylon costing 10,000 talents (Arrian 7.14.8; Plutarch, Alexander 72) or more than 12,000 (Diodorus 17.115) and obtained Ammon's sanction for a hero cult (Arrian 7.14.7, 7.23.6).

Alexander entered Babylon in spring 323 despite a Chaldaean warning (Arrian 7.16–17), received embassies from the Mediterranean and prepared an Arabian expedition. After a long party at the house of Medius he fell ill with fever. The Ephemerides, quoted by Arrian (7.25–26) and Plutarch (Alexander 76), record about eleven days of worsening illness and the army filing past his bed while he could still move his eyes. The Babylonian diary places his death on 29 Aiaru, 11 June 323. He was thirty-two.

Where sources disagree. Diodorus (17.117) and Curtius (10.5) have Alexander hand his ring to Perdiccas and leave the kingdom "to the strongest"; Arrian and Plutarch, following the Diaries, give him no last words and dismiss the story that Antipater's son Iollas poisoned him (Arrian 7.27; Plutarch, Alexander 77). Whether the Diaries are genuine or a propaganda document from the succession struggle (Bosworth) is argued in module 11.7; the Antipater case in module 11.5. Modern diagnoses include malaria, typhoid, West Nile virus, pancreatitis and white hellebore. None can be tested.

09His aims as historians have read them

The nineteenth century, led by J. G. Droysen, made Alexander the founder of Hellenism, the fusion of Greek and Oriental that prepared the ground for Christianity. W. W. Tarn's Alexander the Great (1948) took the line furthest: Alexander was the first to conceive the brotherhood of man, and the proof was the Opis prayer, the Susa marriages, the Iranian satraps and Plutarch's essay On the Fortune of Alexander (329c–d), which has him mixing the lives of men "as in a loving cup". Tarn dismissed the "last plans" in Diodorus 18.4, with their population exchanges between Europe and Asia, as a forgery, because they did not fit.

Ernst Badian's "Alexander the Great and the Unity of Mankind" (1958) dismantled Tarn source by source: the Opis prayer concerns Macedonians and Persians sharing rule, not mankind; Plutarch's essay is a rhetorical exercise; the marriages created a ruling caste, not a fused people. In "The Loneliness of Power" (1962) Badian drew the portrait that has dominated since: a king who destroyed Parmenion, Philotas, Cleitus and Callisthenes because they were in his way, and who ended his life isolated and feared. A. B. Bosworth's Conquest and Empire (1988) and Alexander and the East (1996), which sets the Indian campaign beside the Spanish conquest of Mexico, confirmed the realist reading with closer attention to body counts. Peter Green and Ian Worthington pushed the hostile reading further; N. G. L. Hammond and Robin Lane Fox defended a man of genuine vision shaped by Homer and Macedonian kingship. The present consensus is close to Bosworth: Alexander aimed at conquest and the kingship of Asia for its own sake, used Iranian nobles because he needed them, and claimed divinity because it worked.

10Immediate legacy

Alexander left no adult heir: Roxane was pregnant and his half-brother Arrhidaeus was mentally incapable. The Babylon settlement (Curtius 10.6–10; Diodorus 18.2; Justin 13.2–4) made Arrhidaeus king as Philip III, added Roxane's son as Alexander IV, and gave Perdiccas the regency after infantry and cavalry had nearly fought over the corpse. The satrapies went to the marshals: Ptolemy took Egypt, Antigonus Phrygia, Lysimachus Thrace, Antipater Macedon. News of his death set off the Lamian War; Antipater crushed Athens in 322. Olympias was killed by Cassander in 316, Roxane and Alexander IV in 310 or 309; by 306–305 the surviving generals wore diadems. Ptolemy hijacked the funeral cortege in 321 and buried the body in Egypt.

The durable legacies were structural rather than ideological: Greek-speaking dynasties over the Persian empire for two to three centuries; a scatter of Alexandrias, most of them garrison towns; an Attic-weight silver coinage circulating from Macedon to India; the koine that would carry the Septuagint and the Gospels; and the Persian hoards released into the Mediterranean economy. Culturally he became the measure of kingship and ambition, imitated by Pompey, Caesar and Trajan, and in the Alexander Romance an everyman's hero across Europe and Asia. The historiographical legacy is the one this module began with: a man known almost entirely through the quarrels of those who claimed to have known him.

11Self-test

  1. Which two lost historians does Arrian name as his principal sources, and how does he use them?
    Answer

    Ptolemy son of Lagus and Aristobulus of Cassandreia (Arrian 1 pref.). Where they agree he follows them; where they differ he chooses the more plausible.

  2. What happened at the wedding of Philip and Cleopatra in 337, and what did it reveal?
    Answer

    Attalus prayed for a legitimate heir; Alexander threw a cup at him and Philip drew his sword and stumbled drunk (Plutarch, Alexander 9). It showed Alexander's succession was not secure and depended on his and Olympias's standing.

  3. According to Arrian, who formally decided the fate of Thebes in 335?
    Answer

    The League of Corinth allies, in particular the Plataeans, Orchomenians, Phocians and Thespians, old enemies of Thebes (Arrian 1.9). Alexander could present the destruction as their decision.

  4. What debt did Alexander owe Cleitus the Black, and where did he repay it?
    Answer

    Cleitus severed the arm of Spithridates at the Granicus as the Persian was about to strike Alexander (Arrian 1.15.8). Alexander killed Cleitus with a spear at a drinking party in Maracanda in 328.

  5. Give the two main traditions for the burning of Persepolis.
    Answer

    Arrian: a deliberate act of policy, vengeance for Xerxes, over Parmenion's objection (3.18). Diodorus, Curtius and Plutarch: a drunken revel in which the courtesan Thais urged the king on.

  6. Why does the Philotas affair matter beyond the death of one officer?
    Answer

    It led to the murder of Parmenion at Ecbatana, destroyed the most powerful family in the army, ended the collegial command inherited from Philip, and split the Companion cavalry between Hephaestion and Cleitus.

  7. What was proskynesis, and who is said to have refused it?
    Answer

    The Persian gesture of obeisance before the king, which Greeks took to imply worship. Callisthenes refused it at Bactra in 327 (Arrian 4.10–12; Plutarch, Alexander 54) and was later arrested in the pages' conspiracy.

  8. What does the Babylonian astronomical diary contribute to the chronology of Alexander?
    Answer

    It records the lunar eclipse of 20 September 331 that fixes Gaugamela to 1 October, and the king's death on 29 Aiaru, 11 June 323.

  9. What did Alexander pray for at the Opis banquet, and why did Badian think Tarn misread it?
    Answer

    For concord and partnership in rule between Macedonians and Persians (Arrian 7.11.9). Badian argued this concerns two ruling peoples sharing power, not a brotherhood of all mankind.

  10. Who were the two kings proclaimed at Babylon in 323, and who held the regency?
    Answer

    Philip III Arrhidaeus, Alexander's half-brother, and Alexander IV, Roxane's son, not yet born. Perdiccas held the regency after the infantry and cavalry nearly fought.

12Carry forward

  1. Every narrative of Alexander is late and partisan, resting on lost eyewitnesses who disagreed even about events they saw; Arrian's Ptolemy-Aristobulus line and the Cleitarchan vulgate must always be weighed against each other, never simply trusted.
  2. The conquest changed character at the death of Darius, from a Hellenic war of revenge into Alexander's personal kingship of Asia; every later crisis, Philotas, Cleitus, proskynesis, Opis, is a Macedonian reaction to that shift.
  3. The argument between Tarn's visionary and Badian and Bosworth's ruthless realist is an argument about how to read the same few texts, above all the Opis prayer and the Susa marriages; the Hellenistic world that followed was built by his generals out of the structures he left, not out of any plan he wrote down.

The prompt this answers

Give me an in-depth report on Alexander the Great, 356 to 323 BC. Cover: his upbringing, Aristotle, Olympias and the Philip rivalry; the accession and the destruction of Thebes; the campaign in order: Granicus, Issus, the siege of Tyre, Egypt and Siwa, Gaugamela, Babylon, the burning of Persepolis, the death of Darius, the Bactrian and Sogdian war, Roxane, the Philotas and Cleitus affairs, the proskynesis and the pages' conspiracy, India and the Hydaspes, the mutiny at the Hyphasis, the Gedrosian desert, Susa and the mass marriage, Opis, the death of Hephaestion, the death in Babylon; his aims as historians have read them (Tarn's brotherhood of man versus Badian and Bosworth's ruthless realist); the sources problem (Arrian, Curtius, Diodorus, Plutarch, Justin, all late, resting on lost Ptolemy, Aristobulus and Cleitarchus); and his immediate legacy. Format: about 3000 words, headed sections, name sources, flag disagreements, end with a 10-question self-test with answers and the three ideas I must carry forward.
