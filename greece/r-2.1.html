The first beings: Chaos, Gaia, Tartarus, Eros, Erebus, Nyx

Hesiod's cosmogony proper begins at Theogony 116: "first of all Chaos came to be". Chaos is not disorder: the word belongs with chaskō, "to gape", and names a yawning gap. Next, without any parent, come Gaia, "ever-sure seat of all the immortals" (117), misty Tartarus (119), and Eros, "who loosens the limbs" (120–122). These four simply came to be; Hesiod gives them no cause.

Eros is placed so early for a structural reason: everything after him is born by mating. From Chaos come Erebus (the darkness under the earth) and black Nyx (123); Nyx, lying with Erebus, bears Aither (the bright upper air) and Hemera, Day (124–125). Gaia, still alone, bears Ouranos "equal to herself, to cover her all round" (126–127), then the Mountains and Pontos, the sea (129–132). Two lineages then run in parallel and never intermarry: the line of Nyx yields abstractions and afflictions (Moros, Thanatos, Hypnos, Nemesis, Eris and her brood, 211–232); the line of Gaia yields everything with a face and a cult.

Where sources disagree. Plato (Symposium 178b) and Aristotle (Metaphysics 984b) quote the opening without the Tartarus line, so some editors bracket 118–119 as an interpolation; West (1966) keeps them. Cornford read Chaos as the gap between sky and earth, others as a pre-existing void; the text does not settle it.

Ouranos and the castration

Ouranos and Gaia produce three sets of children: the twelve Titans (132–138); the three Cyclopes, Brontes, Steropes and Arges (139–146); and the three Hundred-Handers, Kottos, Briareos and Gyges, each with a hundred arms and fifty heads (147–153). Ouranos hates them all and, as each is born, thrusts it back into a hollow of Gaia (154–159). Gaia forges an adamantine sickle; only Kronos, "crooked-counselled", volunteers (160–172). When Ouranos comes "bringing on night, longing for love", Kronos reaps his father's genitals from ambush (176–182). From the blood on Gaia come the Erinyes, the Giants "in gleaming armour", and the Melian nymphs (183–187); from the parts in the sea, Aphrodite, ashore at Cythera and Cyprus (188–200). Ouranos names his sons Titans, punning on titainō, "to strain", and tisis, vengeance (207–210).

The castration is the Greek form of the separation of sky from earth, told as a founding crime: after it Ouranos acts only as a prophet, with Gaia, to Kronos (463–465) and to Zeus about Metis (891–893).

Where sources disagree. Hesiod leaves the Cyclopes and Hundred-Handers in limbo: he says Ouranos bound them (617–620) yet has the Cyclopes free to arm Zeus (501–506) while the Hundred-Handers wait till 624. Apollodorus, Library 1.1.2–1.2.1, tidies this: Ouranos imprisons both groups in Tartarus, the Titans free them, Kronos re-imprisons them, Zeus kills their guard Kampe. Which version is older is uncertain; Apollodorus may follow the lost epic Titanomachy.

Hesiod never says all the Titans fought Zeus. Oceanus plainly did not: Styx comes over to Zeus on her father's advice (397–398); Themis and Mnemosyne marry the victor. Homer's "gods below who are called Titans" (Iliad 14.279) are the prisoners, not the generation. Koios and Kreios have no function beyond their marriages; their names are obscure.

Kronos, the swallowed children, and the hiding of Zeus

Warned by Gaia and Ouranos that his own son will overthrow him, Kronos swallows each child as it reaches Rheia's knees (459–465). Pregnant with Zeus, Rheia is sent to Lyktos in Crete; Gaia hides the newborn in a cave on Mount Aigaion (477–484), and Kronos swallows a swaddled stone unexamined (485–491). "Deceived by the crafty suggestions of Gaia", he later brings up the stone, then the children (493–497). Zeus plants the stone at Pytho, "a sign and a marvel for mortal men" (498–500); Pausanias (10.24.6) saw it at Delphi, anointed daily. Zeus then frees the Cyclopes, who give him thunder and the bolt (501–506). The children emerge in reverse order, which is why Hestia, swallowed first, is "first and last" in cult (Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite 22–23).

Where sources disagree. Apollodorus, Library 1.2.1, has Metis administer the drug; Library 1.1.6 moves the cave to Dicte and adds the nymphs, the goat Amaltheia and the Curetes drowning the infant's cries, none of which Hesiod knows. Callimachus (Hymn to Zeus 4–9) records rival Arcadian and Cretan birthplaces.

The Titanomachy

The Titans fight from Othrys, the children of Kronos from Olympus, ten years without result (629–638). Gaia tells Zeus that victory needs the Hundred-Handers; he brings them up, revives them with nectar and ambrosia, and Kottos pledges the three (639–663). In the final battle they hurl three hundred rocks at a time while Zeus, "no longer holding back his might", burns the field with continuous bolts, until the din is "as if Earth and broad Heaven were colliding" (687–712). The Titans are bound in Tartarus, "as far beneath the earth as heaven is above" (717–725), behind bronze gates with the Hundred-Handers as warders (732–735); Atlas holds up the sky in the west (517–520).

The decisive allies are Kronos's own brothers. Zeus wins not by strength alone but by reversing his father's and grandfather's error: he releases the monstrous children instead of burying them.

Where sources disagree. In Hesiod the Titans stay bound; Pindar (Pythian 4.291) says Zeus freed them, and Aeschylus' lost Prometheus Unbound had a chorus of released Titans. Homer's Briareos helps Zeus against a plot by Hera, Poseidon and Athena (Iliad 1.396–406), a different tradition.

Typhon, the Giants, and why the Gigantomachy is a separate event

After the Titans' fall Gaia bears a last child to Tartarus: Typhoeus, with a hundred snake heads and voices shifting from a god's speech to bull, lion, dogs and whistling (820–835). "There would have been a deed past remedy that day" had Zeus not noticed at once (836–838). His thunder scorches the heads, Earth melts like tin, and Typhoeus goes to Tartarus, leaving the destructive storm-winds as his children (839–880). He is the last challenge to the new order, and his birth from Tartarus pairs him with the prison he fills.

Where sources disagree. Hesiod's Zeus wins easily. Apollodorus (Library 1.6.3) has Typhon cut out Zeus's sinews and hide them in Cilicia until Hermes recovers them, ending with Typhon under Etna, as in Pindar, Pythian 1.15–28. The Homeric Hymn to Apollo 305–355 makes Hera his mother. Homer's "land of the Arimoi" (Iliad 2.783) was already disputed in antiquity.

The Gigantomachy is not in Hesiod: the Giants are born armed at 185–186, but no battle follows. The full story is Apollodorus, Library 1.6.1–2: Gaia, angry at the Titans' fate, bears Giants at Phlegra; an oracle says no god can kill them without a mortal ally, so Zeus begets Heracles; the Olympians fight one-on-one (Athena buries Enceladus under Sicily, Poseidon drops Nisyros on Polybotes) and Heracles finishes each Giant with arrows. Pindar (Nemean 1.67–69) knows Heracles at Phlegra; the theme fills Attic vases from about 560 BC, the Siphnian Treasury frieze, the Parthenon's east metopes and the Pergamon Altar.

It is a different event in four ways: the opponents are earthborn and killable, not elder gods; it is a revolt against an established king, not a succession struggle; the whole Olympian family fights, with a mortal as the decisive weapon; and it became the standard image of civilisation defeating barbarism. Homer's Giants are a savage people under Eurymedon (Odyssey 7.59), not divine enemies. The conflation of Titans and Giants is Roman: Horace (Odes 3.4), Ovid (Metamorphoses 1.151–162).

How Zeus distributes honours

When the war is won the Olympians, on Gaia's advice, press Zeus to be king, "and he distributed well among them their honours" (881–885). The principle was laid down beforehand: at 390–396 Zeus promises that any god who fights beside him keeps the honour (timē) held under Kronos, and the honourless will receive some. Styx comes first, so her children Zelos, Nike, Kratos and Bia live with Zeus and she becomes the gods' oath (383–403); Hecate keeps her share "as the division was from the beginning" (421–428). Zeus rules by distributing rather than hoarding: that is the poem's political theology, and the reason his reign is not another turn of the wheel.

The second reason is Metis. Zeus swallows his first wife, Wisdom, while she is pregnant with Athena, so that the son destined to be "king of gods and men" is never born (886–900). The motif that damned Kronos is repeated once and reversed: Zeus internalises counsel. His marriages with Themis (Horai, Moirai), Eurynome (Charites), Demeter, Mnemosyne (Muses), Leto and Hera each add a sphere to the regime (901–929).

Where sources disagree. Homer's cosmos is shared out otherwise: Zeus, Poseidon and Hades drew lots for sky, sea and underworld, with earth and Olympus common to all (Iliad 15.187–193), a division by chance, not by Zeus's gift. Within Hesiod the Moirai are born twice, to Nyx (217) and Themis (904), and most scholars since West hold that the genuine poem ends around 900–930.

Kumarbi, Marduk and Genesis

The Hittite Kumarbi cycle

The Song of Kumarbi (Kingship in Heaven; CTH 344) survives on thirteenth-century BC tablets from Hattusa but is Hurrian in origin. Alalu is king for nine years; his cupbearer Anu (Sky) deposes him. Anu reigns nine years; his cupbearer Kumarbi drags him down, bites off his genitals and swallows them. Anu laughs: Kumarbi is now pregnant with the Storm-god Teshub, the Tigris and Tashmishu. Kumarbi is given a stone to eat in place of the Storm-god, and the stone becomes a cult object; Teshub is born through Kumarbi's body and takes the kingship. In the Song of Ullikummi (CTH 345) the deposed Kumarbi begets a diorite giant on a rock, who grows on the shoulder of the Atlas-like Upelluri until Ea cuts him loose with the copper saw that first separated heaven from earth.

The correspondences are too exact for coincidence: castration of Sky by his successor; the successor swallowing what will destroy him; a venerated substitute stone; three kings ending in a storm-god; a last monster raised by the loser; a sky-bearer at the world's edge. Forrer (1936) and Güterbock (1946) first drew the parallel.

The Babylonian Enuma Elish

The seven-tablet Babylonian epic, probably late second millennium BC, begins with the mingled waters Apsu and Tiamat and no gods. Generations are born inside them down to Anu and Ea. The young gods' noise disturbs Apsu, who plots against them; Ea kills him. Tiamat, roused to vengeance, breeds monsters under Kingu. The divine assembly promises Marduk kingship if he fights; he nets Tiamat, drives the winds into her mouth, shoots her and splits her corpse into sky and earth, then orders the stars, founds Babylon, has mankind made from Kingu's blood, and receives fifty names (Tablets VI–VII).

The shared pattern is generational: an inert elder resents the active young; the youngest wins by combat and is enthroned by consent, honours following. Marduk's fifty names answer to Zeus's timai, Tiamat's brood to Typhon. But Tiamat is female and the sea, the cosmos is built from her body, a motif Hesiod lacks, and Hesiod has no creation of man at all.

Genesis

Genesis 1 opens on earth "formless and void", darkness over the deep (tehom, cognate with Tiamat), and God's wind over the waters. God separates light from darkness, the waters above from below by a firmament, land from sea: the Mesopotamian order with the battle removed. God has no genealogy, rival or predecessor; he creates by command. Yet the combat survives elsewhere: Yahweh "broke the heads of the sea-monsters" and crushed Leviathan (Psalm 74:13–14), pierced Rahab (Job 26:12). Genesis 6:1–4, the Nephilim, "giants" in the Septuagint, is a shadow of the earthborn rebels.

What the parallels tell us

Greek myth is not autochthonous. The Hurro-Hittite material is closest, and Typhon's geography, Cilicia and Mount Kasios, where Ugaritic Baal fought the sea-god Yam, marks the corridor. Hesiod's father emigrated from Kyme on the Anatolian coast (Works and Days 635–640). Philo of Byblos' Phoenician Sanchuniathon, in which Elos-Kronos castrates his father Sky, suggests a Levantine channel too, though the text is Roman-period and possibly Hellenised.

Where sources disagree. When the transfer happened is contested. West (The East Face of Helicon, 1997) and Burkert (The Orientalizing Revolution, 1992) stress the eighth and seventh centuries BC and trade at Al Mina, Cyprus and Cilicia; others posit a Late Bronze Age koine shared by Mycenaeans, Hittites and Levantines; López-Ruiz (2010) emphasises oral Phoenician contact. Kirk warned that succession myths are common worldwide. The swallowed stone is too specific for that objection to cover everything.

What is Greek shows by contrast: Eros among the first four; Chaos as gap rather than water; no corpse-cosmos; and a moral direction from Ouranos's brutality through Kronos's cunning to Zeus's justice. Zeus's name is Indo-European (*Dyēus, the bright sky): the Greeks fused an inherited god with a borrowed plot.

Where Homer and the Orphics diverge

Homer has no Chaos. In Iliad 14 Hera calls Oceanus "the origin of the gods" (14.201) and Hypnos "the origin of all" (14.246); Oceanus and Tethys reared Hera when Zeus thrust Kronos down (14.203–204). Plato (Cratylus 402b) and Aristotle (Metaphysics 983b) already read this as a rival, watery cosmogony. Homer knows Kronos and Iapetos in Tartarus (Iliad 8.478–481), and Zeus defers to Night (14.259–261), a detail more Orphic than Hesiodic. Whether Homer presupposes Hesiod's story is uncertain.

Two witnesses carry the Orphic cosmogonies. The Derveni papyrus, a commentary of about 400 BC on an older Orphic theogony, has Night as oracular first power, then Ouranos "who first was king", Kronos, and Zeus, who swallows a divine aidoion, takes the cosmos into himself and re-creates it: "Zeus the head, Zeus the middle". The later Rhapsodies, quoted by Damascius (De principiis 123) and Proclus, begin with unageing Chronos, who makes Aither, Chaos and an egg; from it hatches winged, bisexual Phanes, who rules, then Nyx, Ouranos, Kronos, Zeus; Zeus swallows Phanes and remakes everything; the sixth king is Dionysus, dismembered by the Titans, from whose remains mankind comes. Aristophanes parodies the egg in Birds 693–702.

The divergences are systematic: Time, not a gap, is first; an egg replaces Gaia's emergence; Eros becomes the demiurge Phanes; the swallowing of Metis is magnified into Zeus swallowing the first-born god and recreating the world, making him creator rather than heir; and the sequence runs past Zeus to Dionysus and an anthropogony Hesiod never offers. Hesiod explains how the present order was won; the Orphics, how the soul got into a body.

Where sources disagree. The Derveni aidoion is "phallus" (Ouranos's severed member: West, Burkert) or "the reverend one", Protogonos himself (Brisson, Betegh). The Dionysus anthropogony is ancient for Bernabé but a Neoplatonic construction for Edmonds (1999); the Derveni text does not mention it.

Self-test

  1. What does the Greek word Chaos actually denote in Hesiod, and which three beings come into existence alongside it without parents?
    Answer

    A yawning gap or opening, not disorder. Gaia, Tartarus and Eros also "came to be" without parents (Theogony 116–122).

  2. Name the three things born from the blood of Ouranos and the one being born from his severed genitals.
    Answer

    The Erinyes, the Giants and the Melian nymphs from the blood (183–187); Aphrodite from the genitals in the sea (188–200).

  3. Which Titan clearly did not fight against Zeus, and what is the evidence?
    Answer

    Oceanus: his daughter Styx joins Zeus on her father's advice (397–398), and Themis and Mnemosyne later marry Zeus.

  4. Where does Hesiod say Zeus was hidden, and what later features of the story are missing from his version?
    Answer

    A cave on Mount Aigaion near Lyktos in Crete (477–484). Hesiod has no Curetes, no Amaltheia, no nymph nurses and no Metis supplying the emetic; those are in Apollodorus.

  5. What happened to the stone Kronos swallowed, and what is its Near Eastern counterpart?
    Answer

    Zeus set it up at Pytho (Delphi) as a sign for mortals (497–500). In the Song of Kumarbi, Kumarbi eats a stone in place of the Storm-god and it becomes a cult object.

  6. Why does Zeus win the Titanomachy, in Hesiod's telling?
    Answer

    Because, on Gaia's advice, he releases and feeds the Hundred-Handers whom Ouranos had bound (624–663), reversing the pattern of burying one's monstrous kin; they bury the Titans under rocks while Zeus burns the battlefield.

  7. Give three reasons the Gigantomachy is a different event from the Titanomachy.
    Answer

    The Giants are earthborn and killable, not elder gods; the war is a revolt against an established king, not a succession struggle; the whole Olympian family fights, and a mortal, Heracles, is needed to finish the Giants. Hesiod does not narrate it; Apollodorus 1.6.1–2 does.

  8. How does Homer's division of the cosmos differ from Hesiod's?
    Answer

    In Iliad 15.187–193 Zeus, Poseidon and Hades drew lots for sky, sea and underworld; in Hesiod (885) Zeus as king distributes honours himself, having promised at 390–396 to preserve or grant timai to his allies.

  9. What is the sequence of kings in the Song of Kumarbi, and how does Teshub come to be born?
    Answer

    Alalu, Anu, Kumarbi, then the Storm-god Teshub. Kumarbi bites off and swallows Anu's genitals and becomes pregnant with Teshub, who is born from his body.

  10. Name two ways the Orphic Rhapsodies diverge from Hesiod at the start and at the end of the succession.
    Answer

    At the start: unageing Chronos and a cosmic egg hatching Phanes replace Chaos and Gaia. At the end: Zeus swallows Phanes and recreates the world, and the kingship passes to a sixth king, Dionysus, with an anthropogony from the Titans' ashes.

Carry forward

  1. The Theogony is a political argument, not a list: each generation's crime (hiding, swallowing) is answered by Zeus's inversion of it (releasing, distributing honours, swallowing Wisdom rather than children), which is why his reign ends the cycle.
  2. Titanomachy, Typhon and Gigantomachy are three distinct events with different opponents, dates and ideological uses; later Greek and Roman writers blur them, and you should not.
  3. Greek cosmogony is a Near Eastern inheritance reshaped: the plot (castration, swallowing, stone, storm-god king, final monster) is Hurro-Hittite and Mesopotamian, while Eros, Chaos as gap, the refusal of a corpse-cosmos and the moral teleology are Hesiod's own.
