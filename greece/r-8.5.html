The oikos and the kyrios

The unit of Greek social life was not the individual but the oikos: household understood as people, land, house, slaves and cult together. Aristotle builds the polis out of households (Politics 1.2, 1252a–1253b) and analyses the household as three relations of rule: master over slave, husband over wife, father over children (1.3, 1253b). The man at the top of all three is the kyrios. Every Athenian woman had one for life: father, then husband, then son or nearest male kin. He gave her in marriage, represented her in court and controlled her property; she could not contract for more than a medimnos of barley (Isaeus 10.10), about a week's food for a family.

The ideology is set out in Xenophon's Oeconomicus 7–10, where Ischomachus trains his wife, married before fifteen (7.5), to run the interior: stores, slaves, textiles. The god made the man for outdoors and the woman for indoors (7.22–30). A wife who ran a large household had real authority within it, but delegated authority, and the text is prescriptive, not descriptive.

Sparta inverts this. Men ate in common messes and lived in barracks until thirty; wives ran the estates, and Aristotle complains (Politics 2.9, 1269b–1270a) that Spartan women were licentious, held nearly two-fifths of the land and had ruined the state. Gorgo's reply that Spartan women rule their men "because we alone give birth to men" (Plutarch, Lycurgus 14.4) is a later anecdote, but it captures the Athenian view that Sparta had the household the wrong way up.

Where sources disagree. Sparta reaches us through Plutarch's Lycurgus (c. AD 100), Xenophon's admiring Constitution of the Lacedaemonians and Aristotle's hostile Politics 2.9. Since Ollier's Le mirage spartiate (1933) much of it is read as idealising construction. Hodkinson (Property and Wealth in Classical Sparta, 2000) holds that land was privately and unequally owned from the start, against the Plutarchan equal lots; Cartledge allows more of an archaic reform. Every "Spartan custom" below is contested.

Marriage, dowry, the epikleros, divorce

Athenian marriage was a transaction between two men. The kyrios pledged the woman in a betrothal (engye) before witnesses; Herodotus 6.130 gives the formula as Cleisthenes of Sicyon hands Agariste to Megacles "according to the laws of Athens". The gamos was the procession and handing over, not a religious rite. Girls married at fourteen or fifteen, men near thirty (Hesiod, Works and Days 695–698; Aristotle, Politics 7.16, 1335a recommends eighteen and thirty-seven). After Pericles' citizenship law of 451/0 (Aristotle, Constitution of the Athenians 26.4) only children of two Athenians were citizens. Demosthenes 59 (Against Neaera) 122 states the purposes:

hetairai for pleasure, concubines for daily care, wives to bear legitimate children

The dowry (proix) went with the bride but never became the husband's outright. If the marriage ended he returned it or paid 18 per cent interest (Demosthenes 59.52; Isaeus 3.36): a large dowry made divorce expensive and gave her family leverage. Divorce itself was easy in law. A husband dismissed his wife with her dowry (apopempsis); a wife could leave by registering with the archon (apoleipsis), though Alcibiades dragging Hipparete home across the agora when she tried (Plutarch, Alcibiades 8) shows the cost. Adultery with a citizen wife was graver than rape because it threatened legitimacy: Lysias 1 defends Euphiletus for killing his wife's lover in the act, and the law then compelled divorce.

The epikleros shows the oikos outranking the woman. A daughter without brothers did not inherit; she went with the estate to the nearest male kinsman, who could claim her through the archon even if she or he was already married (Isaeus 3 and 10; Demosthenes 43). Her son would inherit as the dead man's heir and the line was repaired. Plutarch (Solon 20.2) records a rule that her husband must have intercourse with her three times a month, which shows the point. Sparta's patrouchos was adjudicated by the kings (Herodotus 6.57), but Spartan women could inherit in their own right. Spartan marriage by mock capture, with the bride's head shaved (Plutarch, Lycurgus 15), and the lending of wives to beget children for another man (Xenophon, Constitution of the Lacedaemonians 1.7–9) scandalised Athenians; moderns read them as eugenic policy or as misunderstood archaic survivals.

Women: law, practice, religion and voice

Pericles' funeral oration tells widows that a woman's glory is to be least talked of among men (Thucydides 2.45.2). Orators do not name respectable women; the ideal house had women's quarters, upstairs in Euphiletus' case (Lysias 1.9); women attended no symposia or assemblies, and probably not the theatre.

Two bodies of evidence cut against this. Poorer women worked: Euxitheus' mother sold ribbons in the agora and wet-nursed, which his enemies used against his citizenship (Demosthenes 57.30–35); Aristophanes is full of bread-sellers and garland-makers; grave stelai honour nurses and wool-workers (Brock, "The Labour of Women in Classical Athens", 1994). Seclusion was an ideal the leisured could afford. And religion was a genuine female public sphere: the priestess of Athena Polias was the city's most important religious officer; girls served as arrhephoroi and ran at Brauron; married women alone held the Thesmophoria for three days with their own officers. Connelly (Portrait of a Priestess, 2007) argues priesthoods gave women public honour and legal capacities denied them elsewhere.

Euripides lets a woman state the grievance. Medea (Medea 230–251) tells the Corinthian women they are the most wretched of creatures: they buy a master over their bodies with a dowry, cannot refuse him, cannot divorce without disgrace, and when a man is bored at home he goes out, while a woman must look to one soul alone. A man wrote it for men, which shows the audience knew the cost.

The hetaira, "companion", could attend the symposium, talk and be named because she was not a citizen wife. Aspasia of Miletus, Pericles' partner, was mocked in comedy as a brothel-keeper, credited by Plato (Menexenus 235e–236b) with teaching Socrates rhetoric, and reportedly tried for impiety (Plutarch, Pericles 24, 32); her son by Pericles was made a citizen by special decree after the plague.

Where sources disagree. Gomme (1925) argued from tragedy and vases that Athenian women were not oppressed; Pomeroy (Goddesses, Whores, Wives and Slaves, 1975) reasserted the legal disabilities; Cohen ("Seclusion, Separation and the Status of Women", 1989) proposed separate spheres rather than seclusion, with a female world of wells, neighbours and festivals. The evidence is male-authored and Athenian; conclusions about "Greek women" are conclusions about an ideology.

Childhood, exposure and education

A newborn was not yet a member of the oikos. The father decided within days whether to rear it; a rejected infant was exposed in a pot at a crossroads or shrine, to die or be raised as a slave, the plot of Menander's Epitrepontes and of Oedipus. Acceptance was marked by carrying the child round the hearth at the Amphidromia (Plato, Theaetetus 160e) and naming on the tenth day. Plato (Republic 460c) and Aristotle (Politics 7.16, 1335b) treat exposure of defective infants as policy; at Sparta the tribal elders, not the father, inspected the baby and had the weak thrown into the Apothetae (Plutarch, Lycurgus 16.1–2).

Where sources disagree. Golden (1981) argued from demography that 10 per cent or more of girls were exposed; Engels (1980) that widespread infanticide would have collapsed the population; Harris (1982) that modest rates were sustainable; Patterson (1985) that exposure was not killing in Greek eyes. The practice was legal and real; the numbers are unknown.

An Athenian boy of means went from about seven to the grammatistes for letters, the kitharistes for lyre and poets, and the paidotribes for exercise (Plato, Protagoras 325d–326c; Aristophanes, Clouds 961–983), escorted by a paidagogos, a household slave who carried his things and kept older men off. Socrates teases Lysis that a free boy is ruled by a slave (Plato, Lysis 208c). There was no state schooling; girls learned at home. From 335/4 Athens imposed two years of military training at eighteen (Aristotle, Constitution of the Athenians 42).

Sparta made upbringing public. From seven the boy lived in herds under a paidonomos, barefoot, one cloak, underfed so he would steal and be whipped if caught (Plutarch, Lycurgus 16–18; Xenophon, Constitution of the Lacedaemonians 2–4). Girls trained in running, wrestling and discus to bear strong children (Plutarch, Lycurgus 14), which no Athenian girl did.

Slaves and metics

Slaves came from war, piracy and the markets fed from Thrace, Phrygia, Caria and the Black Sea; Solon had ended debt-bondage of Athenians. Numbers are unknown: Athenaeus (6.272c) reports a census giving 400,000 slaves in Attica, which nearly everyone rejects; estimates run from 60,000 to 150,000 against 30,000–40,000 adult male citizens.

The worst fate was the Laurium silver mines, worked by chained gangs in galleries a metre high. Nicias leased a thousand slaves to a contractor at an obol a day each (Xenophon, Ways and Means 4.14–15); more than twenty thousand slaves, mostly skilled, deserted to the Spartans at Decelea (Thucydides 7.27.5). Household slaves cooked, wove, nursed and fetched water, and the oligarch who wrote the Constitution of the Athenians attributed to Xenophon (1.10–12) complains one cannot strike a slave in the street because he dresses like a citizen. Aristotle's natural slave, a living tool without deliberative faculty (Politics 1.4–7), is the only extended justification, and he admits others thought slavery conventional and unjust (1253b20–23). Finley's answer ("Was Greek Civilisation Based on Slave Labour?", 1959) was yes: the citizen's leisure for politics, philosophy and the gymnasium rested on slaves, even though most farming was done by free smallholders.

Where sources disagree. Jameson (1977) argued that even small farmers normally owned a slave and that slavery was integral to peasant agriculture; Wood (Peasant-Citizen and Slave, 1988) that the independent working peasant, not the slave, was democracy's base; Ste Croix (1981) placed slavery at the centre of the propertied class's surplus. Archaeology cannot settle it; slaves leave few traces.

Spartan helots were different: a conquered Greek population, mainly Messenian, tied to the land, state-owned and assigned to individuals, paying a fixed share of produce. Two thousand helots promised freedom for bravery were made to vanish (Thucydides 4.80); the krypteia sent youths out to kill helots at night, and the ephors declared war on them annually (Plutarch, Lycurgus 28). Thucydides judged most Spartan institutions designed for security against them (4.80.3).

Free resident foreigners at Athens, the metics, registered with a citizen sponsor, paid twelve drachmas a year (six for an independent woman), served in the army and paid liturgies, but could not own land, marry citizens with legitimate issue or address the assembly. They ran trade, banking and crafts: Lysias and his father Cephalus, host of Plato's Republic, were metics. Whitehead (The Ideology of the Athenian Metic, 1977) showed how the city used them and kept them out. Sparta periodically expelled foreigners (Thucydides 1.144.2).

The symposium

The symposion was the central institution of elite male leisure. It met in the andron, the one room built for show, with off-centre door and a raised border for seven or eleven couches, as the houses at Olynthus show. Guests reclined two to a couch after the meal was cleared; a libation of neat wine to the Good Spirit and a paean opened proceedings, and the symposiarch fixed the mixture. Greeks never drank wine neat; Hesiod (Works and Days 596) gives three parts water to one, and the mixing-bowl, the krater, was the room's focus. Eubulus has Dionysus allot three kraters to the sensible, the fourth to hybris, the eighth to the police (Athenaeus 2.36b–c). Vases show the kottabos stand at which drinkers flicked wine-lees at a target, and the aulos-girls who were the only women present.

Entertainment was participatory: skolia sung in turn with a myrtle branch, verses capped, riddles set. The Harmodius song for the tyrannicides (Athenaeus 15.695a–b) was a political creed; much archaic lyric, Theognis to Cyrnus, Alcaeus' faction songs, Anacreon, Xenophanes fr. 1, was composed for this room. Plato's and Xenophon's Symposia are idealisations in which the aulos-girl is sent away and talk takes over; Alcibiades' drunken entrance (Plato, Symposium 212d) shows what was idealised away.

It was political because it bred the hetaireia, the sworn drinking group. Thucydides (8.54.4) has Peisander touring the clubs in 411 to arrange the oligarchic coup; the herm-mutilation of 415 was blamed on such a group (Andocides, On the Mysteries). Democracy distrusted the andron (Murray, Sympotica, 1990).

Sparta replaced the private party with the compulsory public mess: fixed monthly contributions of barley, wine, cheese and figs, black broth eaten in groups of about fifteen, boys present to listen, drunkenness forbidden (Plutarch, Lycurgus 12). Critias (fr. 6 West) praised Spartans for drinking from their own cup without toasts; helots were sometimes made drunk and paraded as a warning (Plutarch, Lycurgus 28.4).

Pederasty, same-sex relations, Sappho and prostitution

Elite Athenian pederasty joined an adult man (erastes) and an adolescent of citizen family (eromenos), roughly from first down to beard. The erastes courted with gifts (hares, cocks on vases), frequented the gymnasium and was expected to educate; the eromenos was to be reluctant, yield slowly, never for money, and not seek pleasure. Pausanias in Plato's Symposium (182a–185c) gives the code: honourable if the boy yields to a good man for virtue, shameful if quickly or for gain. Aeschines' Against Timarchus shows the law: a citizen who had prostituted himself lost his political rights, and Aeschines distinguishes his own honourable loves (1.136) from Timarchus' hired ones. Vase painting, read by Dover (Greek Homosexuality, 1978), shows a stylised courtship, the "up-and-down" gesture of chin and genitals, and consummation standing, between the thighs, never in black-figure the anal penetration of a citizen boy.

Where sources disagree. Dover and Foucault (History of Sexuality 2) read Greek sex as structured by penetration and domination, not by the partner's sex. Davidson (The Greeks and Greek Love, 2007) called this a modern obsession and stressed age-classes and gift-exchange; Lear and Cantarella (2008) re-read the vases. The firm negative is that "homosexual" and "heterosexual" are not Greek categories: men were expected to marry and to desire both, and the question was how, with whom and at what age, not whether.

Spartan evidence contradicts itself. Xenophon (Constitution of the Lacedaemonians 2.12–14) insists Lycurgus allowed love of a boy's soul but made physical relations as shameful as incest, and admits nobody believed him; Plutarch (Lycurgus 18) says every boy of twelve had a lover who shared his punishments. Female relations are attested at Sparta (Plutarch, Lycurgus 18.4) and in Alcman's maiden-songs (fr. 1 and 3), but above all in Sappho of Lesbos, c. 600 BC, whose fr. 1, 16 and 31 voice desire for named women in the first person. Whether her circle was a school, a cult group or aristocratic friendship is unknown; the sexual sense of "Lesbian" is nineteenth-century.

Prostitution was legal, taxed and graded. Tradition credited Solon with state brothels (Philemon fr. 3, Athenaeus 13.569d); the pornai there and in the Kerameikos were slaves or metics at a fixed price, while the hetaira sold company by the evening or the year and might, like Neaera (Demosthenes 59), buy her freedom. Male prostitutes served the same market; the law punished only the citizen who sold himself. Davidson's Courtesans and Fishcakes (1997) treats it as part of an economy of appetite.

Farm, agora, food, clothing, gymnasium

Most citizens farmed smallholdings of a few hectares: barley, some wheat, olives, vines, figs, goats and a few sheep, as Hesiod and Aristophanes' Dicaeopolis (Acharnians) assume. Hanson (The Other Greeks, 1995) argued this intensive family farming created the hoplite and the polis. Attica could not feed itself: the Bosporan kingdom alone sent 400,000 medimnoi of grain a year (Demosthenes 20.31–32), and The agora was market, court, cult-centre and gossip-exchange; everyone frequents some shop, Lysias 24.20 reminds a jury. Spartiates were forbidden crafts and trade, with an iron currency to make wealth useless (Plutarch, Lycurgus 9).

Food was barley cake or wheat bread with opson: olives, cheese, onions, figs, lentils and above all fish, the glutton's mark in comedy; meat came mainly from sacrifice. Plato's healthy city eats exactly this and drinks in moderation (Republic 372a–d). Spartan black broth was so bad that a Sybarite said he now saw why Spartans were willing to die (Athenaeus 4.138d).

Clothing was draped: a pinned rectangle, the chiton or woollen peplos, under a himation. Spartan women's open-sided peplos earned them the name "thigh-showers". Thucydides (1.6.3–5) says Spartans first adopted simple dress and stripped naked to exercise, a habit that spread to all Greeks and separated them from barbarians.

The gymnasium and its wrestling-ground, the palaestra, made and displayed the male body. Athens had three public ones, Academy, Lyceum and Cynosarges, which became philosophical schools because teachers went where young men were. Exercise was naked and oiled; the law fixed opening hours and kept adults from loitering with boys (Aeschines 1.9–12); Plato's Charmides opens with Socrates entering a palaestra to see who is beautiful. At Sparta the whole of male life was in effect the gymnasium, and the women joined it.

Self-test

  1. What were the three relations of rule within the oikos according to Aristotle?
    Answer

    Master over slave, husband over wife, father over children (Politics 1.3, 1253b).

  2. What happened to the dowry on divorce, and why did this matter?
    Answer

    It had to be returned or interest paid at 18 per cent a year; the cost deterred husbands and gave the wife's family leverage.

  3. Who was an epikleros and what could the nearest male kin do?
    Answer

    A brotherless daughter who passed with the estate; the nearest kinsman could claim her in marriage through the archon, even if either was already married.

  4. Name two pieces of evidence that Athenian women worked outside the home.
    Answer

    Euxitheus' mother selling ribbons and wet-nursing (Demosthenes 57); market-women in Aristophanes; grave stelai of nurses and wool-workers.

  5. What did Pericles' citizenship law of 451/0 require?
    Answer

    Both parents Athenian for a child to be a citizen (Aristotle, Constitution of the Athenians 26.4).

  6. Who decided whether a newborn was reared in Athens, and who at Sparta?
    Answer

    The father in Athens; the tribal elders at Sparta (Plutarch, Lycurgus 16).

  7. What is Finley's answer to whether Greek civilisation depended on slavery?
    Answer

    Yes: citizen leisure for politics and culture depended on slave labour, even though most farming was done by free smallholders.

  8. What was the political danger of the symposium in democratic eyes?
    Answer

    It bred the hetaireia, the sworn drinking club, which organised the 411 coup (Thucydides 8.54) and was blamed for the herms affair.

  9. According to Pausanias in Plato's Symposium, when was a boy's yielding honourable?
    Answer

    When granted slowly, to a good man, for the sake of virtue, never for money or quickly (182a–185c).

  10. Why did Athenians regard adultery with a citizen wife as worse than rape?
    Answer

    Because it corrupted the wife's loyalty and threatened the legitimacy of heirs, the core of the oikos (Lysias 1).

Carry forward

  1. The oikos, not the individual, is the unit; women, children and slaves are defined by their relation to its kyrios, and the law's first concern is legitimate succession.
  2. Nearly all evidence is Athenian, male and often prescriptive; distinguish the ideology of seclusion from the practice of poorer households, and the Spartan mirage from Spartan reality.
  3. Greek sexuality is organised by age, status and conduct, not by the sex of the partner; never import modern identity categories into the symposium or the gymnasium.
