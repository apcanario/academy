The Acropolis, building by building

Everything standing on the rock belongs to one generation, roughly 447 to 406 BC, and sits on what the Persians burnt in 480 (Herodotus 8.51–55). Keep that double layer in mind: each Periclean building is a statement about the city that had just defeated Persia, built over the debris of the city Persia destroyed. Walk them west to east, as the ancient visitor did.

Propylaea

Mnesicles designed the gateway, built 437–432 BC and abandoned unfinished when the Peloponnesian War began; the lifting bosses were never trimmed off the walls. It is Doric outside with an Ionic interior, a mixing of orders the Parthenon shares. The north wing was the Pinakotheke, whose paintings Pausanias could still see (Pausanias 1.22.6). The political point is in the stone: a Pentelic-marble gatehouse on a scale no sanctuary had ever given an entrance, paid for from the same programme as the Parthenon.

Where sources disagree. Plutarch, Pericles 12 reports the charge that Pericles dressed the city "like a vain woman" with allied tribute. Many historians accept that League money funded the programme; Lisa Kallet and Aldo Giovannini argue the accounts (IG I³ 436–451) show Athena's own treasury paying and that the tribute story is partisan. Unresolved.

Temple of Athena Nike

On the bastion to your right as you climb stands the small Ionic temple of Athena as Victory, designed by Kallikrates under a decree of about 449/8 (IG I³ 35) but built around 427–424 BC. The bastion is Mycenaean and the Athenians left its masonry visible. Pausanias 1.22.4–5 says the statue was wingless so that Victory could never leave Athens, and places here the leap of Aegeus when he saw Theseus's black sail. The parapet Nikai, one adjusting her sandal, are in the museum. This is a war temple finished in wartime, with the plague and the Archidamian War both running.

Parthenon

Built 447–438 BC, sculpture finished by 432, by Iktinos and Kallikrates under Pheidias's direction (Plutarch, Pericles 13). Doric, eight columns by seventeen, with an Ionic frieze round the cella. It housed Pheidias's gold-and-ivory Athena Parthenos, dedicated 438, described by Pausanias 1.24.5–7 and copied in the Varvakeion statuette in the National Museum. East pediment the birth of Athena, west pediment her contest with Poseidon; metopes with Gigantomachy, Amazonomachy, Trojan War and Centauromachy, four mythic defeats of barbarism read through 480. Note two anomalies: Pausanias never calls it a temple, and no altar or priestess of Athena Parthenos is attested; the ancient cult image stayed in the Erechtheion. The Parthenon was treasury and votive as much as cult building.

Where sources disagree. The frieze is usually read as the Panathenaic procession, which would make it the only Greek temple decorated with contemporary citizens. Joan Connelly (1996) argued it shows the sacrifice of the daughters of Erechtheus from Euripides' lost Erechtheus. Most specialists keep the procession; the peplos scene over the east door remains contested.

Erechtheion and the contest of Athena and Poseidon

The irregular Ionic building on the north side, built about 421–406 BC with a building inspection of 409/8 preserved on stone (IG I³ 474), is the religious heart of the rock. It held the olive-wood Athena Polias, Poseidon's saltwater "sea", the trident marks, the tomb of Erechtheus, altars of Poseidon-Erechtheus, Boutes and Hephaestus (Pausanias 1.26.5–27.1); the split levels answer the cults already in place. The Caryatid porch covers the tomb of Cecrops. The myth it encloses is told by Herodotus 8.55 and Apollodorus, Library 3.14.1: Poseidon struck the rock and produced the sea, Athena planted the olive, Cecrops witnessed and the gods judged for Athena; Poseidon flooded the Thriasian plain in anger. The political reading is the one Athenians made themselves: a city founded on the land's own gift, with the sea god reconciled and sharing the house, which is to say a land polity turned naval empire.

Pandroseion

The open court west of the Erechtheion is the precinct of Pandrosos, the daughter of Cecrops who alone did not open the basket holding the infant Erichthonius (Apollodorus 3.14.6; Pausanias 1.18.2, 1.27.2). Here grew the sacred olive, beside an altar of Zeus Herkeios; a young olive planted in 1917 marks the spot. This is autochthony in a courtyard: the first king born from the soil, nursed by Athena.

Old Temple foundations

Between Parthenon and Erechtheion lie the limestone foundations Wilhelm Dörpfeld uncovered in 1885–86, the Old Temple of Athena Polias, built in the late sixth century under either the Peisistratids or the young democracy and burnt in 480. Its marble Gigantomachy pediment is in the museum. From the Agora side, look at the north wall: column drums and triglyphs of the Older Parthenon, begun after Marathon and destroyed unfinished, were built in as a visible memorial of the sack.

Where sources disagree. Dörpfeld held that the Old Temple was repaired and stood until the fire of 406 (Xenophon, Hellenica 1.6.1). William Dinsmoor argued it was dismantled soon after 480. Whether the Erechtheion rose beside a standing ruin or an empty platform is still debated.

The slopes

Enter from the south-east gate and walk the Peripatos westward. The theatre of Dionysus Eleuthereus is where Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides and Aristophanes were first staged, though the stone you see is the rebuilding of Lycurgus around 330 BC, seating perhaps 17,000, with Roman reworkings and the marble throne of the priest of Dionysus in the front row. The fifth-century theatre was wooden benches on the slope; the temple of Dionysus below holds the older cult brought from Eleutherae under the Peisistratids. West along the slope is the Asclepieion, founded in 420/419 BC by Telemachos of Acharnai after the plague, with its spring and the Doric stoa for incubation; the Telemachos monument (IG II² 4960) records the foundation, and tradition made Sophocles the god's first host (Plutarch, Numa 4). Below runs the Stoa of Eumenes II of Pergamon, a second-century BC promenade ending at the Odeon of Herodes Atticus, built about AD 160–174 in memory of his wife Regilla (Philostratus, Lives of the Sophists 2.1; Pausanias 7.20.6), roofed in cedar and burnt by the Heruli in 267. The slope is a four-century sequence: tyrant's cult, democratic festival, Hellenistic royal gift, Roman private benefaction.

The Areopagus and the Pnyx

The bare rock west of the Acropolis entrance is the Hill of Ares. Here Ares was tried for killing Halirrhothius, son of Poseidon, who had raped his daughter Alcippe (Apollodorus 3.14.2; Pausanias 1.28.5), and here Aeschylus, Eumenides 681–710 has Athena found the court for Orestes, with the rule that a tied vote acquits. The council of ex-archons sat here until Ephialtes's reforms of 462/1 stripped it of political power (Aristotle, Constitution of the Athenians 25), leaving homicide jurisdiction. The Persians camped on it in 480 (Herodotus 8.52); a plaque marks Paul's speech (Acts 17:22–34).

The Pnyx is twenty minutes south-west over the Hill of the Muses. The assembly met here from the Cleisthenic period until it moved to the theatre in the Hellenistic age. The rock-cut bema and the great retaining wall belong to the third phase; the auditorium held perhaps six thousand. There is nothing to see but the view, which is why it matters: this is the one place where you stand where the sovereign body stood.

Where sources disagree. Plutarch, Themistocles 19.4 says the Thirty turned the bema inland because sea-views bred democracy. Homer Thompson tied this to the second phase, about 404/3; others doubt the Thirty built anything, and the third phase has been dated both to Lycurgus and to Hadrian. The sequence is secure, the dates are not.

The Agora

Find the Panathenaic Way first: the gravel road running diagonally from the north-west corner to the Acropolis ramp, the route of the procession and the spine of the civic centre. Along its west side you pass the apparatus of the democracy. The Tholos, a round building of about 470–460 BC, is where the fifty prytaneis of the presiding tribe ate at public expense and a third of them slept, so that someone with authority was always on duty (Aristotle, Constitution 43.3); the official weights and measures were kept here. Behind it is the Bouleuterion: the old chamber of about 500 BC, built for Cleisthenes's Council of Five Hundred and later converted into the Metroon, the state archive, with the new chamber cut into the hill beside it about 415–406.

The Hephaisteion on the hill above is the best-preserved temple in Greece, Doric, six by thirteen, begun about 449 BC and finished with Alkamenes's bronze Hephaestus and Athena in 421–415 (Pausanias 1.14.6). Its metopes show Heracles and Theseus, hence its old name of Theseion. The Altar of the Twelve Gods, dedicated in 522/1 BC by the younger Peisistratos, son of Hippias (Thucydides 6.54.6–7), was the zero point of Attica, from which Herodotus 2.7 measures to Olympia, and a place of asylum (Herodotus 6.108); most of it lies under the railway cut of 1891. The Stoa of Attalos, gift of Attalos II of Pergamon (159–138 BC), rebuilt by the American School in 1953–56, is the museum. Do not miss the kleroterion for allotting jurors, the water clock that timed speeches, the bronze ballots, the ostraka naming Themistocles, the Spartan shield taken at Pylos in 425 and inscribed as such (Thucydides 4.38), and the law of Eukrates of 337/6 against tyranny with Democracy crowning the Demos. This is the democracy's hardware.

The Kerameikos and the Dipylon

Walk down Ermou to the potters' quarter, named for Keramos son of Dionysus and Ariadne (Pausanias 1.3.1). The Themistoclean wall of 479/8 can be seen built in the haste Thucydides 1.93.2 describes, with grave stelai reused in the lower courses. Two gates pierce it: the Sacred Gate, where the Sacred Way to Eleusis begins, and the Dipylon, the double gate on the road to the Academy, the largest in Greece in its fourth-century form. Between them stands the Pompeion, where the Panathenaic procession assembled (Pausanias 1.2.4). Outside the Dipylon ran the Demosion Sema, the public cemetery where the war dead were buried and the funeral orations given; Pericles' speech of 431/0 (Thucydides 2.34–46) was delivered here. The Street of Tombs has the fourth-century family plots: the monument of Dexileos, killed at Corinth in 394/3 aged twenty, the only Athenian grave inscription giving both birth and death years; the bull of Dionysios of Kollytos. The row ends abruptly because Demetrios of Phaleron's law of about 317 banned such monuments (Cicero, De legibus 2.66). The Dipylon vases were found here.

Olympieion, Hadrian's Arch, the Roman Agora and the Tower of the Winds

The Olympieion is a six-century project. The Peisistratids laid out a colossal Doric temple of Zeus about 515 BC; Aristotle, Politics 1313b lists it among the works tyrants build to keep subjects busy and poor. The democracy abandoned it. Antiochus IV of Syria resumed it about 174 BC in Corinthian marble with the Roman architect Cossutius (Vitruvius 7, pref. 15–17) and died in 164 with it unfinished; Hadrian completed it in AD 131/2 with a gold-and-ivory Zeus and statues of himself from every city of the Panhellenion (Pausanias 1.18.6). Fifteen of the 104 columns stand. Beside it Hadrian's Arch carries two inscriptions (IG II² 5185): towards the Acropolis "This is Athens, the ancient city of Theseus", towards the Olympieion "This is the city of Hadrian and not of Theseus".

Where sources disagree. Older scholarship read the arch as a boundary between the old town and a new Hadrianic quarter. Many now take it as an honorific set up by the Athenians presenting Hadrian as a second founder, with no boundary implied. Who paid for it is unknown.

North of the Acropolis the Roman Agora, a colonnaded market court, was built with money from Julius Caesar and Augustus; its Gate of Athena Archegetis is dated by inscription to 11/10 BC (IG II² 3175). It marks the moment commerce left the old Agora and the old Agora became a museum of itself. At its east end is the Tower of the Winds, the Horologion of Andronikos of Kyrrhos (Vitruvius 1.6.4; Varro, On Agriculture 3.5.17): an octagon of Pentelic marble with the eight winds in relief, a sundial on each face, a water clock inside fed from the Klepsydra, and once a bronze Triton weathervane. It is traditionally dated to the mid-first century BC because Varro knows it; Hermann Kienast argues for the second century.

The museums: objects and what they prove

Take the Acropolis Museum after the rock, because its Archaic gallery is the city the Persians destroyed. The Moschophoros (about 570), the Peplos Kore (about 530), the Rampin Horseman and the Old Temple's Gigantomachy pediment all come from the Perserschutt, the debris buried in 479 when the rock was tidied, which is why they are so fresh. The Kritios Boy, about 480, standing with his weight on one leg, is the first surviving statue to break the kouros stance. The Caryatids and the sandal-binding Nike are here; the top-floor Parthenon gallery hangs frieze and metopes in their original orientation, with white casts filling the gaps held in London, which makes the Elgin question visible rather than argued.

Where sources disagree. Lycurgus, Against Leocrates 81 and Diodorus 11.29.3 record an oath sworn before Plataea not to rebuild the burnt temples. Theopompus (FGrH 115 F 153) already called it a forgery, and the thirty-year gap before the Parthenon has been explained both by the oath and by lack of funds. The Perserschutt is real; the oath may not be.

The National Archaeological Museum needs a Metro ride north and half a day. The Mask of Agamemnon, from Shaft Grave V of Grave Circle A at Mycenae (Schliemann, 1876), dates to about 1550–1500 BC, three centuries before any plausible Trojan War: it proves the wealth of early Mycenaean kings and disproves Schliemann's label at once. The Antikythera mechanism, raised from a wreck in 1901, is a geared bronze calculator with Metonic, Saros and Olympiad dials, decoded from 2006 by the Antikythera Mechanism Research Project; it proves that the planetary devices Cicero attributes to Archimedes and Posidonius (De natura deorum 2.88) were real. The Artemision bronze, about 460 BC, from a wreck off Euboea, is one of very few large original bronzes of the Severe Style to escape the melting-pot. The Dipylon amphora, about 760–750 BC, 1.55 metres high, with its prothesis scene of mourners round a bier, is the return of the human figure to Greek art after the Dark Age, made within a generation of the Dipylon oinochoe nearby, which carries the oldest known Greek alphabetic inscription. The Thera frescoes from Akrotiri, the Boxing Boys, the Antelopes, the Spring Fresco, excavated by Spyridon Marinatos from 1967, prove a Cycladic court culture in close contact with Minoan Crete, frozen by the eruption.

Where sources disagree. William Calder suggested in 1999 that Schliemann tampered with or planted the Mask; most Aegean archaeologists reject this while granting that the mask is stylistically odd. The Artemision figure is Zeus with a thunderbolt for most, Poseidon with a trident for a minority. Radiocarbon puts the Thera eruption about 1620–1600 BC, Egyptian-anchored chronology about 1530–1500. The Antikythera mechanism has been dated anywhere between about 205 and 60 BC.

Walking orders

Both plans take the Acropolis at opening, when the light is low and the crowds absent. Everything in the centre is within twenty minutes' walk of everything else; the National Archaeological Museum is the one Metro ride.

The two-day order is chronological as well as geographical: the rock and its museum first, then the procession's road from the Dipylon into the civic centre, the Roman city, and finally the Bronze Age gold the classical Athenians never saw.

Self-test

  1. Why was the Propylaea left unfinished, and what physical trace of this can you still see?
    Answer

    Work stopped in 432 BC at the outbreak of the Peloponnesian War; the lifting bosses were never trimmed off the wall blocks.

  2. According to Pausanias, why was the statue of Athena Nike wingless, and what death does he locate on the Nike bastion?
    Answer

    So that Victory could never fly away from Athens; the leap of Aegeus when he saw Theseus's black sail (Pausanias 1.22.4–5).

  3. Give two reasons for doubting that the Parthenon functioned as an ordinary cult temple.
    Answer

    No altar or priestess of Athena Parthenos is attested, Pausanias never calls it a temple, and the ancient cult image of Athena Polias stayed in the Erechtheion; it served as treasury and votive.

  4. Which three physical tokens of the contest between Athena and Poseidon did the Erechtheion and Pandroseion contain?
    Answer

    Poseidon's saltwater "sea", the trident marks in the rock, and Athena's sacred olive in the Pandroseion (Herodotus 8.55; Pausanias 1.26.5–27.2).

  5. What did Pandrosos do, or not do, to earn her precinct?
    Answer

    Alone of the daughters of Cecrops she did not open the basket containing the infant Erichthonius (Apollodorus 3.14.6).

  6. Who founded the Asclepieion, when, and in what circumstances?
    Answer

    Telemachos of Acharnai in 420/419 BC, in the aftermath of the plague, as recorded on the Telemachos monument.

  7. What does Plutarch say the Thirty did to the Pnyx, and why is the claim doubted?
    Answer

    They turned the bema to face inland because sea-views bred democracy (Themistocles 19.4); the archaeology shows no phase securely datable to 404/3, and the later phases are themselves disputed.

  8. Who dedicated the Altar of the Twelve Gods, and what two civic functions did it serve?
    Answer

    The younger Peisistratos, son of Hippias, in 522/1 BC (Thucydides 6.54); it was the zero milestone of Attica (Herodotus 2.7) and a place of asylum (Herodotus 6.108).

  9. What do the two inscriptions on Hadrian's Arch say, and which way does each face?
    Answer

    Towards the Acropolis: "This is Athens, the ancient city of Theseus"; towards the Olympieion: "This is the city of Hadrian and not of Theseus".

  10. Why does the Mask of Agamemnon disprove its own name, and what does it prove instead?
    Answer

    Shaft Grave V dates to about 1550–1500 BC, roughly three centuries before any plausible Trojan War; it proves the wealth of early Mycenaean rulers, not the existence of Homer's king.

Carry forward

  1. The Acropolis is a double layer: every Periclean building stands on, and quotes, the ruins of 480 BC. The Perserschutt, the Older Parthenon drums in the north wall and the Oath of Plataea debate all turn on this.
  2. Athenian myth is political geography. Autochthony (Erichthonius, Cecrops, the olive) and the contest with Poseidon underwrite the claim to be both the oldest land and the master of the sea.
  3. Athens kept being rebuilt by outsiders who wanted to own its past: Attalos, Antiochus, Caesar and Augustus, Herodes Atticus and above all Hadrian. The Roman city is a commentary on the classical one.
