Diomedes: the hero who fights gods

Diomedes son of Tydeus is the Iliad's substitute Achilles. While Achilles sulks, Diomedes carries the Greek offensive, and the poem lets him do what no other mortal does: strike Olympians. In his aristeia (Homer, Iliad 5) Athena lifts the mist from his eyes so that he can tell gods from men (5.127–8), and tells him to spare every god except Aphrodite. He kills Pandarus, crushes Aeneas' hip with a stone, and when Aphrodite shields her son he wounds her on the wrist, drawing ichor (5.330–43). She flees to Dione, whose list of gods wounded by mortals (5.381–415) also works as a warning: men who fight gods do not live long. Later, with Athena driving his chariot, he spears Ares in the belly, and the war god bellows like nine or ten thousand men (5.855–63).

Book 6 reverses the mood. Meeting Glaucus, Diomedes asks his pedigree, and Glaucus answers that the generations of men are like leaves (6.146) before telling the Bellerophon story. Their grandfathers turn out to have been guest-friends, so the two refuse to fight and swap armour. Homer remarks that Zeus took Glaucus' wits away, since he gave gold for bronze, a hundred oxen's worth for nine (6.234–6). The scene shows xenia (guest-friendship) overriding the war, and lets the narrator make a dry joke at the Lycian's expense.

The night raid of Book 10, the Doloneia, pairs Diomedes with Odysseus. They capture the Trojan spy Dolon, get the dispositions of the camp out of him, kill him, then slaughter the newly arrived Thracian king Rhesus and take his horses. After Troy, the Little Iliad (in Proclus' summary) has the same pair steal the Palladium, the image of Athena whose presence protected the city. A later tale, the source of the proverb "Diomedean necessity", has Odysseus try to murder Diomedes on the way back with the image; it survives only in late compilers and is best treated as uncertain.

Where sources disagree. A scholion to Iliad 10.1 reports that Book 10 was composed separately and inserted into the poem under Peisistratus. Georg Danek (Studien zur Dolonie, 1988) and Bryan Hainsworth (The Iliad: A Commentary, vol. 3, 1993) treat it as a late addition, pointing to its unusual vocabulary and its loose links to the plot. Casey Dué and Mary Ebbott (Iliad 10 and the Poetics of Ambush, 2010) defend it as a deliberately different, night-time kind of episode within the oral tradition. The question remains unresolved.

On the homecoming, Homer and the later tradition part ways. Nestor reports that Diomedes reached Argos safely on the fourth day (Odyssey 3.180–2). The later version, which a scholiast on Lycophron traces to Mimnermus, has Aphrodite take her revenge: his wife Aegialeia takes a lover (Cometes in Apollodorus, Epitome 6.9) and drives him out. He sails to Daunia in Apulia, helps King Daunus, and founds Argyrippa (Arpi), along with other towns, according to Strabo (6.3.9). His companions are turned into seabirds that nest on the Islands of Diomedes, the Tremiti, and are said to welcome Greeks and attack barbarians (Pliny, Natural History 10.126–7; Ovid, Metamorphoses 14.457–511). In Virgil (Aeneid 11.243–95) the old exile turns down the Latin request to fight Aeneas, having learned what fighting Trojans costs. Strabo records that the Heneti at the head of the Adriatic sacrificed a white horse to him (5.1.9). Pindar says Athena made him an immortal god (Nemean 10.7). Diomedes is therefore one of the few Homeric heroes who received cult as a god across a wide stretch of non-Greek Italy.

The two Ajaxes

Ajax son of Telamon

The greater Ajax is the defensive hero. His shield, made by Tychius from seven ox-hides with a bronze facing, is "like a tower" (Iliad 7.219). The image may preserve a memory of the Mycenaean body-shield, which had gone out of use long before Homer. In Book 7 he fights Hector in a formal duel that heralds stop at nightfall, and the two exchange gifts: Hector's silver-studded sword for Ajax's purple belt (7.303–5). In Sophocles that sword is the one Ajax later falls on. He is the only major Greek the Iliad never wounds. In Book 15 he holds the ships alone, striding from deck to deck with a pike twenty-two cubits long (15.674–88). Only when Hector cuts his spear through does he give way and let the fire in (16.102–23), which prompts Achilles to send Patroclus.

His defining story comes after the Iliad: the Judgement of the Arms. When Achilles dies, his armour is to go to the best of the Greeks, and Odysseus wins it. The Little Iliad had Trojan girls overheard comparing the two heroes. In Odyssey 11.543–64 the judges are "children of the Trojans and Pallas Athena", and Ajax's shade turns away from Odysseus in silence. Pindar returns to the episode repeatedly as an example of envy and of the lies that defeat merit (Nemean 7.20–30, 8.23–34). Sophocles' Ajax stages what followed. Athena drives him mad, he slaughters the army's cattle believing them to be the Atreidae, and when he recovers he kills himself on stage, which is rare in tragedy. The second half of the play is a quarrel over his burial that Odysseus settles in his favour. The play's question is whether a heroic code built on honour can survive a world run by votes and speeches.

His cult had a political role. Ajax came from Salamis, which Athens and Megara contested. Iliad 2.557–8 places his twelve ships beside the Athenians, and in antiquity the line was alleged to have been inserted by Solon or Peisistratus to support the Athenian claim (Plutarch, Solon 10; Strabo 9.1.10). When Cleisthenes created ten tribes, Delphi chose ten eponymous heroes, and Ajax was admitted as a "neighbour and ally" although he was a foreigner (Herodotus 5.66). The tribe Aiantis carried his name. Before the battle of Salamis the Athenians called on Ajax and Telamon for help (Herodotus 8.64), and they afterwards dedicated a captured Phoenician trireme to him (8.121). Miltiades' family, the Philaids, traced their line to him (Herodotus 6.35). His tomb mound at Rhoeteion in the Troad was still being visited under Hadrian.

Ajax son of Oileus

The lesser Ajax leads the Locrians: he is small, wears a linen corslet, surpasses all the Greeks with the spear (Iliad 2.527–30) and has no equal at running down men in flight (14.520–2). The Iliad already treats him as someone Athena dislikes. She makes him slip in dung during the footrace (23.773–83). At the sack of Troy he drags Cassandra away from Athena's image, which in some versions turns its eyes away. He escapes stoning by clinging to the goddess's altar (Proclus' summary of the Iliupersis). On the voyage home Poseidon saves him from the wreck, but when he boasts that he survived in spite of the gods, Poseidon splits the rock he is clinging to (Odyssey 4.499–511).

Where sources disagree. The early epic summaries describe a sacrilegious dragging from sanctuary. The explicit rape of Cassandra appears later, in Hellenistic and Roman authors such as Lycophron. Lycophron has Cassandra foretell her own violation (Alexandra 348–64) and Apollodorus states it outright (Epitome 5.22), while Proclus' summary of the Iliupersis speaks only of dragging her from the image. Whether the early epic implied the rape and was discreet, or later writers made the crime worse, is uncertain.

The crime was atoned for by a real ritual. For a thousand years, so the story ran, the Locrians sent two maidens of good family each year to Athena's sanctuary at Ilion. They had to slip in at night while the townsfolk tried to kill them, and then served barefoot with cropped hair (Lycophron, Alexandra 1141–73, with scholia; Aeneas Tacticus 31.24). An early-third-century BC inscription from western Locris (IG IX 1² 3, 706) sets out privileges for the families that supplied the girls, so the practice was historical. Plutarch speaks of it as having ended not long before his own time (On the Delays of Divine Vengeance 557d).

Where sources disagree. Polybius (12.5) and Timaeus argued over the tribute in connection with the origins of Locri in Italy. Strabo (13.1.40) says the maidens were first sent only in the Persian period, which contradicts the claim that the rite went back to the Bronze Age. Modern estimates of when it began range from the seventh century BC to a Hellenistic revival.

Nestor: speech, Pylos and a cup

Nestor has ruled over two generations of men and now rules the third (Iliad 1.250–2). His digressions are not filler. Each recalled exploit, whether against the Centaurs (1.260–72), the giant Ereuthalion (7.132–56) or the cattle raid on the Epeans (11.670–761, the longest in the poem), sets a precedent that the present generation is invited to live up to. His advice is mixed. He proposes marshalling the army by tribe and phratry (2.362–3) and suggests the embassy to Achilles. But his speech in Book 11 also gives Patroclus the idea of going into battle in Achilles' armour (11.796–803), so the counsellor sets the fatal chain in motion. His four-handled gold cup, decorated with doves, which other men can hardly lift (11.632–7), is a deliberately antique object. In Odyssey 3 he is home again, sacrificing to Poseidon on the shore at Pylos.

In 1939 Carl Blegen began digging at Ano Englianos in Messenia and found a Mycenaean palace, now called the Palace of Nestor, together with an archive of Linear B tablets. Once Ventris had deciphered the script in 1952, the tablets showed a palace economy with offerings to po-se-da-o (Poseidon) and a coastline organised against attack. The palace burned around 1180 BC. The rich Griffin Warrior grave nearby (found 2015) shows that the site already had elite wealth in the early Mycenaean period.

Where sources disagree. Even in antiquity it was unclear which Pylos was Nestor's. Strabo (8.3.7) quotes the saying that there is "a Pylos before Pylos" and argues for Triphylian Pylos. Blegen's identification (Blegen and Rawson, The Palace of Nestor at Pylos in Western Messenia, vol. 1, 1966) is a convention, not a proof.

The other Nestor relic is a clay drinking cup from Pithekoussai on Ischia. It is a Rhodian kotyle buried in a boy's cremation grave (no. 168) around 730–720 BC, and an inscription in Euboean script was cut into it after firing. After a damaged first line come two hexameters, which end:

whoever drinks from this cup, desire of fair-crowned Aphrodite will seize him

It is among the earliest Greek verse to survive in writing, and the joke works by contrast: an ordinary clay cup against the heavy golden one of legend.

Where sources disagree. The first line can be restored as "I am Nestor's cup" or "Nestor's cup was good to drink from, but…". Barry Powell (Homer and the Origin of the Greek Alphabet, 1991) takes the joke to show that our Iliad was known in the West by about 720 BC. Christopher Faraone ("Taking the Nestor's Cup Inscription Seriously", Classical Antiquity 15, 1996) reads the lines as a conditional incantation rather than a literary allusion, and others think the cup was already famous in pre-Homeric tradition, which would leave the date of the poem open.

Patroclus and Antilochus: the deaths that define Achilles

Achilles' story turns on two dead companions. Patroclus is the older of the pair (Iliad 11.786–7). He came to Peleus' house as a boy exile after killing a playmate in a quarrel over knucklebones (23.85–8). Wearing Achilles' armour, he kills Sarpedon. He is then stripped by Apollo, wounded by Euphorbus and finished off by Hector (16.786–857), and Achilles' grief and revenge drive the last third of the poem. His ghost asks for their bones to share one urn (23.82–92), and Odyssey 24.76–7 says that they do.

Where sources disagree. Homer never calls them lovers. Aeschylus' Myrmidons did, making Achilles the older lover. Phaedrus in Plato's Symposium (180a) objects that Achilles was the younger and more beautiful of the two. Modern readers remain divided on whether Homer implies an erotic bond: W. M. Clarke ("Achilles and Patroclus in Love", Hermes 106, 1978) argued that he does, K. J. Dover (Greek Homosexuality, 1978) that the pederastic reading is a fifth-century imposition.

Antilochus, Nestor's son, is the young man Achilles likes best after Patroclus (Odyssey 24.78–9). He brings Achilles the news of Patroclus' death (Iliad 18.1–21). In the chariot race he fouls Menelaus, and Achilles smiles at him and gives him a prize anyway (23.555–6). In the lost Aethiopis he dies saving his father from Memnon. Pindar (Pythian 6.28–42) holds him up as the model of a son's devotion. Achilles then kills Memnon, and soon afterwards dies himself, so Antilochus' death repeats the Patroclus pattern of a friend killed, revenge taken and Achilles' own death following.

Where sources disagree. Neoanalytic scholars such as Kakridis and Schadewaldt argued that the Iliad's Patroclus plot was modelled on an older Memnon–Antilochus story. Bernard Fenik (Typical Battle Scenes in the Iliad, 1968) replied that the shared scenes are typical patterns of the tradition, and others that the influence could have run the other way, or that both versions draw on a shared stock of motifs. Because the Aethiopis is lost, the question cannot be settled.

Idomeneus, Menelaus and Teucer

Idomeneus and the Cretans

Idomeneus, grandson of Minos, brings one of the largest contingents: eighty ships from Knossos, Gortyn, Lyktos, Phaistos and other towns (Iliad 2.645–52). The Catalogue calls Crete the island "of a hundred cities", while the Odyssey gives ninety (19.174), a discrepancy ancient critics already discussed. He is middle-aged and half-grey (13.361), and his aristeia in Book 13 is one of a veteran fighter. His squire Meriones wears the boar's-tusk helmet described at 10.261–5, an object archaeology has confirmed as Mycenaean. Homer brings Idomeneus home safely (Odyssey 3.191–2). The later tradition does not. In Servius (on Aeneid 3.121), Idomeneus vows in a storm to sacrifice the first thing he meets on landing, which turns out to be his son. Plague follows, and he is expelled and settles among the Sallentini of southern Italy (Aeneid 3.400–1). In another version his wife Meda is unfaithful with Leucus (Apollodorus, Epitome 6.10).

Menelaus as warrior

Menelaus is more than a wronged husband. He beats Paris in their duel until Aphrodite carries Paris away (Book 3), survives Pandarus' arrow (4.127–47), and volunteers to face Hector until Agamemnon holds him back (7.94–122). His real aristeia is the defence of Patroclus' body in Book 17: he kills Euphorbus over the corpse and strips him (17.43–60), holds the body against Hector until the Ajaxes arrive (17.106–39), and in the end carries it off with Meriones while the two Ajaxes cover them (17.715–61). Apollo, in disguise, there calls him a "soft spearman" (17.588), but the insult is answered by what Menelaus is shown doing in that book. The Odyssey promises him the Elysian plain because he is Helen's husband and so Zeus' son-in-law (4.561–9). He and Helen were worshipped at Therapne near Sparta (Herodotus 6.61), where excavation has found Archaic votives dedicated to Helen.

Teucer and Cypriot Salamis

Teucer, Ajax's half-brother by the Trojan captive Hesione, shoots from behind Ajax's shield "like a child under its mother" (Iliad 8.266–72). When he came home without his brother, Telamon banished him. Euripides' Helen (87–150) shows him on his way to found a new Salamis in Cyprus at Apollo's command. Pindar (Nemean 4.46–7) and Horace (Odes 1.7.21–32) tell the same story. The Teucrid kings of Cypriot Salamis claimed descent from him (Isocrates, Evagoras 18), and so did the priest-dynasts of Zeus at Olba in Cilicia (Strabo 14.5.10).

Where sources disagree. Einar Gjerstad ("The Colonization of Cyprus in Greek Legend", 1944) took the Teucer legend as a distant memory of real Aegean migration to Cyprus in the twelfth and eleventh centuries BC, supported by the Arcado-Cypriot dialect and Late Cypriot III material. Maria Iacovou (American Journal of Archaeology 112, 2008) reads the Hellenisation of Cyprus as a slow process that the founder legend compresses into one landing, which would make Teucer a later dynastic charter that happened to match the archaeology. The evidence fits both readings.

Philoctetes and Neoptolemus: the men fetched late

Philoctetes

Philoctetes holds the bow of Heracles, given to him for lighting the hero's pyre on Oeta. In the Catalogue he is already lying on Lemnos in agony from a water snake's bite, and the Argives "would soon remember" him (Iliad 2.721–5). The Cypria (in Proclus' summary) placed the bite on Tenedos and said he was left behind because of the stench of the wound. The Little Iliad told how the captured seer Helenus revealed that Troy could not fall without the bow. Philoctetes is fetched, healed by Machaon, and kills Paris.

Sophocles' Philoctetes (409 BC) sends Odysseus and the young Neoptolemus to bring him back. Odysseus wants to win him by a lie. Neoptolemus, who has his father's honest nature, goes along with it, then breaks off and returns the bow. Only the appearance of the deified Heracles brings the story to the outcome myth requires. The play sets persuasion against force and fraud, and asks whether a good end can justify deceiving a man who has been wronged. Dio Chrysostom (Oration 52) compares it with the lost versions by Aeschylus and Euripides, both of which had Lemnian choruses. Sophocles' deserted island is his own invention. Homer lists Philoctetes among those who got home safely (Odyssey 3.190). The western tradition has him driven out of Meliboea by civil strife instead. He then founds Petelia and Crimisa in Bruttium (Strabo 6.1.3; Aeneid 3.401–2) and dedicates the bow to Apollo Alaios (ps.-Aristotle, On Marvellous Things Heard 107). The temple of Apollo Alaios has been excavated at Punta Alice near Cirò Marina.

Neoptolemus

Achilles' son by Deidamia grew up on Scyros (Iliad 19.326–7). In the Little Iliad Odysseus fetches him and gives him his father's armour, and in Odyssey 11.505–37 Odysseus praises him to Achilles' ghost. At the sack he kills Priam at the altar of Zeus Herkeios and takes Andromache as his prize. Euripides' Andromache (1243–9) makes their son Molossus the ancestor of the Molossian kings, the dynasty that later produced Pyrrhus and Olympias.

Where sources disagree. Proclus' summary of the Iliupersis has Odysseus kill Astyanax. A fragment of the Little Iliad (quoted by Tzetzes on Lycophron 1268) has Neoptolemus throw the child from the walls. Euripides' Trojan Women presents the killing as a decision of the whole Greek army.

He died at Delphi. Pindar (Paean 6.112–20) says Apollo had sworn that the man who killed Priam at an altar would never reach home or old age, and that Neoptolemus was cut down in a quarrel over sacrificial meat. Euripides makes Orestes the planner of the murder (Andromache 1085–165). Nemean 7 (35–47) presents his death more favourably: he lies within the sanctuary as overseer of its rites. A scholiast says the ode was meant to mollify Aeginetans offended by the paean, but many modern scholars doubt that story. Pausanias saw his tomb, which received annual offerings (10.24.6), and reports that his ghost helped defend Delphi against the Gauls in 279 BC (1.4.4). The killer at the altar ended up guarding the sanctuary where he was killed.

Calchas, the seers, and Thersites

Calchas son of Thestor is "best of bird-interpreters", who knew what is, what will be and what was before, and guided the fleet to Troy (Iliad 1.69–72). He reads the omen of the snake and the nine sparrows at Aulis as nine years of war (2.299–330). In Book 1 he names Agamemnon's offence and is called a "prophet of evils" for it (1.106). The Cypria and Aeschylus (Agamemnon 104–257) make him the man who demands Iphigenia. His death fits the fate of seers: Hesiod's Melampodia, as cited by Strabo (14.1.27), has him die of grief at Claros after losing a contest in counting figs to Mopsus. Strabo (6.3.9) also records a hero-shrine of Calchas on a hill in Daunia, where people seeking oracles slept on the fleece of a black ram, with a shrine of the healer Podalirius nearby. The army's experts were a seer and two healers, Machaon and Podalirius (Iliad 2.729–33). Their later cults followed the same western routes as the warriors'.

Thersites is the only character in the Iliad whose ugliness is described in detail: bandy-legged, lame, hunched, with a pointed, balding head (2.216–19). His speech against Agamemnon repeats Achilles' grievances almost point for point (2.225–42). Odysseus beats him with the sceptre, and the army laughs and calls it the best thing Odysseus has ever done. The scene makes the same argument sound contemptible in the mouth of a man without status. In the Aethiopis Achilles kills him for mocking his grief over Penthesilea. In Plato's myth of Er his soul chooses to be reborn as an ape (Republic 620c).

Where sources disagree. Homer gives Thersites no father, and many critics read him as the voice of the common soldier being silenced. The later tradition makes him the son of Agrius and so a cousin of Diomedes' father Tydeus (Apollodorus 1.8.5–6), an Aetolian aristocrat. That turns the class reading into a question about manners within the elite.

Cults, colonies and the uses of Troy

Put the later fates side by side and a pattern appears. The heroes whose homecomings fail become founders. Diomedes' unfaithful wife, Idomeneus' vow, Philoctetes' expulsion and Teucer's exile all leave the hero unable to return home, so he goes west or east and settles somewhere new. In the stories that survive, Diomedes, Idomeneus, Philoctetes, Calchas and Podalirius, along with Epeius at Lagaria and Nestor's Pylians at Metapontum (Strabo 6.1.15), are spread along the coasts of southern Italy. Teucer goes to Cyprus, Mopsus and Amphilochus to Cilicia, and Neoptolemus to Epirus. Irad Malkin's The Returns of Odysseus argues that these nostoi served as a shared language between Greek newcomers and native peoples. A Daunian elite could take Diomedes as its own founder and so claim a place in Greek legend without being colonised, as Rome later did with the Trojan Aeneas.

At home, the same heroes supported land claims and political identities. Athens used a disputed line of Homer and Ajax's cult to claim Salamis, and his name for a Cleisthenic tribe. It argued over Sigeion with Mytilene by appeal to Trojan precedent (Herodotus 5.94–5). Spartan and Athenian envoys cited Agamemnon and the Catalogue to Gelon of Syracuse (Herodotus 7.159–61). Royal houses such as the Philaids, the Teucrids of Salamis and the Molossian Aeacids used the heroes as ancestors. Tombs anchored cult in a specific landscape: Ajax at Rhoeteion, Neoptolemus at Delphi, Calchas in Daunia.

Where sources disagree. Mycenaean pottery at Apulian and Calabrian sites such as Scoglio del Tonno and Roca Vecchia has been read as the basis for a Bronze Age memory behind the western nostoi, the view Malkin argues against. Malkin (The Returns of Odysseus, 1998) dates the stories as we have them to the Archaic and Classical periods. They were collected by Timaeus and turned into poetry by Lycophron, long after any such memory could have survived. What remains uncertain is how far native communities shaped these myths, rather than simply receiving them from Greeks.

The Trojan legend was flexible enough to be used in all these ways. The heroes it pushed into exile, defeat or sacrilege were especially useful for founding new communities, because their stories left them with nowhere to return to.

Self-test

  1. Why does Athena allow Diomedes to wound Aphrodite in Iliad 5, and which other god does he wound?
    Answer

    Athena clears his sight so he can recognise gods and tells him to fight none except Aphrodite, whom he wounds on the wrist (5.330–43). Later, with Athena as his charioteer, he also wounds Ares (5.855–63).

  2. What does the Glaucus–Diomedes exchange in Book 6 show, and what is the narrator's joke?
    Answer

    Guest-friendship inherited from their grandfathers outweighs the war, so they refuse to fight and exchange armour. The joke is that Zeus took Glaucus' wits, since he gave gold armour worth a hundred oxen for bronze worth nine (6.234–6).

  3. What ancient doubt attaches to Book 10, and who takes part in the raid?
    Answer

    A scholion says the Doloneia was composed separately and added under Peisistratus. Diomedes and Odysseus capture and kill Dolon, then kill Rhesus and take his horses.

  4. How does the Homeric account of Diomedes' return conflict with the later tradition?
    Answer

    In Odyssey 3.180–2 he reaches Argos safely. Later sources give him an unfaithful wife, Aegialeia, and exile to Daunia, where he founds Arpi, his companions become birds, and he is worshipped in Italy.

  5. How did Athens make political use of Ajax son of Telamon?
    Answer

    Through the disputed line Iliad 2.558 supporting the Athenian claim to Salamis, through the Cleisthenic tribe Aiantis (Herodotus 5.66), through calling on Ajax before the battle of Salamis (8.64), and through the Philaid claim of descent from him.

  6. What was the Locrian maidens' tribute, and what shows it was historical?
    Answer

    As atonement for the lesser Ajax's sacrilege against Cassandra, the Locrians sent two maidens each year to serve Athena at Ilion, entering at night at risk of their lives. An early-third-century BC Locrian inscription regulating the supplying families, together with Polybius, Strabo and Plutarch, shows the practice was real. Its date of origin is disputed.

  7. What does Nestor's cup from Pithekoussai say, and why is it debated?
    Answer

    It is a kotyle of about 730–720 BC whose inscription ends in two hexameters promising that the drinker will be seized by the desire of Aphrodite. Scholars debate whether its joke presupposes our Iliad (11.632–7) or an older traditional cup of Nestor.

  8. How does Antilochus' death relate to Patroclus', and what theory links them?
    Answer

    In the Aethiopis Memnon kills Antilochus while he saves Nestor, Achilles takes revenge, and Achilles' own death follows, which mirrors the Patroclus–Hector sequence. Neoanalysts argue that the Iliad adapted an earlier Memnon story. Critics dispute the direction of influence.

  9. What ethical problem does Sophocles' Philoctetes stage, and where did Philoctetes end up in the western tradition?
    Answer

    Whether a needed end justifies deceiving a wronged man: Neoptolemus' honesty against Odysseus' fraud, settled only by Heracles. In the West he founds Petelia and Crimisa in Bruttium and dedicates the bow to Apollo Alaios.

  10. How did Neoptolemus die, and what was his status at Delphi afterwards?
    Answer

    He was killed at Delphi, in a quarrel over sacrificial meat according to Pindar's Paean 6, or in a plot by Orestes according to Euripides. This was Apollo's punishment for killing Priam at an altar. He received annual hero cult at his tomb there (Pausanias 10.24.6) and was said to have helped defend the sanctuary against the Gauls.

Carry forward

  1. A failed homecoming is how the myth produces founders: heroes who cannot return home through exile, betrayal or vow found cities instead, so the nostoi serve as charter myths for the Greek and Italian West and for Cyprus.
  2. Homer and the later tradition often disagree about the same hero (Diomedes' safe return against his exile, Odysseus or Neoptolemus as killer of Astyanax), so always ask which source and which century a version comes from.
  3. Hero cult was real and had political uses: tombs, tribes, rituals and genealogies (Ajax at Athens, the Locrian maidens, Neoptolemus at Delphi, the Teucrids) turned epic figures into supports for land claims and dynastic legitimacy.

Cover: Diomedes (his aristeia in Book 5 including wounding Aphrodite and Ares, the exchange with Glaucus in Book 6, the Doloneia, the Palladium, his return to an unfaithful wife and his second life in Italy with cult in Apulia); Ajax son of Telamon (the duel with Hector, the defence of the ships, the shield like a tower, the Judgement of the Arms, the madness and suicide in Sophocles, his cult at Salamis and the Athenian tribe); Ajax son of Oileus (the rape of Cassandra, the shipwreck, the Locrian maidens' tribute to Athena at Troy as a historical ritual); Nestor (the garrulous counsellor, his Pylos and the Linear B palace, Nestor's cup from Pithekoussai); Patroclus and Antilochus as the two whose deaths define Achilles; Idomeneus and the Cretan contingent; Menelaus as warrior; Philoctetes (the bow of Heracles, Lemnos, Sophocles' play and its ethics, his Italian foundations); Neoptolemus (Scyros, the killing of Priam and Astyanax, Andromache, his death at Delphi and his cult there); Teucer and the founding of Cypriot Salamis; Calchas and the seers; Thersites as anti-hero. End with what the spread of these heroes' cults across the Greek and Italian world tells us about how the Trojan legend was used to found cities and claim land. Format: about 2500 words, headed sections, name sources, flag disagreements, end with a 10-question self-test with answers and the three ideas I must carry forward.
