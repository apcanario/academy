What collapsed, and when

Between about 1225 and 1150 BC the interlocking palace economies of the eastern Mediterranean failed one after another. The Mycenaean world went first and most completely. The palace at Thebes burned in LH IIIB1, probably in the later thirteenth century. Mycenae suffered a destruction around 1250 and a far heavier one at the end of LH IIIB, about 1190–1180, alongside Tiryns and Midea. The palace at Pylos burned at roughly the same moment, so hot that its unbaked Linear B tablets were fired and preserved: the last administrative year of a Mycenaean state, frozen. Nothing like the palaces was rebuilt. Writing vanished with them, because Linear B was a palace instrument for recording rations, flocks, bronze and labour, and when the bureaucracies died the script had no other users. Greece was illiterate for some four centuries, until an alphabet adapted from Phoenician appears on the Dipylon oinochoe (c. 740) and Nestor's cup from Pithekoussai (c. 730).

The same decades saw Hattusa abandoned under its last king, Suppiluliuma II; Ugarit destroyed, its final letters never sent; Emar on the Euphrates sacked; Troy VIIa burnt; and Hazor, Lachish and Megiddo levelled over a longer spread of dates. Egypt fought off two assaults by coalitions of "peoples of the sea", under Merneptah (c. 1208) and Ramesses III (c. 1177), survived, and then slid into the weak Third Intermediate Period. Assyria contracted but did not fall; Cyprus and the Phoenician coast recovered quickly; Carchemish carried a Hittite dynasty into the Iron Age.

The population crash is the hardest element to measure and the most often overstated. Anthony Snodgrass (The Dark Age of Greece, 1971) counted roughly 320 known LH IIIB sites in Greece against about 130 in LH IIIC and some 40 Submycenaean. Site counts are not head counts: survey has filled gaps, and a population that moves to fewer or less visible places shrinks in the record without shrinking in life. But Messenia, where the Minnesota survey mapped over 200 Bronze Age sites, falls to a dozen or so in the eleventh century, and nobody denies a severe decline by about 1050. With it went the crafts the palaces had paid for: fresco, monumental masonry, ivory, seal engraving, and long-range exchange in tin and copper.

Two corrections to the old picture matter. The collapse was not instantaneous: LH IIIC (c. 1190–1070) is a real period, in which Tiryns grew a large lower town after its palace fell, Mycenae was reoccupied, Perati in Attica buried a community with Egyptian and Levantine imports, and a pictorial pottery of warriors and ships flourished. And it was regional in severity: the Argolid, Messenia and Boeotia lost most; Euboea, Attica, the Cyclades, Crete and Cyprus kept more.

Explanations, and how scholars weigh them now

Sea Peoples

The oldest explanation is the Egyptians' own. Ramesses III's inscription at Medinet Habu names the Peleset, Tjeker, Shekelesh, Denyen and Weshesh, who "made a conspiracy in their islands", and claims that no land could stand before them, listing Hatti, Kode, Carchemish, Arzawa and Alashiya. Ammurapi, last king of Ugarit, wrote to Cyprus that enemy ships had burnt his towns while his fleet was in Lukka and his army in Hatti (RS 20.238). The Peleset are almost certainly the Philistines, whose twelfth-century pottery at Ashdod, Ashkelon and Ekron is locally made Aegean LH IIIC ware. So Aegean and Anatolian groups certainly moved and fought. But the Egyptian lists are triumphal, the other identifications (Denyen as Danaans, Ekwesh as Achaeans, Shekelesh as Sicels) are guesses, and no destruction in Greece can be tied to a named invader. Most scholars now treat the Sea Peoples as symptom rather than cause: populations displaced by a crisis already under way, who then deepened it.

Drought and climate

Rhys Carpenter proposed drought in 1966 with no data. The data now exist: pollen cores from coastal Syria and Cyprus (Kaniewski and colleagues, 2010, 2013) and the Sea of Galilee (Langgut, Finkelstein and Litt, 2013), and a speleothem from Mavri Trypa near Pylos (Finné and colleagues, 2017), all show a drier phase from about 1200 into the Iron Age. Texts agree: Merneptah sent grain "to keep alive the land of Hatti", and a Hittite king demanded a grain ship from Ugarit as "a matter of life and death". The weakness is resolution, roughly a century, and the fact that drought alone does not burn a citadel.

Earthquakes

Claude Schaeffer blamed earthquakes at Ugarit; Klaus Kilian found skeletons under collapsed walls at Tiryns around 1200. Amos Nur and Eric Cline (2000) proposed an "earthquake storm" along the Aegean and Anatolian faults. Several destructions do show seismic signatures. But earthquakes had hit these sites before and been followed by rebuilding; something else prevented rebuilding this time.

Systems collapse

Colin Renfrew (1979) and Joseph Tainter (The Collapse of Complex Societies, 1988) shifted attention from triggers to structure. Palace economies were centralised, dependent on redistributing staples and on distant bronze supply, and ran with little slack. A failure in one node propagated through the network, and extracting more from the countryside made the next failure worse. The Pylos tablets have been read in this light: the coast-watchers (An 657), the gathering of bronze from sanctuaries (Jn 829) and the extraordinary offerings of Tn 316, though whether these record an emergency or routine business is uncertain.

Internal revolt and military change

Linear B shows a sharply stratified society, and some have imagined the lower orders rising against the wanax; there is no direct evidence, and a revolt does not explain Ugarit or Hattusa. Robert Drews (The End of the Bronze Age, 1993) argued instead that massed infantry with javelins, Naue II slashing swords and round shields overturned chariot armies and the aristocracies that owned them. The weapons did spread about 1200; the battlefield mechanism remains conjecture.

Where sources disagree. Eric Cline's 1177 B.C. (2014) presents a "perfect storm" of drought, earthquake, famine, migration and systems failure. Bernard Knapp and Sturt Manning (2016) object that the destructions span half a century and that lumping them into one event imposes a narrative the evidence does not bear. Guy Middleton (2017) questions whether "collapse" fits Greece at all, given a century of LH IIIC life. The consensus is multicausal, with climate rising in weight over the last fifteen years and migration falling.

The Dark Age in the ground

Vincent Desborough (The Greek Dark Ages, 1972) and Snodgrass gave the term content; Oliver Dickinson (2006) and Irene Lemos (The Protogeometric Aegean, 2002) have since softened it. The period divides into Submycenaean (c. 1070–1025), Protogeometric (c. 1025–900) and Early Geometric (c. 900–850), labels taken from Athenian pottery because Athens, undestroyed around 1200, has the fullest sequence.

Protogeometric pottery

Desborough defined the style in Protogeometric Pottery (1952). Its signature is concentric circles and semicircles drawn with a multiple brush on a compass, replacing Submycenaean's freehand wavy lines; shapes tighten, the wheel is faster, the glaze better fired. It is a modest thing to hang an age on, but it is the first new idea in Greek material culture after the collapse, it spreads from Athens to Euboea, Thessaly, the Argolid, the Dodecanese and Ionia, and it dates a cemetery to within a generation.

Lefkandi

Lefkandi on the Euboean Gulf broke the Dark Age open. The settlement at Xeropolis ran unbroken from LH IIIC through Geometric, growing when others shrank. On the Toumba hill, in 1981, Mervyn Popham, Hugh Sackett and Petros Themelis found an apsidal building nearly 50 metres long with a wooden veranda, built and then deliberately buried under a mound around 950 BC. In its central room one shaft held a man's cremated bones wrapped in cloth inside a Cypriot bronze amphora already two centuries old, with iron sword and spear, and beside him an inhumed woman with gold earrings, a gold breast ornament and an iron knife by her head; the other shaft held four horses, one with an iron bit in its mouth. A cemetery of rich graves with gold, faience and Phoenician imports then grew around the mound for a century and a half, and produced the terracotta Centaur of about 900, among the earliest mythological images of the Iron Age. The man was buried as Patroclus is in Iliad 23: pyre, bronze urn, mound, slaughtered horses.

Nichoria

Nichoria in Messenia, dug by the Minnesota expedition under William McDonald, is the contrast. The Mycenaean town was abandoned; a village returned in the eleventh century, and in Dark Age II (c. 975–850) its largest structure, Unit IV-1, was an apsidal house about 10 by 7 metres with hearth and storage, read by William Coulson as a chieftain's dwelling. This is the scale of most Dark Age Greece: a few dozen families and a leading household rather than a state. The basileus of Linear B, a minor local official, becomes the chief of such a place; the wanax survives only as the poetic title anax.

Where sources disagree. Whether the thin burial record means few people or a small burying class (Morris against Snodgrass) is argued in module 5.1; settlement evidence shrinks too, which is the point against Morris here. Whether the Toumba building was a chief's house turned into a tomb or a funerary building from the start is still argued; the excavators themselves changed their minds.

Movements of people: Ionia and the Heracleidae

The Ionian migration

Herodotus (1.142–148) lists the twelve Ionian cities sharing the Panionion at Mycale, and at 1.146 deflates the Athenian claim to purity: the settlers were Abantes from Euboea, Minyans, Cadmeians, Dryopians, Phocians, Molossians, Arcadian Pelasgians and Epidaurian Dorians, and those who took Miletus killed the Carian men and married their wives. Pausanias (7.2–5) and Strabo (14.1.3) have sons of the Athenian king Codrus lead the expeditions, Neleus to Miletus and Androclus to Ephesus. Mimnermus (fr. 9 West) remembered Colophon as founded from Pylos. Thucydides (1.12) puts the settlement after the Dorian conquest, in the unsettled generations following Troy.

Archaeology supports a real movement, not a single expedition. Miletus had been Minoan and then Mycenaean, so Greeks were in Ionia before the collapse. Attic-type Protogeometric pottery appears at Miletus, Ephesus, Klazomenai and Old Smyrna from the tenth century, and Smyrna's oval houses of about 900 resemble Nichoria's. The picture is gradual seepage by small groups through the eleventh and tenth centuries, later reorganised into a heroic foundation story that gave Athens a mother-city's claim; Naoise Mac Sweeney (Foundation Myths and Politics in Ancient Ionia, 2013) has shown how varied and political those stories were.

The return of the Heracleidae

The second tradition explained the Dorian Peloponnese. Heracles' children, expelled by Eurystheus and sheltered at Athens, tried to return under Hyllus, who fell in single combat to Echemus of Tegea (Herodotus 9.26); an oracle told them to wait for "the third fruit", which they misread as harvests rather than generations. In the third generation Temenus, Cresphontes and the sons of Aristodemus crossed from Naupactus, killed the Pelopid Tisamenus and divided the land by lot: Argos, Messenia, and Lacedaemon to Aristodemus' twins, ancestors of Sparta's two royal houses. The fullest versions are Apollodorus, Library 2.8.2–5, Pausanias 2.18.7–9 and 4.3.3–8, and Diodorus 4.57–58. Thucydides (1.12.3) dated the conquest eighty years after Troy; Tyrtaeus (fr. 2 West) sang that Zeus gave Sparta to the Heracleidae, with whom "we left windy Erineos".

For a century archaeologists hunted the Dorian invasion in cist graves, handmade burnished pottery, iron, the Naue II sword and the long dress pin. Each has another explanation or a wider distribution. There is no Dorian material culture, and the dialect map, Doric in the Peloponnese, Crete and Rhodes, with Arcadian preserving Mycenaean-like forms in the mountains, is compatible with movement but does not require conquest. John Chadwick (1976) argued the Dorians were already present as a subject population speaking a lower-status dialect, so the "return" was a change of masters; Jonathan Hall (Ethnic Identity in Greek Antiquity, 1997) argued that Dorian identity was constructed in the Archaic period, with the Heracleid genealogy as its charter.

Where sources disagree. Herodotus 9.26 has the Heracleidae withdraw for a hundred years; Apollodorus and Diodorus give three generations. Pausanias makes the lot the origin of Argive, Messenian and Spartan kingship; Tyrtaeus knows the Spartan Heracleidae but no lot. Modern views run from a small real Dorian movement in the eleventh century (Desborough; Dickinson with reservations) to no migration at all (Hall). The Peloponnese changed hands in some fashion and the Greeks of 700 BC remembered it as a conquest; how much of the memory is memory is uncertain.

How the heroic age became a golden age

This is the idea the rest of the course depends on. The Mycenaean world was not forgotten; it was transformed into myth by being remembered the only way an illiterate society can remember, in sung verse. Milman Parry and Albert Lord showed that the Homeric poems are the product of oral composition in formulae, which conserves phrases and whole scenes across generations while reshaping their content to what singer and audience know. Such a tradition carries names, places and objects well, and institutions badly.

The conservation is real. Homer's heroes fight in bronze in a poem composed by iron-users; iron appears chiefly as a prize (Iliad 23.826–835). Meriones lends Odysseus a helmet set with boars' tusks (Iliad 10.261–265), an object known from shaft graves and frescoes and extinct for four centuries. Ajax carries a tower shield "like a wall"; Nestor's cup with its doves (Iliad 11.632–637) recalls the gold cup from Shaft Grave IV. The Catalogue of Ships (Iliad 2.494–759) maps a Mycenaean Greece: Pylos and Mycenae great, Thebes reduced to "Lower Thebes" as if the Kadmeia were already a ruin, no Dorians in the Peloponnese, no Ionia. Linear B preserves Zeus, Hera, Poseidon, Hermes and Dionysus, and the titles wanax and basileus.

The distortion is equally real. Chariots are taxis to the battlefield, because singers who had never seen a chariot charge rationalised one from the formulae. The dead are cremated and mounded, Iron Age fashion, not laid in tholoi. The basileus, a village official at Pylos, is a king in Homer because that is what the chief of Nichoria or Lefkandi was called. The palaces have become halls of chiefs with a hundred retainers, their bureaucracy wholly forgotten: nobody in Homer writes except the "baneful signs" of Bellerophon's folded tablet (Iliad 6.168–169). The fortifications survived as walls only Cyclopes could have built (Pausanias 2.16.5), the tholoi as the treasuries of Atreus and Minyas. Moses Finley (The World of Odysseus, 1954) placed Homeric society in the tenth and ninth centuries; Snodgrass (1974) called it an amalgam no society had lived; Ian Morris (1986) put it in the eighth. All three are partly right: the poems are a palimpsest.

The decisive step is that memory became nostalgia. Hesiod, Works and Days 156–173, makes the heroes a separate race of demigods, "nobler and more just", who fought at Thebes and Troy and were removed to the isles of the blessed, before the iron race of his own day. The bronze men before them worked bronze because "black iron did not exist" (150–151): a technological memory turned into a scheme of decline. Herodotus set Homer four hundred years before himself (2.53) and Eratosthenes fixed the fall of Troy at 1184/3, within a decade of the real end of LH IIIB. The Greeks knew their great age lay beyond a gap, and the gap made it great. The ruins helped: from the eighth century, offerings reappear at Mycenaean tombs across the Argolid, Messenia and Attica, and hero shrines rise at Menelaus' supposed house near Sparta and Agamemnon's at Mycenae. The men who had sung of heroes began to worship them.

Where sources disagree. Nicolas Coldstream (1976) tied eighth-century tomb cult to the spread of epic. Carla Antonaccio (An Archaeology of Ancestors, 1995) separated the two: offerings at Bronze Age tombs are local claims on ancestors and land, distinct from the later cult of named epic heroes. On Homer's own date the range runs from the late eighth century (the majority) to the mid-seventh (Martin West).

Self-test

  1. Why did Linear B disappear when the palaces fell, when Phoenician and Egyptian scripts survived the same crisis?
    Answer

    Linear B was an instrument of palace administration with no other users: no literature, letters or private documents. When the bureaucracies that needed it were destroyed, the skill had no reason to be passed on. Levantine and Egyptian scripts served temples, merchants and courts that survived.

  2. What single archaeological accident gives us the last year of the kingdom of Pylos, and roughly when was it?
    Answer

    The fire that destroyed the palace around 1180 baked its unbaked clay Linear B tablets, preserving the current year's records, including the coast-watcher and bronze-collection documents.

  3. Name the five groups Ramesses III lists at Medinet Habu, and the one identification nearly all scholars accept.
    Answer

    Peleset, Tjeker, Shekelesh, Denyen and Weshesh. The Peleset are accepted as the Philistines, whose early pottery is locally made Aegean-style LH IIIC ware.

  4. What kinds of evidence support a drought around 1200 BC, and what is their main limitation?
    Answer

    Pollen cores from Syria, Cyprus and the Sea of Galilee, a speleothem from Mavri Trypa near Pylos, and textual grain requests from Hatti and Ugarit. The limitation is dating resolution, roughly a century, and the fact that drought alone cannot explain burnt citadels.

  5. Why is LH IIIC important for correcting the idea of a sudden collapse?
    Answer

    It shows a century of post-palatial life: Tiryns' lower town grew, Mycenae was reoccupied, Perati traded with Egypt and the Levant, and a new pictorial pottery style flourished. Collapse of the palaces was not collapse of society.

  6. What was found in the Toumba building at Lefkandi, and why does it matter for Homer?
    Answer

    A man's cremated bones in a two-centuries-old Cypriot bronze amphora with iron weapons, a richly adorned inhumed woman, and four sacrificed horses, under a mound of about 950 BC. The rite matches the funeral of Patroclus in Iliad 23: pyre, bronze urn, mound, horses.

  7. What defines the Protogeometric style and where did it originate?
    Answer

    Concentric circles and semicircles drawn with a compass-mounted multiple brush, tighter shapes and better-fired glaze, beginning at Athens around 1025 BC and spreading across the Aegean to Ionia.

  8. According to Herodotus 1.146, who made up the Ionian settlers, and what does this do to the Athenian claim?
    Answer

    A mixture of Abantes, Minyans, Cadmeians, Dryopians, Phocians, Molossians, Arcadian Pelasgians and Epidaurian Dorians, who at Miletus married Carian women after killing their men. It undercuts the claim that Ionia was a purely Athenian foundation.

  9. Give two reasons archaeologists abandoned the search for a material "Dorian invasion".
    Answer

    Every proposed marker (cist graves, handmade burnished pottery, iron, Naue II swords, long pins) has other origins or wider distributions; and the dialect map is compatible with movement but does not require conquest. Chadwick proposed Dorians already present as a subject class; Hall argued Dorian identity was an Archaic construction.

  10. Give one example each of accurate Mycenaean memory and of distortion in the Homeric poems.
    Answer

    Accurate: the boar's-tusk helmet (Iliad 10.261–265), bronze weaponry, or the Catalogue of Ships' Mycenaean geography. Distorted: chariots used as battlefield taxis, cremation under mounds, or the basileus promoted from local official to king.

Carry forward

  1. The collapse of c. 1200 BC was multicausal, regional and drawn out over a century; its decisive Greek consequence was the loss of the palace state, its writing and its scale of organisation, not the loss of the Greek people.
  2. The Dark Age was a world of chiefs' households (Lefkandi, Nichoria) in which the basileus became a king, cremation and iron became normal, and small groups drifted east to Ionia; its migration stories, Ionian and Heracleid, are Archaic charters built on real but unrecoverable movements.
  3. Oral epic carried the Mycenaean world across the gap as names, objects and places while rewriting its institutions, and Hesiod's scheme of declining races turned that remembered past into a lost golden age of demigods, the frame through which every later Greek saw the heroes.
