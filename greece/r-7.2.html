Alexandria: the Museum and the Library

After Alexander's death in 323 BC his generals carved the conquered world into kingdoms, and the Ptolemies of Egypt decided that dynastic prestige could be bought in books. Ptolemy I Soter (king 305–282) founded the Museum (Mouseion, a shrine of the Muses) in the palace quarter of Alexandria; Ptolemy II Philadelphus (282–246) enlarged it. Strabo (Geography 17.1.8), who saw it in the 20s BC, describes a covered walk, an exedra, and a hall where salaried scholars dined together under a priest appointed by the king.

The innovation was royal money, a salaried scholarly community and a systematic hunt for texts. Galen (Commentary on Hippocrates' Epidemics 3) says ships entering the harbour were searched for books, and that Ptolemy III forfeited a fifteen-talent deposit rather than return the Athenian state copies of the tragedians. Callimachus produced the Pinakes, a 120-book catalogue of authors and works by genre, the ancestor of every bibliography. Ancient figures for the holdings run from 490,000 rolls (Tzetzes) to 700,000 (Aulus Gellius 7.17.3); many were duplicates.

A papyrus list (P.Oxy. 1241) gives the librarians: Zenodotus of Ephesus, Apollonius of Rhodes, Eratosthenes, Aristophanes of Byzantium, Apollonius "the classifier", Aristarchus of Samothrace. After Aristarchus fled the purges of Ptolemy VIII around 145 BC, scholarship dispersed to Pergamum, Rhodes and Rome.

Where sources disagree. Plutarch (Caesar 49) and Seneca (On Tranquillity 9.5, giving 40,000 rolls) say Caesar's fire of 48 BC destroyed the Library; Dio Cassius 42.38 says only harbour warehouses burned, and Strabo describes the Museum as functioning afterwards. The tale of Arab conquerors burning the books as bath fuel in 642 first appears six centuries later. Most historians now favour slow decline from the second century BC over any single catastrophe.

Editing Homer: Zenodotus, Aristophanes, Aristarchus

Ptolemaic papyri from before about 150 BC show a "wild" Homer, with extra lines and variant phrasing; after that date the papyri settle into the text the medieval manuscripts transmit. Something standardised Homer in the second century BC, and the three great librarians are the obvious candidates.

Zenodotus (fl. c. 280) made the first diorthosis, or corrected edition, of both poems. He marked suspect lines with a horizontal stroke (the obelos) rather than deleting them, and compiled a Homeric glossary; the division of each epic into twenty-four books is traditionally his. His readings often look like conjectures driven by taste: he objected to gods behaving undignifiedly and emended accordingly.

Aristophanes of Byzantium (c. 257–180) sharpened the tools: the system of accents and punctuation, the arrangement of lyric poetry in metrical lines (colometry), and more critical signs. Aristarchus of Samothrace (c. 216–144), his pupil, dominates the Homeric scholia preserved in the tenth-century manuscript Venetus A. He produced two editions and running commentaries (hypomnemata), and extended the marginal signs. His method, summarised by Porphyry as "clarifying Homer from Homer", settled a word's meaning by its usage elsewhere in the poems rather than by etymology or myth. He athetised lines he thought un-Homeric but kept them in the text, which is why the Iliad still contains lines its greatest ancient editor rejected.

Where sources disagree. Martin West (Studies in the Text and Transmission of the Iliad, 2001) argues the standard text was already circulating in the book trade and that Aristarchus' own readings rarely entered it; the disappearance of plus-verses reflects commercial copying from a good exemplar, not editorial decree. Others give the Library a larger role. The timing is clear from the papyri; the mechanism is uncertain.

Hellenistic poetry: Callimachus, Theocritus, Apollonius

The poets of Alexandria were also its scholars, and that explains their verse. Writing for readers who knew the classics in catalogued editions, they prized compression, allusion and the obscure version of a myth over the familiar one. Callimachus of Cyrene (c. 310–240) set out the aesthetic in the prologue to his Aetia (fr. 1 Pfeiffer), the "Reply to the Telchines": his critics complain that he has not written one continuous poem of thousands of lines about kings and heroes; he answers that poetry is judged by art, not length, and that Apollo told him to keep his Muse slender and drive on untrodden paths. Epigram 28 Pf. begins "I hate the cyclic poem".

The Aetia, four books of elegiac "causes" explaining obscure rites, survives in fragments; its "Lock of Berenice" (fr. 110) was translated by Catullus (poem 66). Theocritus of Syracuse (fl. 270s) invented the bucolic idyll: herdsmen competing in song (Idylls 1, 7), the lovesick Cyclops singing to Galatea (Idyll 11), two Syracusan housewives at Ptolemy's Adonis festival (Idyll 15). The monster of Odyssey 9 recast as a comic adolescent is the Hellenistic move in miniature.

Apollonius of Rhodes answered the call for brevity with an epic, but a short one: the Argonautica, four books and under six thousand lines against the Iliad's fifteen thousand. Jason is hesitant and amechanos, "at a loss"; the poem's centre is Book 3, where Medea's falling in love is traced with an inwardness no earlier epic attempted, in a passage Virgil reworked for Dido. It is thick with geography, aetiology and Homeric phrases redeployed in new contexts: epic turned into a scholar's game for readers who would notice.

Where sources disagree. The ancient Lives report a feud between Callimachus and Apollonius, with the lost invective Ibis aimed at his former pupil. Mary Lefkowitz (The Lives of the Greek Poets) argues the quarrel is a fiction spun from the Aetia prologue, whose Telchines are unnamed; the Argonautica shares Callimachus' aesthetic in most respects.

Science: measuring the world

Euclid taught in Alexandria under Ptolemy I (Proclus, Commentary on Euclid I, prologue 2). His Elements in thirteen books organised earlier Greek mathematics into a deductive system and remained the model of proof until the nineteenth century. Archimedes of Syracuse (c. 287–212) found the volume and surface of the sphere (On the Sphere and Cylinder), bounded pi between 3 10/71 and 3 1/7 (Measurement of a Circle), founded hydrostatics (On Floating Bodies), and in the Method, recovered from a palimpsest in 1906, showed that he found results by mechanical reasoning before proving them geometrically. His death at the fall of Syracuse is in Plutarch (Marcellus 19) and Livy 25.31.

Eratosthenes of Cyrene measured the earth. Cleomedes (On the Heavens 1.7) gives the method: at midsummer the sun stood overhead at Syene while at Alexandria, assumed on the same meridian, a gnomon's shadow put it 1/50 of a circle from the zenith; the cities being 5,000 stades apart, the circumference was 250,000 stades, later rounded to 252,000 for divisibility. Whether the result is nearly exact or sixteen per cent too large depends on which stade he used, which is unknown.

Aristarchus of Samos (c. 310–230) proposed that the earth rotates and revolves around a stationary sun. We know this only from Archimedes (Sand-Reckoner 1.4–7) and Plutarch (On the Face in the Moon 6, where Cleanthes thinks he should be charged with impiety); his surviving treatise On the Sizes and Distances of the Sun and Moon is geocentric in frame. Hipparchus of Nicaea (c. 190–120) discovered the precession of the equinoxes, compiled a star catalogue, built the first table of chords and used Babylonian eclipse records; nearly all of it survives only through Ptolemy's Almagest (7.1–3).

In medicine, Herophilus of Chalcedon and Erasistratus of Ceos, in early third-century Alexandria, carried out systematic human dissection, forbidden by scruple elsewhere and again for over a millennium after. Herophilus described the brain's ventricles, distinguished sensory from motor nerves, and named the duodenum and prostate (fragments in von Staden). Celsus (On Medicine, preface 23–26) adds that they dissected living criminals from the royal prisons, which Tertullian (On the Soul 10) turns into the charge of butchery; whether vivisection occurred is debated.

Hero of Alexandria, whose Dioptra 35 describes an eclipse of AD 62, left the Pneumatica with its steam-driven aeolipile, temple automata and the Metrica; Roman in date, he is wholly Alexandrian in tradition. The Antikythera mechanism, recovered from a shipwreck by sponge divers in 1900–1901, is the physical proof of what that tradition could build: a geared bronze device with Metonic and Saros dials, an Olympiad counter, and pin-and-slot gearing that reproduces the moon's variable speed on exactly Hipparchus' model (Freeth and colleagues, Nature 2006 and 2008). It dates from roughly 150–60 BC.

Where sources disagree. Cicero describes planetary spheres by Archimedes (On the Republic 1.21–22) and Posidonius at Rhodes (On the Nature of the Gods 2.88), and Rhodes was long the favoured origin of the mechanism; the Corinthian month-names on its dial point instead to a Corinthian colony, possibly Syracuse (Freeth and Jones 2012). The 2021 reconstruction of a full planetary display is hypothesis, since those gears do not survive.

Stoicism

Zeno of Citium (334–262), a Phoenician from Cyprus who studied under the Cynic Crates, began teaching in the Painted Colonnade (Stoa Poikile) in Athens around 300 BC. Chrysippus of Soli (c. 280–206) systematised the school in over seven hundred books, so that "had there been no Chrysippus, there would be no Stoa" (Diogenes Laertius 7.183). No Hellenistic Stoic work survives whole; the doctrines are reconstructed from Diogenes Laertius book 7, Cicero (On Ends 3, On Fate), Stobaeus, Plutarch and Galen.

In physics everything is body, and the cosmos is a single living thing pervaded by logos, divine reason, identified with god, fate, creative fire and the tensile breath (pneuma) that holds each thing together. The world is periodically consumed in conflagration and reborn identical in every detail. Fate is a seamless chain of causes, yet Chrysippus insisted that action remains "up to us": a pushed cylinder rolls because of its own shape (Cicero, On Fate 42–43). In logic they built the first propositional calculus and a theory of the "cognitive impression" that the sceptics attacked for two centuries.

The end is "living in agreement with nature", which for a rational being means living by reason. Virtue is the only good, vice the only evil; health, wealth, pain and reputation are "indifferent", though some indifferents are "preferred". The passions (pathe) are not forces overwhelming reason but false judgements, assents to the belief that an indifferent thing is good or bad; the sage, having no false beliefs, has no passions, only the rational "good feelings" of joy and caution. Cosmopolitanism is the political face of this: since reason is shared by all and one law governs the cosmos, the world is a single city of gods and men. Plutarch (On the Fortune of Alexander 329a–b) says Zeno's Republic dreamed of one way of life for all people as fellow citizens.

Where sources disagree. Zeno defined a passion as "an excessive impulse", Chrysippus as a judgement. Galen (On the Doctrines of Hippocrates and Plato 4–5) reports that Posidonius rejected Chrysippus' intellectualism and reintroduced irrational parts of the soul to explain why passions persist against better judgement. Whether this was a return to Zeno or a Platonising departure is contested, as is Galen's reliability as a witness.

Epicureanism

Epicurus (341–270) bought a garden outside Athens in 306 and taught a community of friends, including women and slaves, for the rest of his life. Diogenes Laertius book 10 preserves three letters (to Herodotus on physics, Pythocles on meteorology, Menoeceus on ethics) and the forty Principal Doctrines; fragments of his On Nature survive charred from Herculaneum; Lucretius' On the Nature of Things gives the system in Latin hexameters.

The physics is Democritus revised: only atoms and void exist, worlds form and dissolve, and the soul is a fine atomic compound that disperses at death. To rescue free action from determinism the atoms occasionally "swerve" by a minimum (Lucretius 2.216–293; Cicero, On Fate 22). The gods exist, as universal belief attests, but are blessed and imperishable and take no interest in us (KD 1). Atomism removes the fear of death, since "death is nothing to us" (KD 2; Letter to Menoeceus 124–127): where death is, we are not, and the time before our birth troubled no one, as Lucretius' symmetry argument (3.830–1094) presses.

Pleasure is the end, but not the hedonist's. The limit of pleasure is reached when all pain is removed; "static" pleasure (aponia in the body, ataraxia in the mind) outranks the "kinetic" pleasures of variation (Diogenes Laertius 10.136). The wise person lives on little, prizes friendship, avoids politics ("live unnoticed") and knows that pain, if intense, is brief and, if long, mild (KD 4). Philodemus' "fourfold remedy" compresses it: nothing to fear in god, nothing to feel in death, good is easy to get, evil easy to endure.

Where sources disagree. On the "realist" reading (Konstan) Epicurus' gods are atomic compounds living between worlds; on the "idealist" reading (Long and Sedley) they are thought-constructs, idealisations of the blessed life. The texts support both. Whether the swerve was Epicurus' own idea or a later addition is also disputed, since it is absent from his surviving words.

Scepticism and the Cynics

Pyrrho of Elis (c. 365–275) accompanied Alexander to India and wrote nothing; his pupil Timon, in a summary preserved by Aristocles (Eusebius, Preparation for the Gospel 14.18), says he held things to be equally indifferent, unmeasurable and undecidable, and the outcome first speechlessness and then freedom from disturbance. The parallel with the Stoic and Epicurean goal of ataraxia is deliberate: Hellenistic philosophy, across its divisions, sells tranquillity.

The more consequential scepticism grew inside Plato's Academy. Arcesilaus (scholarch c. 268–241) turned the school to arguing against every thesis, attacking Zeno's cognitive impression: for any true impression an indistinguishable false one can be produced, so assent should be withheld (epoche). Asked how one then lives, he answered that "the reasonable" suffices for action (Sextus, Against the Mathematicians 7.158). Carneades of Cyrene (c. 214–129) refined this into a graded account of "persuasive" impressions that could guide life without claiming truth (Cicero, Academica 2.99–104); on an embassy to Rome in 155 BC he argued for justice one day and against it the next (Cicero, On the Republic 3, via Lactantius). Aenesidemus in the first century BC revived a "Pyrrhonism" of ten modes, which Sextus Empiricus codified around AD 200.

The Cynics were a way of life, not a school. Diogenes of Sinope (c. 412–323) took a dog's shamelessness as his emblem, lived in a storage jar, "defaced the currency" of convention, and, asked where he came from, said "I am a citizen of the world" (Diogenes Laertius 6.63), the first recorded kosmopolites. Zeno was the pupil of his successor Crates, and Stoicism carried the Cynic conviction that virtue alone matters into a system. Menippus turned Cynic preaching into the satire that Lucian and Rome inherited.

Where sources disagree. Diogenes Laertius makes Antisthenes the Socratic founder of Cynicism; Dudley (1937) and Goulet-Cazé argue this pedigree was constructed later and Diogenes is the real beginning. Richard Bett reads Timon's summary of Pyrrho as a metaphysical thesis that reality is indeterminate rather than a refusal to judge, so that Sextus' "Pyrrhonism" was largely Aenesidemus' creation.

Koine and the Septuagint

The medium of all this was a new Greek. Alexander's armies carried a simplified Attic across Egypt, Syria and Anatolia, where it became the "common" dialect, koine. It dropped the dual and most of the optative, regularised verbs and favoured prepositions over bare cases. Egyptian papyri show it as the language of tax, farming and letters; the Rosetta Stone (196 BC) shows it as the language of government. From the first century BC grammarians reacted with Atticism, a cult of the old dialect that split written from spoken Greek until 1976.

Its most consequential product was the Septuagint, the Greek Bible of Egyptian Jewry. The Letter of Aristeas, probably second century BC, tells how Ptolemy II, at Demetrius of Phalerum's suggestion, summoned seventy-two elders from Jerusalem who translated the Law in seventy-two days; Philo (Life of Moses 2.25–44) and Josephus (Antiquities 12.11–118) repeat it. The story is legend (Demetrius was exiled by Ptolemy II), but the Pentateuch was translated in third-century Alexandria, the rest over two centuries, for a community that no longer read Hebrew easily.

The Septuagint matters beyond itself for three reasons. It made Jewish scripture part of Greek literature: Ezekiel the Tragedian put the Exodus into iambic trimeters. It became the Bible of the early church and the New Testament's usual text for quotation, so Christian theology was built in koine. And it preserves a Hebrew text older than the medieval Masoretic one: the Greek Jeremiah is about an eighth shorter and differently ordered, and the Qumran fragment 4QJer-b agrees with the Greek, showing the translators worked from a different Hebrew rather than carelessly.

Where sources disagree. Alexandria or Palestine as the place; synagogue, schoolroom, royal library or Ptolemaic legal need as the motive; one event or several: all are debated. Later Jewish revisers (the "kaige" group, Aquila, Theodotion, Symmachus) pulled the Greek towards the emerging standard Hebrew, and Origen laid them side by side in his Hexapla; the Septuagint we print is thus itself a layered text, like the Alexandrian Homer.

Self-test

  1. Which ancient author describes the Museum's buildings and communal dining from eyewitness knowledge, and where?
    Answer

    Strabo, Geography 17.1.8, who visited Alexandria in the 20s BC.

  2. What was Callimachus' Pinakes?
    Answer

    A 120-book catalogue of Greek authors and works arranged by genre, the first systematic bibliography, compiled at the Alexandrian Library.

  3. What is the obelos, who introduced it, and why does it matter that athetised lines were kept?
    Answer

    A marginal stroke marking a line as spurious, introduced by Zenodotus. Because Alexandrian editors marked rather than deleted, the vulgate still contains lines they rejected; the papyri show plus-verses vanishing after c. 150 BC, but whether editorial authority or book-trade copying caused this is disputed.

  4. Summarise the aesthetic programme of the Aetia prologue.
    Answer

    Callimachus answers the Telchines that poetry is judged by art, not length; Apollo tells him to keep his Muse slender and follow untrodden paths. Learned, small-scale, allusive verse over long heroic epic.

  5. How did Eratosthenes measure the earth, and why is the accuracy of his result uncertain?
    Answer

    He compared the sun overhead at Syene with its 1/50-circle angle from the zenith at Alexandria at midsummer and multiplied the 5,000-stade distance by 50 to get 250,000 (later 252,000) stades. Accuracy depends on the length of his stade, which is unknown.

  6. What is our evidence for Aristarchus of Samos' heliocentrism?
    Answer

    Reports in Archimedes, Sand-Reckoner 1.4–7, and Plutarch, On the Face in the Moon 6; his own surviving treatise on the sizes and distances of sun and moon is geocentric in frame.

  7. What does the Antikythera mechanism's pin-and-slot gearing reproduce, and what does that imply?
    Answer

    The moon's variable apparent speed on Hipparchus' lunar theory, showing Hellenistic astronomy and precision mechanics combined before 60 BC.

  8. Why is a passion a false judgement for Chrysippus, and what follows about the sage?
    Answer

    A passion is assent to the belief that an indifferent thing (wealth, pain, death) is good or bad; since virtue alone is good, the belief is false. The sage, holding no false beliefs, has no passions, only rational good feelings.

  9. State Epicurus' argument that death is nothing to us, and the extra argument Lucretius adds.
    Answer

    Good and evil consist in sensation; death ends sensation because the soul's atoms disperse; so death cannot be bad for the one who dies (KD 2; Letter to Menoeceus 124–127). Lucretius 3 adds the symmetry argument: we were untroubled by the time before birth, so should be by the time after death.

  10. What is the Letter of Aristeas, what does it claim, and what internal flaw exposes the claim as legend?
    Answer

    A Hellenistic Jewish text (probably 2nd century BC) claiming Ptolemy II commissioned seventy-two elders to translate the Law at Demetrius of Phalerum's suggestion. Demetrius had been exiled by Ptolemy II and could not have advised him; the Pentateuch was nonetheless translated in third-century Alexandria.

Carry forward

  1. The texts you read of Homer, and of the Hebrew Bible in Greek, are Hellenistic editorial products: Alexandria standardised the Iliad and created the Septuagint, and every later module that quotes either depends on choices made there.
  2. Hellenistic poetry retold myth for readers who already knew it, so allusion, variant versions and learned aetiology became the norm; Roman poets (Catullus, Virgil, Ovid) inherit this mode, not the archaic one.
  3. Stoics, Epicureans and Sceptics all promise tranquillity by correcting belief; their vocabulary (logos, ataraxia, cosmopolitanism, the fear of death) is what Rome, early Christianity and the Renaissance received as "Greek philosophy".
