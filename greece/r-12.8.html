01Pyrrho and Timon

Module 7.2 gives Pyrrho's journey with Alexander and Timon's summary. The life is Diogenes Laertius 9.61–108, drawn largely from the biographer Antigonus of Carystus. Pyrrho of Elis began as a painter and followed Anaxarchus east, where he met the Indian naked sages and the Magi (9.61); an Indian's rebuke of Anaxarchus for courting kings sent him into private life (9.63). Elis made him high priest (9.64). He lived with his sister, a midwife, and took poultry and pigs to market; frightened by a dog, he said it was hard to strip off the human being entirely (9.66). He wrote nothing.

Timon of Phlius (c. 320–230), a former dancer, studied with Stilpo and then with Pyrrho (9.109–112); his Silloi mock every dogmatist except Pyrrho (9.65, 9.111). The summary comes through a chain of reporters: Eusebius (Preparation for the Gospel 14.18.1–5) quotes the hostile Peripatetic Aristocles, who quotes Timon, who reports Pyrrho. Things are equally indifferent, unmeasurable and undecidable; for this reason our perceptions and opinions neither tell the truth nor lie; so we should trust neither, but say of each thing that it no more is than is not, or both, or neither. The outcome is speechlessness, then freedom from disturbance; Aenesidemus, Aristocles adds, said pleasure (14.18.4).

Where sources disagree. Antigonus said Pyrrho avoided neither carts nor cliffs and was kept alive by friends; Aenesidemus answered that he suspended judgement in philosophy but was not improvident in daily life (Diogenes Laertius 9.62). Moderns divide over Aristocles' text. Its manuscripts make the nature of things the reason why perceptions are neither true nor false, and Bett (Pyrrho, his Antecedents, and his Legacy, 2000), whose metaphysical reading module 7.2 gives, keeps them. Zeller emended "for this reason" to "because", and Stopper (1983) defended the change: things are undecidable for us because the senses fail, and Pyrrho becomes Sextus' epistemic sceptic.

02Arcesilaus and Carneades: the Academy turns

Module 7.2 outlines Arcesilaus' attack on Zeno, his "reasonable" and Carneades' persuasive impressions. What needs explaining is why Plato's school turned. Arcesilaus of Pitane (c. 316–241), head from about 268, wrote nothing (Diogenes Laertius 4.28–45). Cicero, speaking for the New Academy, says he denied that anything can be known, not even the one thing Socrates had left himself (Academica 1.45), and adds that Plato's books affirm nothing (1.46): the reading of the dialogues that module 12.3 sets against the Neoplatonists'.

His method was to argue against any thesis until the sides balanced, and his target was the Stoa. Zeno defined the cognitive impression as one that comes from what is, stamped exactly in accordance with it; Cicero says he added, under Arcesilaus' pressure, that it must be such as could not come from what is not (Academica 2.77). Arcesilaus answered that any true impression could have an indistinguishable false twin, and turned the Stoic premise that the wise man never opines against its owners: if nothing is cognitive, the wise man assents to nothing (Against the Mathematicians 7.150–157). Plutarch reports the reply that action needs impression and impulse, not assent (Against Colotes 1122a–d).

Carneades of Cyrene (214/13–129/8) reportedly said that without Chrysippus there would have been no Carneades (Diogenes Laertius 4.62). Sextus gives his graded criterion: an impression may be persuasive; persuasive and unreversed; or persuasive, unreversed and tested, the higher grades being for weightier matters (Against the Mathematicians 7.166–189). He turned the method on Stoic theology, with a sorites from Zeus to the gods of rivers (9.182–184), and on fate, arguing against the Epicureans that a voluntary motion of the mind needs no swerve, being its own cause (Cicero, On Fate 23). His embassy to Rome in 155 is in Rome module 4.5.

Where sources disagree. Did the Academics hold their scepticism or only argue it? Sextus says Arcesilaus called suspension good, which is a kind of dogma, and repeats a rumour that he was a Platonist testing his pupils (Outlines of Pyrrhonism 1.232–234). Clitomachus never discovered what Carneades approved; Metrodorus and Philo said he let the wise man opine, and Cicero, trusting Clitomachus, thinks this was argued rather than approved (Academica 2.78, 2.139). Couissin (1929) and Striker (1980) read the arguments as dialectical, drawn from Stoic premises the Academics did not share; Frede (1979, 1984) and Cooper (2004) think the Academics came to hold, without claiming knowledge, the views their arguments left them with.

03Clitomachus, Philo and Antiochus

Clitomachus, a Carthaginian named Hasdrubal, wrote more than four hundred books (Diogenes Laertius 4.67); Cicero follows one of them for two kinds of assent, withheld in the strict sense but given to the persuasive in action (Academica 2.98–104). Philo of Larissa (159/8–84/3), head from 110/09, moved from that line to a scepticism that allowed opinion. The Mithridatic war drove him to Rome in 88 (Cicero, Brutus 306), where his Roman Books denied that there had ever been two Academies (Academica 1.13) and held things inapprehensible by the Stoic criterion but apprehensible in their own nature (Sextus, Outlines 1.235).

His pupil Antiochus of Ascalon read them at Alexandria, answered with a book called Sosus (Academica 2.11–12) and returned to doctrine: Plato, Xenocrates, Polemo and Aristotle had shared one philosophy, the Old Academy, whose terms Zeno revised (Academica 1.15–42). He accepted the cognitive impression and demanded that the Academics at least grasp their own thesis that nothing can be grasped (2.28–29). Cicero calls him, but for a very few changes, a most genuine Stoic (2.132). His Roman following belongs to Rome module 13.3.

Where sources disagree. The Roman Books are lost, recovered from Antiochus' outrage as Cicero reports it and from one sentence of Sextus. Tarrant (Scepticism or Platonism?, 1985) read them as a turn towards a Platonist theory of knowledge; Brittain (Philo of Larissa: The Last of the Academic Sceptics, 2001) reads them as fallibilism, knowledge without the Stoic guarantee that an impression could not be false. Either way the sceptical Academy ended with Philo as a school; its heir was Aenesidemus.

04Aenesidemus and Sextus Empiricus

Aenesidemus dedicated his Pyrrhonian Discourses to the Academic Lucius Tubero and complained, Photius reports, that the Academics of his day were Stoics fighting Stoics (Bibliotheca 212). If Tubero is Cicero's friend, as usually assumed, the break belongs to the first century BC; Aristocles calls the revival recent and places it in Alexandria (Eusebius, Preparation for the Gospel 14.18.29). Aenesidemus took Pyrrho's name for a scepticism that would not assert even that things are inapprehensible, and collected the modes, argument-forms that produce suspension: ten in Diogenes (9.79–88), nine in Aristocles (14.18.11), and eight more against causal explanation in Sextus (Outlines 1.180–186).

Sextus Empiricus, last but one in Diogenes' succession of Pyrrhonists (9.116), was a doctor of the Empiric school and wrote about AD 200, a guess. Besides the three books of Outlines, Against the Mathematicians 1–6 attacks the arts and books 7–11 the dogmatists' logic, physics and ethics. Scepticism is an ability to oppose appearances and thoughts so that, from the equal force of the opposed accounts, we reach first suspension and then tranquillity (Outlines 1.8). Tranquillity follows by luck, as when the painter Apelles, failing to paint a horse's foam, threw his sponge at the picture and produced it (1.28–29). The sceptic lives by appearances, nature, feeling, custom and craft (1.23–24), granting that honey appears sweet and doubting only whether it is (1.20). His slogans expel themselves like purgatives (1.206), and the argument against proof is kicked away like a ladder once climbed (Against the Mathematicians 8.481).

Sextus quotes Xenophanes' fragment DK 21 B 34 as, on some readings, a denial that anything can be grasped, likening inquirers to men hunting gold in a dark room who cannot know when they hold it (Against the Mathematicians 7.49–52). Yet he refuses Xenophanes the name, since his one god is dogma, and quotes Timon calling him only half free of vanity (Outlines 1.224–225). The ten modes, in Sextus' order (1.35–163):

ModeOutlines 1Example
1. Differences among animals40–78Hemlock feeds quails and kills men
2. Differences among humans79–90Alexander's servant Demophon shivered in the sun
3. Differences among the senses91–99Is an apple one quality or many?
4. Circumstances100–117Sleep, sickness, age, love, hunger
5. Positions, distances, places118–123A colonnade seen from one end tapers
6. Admixtures124–128Nothing reaches the senses unmixed
7. Quantities and compositions129–134Goat's horn filings look white, the horn black
8. Relativity135–140Everything appears relative to the judge and to its company
9. Frequency and rarity141–144A comet astonishes; the sun does not
10. Customs, laws, myths, dogmas145–163Peoples' beliefs about the gods conflict

The five modes of "the more recent sceptics" (1.164–169), whom Diogenes heads with Agrippa (9.88), are sharper: disagreement, regress, relativity, hypothesis and circularity. Any proof rests on an endless chain, an assumption or itself.

Where sources disagree. Sextus says the sceptic holds no belief in the sense of assent to what is unclear, but does not reject what appearances force on him (Outlines 1.13). Burnyeat ("Can the Sceptic Live His Scepticism?", 1980) took this to rule out belief altogether, so that the Pyrrhonist cannot live his scepticism consistently; Frede ("Des Skeptikers Meinungen", 1979) argued that only theoretical belief is renounced and everyday belief remains. Barnes (1982) called the two Pyrrhonists rustic and urbane; Sextus can be cited for each.

05Middle Platonists and Plotinus

Between Antiochus and Plotinus Platonism became dogma again. Alcinous' Handbook of Platonism makes the first god an intellect thinking itself and the Forms its thoughts (9–10), the Timaeus fused with Aristotle's god. Plutarch (module 7.4) read the Timaeus' creation literally (module 12.3) and traced disorder to a pre-cosmic irrational soul (On the Generation of the Soul in the Timaeus). Numenius of Apamea set a first god above the Demiurge and asked, Clement reports, what Plato is but Moses speaking Attic (Stromateis 1.22).

Module 7.4 gives Plotinus' hierarchy and the edition; here are the arguments. Plotinus (204/5–270) found in Ammonius Saccas the teacher he sought, followed Gordian III east to learn Persian and Indian wisdom, and settled at Rome at forty (Porphyry, Life of Plotinus 3). He called himself an exegete: the Parmenides distinguishes a first One, a One-many and a One-and-many, so the three hypostases are Plato's (Enneads 5.1.8). That is the Neoplatonist theology module 12.3 hands on.

The One is argued from unity. Everything that is, is by being one; an army or a house ceases to be when its unity goes (6.9.1). The composite depends on what makes it one, so the first must be simple. Intellect cannot be first, because thinking is two, thinker and thought, even in Aristotle's self-thinking god (5.3.10–11; treatise 5.6). The first is beyond being, as Plato said of the Good (Republic 509b), and beyond speech.

Why does anything come from it? Whatever is perfect produces, as fire warms and snow cools (5.4.1); each thing has an activity that is its being and one that goes out from it (5.4.2). The One gives without loss, like the light round the sun (5.1.6), and what proceeds is constituted by turning back: Intellect is a sight that becomes seeing by looking towards the One (5.3.11). Intellect is the world of Forms, which are not outside it, or it would hold images, not truth (treatise 5.5). Soul stands to Intellect as spoken word to thought (5.1.3) and thinks one thing after another, where Intellect is all things at once (5.1.4); so it orders the cosmos in succession, and time is its life passing from one thing to the next (3.7.11). At the bottom is matter, privation and evil itself (treatise 1.8). The descent maps the return: the last treatise ends with the flight of the alone to the alone (6.9.11), and Porphyry says Plotinus reached union four times in their six years together (Life 23).

06Porphyry, Iamblichus and Proclus

Porphyry (c. 234–c. 305), whose works module 7.4 lists, hesitated over ritual: Augustine reports that he let theurgy purify the lower, "spiritual" soul but not the intellect (City of God 10.9). His Letter to Anebo put his doubts to an Egyptian priest, and Iamblichus (c. 245–c. 325) replied in a priest's name with On the Mysteries. Its decisive argument: thought does not join the theurgist to the gods, or philosophers would be joined by theorising; the performance of ineffable acts and symbols understood by the gods alone does (2.11).

Where sources disagree. Plotinus, daring, he says, to speak against the opinion of others, held that part of our soul never descends but stays always in the intelligible (Enneads 4.8.8). Iamblichus rejected this, and Proclus states the rejection as a theorem: every particular soul descends entire (Elements of Theology, prop. 211). The stakes are practical: if the higher soul never fell, contemplation can reach the One unaided; if the whole soul fell, it needs help from above, which is theurgy's case. Module 7.4 has the modern quarrel over whether theurgy was magic or theology.

Proclus (412–485), head at Athens after Syrianus, built the system as geometry. The Elements of Theology proves 211 propositions in order: every manifold participates in unity (prop. 1); every productive cause is superior to its product (prop. 7); every effect remains in its cause, proceeds from it and reverts upon it (prop. 35), so causation is a circle, not a chain. Between the One and Intellect he set the henads, gods as unities beyond being, which brought the pantheon inside the system. Marinus' Life of Proclus shows the practice: hymns, Chaldaean rites, and a wish that of all ancient books only the Chaldaean Oracles and the Timaeus might survive.

07Hypatia as a philosopher

Module 7.4 tells Hypatia's murder in 415. Her father Theon headed his commentary on book 3 of Ptolemy's Almagest as an edition revised by his daughter, the philosopher Hypatia, and the Suda credits her with commentaries on Diophantus and on Apollonius' Conics. Socrates Scholasticus says she succeeded to the Platonic school descended from Plotinus (Church History 7.15); Damascius, through the Suda, that she expounded Plato and Aristotle in the philosopher's cloak to anyone who wished. Her pupil Synesius of Cyrene, later a bishop, calls her mother, sister and teacher and sends her his books for judgement.

Where sources disagree. Socrates Scholasticus, a Christian, makes her the heir of Plotinus' school; Damascius, a pagan writing a century later, says Isidore differed from her not only as a man from a woman but as a genuine philosopher from a geometer. Watts (Hypatia, 2017) takes her for a Platonist who kept theurgy out of her teaching, which would explain why Christians such as Synesius studied with her. No philosophical writing of hers survives to test either verdict.

08529, Damascius and the road to Persia

Module 7.4 gives the measure and the dispute between Cameron and Watts over whether it closed the school. Malalas says that in the consulship of Decius (529) a decree went to Athens that no one should teach philosophy or expound the laws (Chronicle 18.47); Justinian's Code barred pagans from teaching (Codex Justinianus 1.11.10). Alexandria shows what the measure missed. Ammonius son of Hermias had come to terms with the bishop there, as Damascius sneers (Life of Isidore); the Christian John Philoponus published Against Proclus on the Eternity of the World in 529 itself; and the pagan Olympiodorus was still lecturing on Plato and Aristotle after 565.

Damascius, the last head at Athens, pushed past Proclus' first principle. His Problems and Solutions concerning First Principles opens with a dilemma: if the principle of all things is one of them, it is not the principle of all; if it is beyond them, "all" is not all. Above the One he set the wholly ineffable. Simplicius, pupil of Ammonius and of Damascius, wrote his commentaries on Aristotle, usually dated after 532, attacking Philoponus on the eternity of the world and preserving much of the Presocratics (module 12.1).

Agathias, writing in the 570s, names the seven who went to Khusro I: Damascius, Simplicius, Eulamius, Priscian of Lydia, Hermias, Diogenes and Isidore of Gaza. Disliking the prevailing belief about the divine and hearing that Persia had a philosopher-king, they found the Persians arrogant and lawless, and came home under a clause of the peace of 532 that let them keep their beliefs unmolested (2.30–31).

Where sources disagree. Agathias makes Khusro a philosophical pretender, dazzled by a Syrian charlatan named Uranius, and the visit a disillusionment. Two survivals tell against the caricature: Priscian's Solutions to the Problems Raised by Khosroes, extant in Latin translation, answers the king's questions on the soul, sleep, the tides and other matters of natural philosophy, and Paul the Persian dedicated a treatise on Aristotle's logic to him. Whether Khusro's interest was serious or a court fashion, the sources do not settle.

09Self-test

  1. Through whom does the summary of Pyrrho's philosophy reach us, and what textual choice divides Bett from Stopper?
    Answer

    Eusebius quotes the hostile Peripatetic Aristocles, who quotes Timon, who reports Pyrrho (Preparation for the Gospel 14.18.1–5). The manuscripts make the indeterminacy of things the reason why perceptions are neither true nor false, which Bett keeps as a metaphysical thesis; Zeller's emendation to "because", defended by Stopper, makes things undecidable for us because the senses fail.

  2. How did Arcesilaus use the Stoics' own premises to reach universal suspension?
    Answer

    The Stoics held that the wise man never opines and assents only to cognitive impressions. Arcesilaus argued that for any true impression an indistinguishable false one is possible, so nothing is cognitive; by the Stoic premise the wise man must then assent to nothing (Against the Mathematicians 7.150–157).

  3. What are Carneades' grades of persuasive impression, and which matters call for the higher grades?
    Answer

    Persuasive; persuasive and unreversed; persuasive, unreversed and tested, the higher grades used for weightier matters (Against the Mathematicians 7.166–189).

  4. What does Cicero report about Carneades and opinion, and why does it matter for the dialectical reading?
    Answer

    Metrodorus and Philo said Carneades let the wise man opine; Clitomachus said he never discovered what Carneades approved, and Cicero sides with Clitomachus that the thesis was argued, not approved (Academica 2.78, 2.139). If Clitomachus is right, Carneades' positions may all be dialectical, as Couissin and Striker hold.

  5. What did Philo's Roman Books claim, and how did Antiochus respond?
    Answer

    That there had only ever been one Academy (Academica 1.13), and that things are inapprehensible by the Stoic criterion but apprehensible in their own nature (Outlines 1.235). Antiochus answered with the Sosus (2.11–12) and returned to doctrine: one Old Academy whose terms Zeno revised, which made him, Cicero says, but for a very few changes, a most genuine Stoic (2.132).

  6. What does Proclus' proposition 35 assert, and why does it make causation a circle rather than a chain?
    Answer

    Every effect remains in its cause, proceeds from it and reverts upon it (Elements of Theology, prop. 35). The product is never cut off from its source, and its return completes the movement its procession began.

  7. How does Sextus define scepticism, and how does tranquillity follow from it?
    Answer

    As an ability to oppose appearances and thoughts so that, from the equal force of opposed accounts, we reach suspension and then tranquillity (Outlines 1.8). Tranquillity is not argued for but follows suspension by luck, like Apelles' sponge producing the foam he could not paint (1.28–29).

  8. What does Alexandria show about the reach of Justinian's measure of 529?
    Answer

    Malalas' decree went to Athens (Chronicle 18.47). At Alexandria Ammonius had come to terms with the bishop, Philoponus published Against Proclus in 529, and the pagan Olympiodorus still lectured on Plato and Aristotle after 565, so the measure struck Athens and left Alexandria teaching.

  9. Why, for Plotinus, can Intellect not be the first principle?
    Answer

    Whatever is, is by being one (Enneads 6.9.1), so the first must be simple. Thinking always involves the duality of thinker and thought, even when it thinks itself (5.3.10–11; treatise 5.6), so the first is beyond thought and being, as Plato said of the Good.

  10. On what do Plotinus and Proclus disagree about the soul, and why does it bear on theurgy?
    Answer

    Plotinus held that part of the soul never descends (Enneads 4.8.8); Proclus, following Iamblichus, that every particular soul descends entire (Elements of Theology, prop. 211). A wholly fallen soul cannot rise by thought alone and needs the rites and symbols Iamblichus defends (On the Mysteries 2.11).

10Carry forward

  1. Whether the soul can rise by thought alone, as Plotinus' undescended soul allows (Enneads 4.8.8), or fell entire and needs rites given from above (Iamblichus; Proclus, Elements of Theology, prop. 211), is the late form of the question of philosophy's relation to religion; Iamblichus' answer made Proclus' school at Athens a community of cult as well as of study.
  2. Ancient scepticism was a technique before it was a thesis: argue from the opponent's premises, set equal accounts against each other, and expose regress, assumption or circularity in any proof. Through the Latin Outlines of 1562 it returned to Montaigne and Descartes.
  3. The late Platonist universe of procession and return (Elements of Theology, prop. 35) outlived the schools: it passed into Christian theology through the pseudo-Dionysius and into Arabic and Latin philosophy through the Theology of Aristotle and the Book of Causes, while the closure of 529 struck Athens and left Alexandria teaching.

The prompt this answers

Give me an in-depth report on Greek scepticism and the later Platonists, from Pyrrho to AD 529, extending modules 7.2 and 7.4, which outline both, carry the disputes over Pyrrho, over theurgy and over the closing of the Academy, and already tell Hypatia's death and the journey of Damascius and Simplicius to Khusro's court and back in 532: point back to them and go deeper. Cover: Pyrrho and Timon; the Academy's sceptical turn (Arcesilaus, Carneades, Clitomachus, Philo of Larissa) and Antiochus of Ascalon's return to doctrine; Aenesidemus and Sextus Empiricus (Outlines of Pyrrhonism and the modes); Middle Platonism in a paragraph (Plutarch and Alcinous); Plotinus (the Enneads, Porphyry's Life of Plotinus), with the One, Intellect and Soul argued rather than listed; Porphyry, Iamblichus and theurgy, and Proclus; Hypatia as a philosopher (module 7.4 tells her death); Justinian's measure of 529 and the argument over what it did; and Damascius, Simplicius and the philosophers who went to the Persian court (Agathias 2.30–31). Format: about 2500 words, headed sections, name sources, flag disagreements, end with a 10-question self-test with answers and the three ideas I must carry forward.
