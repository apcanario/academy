01Draco's code (621/0)

Seventh-century Athens was run by the Eupatridai through nine annual archons chosen by birth and wealth and through the Areopagus, a council of ex-archons for life (Athenaion Politeia 3). Around 632 Cylon, an Olympic victor, seized the Acropolis; his supporters were lured out of sanctuary and killed, and the blood guilt fell on the Alcmaeonid archon Megacles (Herodotus 5.71; Thucydides 1.126). That curse would be invoked against Alcmaeonids for a century and a half. Draco legislated a decade later, in the archonship of Aristaechmus (Athenaion Politeia 4.1). What survives securely is his homicide law, republished on stone in 409/8 (IG I³ 104). It distinguishes intentional from unintentional killing, gives jurisdiction to the basileis and the fifty-one ephetai, lays out which kinsmen may pardon, and protects a man who kills a thief or adulterer caught in the act. Its importance is procedural: killing moved from vendetta into a written public process that aristocratic judges could no longer make up case by case.

The rest is reputation: Demades' quip that Draco wrote in blood, with death for idleness and for stealing vegetables (Plutarch, Solon 17); Aristotle's verdict that nothing was notable but the severity (Politics 1274b). Solon repealed everything except the homicide law (Athenaion Politeia 7.1), which is why only that law survived to be reinscribed.

Where sources disagree. Athenaion Politeia 4 describes a full "constitution of Draco": citizenship for all who bore arms, a council of 401 by lot, fines reckoned in minae. Rhodes, Hignett and Wallace treat the chapter as a late fifth- or fourth-century oligarchic fiction inserted into the text; it assumes coinage Athens did not yet have and mirrors the programme of the 411 oligarchs. Treat Draco as a lawgiver, not a constitution-maker.

02The crisis of debt bondage

Athenaion Politeia 2 and Plutarch (Solon 13) describe Attica a generation later: the many enslaved to the few; the poor farming the land of the rich as pelatai and hektemoroi ("sixth-parters"); every loan secured on the borrower's person, so that defaulters and their families were sold, some abroad. Solon's own verses confirm Athenians sold into slavery and men fleeing debt (fr. 36 West). The horoi he says he uprooted were markers recording the obligation on the land or the man who worked it. Behind this lay population pressure on poor soil, bad harvests that forced smallholders to borrow seed from richer neighbours, and a law that gave creditors the debtor's body; there was no coinage, and obligations were in produce.

Where sources disagree. Plutarch says the hektemoroi paid a sixth of their produce; some moderns read the name as meaning they kept a sixth, a servile tenancy. Wider still: were they free smallholders who had mortgaged ancestral plots (Woodhouse, Finley) or a hereditary dependent class whose status Solon abolished without touching land ownership (Rhodes, Gallant)? Solon fr. 34 says he refused to give good and bad an equal share of the land, so there was no redistribution. The economics remain uncertain.

03Solon's reforms (594/3)

Solon, a Eupatrid of moderate means and a poet known for a Salamis elegy, was elected archon and mediator for 594/3 (Plutarch, Solon 14.3; Athenaion Politeia 5.2). Athenaion Politeia 12 lets him speak: he stood "like a wolf among many dogs" (fr. 37), holding a shield over both parties.

The seisachtheia

The "shaking-off of burdens" cancelled existing debts and banned for the future all loans secured on the person (Athenaion Politeia 6.1; Plutarch, Solon 15). Solon bought back Athenians sold abroad, some of whom "no longer spoke the Attic tongue" (fr. 36), and freed those enslaved at home. Both sources report, and Athenaion Politeia rejects, the charge that his friends borrowed heavily knowing cancellation was coming.

The four property classes

Wealth, measured in annual produce, replaced birth as the qualification for office (Athenaion Politeia 7.3–4; Plutarch, Solon 18):

ClassQualificationAccess
Pentakosiomedimnoi500 measuresAll offices; treasurers of Athena from this class only
Hippeis300 measuresArchonship and major offices
Zeugitai200 measuresLesser offices; the hoplite class
Thetesbelow 200Assembly and lawcourts only

Athenaion Politeia 7.4 cites an Acropolis dedication by Anthemion son of Diphilus recording his rise from thete to hippeus. The classes may be older military categories; Solon tied political rights to them.

The Council of 400

Solon is said to have created a council of 400, a hundred from each of the four Ionian tribes, to prepare business for the assembly, leaving the Areopagus as guardian of the laws (Athenaion Politeia 8.4; Plutarch, Solon 19, who calls the two councils the ship's twin anchors).

Where sources disagree. Hignett argued the Council of 400 never existed and was invented to give the oligarchs of 411 a precedent. Rhodes and most recent work accept it, noting the "Boule" that resists Cleomenes in Herodotus 5.72 before a Cleisthenic council can have met. Separately, Athenaion Politeia's intervals between Solon and Peisistratus do not fit 594/3, and Hammond and Sealey have moved the legislation to 592/1 or later.

The Heliaia and the right of appeal

Athenaion Politeia 9.1 names Solon's three most "democratic" measures: the ban on loans on the person; the right of any citizen who wished (ho boulomenos) to prosecute on behalf of a wronged party; and ephesis, appeal from a magistrate's verdict to the dikasterion, the people sitting as a court. Plutarch (Solon 18.2–3) adds that the thetes' admission to the courts seemed trivial and proved decisive. Whether the Heliaia was the assembly sitting judicially (Hansen) or a separate sworn body is debated.

Other laws and his poetry

The laws were inscribed on wooden axones. Solon forbade the export of produce except olive oil (Plutarch, Solon 24), regulated wills and funerals, and is credited by Athenaion Politeia 10 with reforming weights, measures and coinage, though Athens struck no coins before the mid sixth century. Herodotus 1.29 says the Athenians swore to keep the laws ten years; Plutarch and Athenaion Politeia say a hundred. His meeting with Croesus (Herodotus 1.30–33) is chronologically impossible.

His poetry is the only contemporary voice in the whole period. Fr. 4 preaches that dysnomia brings countless ills and eunomia sets all in order; fr. 36 is his defence before the "black earth, mother of the Olympian gods", blaming the leaders' greed, not the gods. Fr. 5 states his principle:

I gave the people as much privilege as suffices.

Herodotus barely treats Solon as a reformer; the detail depends on Athenaion Politeia and Plutarch, writing over two centuries later and already shaping Solon into the father of democracy.

04The regional factions

Solon pleased nobody fully. Athenaion Politeia 13 records two years with no archon (probably 590/89 and 586/5), then Damasias, who clung to the archonship for two years and two months until forced out, and a compromise board of ten archons: five Eupatrids, three farmers, two craftsmen. Herodotus 1.59 and Athenaion Politeia 13.4 then describe three regional factions: the Plain under Lycurgus son of Aristolaides, oligarchic and landed; the Coast under Megacles son of Alcmaeon, "moderate"; and the men beyond the hills (Herodotus' hyperakrioi, Athenaion Politeia's diakrioi) under Peisistratus, including the poorest and those "of impure descent" who feared a review of the citizen roll. The reality was three aristocratic networks, each rooted in a district.

05Peisistratus: three attempts and his rule

Peisistratus, of a family claiming Neleid descent and based at Brauron, won fame against Megara by capturing Nisaea. Herodotus 1.59–64 and Athenaion Politeia 14–17 tell the story.

  1. First attempt (c. 561/0). He wounded himself and his mules, drove into the Agora claiming an ambush, and on Aristion's motion was voted a bodyguard of club-bearers, with which he seized the Acropolis. He ruled without disturbing the offices until Megacles and Lycurgus combined to expel him, in the sixth year (Athenaion Politeia 14.3).
  2. Second attempt. Megacles, losing to Lycurgus, offered Peisistratus his daughter and restoration. A tall woman, Phye of Paeania, was dressed as Athena and driven into the city with heralds announcing the goddess was bringing Peisistratus home; Herodotus (1.60) calls it by far the silliest trick ever played on people reputed the cleverest in Greece. Peisistratus refused to have children by the Alcmaeonid bride, Megacles turned, and Peisistratus left Attica.
  3. Third attempt (c. 546). Ten years of exile: Rhaecelus on the Thermaic Gulf, silver from Mount Pangaeum, money and men from Thebes, Eretria, Lygdamis of Naxos and Argos. Landing at Marathon, he caught the Athenians at Pallene after their midday meal and routed them (Herodotus 1.62–63), then lodged hostage sons of his opponents with Lygdamis, whom he made tyrant of Naxos.
Where sources disagree. Athenaion Politeia 17.1 gives thirty-three years from first coup to death, nineteen in power; Aristotle, Politics 1315b, gives seventeen in power. Herodotus' exile lengths and the archon-dated intervals cannot all be reconciled; 561/0, 546/5 and 527/6 are conventions.

Herodotus says Peisistratus governed by the existing laws and governed well; Athenaion Politeia 16 calls his rule "the age of Cronus" and credits loans to small farmers, travelling deme judges, and a policy of keeping the people busy in the countryside. Thucydides 6.54.6 adds that a Peisistratid always held one of the offices. Athenaion Politeia 16.4 has him take a tithe on produce; Thucydides 6.54.5 says a twentieth, perhaps a reduction under the sons.

The Panathenaia, Homer and the buildings

The Great Panathenaia, every fourth year with athletic and musical contests, was reorganised in 566/5 under the archon Hippocleides (Pherecydes, FGrH 3 F 2), before the first coup; the tyrants enlarged it. Plato's Hipparchus (228b–c) says Hipparchus made the rhapsodes recite Homer at the festival in relay, each taking up where the last stopped, and brought Anacreon and Simonides to Athens.

Where sources disagree. Cicero (De oratore 3.137), Pausanias 7.26.13 and the scholia credit Peisistratus with first assembling the books of Homer, the "Peisistratean recension". No source before the Hellenistic period mentions it; Davison and West treat it as a late construction, Merkelbach defended a core, and Nagy's model gives the Panathenaic rule a real part in fixing the text without any editor. The performance is attested; the edition is not.

The buildings are attested in stone. Peisistratus the Younger, the tyrant's grandson, as archon in 522/1 dedicated the Altar of the Twelve Gods in the Agora and the altar of Pythian Apollo, whose inscribed base survives (Thucydides 6.54.6–7). The dynasty began the colossal Olympieion, which Aristotle (Politics 1313b) lists among tyrants' devices for keeping subjects busy; the Enneakrounos fountain-house (Pausanias 1.14.1), the first telesterion at Eleusis and work at Brauron belong to them. Hipparchus set herms along the roads inscribed with maxims such as "walk thinking just thoughts" (Plato, Hipparchus 228d–229b).

06Hippias, Hipparchus and the tyrannicides

Peisistratus died in 527/6 and his eldest son Hippias ruled, with Hipparchus as patron of poets and Thessalus, son of an Argive wife, as a hot-headed third. Thucydides (1.20, 6.54–59) insists on Hippias' rule against the popular belief that Hipparchus was tyrant when killed.

In 514, at the Great Panathenaia, Harmodius and Aristogeiton, Gephyraeans, killed Hipparchus by the Leocoreion as he marshalled the procession (Herodotus 5.55–56; Thucydides 6.56–57). Thucydides' motive is a love quarrel: Hipparchus courted Harmodius, Aristogeiton's beloved, was rebuffed, and had Harmodius' sister publicly barred from carrying a basket in the procession. The plotters meant to kill Hippias too, panicked when they saw an accomplice chatting with him, and struck only Hipparchus. Harmodius died on the spot, Aristogeiton under torture. Herodotus 5.55 and Thucydides 6.59 agree the tyranny then grew harsher: executions, disarmament of the citizens (Athenaion Politeia 18.4), and a marriage alliance with Lampsacus for its influence with Darius.

Where sources disagree. Athenaion Politeia 18 makes Thessalus, not Hipparchus, the rejected lover who insulted Harmodius, and adds that Aristogeiton under torture named Hippias' own friends so that the tyrant killed his supporters. Thucydides wrote his digression precisely because most Athenians had the facts wrong. Which brother gave the insult is unrecoverable.

The legend was built after 510. Antenor's bronze pair, the first statues of mortals in the Agora, was carried off by Xerxes and replaced by Kritios and Nesiotes in 477/6; the descendants received meals in the Prytaneion and tax exemption (Demosthenes 20.18); drinking songs sang that they "killed the tyrant and made Athens isonomous" (PMG 893–896). Herodotus 6.123 protests that the pair only enraged the surviving Peisistratids and that the Alcmaeonids were the real liberators. Two families and a city were competing for credit, and the tyrannicides won because they were dead, Athenian and unembarrassing, whereas the truth involved Sparta and a cursed aristocratic house.

07Sparta, Isagoras and the reforms of Cleisthenes

The exiled Alcmaeonids failed by force at Leipsydrion (Athenaion Politeia 19.3), then rebuilt the Delphic temple and, Herodotus says (5.62–63), bribed the Pythia to tell every Spartan enquirer to free Athens. Sparta's first expedition, by sea under Anchimolius, was destroyed at Phalerum by Thessalian cavalry; the second, under King Cleomenes, besieged the Peisistratids on the Acropolis, captured their children being smuggled out, and forced Hippias to leave for Sigeum within five days (Herodotus 5.64–65; Athenaion Politeia 19.5–6), in 511/10.

Two aristocrats then fought for the city: Isagoras son of Teisander, archon for 508/7, and Cleisthenes the Alcmaeonid, grandson of the tyrant of Sicyon. Herodotus 5.66 says Cleisthenes, losing in the contest of aristocratic clubs, "took the demos into his faction" and set about reforming the tribes. Isagoras summoned Cleomenes, who demanded expulsion of the "accursed"; Cleisthenes withdrew, seven hundred households were driven out, and Cleomenes tried to dissolve the Council and install three hundred of Isagoras' friends. The Council refused, the people rose, and the Spartans and Isagoras were besieged on the Acropolis and let out under truce on the third day; their Athenian allies were executed (Herodotus 5.70–73; Athenaion Politeia 20). Cleisthenes was recalled; Cleomenes' revenge invasion of 506 collapsed at Eleusis when the Corinthians went home (Herodotus 5.74–77).

Where sources disagree. Herodotus puts the reforms before Cleomenes' intervention; Athenaion Politeia 20–21 puts them after, in Isagoras' archonship. The "Boule" that defied Cleomenes may therefore be the Solonian 400 (Rhodes) or the new 500 (Wade-Gery). Herodotus 5.73 also records an Athenian embassy to Sardis offering alliance to Persia, which the democratic tradition preferred to forget.

The reforms

Athenaion Politeia 21 gives the structure. Ten new tribes replaced the four Ionian tribes; the Pythia chose their eponymous heroes from a list of a hundred. Attica's villages became about 139 demes, each with a demarch, a register and an assembly. Membership was hereditary, so a man kept his deme wherever he moved, and citizens were to be named by deme rather than father so that the newly enrolled could not be exposed. Demes were grouped into thirty trittyes, ten each from city, coast and inland, and every tribe drew one trittys from each region by lot. The Council of 500 took fifty from each tribe; phratries, clans and priesthoods were left alone. Ten tribal regiments followed, and from 501/0 ten generals, one per tribe, and the bouleutic oath (Athenaion Politeia 22.2). Athenaion Politeia 22.1 adds ostracism: the assembly voted yearly whether to hold one, a quorum of six thousand scratched names on sherds, and the man named most often left for ten years without losing his property.

Where sources disagree. Ostracism was first used in 487, against the Peisistratid connection Hipparchus son of Charmus, and Athenaion Politeia strains to explain the twenty-year gap. Androtion (FGrH 324 F 6) seems to date the law to just before 487; Rhodes thinks Harpocration misreports him, others take Androtion literally. On motives, D. M. Lewis showed that trittys boundaries sometimes cut across rival clans' cult centres while keeping Alcmaeonid strongholds together, a gerrymander; Andrewes saw rational administration. Both may be true.

The problem solved is the one this module has traced: regional aristocratic power. Plain, Coast and Hill had each been a geographical bloc of dependants; once every tribe had to contain city, coast and inland demes, no district could become a faction. The deme register also settled the status of men of doubtful descent whom a review of the citizen roll had threatened. Herodotus compares the renaming to Cleisthenes of Sicyon's, done from contempt; Athenaion Politeia says the aim was "to mix up the people so that more should share in the state". The second is the better explanation.

08The earliest meaning of "demokratia"

The word does not occur in the sixth century. Herodotus 6.131 says Cleisthenes "set up the tribes and the democracy", but that is fifth-century usage. The drinking songs of the 500s praise the tyrannicides for making Athens isonomos; isonomia, equal share in the law, is the term Otanes uses for popular rule in Herodotus 3.80 and Aristagoras at Miletus (5.37); isegoria, equal right to speak, is Herodotus' own word for what made Athens strong after 510 (5.78). Aeschylus, Suppliants 604 (463), has "the people's ruling hand" as a periphrasis; Herodotus 6.43 applies demokratia to the regimes Mardonius set up in Ionia in 492; and an Athenian named Demokrates was born about 470. The compound belongs to the 470s or 460s.

Kratos is power or victory, not mere rule: Sealey argues the word was coined by opponents to mean the mob's grip, Hansen holds it neutral from the start. And demos meant both the whole citizen body and the common people against the few, carrying both senses at once. What Cleisthenes made was not called democracy by its makers; it was a reorganisation of the citizen body that gave the whole demos machinery to act. Herodotus' generation named it and credited Cleisthenes; the fourth century, uneasy with the radical version, moved the founding back to Solon (Athenaion Politeia 9; Mossé). Both attributions are retrospective.

09Self-test

  1. What is the only law of Draco known to have survived Solon's repeal, and how do we know its text?
    Answer

    The homicide law. It was reinscribed on stone in 409/8 (IG I³ 104), and Athenaion Politeia 7.1 says Solon kept only the homicide law.

  2. Why do most scholars reject Athenaion Politeia 4, the "constitution of Draco"?
    Answer

    It presupposes coinage Athens did not yet have, matches the oligarchic programme of 411 (a council of 401 by lot, citizenship for those bearing arms); it is regarded as a late insertion.

  3. Name Solon's three "most democratic" measures according to Athenaion Politeia 9.
    Answer

    The ban on loans secured on the person; the right of any volunteer (ho boulomenos) to prosecute on behalf of a wronged party; and ephesis, appeal to the people's court (dikasterion or Heliaia).

  4. List Solon's four property classes with their qualifications.
    Answer

    Pentakosiomedimnoi (500 measures), hippeis (300), zeugitai (200), thetes (below 200; assembly and courts only).

  5. Who led the three regional factions after Solon, and which groups did Athenaion Politeia place in the Hill party?
    Answer

    Lycurgus son of Aristolaides (Plain), Megacles son of Alcmaeon (Coast), Peisistratus (Hill). The Hill party included the poorest and those of doubtful descent who feared a review of the citizen roll.

  6. Describe the trick by which Peisistratus returned the second time, and Herodotus' verdict on it.
    Answer

    A tall woman, Phye, was dressed as Athena and driven into the city with heralds proclaiming the goddess was restoring Peisistratus. Herodotus 1.60 called it by far the silliest trick ever played on people reputed the cleverest in Greece.

  7. What does the "Peisistratean recension" claim, and what is its evidential status?
    Answer

    That Peisistratus first collected and arranged the books of Homer (Cicero, De oratore 3.137; Pausanias 7.26.13). No source before the Hellenistic period mentions it; Davison and West treat it as a late construction, though the Panathenaic recitation rule (Plato, Hipparchus 228b) is genuine.

  8. On what two points does Thucydides correct the popular Athenian story of the tyrannicides?
    Answer

    That Hippias, not Hipparchus, was the ruling tyrant in 514; and that the murder arose from a love quarrel and panic, not a planned liberation, after which the tyranny grew harsher. Herodotus 6.123 adds that the Alcmaeonids, not the pair, actually freed Athens.

  9. What were the trittyes, and what political problem did they address?
    Answer

    Thirty groups of demes, ten each from city, coast and inland; each tribe took one from each region by lot. This broke up the regional blocs that had sustained the factions of Plain, Coast and Hill.

  10. Why is the Cleisthenic date of ostracism disputed?
    Answer

    Athenaion Politeia 22.1 credits the law to Cleisthenes, but it was first used in 487, twenty years later; Androtion seems to date the law to just before 487, and scholars disagree over whether Harpocration's report of Androtion is accurate.

10Carry forward

  1. Athenian constitutional history is reconstructed from writers two to four centuries later (Athenaion Politeia, Plutarch) who projected the needs of their own day back onto Draco, Solon and Cleisthenes; only Solon's verses are contemporary. Always ask who is remembering and why.
  2. The Cleisthenic tribes, trittyes, demes and Council of 500 are the skeleton of every later Athenian institution: the army, the generals, the courts, the Council's prytanies and the citizen's very name all run through them.
  3. Rival memories of the liberation of 510 (tyrannicides, Alcmaeonids, Sparta) show how a city invents its founding story; the same contest over credit, and over the words isonomia and demokratia, continues into the fifth century.

Use Herodotus, the Athenaion Politeia and Plutarch's Solon. Format: about 2500 words, headed sections, name sources, flag disagreements, end with a 10-question self-test with answers and the three ideas I must carry forward.
