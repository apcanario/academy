The proem and the theodicy of Book 1

The first word is andra, "man"; the first epithet, polytropos, "of many turns", means much-travelled, versatile or elusive. Of the whole voyage the proem names only the cattle of Helios, and says the companions died "by their own recklessness" (atasthaliēisin). The poem opens by assigning blame.

"Tell me, Muse, of the man of many turns." (1.1)
"They perished by their own recklessness, the fools." (1.7)
"From some point in it, goddess, tell us too." (1.10)

The Muse begins in medias res, and the hero is postponed until Book 5. The first divine speech supplies the moral frame: Zeus, musing on Aegisthus, just killed by Orestes, complains that mortals suffer "beyond their portion" (hyper moron) through their own folly, though Hermes was sent to warn him (1.37–43). The Oresteia becomes the poem's paradigm, repeated by Athena (1.298–302), Nestor (3.193–200) and Agamemnon's ghost in Books 11 and 24.

"How mortals blame the gods! They say evils come from us." (1.32)
"But they themselves, by their own recklessness, suffer beyond their portion." (1.33–34)

The pattern is exact: Aegisthus, the companions (11.104–115, 12.271–276) and the suitors (Halitherses, 2.161–176; Odysseus to Amphinomus, 18.125–150) are each warned, each consume what is not theirs, and each die.

Where sources disagree. Whether the Odyssey actually delivers the justice Zeus announces is contested. Lloyd-Jones (The Justice of Zeus, 1971) argued that the Iliad's Zeus already upholds justice and that the Odyssey only makes the theme explicit; Allan ("Divine Justice and Cosmic Order in Early Greek Epic", JHS 126, 2006) extended the case. Clay (The Wrath of Athena, 1983) reads the opening theodicy as a frame the narrative does not honour: Poseidon persecutes Odysseus for blinding a son who ate six of his men, which is vengeance, not justice; and the Phaeacians are punished for their hospitality, their ship turned to stone (13.125–187). The theodicy may be a programmatic claim the narrative only partly honours.

The Telemachy (Books 1–4)

For four books the hero is absent and the poem studies his absence: a house being eaten, a wife unable to choose, a son who does not know whether he has a father. Athena's stated aim in sending Telemachus away is not information but reputation, "that good fame (kleos) may be his among men" (1.95). The journey to Pylos and Sparta is an apprenticeship in speech, guest-friendship and bearing.

"My mother says I am his; no one knows his own begetting." (1.215–216)
"Speech shall be the concern of men, and mine most of all." (1.358–359)

That second line silences Penelope and is a pointed adaptation of Hector's "war shall be the concern of men" (Iliad 6.492); the formula returns at 21.352 with "the bow". Telemachus asserts manhood by imitating a fixed phrase, and the poem marks his growth by the occasions on which he uses it.

Sparta supplies competing images of the father and of marriage. Helen drugs the wine with nepenthes (4.220–221) and tells how she kept the disguised Odysseus' secret at Troy (4.240–264); Menelaus answers with Helen at the Wooden Horse imitating the Greeks' wives to lure them out (4.266–289). The stories disagree about Helen, and the poem lets them stand.

"You hold Helen, and to them you are son-in-law of Zeus." (4.569)

Proteus promises Menelaus an Elysian afterlife earned by marriage: an immortality Odysseus will refuse from Calypso.

Where sources disagree. Nineteenth-century analysts (Kirchhoff, Die homerische Odyssee, 2nd ed. 1879) treated the Telemachy as a separate poem stitched on, noting the duplicated divine council of Book 5. Unitarians (Klingner, "Über die vier ersten Bücher der Odyssee", 1944) reply that the doubled council handles simultaneous action and that the Telemachy sets the stakes, including the suitors' ambush plot (4.663–672). Most current scholarship is unitarian here, but the seams are real.

Calypso, Nausicaa and the Phaeacians (Books 5–8)

Odysseus is first seen weeping on a beach, his life "ebbing away" (5.152–153). Calypso, whose name suggests "concealer", offers immortality; his refusal is the poem's choice of mortal identity over oblivion. He answers with tact rather than passion: Penelope is lesser in looks, but he wants to go home.

"Even so I wish and long each day to reach my home." (5.219–220)
"Thrice blessed the Greeks who died at Troy." (5.306)

The second line, spoken on the raft, is an Iliadic wish for a hero's death and fame; the Odyssey's hero will have to survive instead. Washed up on Scheria, he hides under a double bush "like a brand in black ashes" (5.488–491), the seed of fire kept alive.

Nausicaa's episode is Homer's most delicate social comedy. A naked, brine-crusted man emerges "like a mountain lion" (6.130), supplicates with words rather than touch, and compares her to Artemis and a Delian palm (6.149–169). His blessing frames the poem's marriage plot.

"Nothing is better than when man and wife keep house with one mind." (6.182–184)
"Remember me at home, for you owe me your life first." (8.461–462)

Nausicaa's hope of such a husband (6.244–245) is quietly closed off. Scheria is a hinge between fairy-tale and Ithaca: Arete, not Alcinous, must be won over (6.310–315, 7.53–77), a rehearsal for Penelope.

Demodocus' songs of the quarrel of Odysseus and Achilles (8.72–82) and of the Wooden Horse (8.499–520) make Odysseus weep, and the simile for his tears is the Odyssey's sharpest comment on the Iliad.

"As a woman weeps, flung on her husband's body, fallen before his city." (8.523–524)
"The gods spun ruin for men, so there might be song hereafter." (8.579–580)

The sacker of cities weeps like a captive widow of a sacked city. The reverse simile asks the listener to see the war from the side of its victims, and Alcinous' gloss turns suffering into material for poetry.

The Apologoi (Books 9–12) and the Cyclops

For four books Odysseus narrates his own adventures to the Phaeacians. No other witness survives to check him. He opens by naming himself with Iliadic pride.

"I am Odysseus son of Laertes, known to all for my wiles." (9.19–20)

The Cyclops read in full

The episode is framed by ethnography: no assemblies, no laws (themistes), no ships, no ploughing (9.106–115), and a goat island viewed with a colonist's eye (9.116–141). Odysseus brings Maron's strong wine (9.196–211), anticipating trouble. In the cave his men urge him to take cheeses and lambs and run; he stays, wanting guest-gifts.

"I would not listen, though it would have been far better." (9.228)

The narrator concedes his own error. Polyphemus inverts every rule of xenia: he asks the guests' names only after eating two of them, mocks Zeus (9.275–278), and promises Odysseus the "gift" of being eaten last (9.369–370). Odysseus' reply rests on a pun.

"Nobody is my name. Nobody they call me." (9.366)
"Nobody is killing me by trickery, not by force." (9.408)

The neighbours answer "if nobody (mē tis) is harming you", and Odysseus' heart laughs that his name and metis deceived them (9.410–414). The blinding uses similes of a shipwright's drill and a smith's quenching (9.384–394), civilised craft turned on the uncivilised. Triumph is then complicated by Polyphemus' tender speech to the ram hiding Odysseus (9.447–460). Finally Odysseus abandons outis for kleos and shouts name, patronymic and home (9.502–505), enabling the curse.

"Let him come late and badly, lose all his comrades, on another's ship." (9.534–535)

Polyphemus' prayer, ending "and find troubles in his house", is an exact summary of the rest of the poem. The episode dramatises the tension between the self-effacing cunning that saves Odysseus and the heroic need to be known that endangers him.

An unreliable narrator?

In the Ithacan half Odysseus tells long false autobiographies, the Cretan tales (13.256–286, 14.192–359, 19.165–202), and the narrator comments that "he told many lies like truths" (19.203), a line echoing Hesiod's Muses (Theogony 27). Alcinous, at the intermezzo, insists the opposite about the Apologoi.

"You have told your tale with skill, as a singer does." (11.367–368)
Where sources disagree. Most ("The Structure and Function of Odysseus' Apologoi", TAPA 119, 1989) reads the Apologoi as a performance tailored to win Phaeacian gifts and passage, perhaps embroidered. Richardson ("Truth in the Tales of the Odyssey", Mnemosyne 49, 1996) and others note that the primary narrator independently corroborates key facts (the cattle, 1.7–9; Poseidon's anger over the Cyclops' eye, 1.68–75), and that Alcinous' praise is designed to exclude the comparison with liars. Antiquity was split too: Juvenal (15.13–26) mocks the tall tales, and "a tale of Alcinous" became proverbial for a long improbable story (Plato, Republic 614b). The poem may want the question left open.

The Nekyia (Book 11)

Odysseus does not descend; he pours blood into a pit and the dead come up to drink. Elpenor comes first, unburied (11.51–80); then Tiresias, prophesying the inland journey with an oar and a gentle death ex halos, ambiguously "away from" or "out of" the sea (11.121–137); then Anticleia, dead of longing for him (11.197–203).

"Three times she flew from my hands like a shadow or a dream." (11.207–208)

The catalogue of women

Persephone sends a procession of heroines (11.225–332): Tyro, Antiope, Alcmene, Megara, Epicaste, Chloris, Leda, Iphimedeia, Phaedra, Procris, Ariadne, Maera, Clymene and Eriphyle. It is Hesiodic in form, close to the Catalogue of Women attributed to Hesiod, and it is aimed at its audience: when Odysseus breaks off, it is Arete who speaks first and claims him as "my guest" (11.336–341).

Where sources disagree. In the catalogue, Epicaste (Jocasta) hangs herself, but Oedipus goes on ruling in Thebes "suffering pains" (11.271–280); there is no self-blinding and no exile. This is earlier than, and different from, the version Sophocles made canonical.

Agamemnon and Achilles

Agamemnon tells his murder at a feast (11.405–434) and draws the moral that makes Penelope's fidelity the poem's measure. He praises her (11.444–446), then advises secrecy anyway.

"There is no more trusting women." (11.456)

Achilles' answer to Odysseus' consolation is the most quoted repudiation of the Iliad's value system in Greek.

"I would rather be a hired hand to a landless man, alive." (11.489–490)
"Than be lord over all the perished dead." (11.491)

The thēs was the lowest free labourer, below the oikos itself. Achilles is consoled only by news of Neoptolemus (11.538–540): kleos survives as a father's pleasure, not a dead man's compensation. Ajax's silent turning away (11.563–564) is the other answer.

Tityos, Tantalus, Sisyphus and Heracles' phantom

At 11.568 the scene changes. Odysseus, still at the pit, suddenly sees Minos judging, Orion hunting, Tityos with vultures at his liver (11.576–581), Tantalus reaching for water and fruit (11.582–592), Sisyphus pushing his stone (11.593–600), and Heracles.

"Then I saw mighty Heracles, his phantom; he himself is with the gods." (11.601–602)
Where sources disagree. The scholia report that 11.602–604, which split Heracles into a phantom below and a deified self married to Hebe on Olympus, were suspected as an insertion by Onomacritus, the Peisistratid editor. Aristarchus athetised the whole stretch from 11.568 to 627 (the scholia's objection is that Odysseus could not see into Hades from his pit), and modern analysts (Page, The Homeric Odyssey, 1955, among them) keep the suspicion on roughly 11.565–627: the viewpoint shifts from a pit to a tour of Erebus, and eternal punishment is otherwise absent from Homer. Defenders (Heubeck in the Oxford commentary, vol. 2, 1989) note that the sinners offended gods directly, an archaic idea, and that Heracles' labour in Hades (11.617–626) mirrors Odysseus' own. Interpolation of 602–604 is likely; of the larger passage, uncertain.

The slow recognitions (Books 13–24)

The second half is built from recognitions deliberately delayed and differentiated by character. Athena opens it by appreciating his lies, "crafty would he be who outdid you" (13.291), and claiming kinship in cunning (13.296–299). Telemachus recognises him only by being told.

"I am no god. Why liken me to the immortals? I am your father." (16.187–188)

Argos, neglected on the dung heap, recognises him first and unprompted, and dies (17.290–327). In the same scene Eumaeus, himself a nobleman's son sold into slavery (15.403–484), states the poem's harshest social axiom.

"Zeus takes half a man's worth the day slavery seizes him." (17.322–323)
"Death seized Argos, having seen Odysseus in the twentieth year." (17.326–327)

Eurycleia and the scar

Penelope orders the old nurse to wash the beggar's feet. As her hand touches the scar the narrative stops for some seventy lines (19.393–466) to tell how the boy was named by his grandfather Autolycus, "a man who has caused pain (odyssamenos) to many" (19.406–409), and gored by a boar on Parnassus. Only then does she drop his foot in the basin.

"You are Odysseus, dear child, and I did not know you." (19.474–475)

Erich Auerbach's "Odysseus' Scar" (Mimesis, 1946, ch. 1) made this the type of Homeric style, everything foregrounded, against the "fraught with background" Genesis 22. Critics object that the digression is placed with purpose: it gives the origin of the name at the moment identity is exposed. Odysseus then grips her throat (19.479–481): recognition is a danger, not a reward.

Penelope's tests

Penelope extracts gifts from the suitors as Odysseus watches approvingly (18.274–283), tells her dream of the eagle and geese (19.535–553), distinguishes the gates of horn and ivory (19.560–567), and announces the bow contest (19.572–581). After the slaughter she still withholds recognition until the test of the bed built round an olive trunk (23.177–204), the only time Odysseus is caught off guard. The embrace simile again reverses roles.

"As welcome as land to swimmers whose ship Poseidon has smashed." (23.233–234)
Where sources disagree. Does Penelope recognise Odysseus before Book 23? Harsh ("Penelope and Odysseus in Odyssey XIX", AJP 71, 1950) argued for an early, concealed recognition in Book 19; Winkler ("Penelope's Cunning and Homer's", in The Constraints of Desire, 1990) and Katz (Penelope's Renown, 1991) read her strategic indeterminacy as the point; Heubeck in the Oxford commentary (vol. 3, 1992) keeps her ignorant until the bed test. The text supports suspicion; it does not settle it.

Laertes

Odysseus finds his father digging in rags and tests him with "mocking words" (24.240): gratuitous cruelty to some readers, a release of grief to others. The scene completes the poem's pattern: every recognition has been a test, and the last is made of the one person who could never have been an enemy, so that the testing is shown as a habit of the man and not only a tactic. The proof is the trees Laertes gave the boy, thirteen pear, ten apple, forty fig (24.340–342): the orchard as inheritance.

"What a day, dear gods: son and grandson vie in courage." (24.514–515)

The bow, the slaughter and the maids (Books 21–22)

The suitors cannot string the bow; Odysseus does so like a singer stringing a lyre, and the string sings "like a swallow" (21.406–411). The simile completes the identification of hero and bard made in Book 11. Book 22 opens with him shedding his rags (22.1) and shooting Antinous through the throat mid-drink (22.8–21).

"You dogs, you thought I would never come home from Troy." (22.35)
"It is unholy to exult over slain men." (22.412)

The killing is sorted by status: the prophet Leodes dies despite supplication (22.310–329); the bard Phemius and herald Medon are spared (22.330–380). Eurycleia names twelve of fifty female slaves as disloyal (22.421–425). Odysseus orders them to clean the hall and be put to the sword; Telemachus changes the method.

"Let them not die a clean death, these who poured shame on us." (22.462–463)
"Their feet twitched for a little, not for long." (22.473)

The hanging is described with a simile of thrushes or doves caught in a net (22.468–472), after which Melanthius is mutilated (22.474–477). The scene was little discussed for centuries and is now one of the most discussed in the poem.

Wilson and the women and slaves

Emily Wilson's translation (Norton, 2017), widely billed as the first complete English Odyssey by a woman, kept the Greek line count in iambic pentameter and rendered andra polytropon as "a complicated man". Its larger effect was on the vocabulary of the household. Where earlier versions often said "maids" or "servants" for dmōai, Wilson says "slaves", and she pointed out that some modern translators had given the hanged women abusive labels ("sluts", "whores") absent from the Greek, which uses neutral demonstratives and a verb for sleeping with the suitors. She stressed how little choice unfree women had with their master's guests. Attention shifted from Penelope as the poem's only significant woman to the women whose bodies sustain the oikos and are destroyed by it. Margaret Atwood's The Penelopiad (2005), partly narrated by the maids, had prepared the ground.

Where sources disagree. The objections to Wilson's choices are three: "complicated" loses the travel sense of polytropos; the plain register flattens formula; and the contrast with earlier translators is overstated, since "servant" was often period convention, as Lattimore's and Fitzgerald's versions show. Few dispute that the poem presents the hanging as justified within its frame; the debate is how a translation should render that frame.

The ending: 23.296 and Book 24

"Gladly they went to the rite of their old bed." (23.296)

The scholia on this line report that Aristophanes of Byzantium and Aristarchus called it the peras or telos of the Odyssey. They also preserve objections, associated with Aristarchus, to the second Nekyia (24.1–204), where Agamemnon sets Penelope's undying kleos against Clytemnestra's infamy (24.192–198). Then come Laertes, the revolt of the suitors' kin under Eupeithes, and Athena's abrupt halt (24.531–548).

"Stop, men of Ithaca, from grievous war." (24.531)
Where sources disagree. Whether "telos" meant the textual end or the plot's fulfilment is itself disputed. Page (The Homeric Odyssey, 1955) argued the continuation is later, citing linguistic anomalies and Hermes' unusual role as escort of souls. Defenders, notably Wender (The Last Scenes of the Odyssey, 1978) and Heubeck in the Oxford commentary, answer that the poem has prepared Laertes (1.189–193, 11.187–196) and the blood-feud (20.41–43, 23.117–122), so stopping at 23.296 leaves a vendetta unresolved. Most now accept Book 24 as integral, if rougher; the abrupt divine halt still dissatisfies many.

Against the Iliad: nostos, metis, the household

Nostos against kleos

Achilles chooses between return without kleos and death at Troy with fame "unwithering" (Iliad 9.412–416). Odysseus refuses immortality, wishes once for Iliadic death (5.306), and hears Achilles call kleos worthless to the dead (11.489–491). Yet the boast to the Cyclops shows kleos still compelling, and the last word on fame goes to Penelope (24.196–198), whose kleos is won by staying.

Metis against bie

The Iliad knows that cunning beats strength (Nestor, Iliad 23.315–318), but its decisive acts are of force. Gregory Nagy (The Best of the Achaeans, 1979) read the poems as rival claims to be "best of the Achaeans", by bie or by metis; Demodocus' first song stages that quarrel (8.75–78). Yet the Odyssey ends in massacre, and the bow is both force and skill.

The household against the battlefield

The Iliad's house is Hector's, glimpsed in Book 6 and doomed. The Odyssey's subject is the oikos: stores, herds, slaves, bed, trees. Its values are hospitality and like-mindedness; its villains are guests who consume. The reverse similes carry war and shipwreck into the house, and Book 22's battle is fought in a dining hall. The cost of restoration falls on those who belonged to the household without owning anything in it.

Self-test

  1. Which single episode of the voyage does the proem name, and what moral word is attached to the companions' deaths?
    Answer

    The cattle of Helios (1.8–9); the companions died by their own recklessness, atasthaliēisin (1.7).

  2. What is the point of Zeus' speech about Aegisthus at 1.32–43?
    Answer

    Mortals suffer "beyond their portion" through their own recklessness despite divine warnings (Hermes warned Aegisthus); it frames the companions' and suitors' fates and makes the Oresteia the poem's recurring paradigm.

  3. Which Iliadic line does Telemachus adapt at 1.358–359, and where does the formula recur?
    Answer

    Hector's "war shall be the concern of men" (Iliad 6.492); Telemachus substitutes "speech" (mythos), and the formula recurs with "the bow" at 21.352.

  4. What does the simile at 8.523–531 do?
    Answer

    It compares Odysseus, weeping at the song of the Wooden Horse, to a woman mourning her husband killed defending their city: the sacker of cities likened to his victims.

  5. Explain the pun at 9.410–414.
    Answer

    The Cyclopes say "if nobody (mē tis) is harming you"; Odysseus laughs that his name and metis (cunning) deceived them. Outis/mē tis/metis fuse anonymity and intelligence.

  6. Why does Odysseus' boast at 9.502–505 matter for the rest of the poem?
    Answer

    By giving his full name he enables Polyphemus' curse to Poseidon (9.528–535), which predicts the late, solitary return on another's ship and trouble in his house.

  7. What does Achilles say at 11.489–491, and what consoles him?
    Answer

    He would rather be a hired labourer (thēs) to a landless man than rule all the dead; he is consoled only by news of his son Neoptolemus' prowess.

  8. Which lines of the Nekyia were suspected in antiquity, and on what grounds is a larger passage suspected now?
    Answer

    11.602–604 (Heracles' phantom and deified self), attributed in the scholia to Onomacritus; modern analysts suspect roughly 11.565–627 because Odysseus, fixed at the pit, suddenly views Erebus and its eternal punishments, otherwise un-Homeric.

  9. What did Auerbach argue from the scar digression (19.393–466), and what is the usual objection?
    Answer

    That Homer foregrounds everything with no background or suspense, unlike Genesis; critics reply that the digression on Odysseus' naming is purposefully placed at the moment of exposure of identity.

  10. What did Aristophanes and Aristarchus say about 23.296, and what is the main argument for Book 24?
    Answer

    They called it the telos or peras of the poem; defenders of Book 24 argue that the poem has prepared both the Laertes reunion and the blood-feud with the suitors' families, which require resolution.

Carry forward

  1. The Odyssey announces a moral frame at 1.32–34 (suffering through one's own recklessness after warning) and then tests it; read its justice, including the hanging of the slave women, as a claim the poem makes, not a fact it proves.
  2. Odysseus is a narrator as well as a hero; the poem identifies him with the bard (11.368, 21.406–411) and with the liar (19.203), so every first-person account in Greek literature after it inherits the question of reliability.
  3. The Odyssey defines itself against the Iliad: nostos over kleos, metis over bie, oikos over battlefield, yet it keeps the older values alive inside the new ones, and the cost of the restored household falls on those without power in it. Whether that contrast is allusion to a fixed text (Pucci, Odysseus Polutropos, 1987) or rivalry between two traditions still forming (Nagy, The Best of the Achaeans, 1979) is itself open.
