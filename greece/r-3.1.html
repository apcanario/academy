01What a hero was to the Greeks

In Homer the word heros is almost a social title: every warrior at Troy is a hero, and so is the herald and the bard. Only once, at Iliad 12.23, does the poet call the men who fell before Troy a "race of half-divine men" (hemitheon genos andron), and the phrase is unusual enough that scholars suspect a later religious idea leaking into the epic. Hesiod makes that idea explicit. In Works and Days 156–173 the fourth race of mankind is the hemitheoi, the heroes who fought at Thebes and Troy; some died, others Zeus settled on the Isles of the Blessed. The heroes belong to a closed past, the generations around the Trojan War, and their defining feature is proximity to the gods: a divine parent, a divine lover, a god at their elbow in battle.

By the archaic period a second meaning had attached to the word, and it is the one that matters for religion. Walter Burkert's definition is the standard shorthand: a hero is a dead person who exercises power from the tomb for good or ill and demands appropriate honour. The two senses overlap but are not identical. Some cult heroes have no epic past at all, such as Battus the founder of Cyrene, buried in the agora (Pindar, Pythian 5.93–95), or the hero of Temesa, a drowned sailor whose ghost demanded a maiden every year (Pausanias 6.6.7–11). Conversely, some epic heroes attracted little or no cult.

The cult at the tomb

Hero cult is tied to a place: a tomb, real or supposed, often inside the city or at its gate, where the ordinary dead were kept outside the walls. The bones were what mattered. Sparta fetched what it claimed were the bones of Orestes from Tegea on Delphi's instruction (Herodotus 1.67–68); Athens brought Theseus home from Skyros in 476/5 (Plutarch, Theseus 36); the shoulder-blade of Pelops was kept at Olympia (Pausanias 5.13.4–6). Possession of a hero meant possession of his protection, which is the logic of Sophocles' Oedipus at Colonus, where Athens and Thebes compete for the grave of a polluted man precisely because he will be a powerful dead. Herodotus 5.67 shows the other face: Cleisthenes of Sicyon wanted to expel the hero Adrastus from his city, could not, and imported Adrastus' enemy Melanippus to counterbalance him.

Herodotus 2.44 notes that Heracles received two kinds of rite: Greeks sacrifice to him "as to an immortal" (thuousi) and "as to a hero" (enagizousi). Pindar calls him heros theos, "hero-god" (Nemean 3.22); the hybrid term shows the Greeks saw a boundary and knew Heracles sat on it.

Where sources disagree. The older scholarly picture, from Erwin Rohde's Psyche (1894) onward, opposed Olympian sacrifice (thusia, burnt and eaten) to heroic or chthonic sacrifice (enagismos, destroyed or poured into the ground). Gunnel Ekroth (The Sacrificial Rituals of Greek Hero-Cults, 2002) found that most hero cults in fact used ordinary thusia with a shared meal, and that the "chthonic" rite was the exception. Herodotus' Heracles is now read as a special case, not the rule.

From the eighth century BCE Greeks began leaving offerings at Mycenaean tombs untouched for four hundred years. Nicolas Coldstream linked this to the spread of epic: communities were identifying the old tombs as graves of heroes they now heard about. Carla Antonaccio (An Archaeology of Ancestors, 1995) argued these tomb cults were short-lived and distinct from the organised hero cults of the later city; Gregory Nagy (The Best of the Achaeans, 1979) treated cult as older than epic. Which came first, the song or the shrine, is unresolved.

02The hero pattern

Folklorists noticed early that the biographies of Greek heroes follow a recurring shape. Johann Georg von Hahn's "exposure-and-return formula" (1876) and Otto Rank (1909) were early formulations; Lord Raglan's The Hero (1936) produced the best-known checklist of twenty-two points, and Joseph Campbell's monomyth (1949) popularised a looser version. Stripped of theory, the Greek pattern runs as follows.

StageTypical formPerseus

Raglan scored Perseus at eighteen of twenty-two, behind only Oedipus and Theseus. The one stage Perseus lacks is the strange death: no ancient source describes how he died, which is itself a variant of the pattern, since the hero who simply vanishes is a recognised type. Walter Burkert (Structure and History in Greek Mythology and Ritual, 1979) isolated a complementary sequence he called the "girl's tragedy" (seclusion, seduction by a god, punishment, rescue, birth of a hero), with Danae among his examples alongside Io, Callisto and Auge. Structurally the Perseus story is a girl's tragedy followed by a hero's quest.

Where sources disagree. Raglan's criteria are vague enough that almost any biography scores well, the points are not independent, and the method cannot explain why particular heroes have particular adventures. Folklorists after Alan Dundes defend the pattern as a real description of how oral narrative shapes biography; most historians of Greek religion use it as a descriptive convenience and refuse to treat it as evidence for ritual origins or a universal psychology.

03The modern word and the ancient one

The modern hero is a moral category: someone who acts admirably at cost to themselves. The Greek hero is a category of power and status, and his morality is irrelevant to his standing. Heracles murders his music teacher, his guest-friend Iphitus and his own children. Achilles mutilates a corpse. Oedipus is a parricide. None of this disqualified them; excess in every direction, including violence, was the mark of men closer to the gods than ordinary mortals. The Greek hero is also dangerous after death: the hero of Temesa had to be fought, the ghost of Achilles demanded Polyxena. Nobody aspired to be a hero, and the word was not applied to the living until the Hellenistic period.

The ancient hero is also local, bound to a tomb, a city and a family claiming descent; Heracles, worshipped everywhere, is the exception the Greeks marked by making him a god. And he is historical in Greek eyes: Herodotus and Thucydides treat Perseus, Heracles and Troy as a remote but real past, datable by generations. The modern sense of "hero" as simply a story's protagonist is the latest and thinnest meaning.

04The sources for Perseus

Perseus is established in the earliest poetry. Zeus lists Danae among his conquests as mother of "Perseus, pre-eminent among all men" (Iliad 14.319–320); Hesiod gives the Gorgons and the birth of Pegasus from Medusa's neck (Theogony 270–283); the Shield of Heracles 216–237 shows Perseus in flight with sandals, kibisis and cap. Simonides' lament of Danae in the chest (fr. 543 PMG) survives; the tragedies do not: Aeschylus' Phorcides, Sophocles' Andromeda, Euripides' Andromeda of 412. The continuous narratives are:

05Argos and Seriphos: the oracle, the golden rain, the chest

Acrisius and Proetus, twin sons of Abas of Argos, quarrelled in the womb and later fought for the kingdom; Proetus was driven out and took Tiryns, which the Cyclopes walled for him (Apollodorus 2.2.1). Acrisius, with only a daughter, Danae, asked the oracle how he might have sons and was told instead that his daughter's son would kill him. He shut her in an underground chamber of bronze with a nurse. Zeus came through the roof as a stream of gold and fathered Perseus (Apollodorus 2.4.1; Pherecydes F 10). Pindar's phrase is "born of self-flowing gold" (Pythian 12.17–18), and Sophocles' chorus compares the entombed Antigone to Danae (Antigone 944–950).

Where sources disagree. Apollodorus records that "some say" Proetus seduced his niece, and that this caused the brothers' quarrel (2.4.1). It is the only strand that denies Perseus a divine father, and Ovid makes the denial a plot point: Acrisius refuses to believe Perseus is Jupiter's son (Metamorphoses 4.608–614).

Acrisius shut mother and child in a wooden chest (larnax) and had it thrown into the sea. It came ashore at Seriphos, a small rocky island in the western Cyclades, where Dictys, a fisherman, found it and raised the boy. Pherecydes makes Dictys son of Peristhenes and brother of Polydectes, the island's king; Apollodorus agrees, which is why Dictys later inherits the throne.

Polydectes fell in love with Danae. In the version Pherecydes and Apollodorus share, he summoned the nobles to an eranos, a contributory feast, announcing he was collecting a bride-price of horses for Hippodameia. Perseus, asked what he would bring, said the Gorgon's head, and Polydectes took him at his word. In Apollodorus 2.4.2 Perseus volunteers the rash promise; in Pherecydes the king more deliberately demands it. Hyginus and Ovid drop the eranos and have Polydectes simply send Perseus to his death.

06The Graiae, the gifts and Medusa

Guided by Hermes and Athena, Perseus went first to the Graiae, daughters of Phorcys and Ceto and sisters of the Gorgons: Enyo, Pephredo and Deino, born old, sharing a single eye and a single tooth passed from hand to hand. Perseus intercepted both as they changed hands and would not return them until the Graiae told him the way to the nymphs (Apollodorus 2.4.2; Pherecydes F 11, the earliest full telling and the source of Aeschylus' Phorcides). From the nymphs he received the winged sandals, the kibisis (a pouch in which the head could be carried safely) and the cap of Hades, which made its wearer invisible; Hermes added the adamantine sickle, the harpe. Their ownership is revealing: at the end Perseus returns the sandals, kibisis and cap through Hermes to the nymphs and gives the head to Athena. The hero borrows divine equipment; he does not own it.

He flew to the Gorgons at the edge of Ocean. Of Stheno, Euryale and Medusa only Medusa was mortal (Theogony 276–277). Perseus approached as they slept, Athena guiding his hand while he watched Medusa's reflection in a bronze shield, and cut off her head. From the neck sprang Pegasus and Chrysaor, Poseidon's children (Theogony 280–283; Apollodorus 2.4.3; Ovid 4.782–786). The sisters pursued but the cap hid him. Pindar turns the pursuit into an aetiology: Athena imitated the wail of Euryale and so invented the music of the aulos (Pythian 12.6–27).

Where sources disagree. In Hesiod the Gorgons are monstrous from birth and Medusa's union with Poseidon "in a soft meadow among spring flowers" is told without blame (Theogony 278–279). Apollodorus (2.4.3) reports a variant that she was beheaded for comparing her beauty with Athena's. Ovid (4.794–803) gives the version now most familiar: Medusa was a beauty whom Neptune raped in Minerva's temple, and Minerva punished the violation of her sanctuary by turning the girl's hair to snakes. That story is attested nowhere before Ovid and should not be read back into the Greek sources. The Gorgons' location is likewise unstable: Hesiod puts them beyond Ocean near the Hesperides, Pindar sends Perseus to the Hyperboreans in the far north (Pythian 10.29–48), Herodotus 2.91 and Ovid route the flight over Libya.

07Andromeda and the sea monster

Flying home over Ethiopia, Perseus saw a girl chained to a rock: Andromeda, daughter of Cepheus and Cassiopeia. Her mother had boasted she was lovelier than the Nereids; Poseidon sent a flood and a sea monster (ketos, Latinised as Cetus), and the oracle of Ammon told Cepheus the remedy was to expose his daughter. Perseus offered to kill the monster for Andromeda's hand and did so (Apollodorus 2.4.3). Cepheus' brother Phineus, to whom she had been promised, plotted against Perseus and was petrified with his fellow conspirators.

Ovid makes this the longest set-piece of his account (4.663–5.235): an aerial combat in which Perseus stabs the monster with his curved sword, a wedding banquet, and a Homeric brawl when Phineus bursts in, ending with two hundred men turned to stone. Hyginus (Astronomica 2.9–12) sets the whole cast among the stars as Perseus, Andromeda, Cepheus, Cassiopeia and Cetus.

Where sources disagree. Andromeda is absent from the surviving Pherecydes and from Pindar; her first secure appearance is on Attic vases and in fifth-century tragedy. Her location moved: Apollodorus and Ovid say Ethiopia, but Strabo (16.2.28), Pliny (Natural History 5.69), Josephus (Jewish War 3.420) and Pausanias (4.35.9) place the rock at Joppa (Jaffa), where Pausanias was shown a spring reddened by the blood Perseus washed off. Whether this is an older eastern setting or Hellenistic local pride is uncertain. Ovid kills the monster with the sword; Lucian (Dialogues of the Sea-Gods 14) and some later art petrify it with the head.

08The return, the discus and the Perseid dynasty

Back on Seriphos, Perseus found Danae and Dictys sheltering at the altars from Polydectes. He went to the palace where the king and his friends were gathered, told them to look, and held up the head; they became stone. He made Dictys king, returned the gifts, and gave the head to Athena for her shield (Apollodorus 2.4.3; Pherecydes F 11). Pindar calls it the "stony death" Perseus brought to Seriphos (Pythian 10.47–48; 12.12), and later Greeks read the island's barren rocks as the petrified population (Strabo 10.5.10). Pausanias 2.18.1 reports a hero shrine of Perseus beside the road from Mycenae to Argos, Perseus still worshipped on Seriphos in the second century CE, and at Athens a precinct of Perseus with an altar to Dictys and Clymene as "the saviours of Perseus".

He then went to Argos. Acrisius fled to Larissa in Thessaly, where King Teutamides was holding funeral games for his father; Perseus competed in the pentathlon, his discus went astray, struck Acrisius on the foot and killed him (Apollodorus 2.4.4; Pausanias 2.16.2; Hyginus, Fabulae 63). The oracle was fulfilled without intent, exactly as Oedipus fulfils his. Perseus buried his grandfather and, ashamed to take the kingdom of the man he had killed, exchanged it with Megapenthes son of Proetus: Megapenthes took Argos, Perseus took Tiryns, and from Tiryns he walled Mycenae and Midea (Apollodorus 2.4.4).

Pausanias 2.16.3 derives the name Mycenae from the cap (mykes) of Perseus' scabbard or a mushroom (also mykes) he pulled up to find a spring. The claim that one hero founded the great Bronze Age citadel is the Greeks' explanation of ruins they could see but not date, and the clearest point at which the story works as charter myth for a dynasty.

Where sources disagree. Ovid ends differently. In Metamorphoses 5.236–249 Proetus has driven Acrisius out of Argos, and Perseus avenges his grandfather by petrifying Proetus, then petrifies Polydectes for mocking the Gorgon's head as a fiction. No discus, no Larissa, and Acrisius apparently still alive. Whether Ovid follows a lost Hellenistic source or reshapes the ending himself is unknown. Herodotus 6.53–54 adds that Perseus alone among Greek heroes has no mortal father named at all, which he takes as support for the Persian claim that Perseus was an Assyrian who became Greek.

From Perseus to Heracles

Perseus and Andromeda had a daughter, Gorgophone, and six sons: Perses, left with Cepheus and claimed by the Persian kings as ancestor (Herodotus 7.61, 7.150), Alcaeus, Sthenelus, Heleus, Mestor and Electryon (Apollodorus 2.4.5). Three lines converge on Heracles. Alcaeus fathered Amphitryon; Electryon, king of Mycenae, fathered Alcmene; Sthenelus fathered Eurystheus. Amphitryon killed Electryon by accident, again with a thrown object, a club aimed at a cow, and fled with Alcmene to Thebes, where Zeus visited her in Amphitryon's shape. Heracles was born in Thebes but was a Perseid on both sides, and his cousin Eurystheus held the throne of Mycenae and Tiryns. Hera's trick that brought Eurystheus to birth first (Iliad 19.95–133) and the labours imposed from Mycenae follow directly from the dynastic geography Perseus created when he swapped Argos for Tiryns.

Zeus + Danae (dau. of Acrisius of Argos)

Gorgophone's line matters too: through Tyndareus she is grandmother of Helen, Clytemnestra and the Dioscuri, so the Spartan house as well as the Argive descended from Perseus. The Heraclid kings of Sparta and Macedon, the Persian claim to Perses, and the citadel of Mycenae itself were all anchored to the son of the golden rain. That is what a hero was for.

09Self-test

  1. What is the only passage in Homer that calls the heroes "half-divine", and why is it significant?
    Answer

    Iliad 12.23, hemitheon genos andron. It is the sole Homeric use of the idea, which otherwise first appears in Hesiod, Works and Days 156–173, and scholars suspect it is a later religious idea leaking into the epic.

  2. Give Burkert's working definition of a cult hero and one example of a hero with no epic past.
    Answer

    A dead person who exercises power from the grave for good or ill and demands honour accordingly. Examples: Battus of Cyrene (Pindar, Pythian 5.93–95) or the hero of Temesa (Pausanias 6.6.7–11).

  3. What did Ekroth's study change in the understanding of hero sacrifice?
    Answer

    It showed that most hero cults used ordinary Olympian-style thusia with a shared meal; the "chthonic" destruction sacrifice (enagismos) described by Herodotus 2.44 for Heracles was exceptional, not the norm.

  4. Which stage of the standard hero pattern does Perseus lack, and how might that itself fit the pattern?
    Answer

    The strange or violent death: no source narrates how he died. The hero who simply disappears is itself a recognised type, and Perseus still received cult (Pausanias 2.18.1).

  5. Name the three Graiae and explain what Perseus took from them.
    Answer

    Enyo, Pephredo and Deino. He seized the single eye and single tooth they shared as they passed them between themselves, and traded them back for directions to the nymphs (Apollodorus 2.4.2; Pherecydes F 11).

  6. List the four implements Perseus used against Medusa and say who provided each.
    Answer

    Winged sandals, the kibisis and the cap of Hades from the nymphs; the adamantine harpe from Hermes. He returned the first three via Hermes and gave the head to Athena.

  7. Where does the story that Medusa was a beautiful woman raped by Poseidon and punished by Athena first appear?
    Answer

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 4.794–803. Hesiod's Medusa is a monster from birth; Apollodorus reports a variant that she rivalled Athena's beauty. The rape-and-punishment version is Roman.

  8. How does Ovid's account of Acrisius' fate differ from Apollodorus'?
    Answer

    Apollodorus (2.4.4) has Perseus kill Acrisius accidentally with a discus at the funeral games in Larissa. Ovid (5.236–249) has Proetus expel Acrisius and Perseus petrify Proetus; there is no discus, and Acrisius is apparently still alive.

  9. Why did Perseus not rule Argos, and what did he do instead?
    Answer

    He was ashamed to inherit the kingdom of the man he had killed, so he exchanged it with Megapenthes son of Proetus, took Tiryns, and fortified Mycenae and Midea (Apollodorus 2.4.4).

  10. Trace Heracles' descent from Perseus on both sides.
    Answer

    Perseus' son Alcaeus fathered Amphitryon, Heracles' mortal father; Perseus' son Electryon fathered Alcmene, Heracles' mother. Perseus' son Sthenelus fathered Eurystheus, who imposed the labours.

10Carry forward

  1. A Greek hero is defined by power and proximity to the gods, not by virtue: a semi-divine mortal of the closed heroic age who, dead, works from his tomb and is honoured there. The modern moral sense of the word is a late development and will mislead you if you import it into Achilles, Heracles or Oedipus.
  2. The hero pattern (prophecy, divine conception, exposure, foreign upbringing, quest with borrowed divine equipment, monster, return, accidental fulfilment of the prophecy, founding, strange death and cult) is a descriptive tool. Perseus fits it almost perfectly; use him as the template against which to measure Heracles, Theseus, Jason and Oedipus in later modules.
  3. Hero myth is dynastic charter. Perseus' exchange of Argos for Tiryns and his walling of Mycenae set up the kingdom Eurystheus holds and Heracles is owed; his sons supply ancestors for Argos, Sparta and Persia alike. When you reach Heracles and the Heraclids, the geography and the grievance both start here.
