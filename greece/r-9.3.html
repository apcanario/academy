The Roman recasting

Rome did not receive Greek myth as a finished canon; it rebuilt it for its own purposes and, because Latin became the schoolroom language of the West for fifteen centuries, the rebuilt version is the one most Europeans inherited. Three authors did most of the work.

Virgil's Aeneid (composed 29–19 BC) took a minor Trojan from Iliad 20.302–8 and made him the ancestor of Rome, inverting the Homeric sympathy so that the Greeks became the sackers and the Trojans the founders. Ovid's Metamorphoses (c. AD 8) is the decisive text: a continuous poem of some 250 transformations from chaos to Augustus, witty, erotic and relentlessly visual. For a medieval or Renaissance reader who knew no Greek, Ovid simply was Greek mythology; the early fourteenth-century Ovide moralisé and Boccaccio's Genealogia deorum gentilium (c. 1360) are built on him. Seneca's tragedies (Medea, Phaedra, Thyestes, Oedipus, Hercules Furens) transmitted Attic plots in a rhetorical, gory, Stoic register, and it was Seneca, not Sophocles, whom the Elizabethans read; Titus Andronicus and Hamlet owe him more than they owe Athens.

Roman names stuck for a plain institutional reason. Greek was effectively lost in the Latin West from the sixth century to the late fourteenth, so every schoolboy met Jupiter, Venus and Hercules before anyone had heard of Zeus, Aphrodite or Heracles. The Greek forms re-entered general use only in the nineteenth century, through German scholarship and the Victorian schools.

Where sources disagree. Whether Ovid's gods are a serious religious statement or a sustained joke is still argued. Brooks Otis read the Metamorphoses as a coherent epic of order; Karl Galinsky and later Stephen Hinds stressed its irony and play. The distinction matters because every later moralising reading of Ovid assumes the first view.

Byzantium and the Arabic transmission

The Greek texts survived because Byzantine schoolmasters kept copying them. The ninth-century revival under Photius (whose Bibliotheca summarises 279 books, many now lost) and the tenth-century Suda lexicon preserved an enormous amount of mythographic detail. The school syllabus narrowed the drama to "triads": three plays each of Aeschylus (Prometheus, Seven against Thebes, Persians), Sophocles (Ajax, Electra, Oedipus Tyrannus) and Euripides (Hecuba, Orestes, Phoenician Women). Nine further Euripides plays survive only because one manuscript (Laurentianus 32.2) happened to preserve a stretch of an alphabetically ordered complete edition.

The Palaeologan period (1261–1453) produced the first editors in a recognisably modern sense. Maximus Planudes compiled the Greek Anthology and translated Ovid's Metamorphoses into Greek, a rare reverse flow. Demetrius Triclinius (c. 1280–1340) understood lyric metre well enough to correct the texts of Pindar and the tragedians, and his autograph manuscripts underpin modern editions.

The Arabic transmission was a different channel carrying different cargo. Under the Abbasids in Baghdad, Hunayn ibn Ishaq (d. 873) and his circle translated the bulk of Galen and, with Ishaq ibn Hunayn, most of Aristotle; Ibn Rushd (Averroes) wrote the commentaries that twelfth-century Toledo (Gerard of Cremona, Michael Scot) rendered into Latin, so that Paris read Aristotle through Arabic before it read him in Greek. But poetry and myth did not travel this route: Homer was known only by name, and Abu Bishr Matta's version of the Poetics construed tragedy as "the art of praise", there being no theatre to make sense of it. Arabic transmission saved Greek science and philosophy, not Greek story.

Where sources disagree. The "House of Wisdom" as an organised translation academy is doubted: Dimitri Gutas (Greek Thought, Arabic Culture, 1998) argues the Bayt al-Hikma was a palace library and the translation movement was driven by patrons and physicians. Sylvain Gouguenheim's Aristote au Mont Saint-Michel (2008) claimed direct Greek-to-Latin transmission via James of Venice mattered more than the Arabic route; most medievalists judged this polemical and overstated, though James of Venice's translations are real.

The Renaissance recovery

Petrarch owned a Homer he could not read, a gift from the Byzantine envoy Nicholas Sigeros in 1354; he and Boccaccio commissioned Leontius Pilatus to produce a crude Latin prose version in the 1360s. Real Greek teaching began when Manuel Chrysoloras came to Florence in 1397 at the invitation of Coluccio Salutati, and his grammar, the Erotemata, became the first Greek textbook of the West.

Marsilio Ficino's complete Latin Plato (printed 1484) and his Plotinus (1492) made Greek philosophy available as a living alternative to Aristotelian scholasticism and supplied the allegorical key by which painters could present nude goddesses as moral emblems. The Aldine press in Venice did for texts what Ficino did for ideas: Aldus Manutius printed the Greek Aristotle (1495–98), Aristophanes (1498), Sophocles (1502), Herodotus and Thucydides (1502), Euripides (1503), Homer (1504) and Plato (1513).

Painting followed the texts. Botticelli's Primavera (c. 1482) and Birth of Venus (c. 1485) draw on Ovid's Fasti 5.195–220, Lucretius 5.737–40 and Poliziano's vernacular Stanze. Titian's six poesie for Philip II (1551–62: Danaë, Venus and Adonis, Perseus and Andromeda, Diana and Actaeon, Diana and Callisto, Rape of Europa) are Ovid again, read through Lodovico Dolce's Italian translation, and they set the visual grammar of mythological painting until Rubens and beyond. The Renaissance recovered Greek texts but kept painting Roman stories.

Where sources disagree. Gombrich (1945), Panofsky and Edgar Wind read Botticelli's mythologies as Ficinian Neoplatonic allegories; Charles Dempsey (The Portrayal of Love, 1992) argued they are vernacular poetry made visible, closer to Ovid and Poliziano than to Plotinus.

Enlightenment, Winckelmann and Romantic Hellenism

Johann Joachim Winckelmann's Thoughts on the Imitation of Greek Works (1755) coined the phrase that governed European taste for a century: Greek art showed edle Einfalt und stille Grösse, "noble simplicity and quiet grandeur". His History of the Art of Antiquity (1764) was the first to arrange ancient art in a developmental sequence of styles. The irony is structural. He never visited Greece, judged from Roman copies in Roman collections, took the Apollo Belvedere and the Laocoön as Greek, and assumed the marble was meant to be white. Lessing's Laokoon (1766) and Goethe built on him; the arrival of the Parthenon sculptures in London (1801–12) slowly replaced Roman copies with Attic originals as the standard.

Romanticism turned admiration into politics. Keats's "On First Looking into Chapman's Homer" (1816) and "Ode on a Grecian Urn" (1819), Shelley's Prometheus Unbound (1820) and Hellas (1822), with its preface declaring "We are all Greeks", and Hölderlin's Hyperion made Greece a lost ideal to be reborn. When the Greek revolt broke out in 1821, philhellenic committees in London, Paris and Geneva raised money and volunteers on the strength of that ideal. Byron, already famous for the Greek cantos of Childe Harold and "The Isles of Greece" in Don Juan III, sailed to Missolonghi and died there of fever in April 1824; his death did more for the cause than his presence had.

Where sources disagree. Whether modern Greeks were the ancient Greeks' descendants became a scholarly weapon. Jakob Philipp Fallmerayer (1830) argued the medieval population was Slavic and Albanian, and that no "Hellenic blood" survived; Greek and philhellene scholars answered with claims of unbroken continuity. Both positions were racial constructions; the modern consensus treats continuity as linguistic and cultural, not genetic, and the question as badly framed.

Victorian schools, Bulfinch and Schliemann

In Britain the Greek revival was institutionalised through the public schools and the two ancient universities, where Greek remained a compulsory entrance requirement until 1919 (Cambridge) and 1920 (Oxford). Thomas Arnold's Rugby, Jowett's Plato (1871), Grote's twelve-volume History of Greece (1846–56) with its defence of Athenian democracy, and Gladstone's Studies on Homer (1858) made Greece the moral mirror of a liberal empire. Richard Jenkyns's The Victorians and Ancient Greece (1980) shows how Sparta was recruited for the games field, Athens for Parliament, and Plato for a coded language of male love.

For those outside the classical schools, mythology arrived through Thomas Bulfinch's The Age of Fable (Boston, 1855), a Latin-named, Ovid-based, carefully desexualised handbook that remained the standard popular reference in the English-speaking world for a century. The modern expectation that myth should be wholesome owes more to Bulfinch than to any ancient source.

Heinrich Schliemann invented Bronze Age archaeology almost by accident. Digging at Hisarlik from 1870, on a site Frank Calvert had already identified, he cut a great trench through the mound, destroyed much of the Troy VI and VII levels he was looking for, and announced "Priam's Treasure" in 1873. At Mycenae in 1876 he opened the shaft graves and the gold funerary mask he called Agamemnon's, which is in fact three centuries older than any possible Trojan War. Dörpfeld's stratigraphy and Arthur Evans's Knossos (from 1900) created a Greek Bronze Age that Herodotus never knew; Ventris's decipherment of Linear B (1952) showed its rulers spoke Greek.

Where sources disagree. Schliemann's honesty about the treasure and the mask is argued in modules 10.10 and 10.2; what matters for reception is that the doubts never dented the public legend. Which level is "Homer's Troy" (VI or VIIa) remains open.

Nietzsche and Freud

Friedrich Nietzsche's The Birth of Tragedy (1872) rejected Winckelmann's serene Greece. Tragedy, he argued, arose from the fusion of two drives: the Apollonian (form, individuation, the dream image) and the Dionysian (music, intoxication, dissolution of the self in the chorus), and it died when Euripides and Socratic rationalism tipped the balance. The book was a philological scandal: Ulrich von Wilamowitz-Moellendorff's pamphlet Zukunftsphilologie! (1872) attacked its method, Erwin Rohde defended it, and Nietzsche himself, in the 1886 "Attempt at Self-Criticism", called it badly written and too Wagnerian. Yet its core insight, that Greek culture was not calm but a hard-won victory over something dark, shaped Jane Harrison, Walter Otto and above all E. R. Dodds's The Greeks and the Irrational (1951).

Sigmund Freud took one play and made it a universal. The Interpretation of Dreams (1899) reads Sophocles' Oedipus Tyrannus as the dramatisation of a wish every male child shares; the term "Oedipus complex" appears in 1910. Rank and Jung extended the method to the whole corpus, and Narcissus, Electra and Prometheus entered the clinical vocabulary. The reading made Greek myth seem psychologically necessary rather than historically contingent, and most twentieth-century novelists and film-makers absorbed it unexamined.

Where sources disagree. Jean-Pierre Vernant's "Oedipus without the Complex" (1967) is the classic objection: Sophocles' Oedipus does not desire Jocasta; he does not know who she is, and the play is about knowledge and pollution, not repressed wish. Deleuze and Guattari's Anti-Oedipus (1972) attacked the complex as a family romance imposed on the unconscious.

Structuralists, ritualists, feminists and the post-colonial turn

Claude Lévi-Strauss's "The Structural Study of Myth" (1955) treated the Oedipus cycle as a logical machine for mediating a contradiction (are humans born from the earth or from two parents?) and declared that myth works like language, through oppositions rather than through what any single version says. The Paris school applied this to Greece with far more philological care. Jean-Pierre Vernant (Myth and Thought among the Greeks, 1965; with Pierre Vidal-Naquet, Myth and Tragedy, 1972) read myth as the thinking of the city about itself. Marcel Detienne's Gardens of Adonis (1972) dismantled Frazer's dying-god Adonis by setting the myth within Greek codes of spices, seduction and marriage, and his Invention of Mythology (1981) argued that "mythology" as a category was itself a Greek creation.

Walter Burkert came from the other direction. Homo Necans (1972) derived sacrifice and the myths attached to it from the guilt and solidarity of Palaeolithic hunting, extending Karl Meuli; Structure and History in Greek Mythology and Ritual (1979) defined myth as "a traditional tale with secondary, partial reference to something of collective importance"; Greek Religion (1977) remains the standard synthesis. Where the Paris school was synchronic and social, Burkert was diachronic and biological, and the two camps spent the 1980s politely disagreeing. G. S. Kirk (Myth, 1970) warned against any single theory; most scholars now follow him.

Feminist scholarship began with Sarah Pomeroy's Goddesses, Whores, Wives, and Slaves (1975) and matured in Nicole Loraux's Children of Athena (1981) and Tragic Ways of Killing a Woman (1985), Froma Zeitlin's Playing the Other (1996) and Mary Lefkowitz's Women in Greek Myth (1986), which argued the myths are less uniformly misogynist than first-wave readings claimed. Post-colonial work ran two ways: Edith Hall's Inventing the Barbarian (1989) showed how Athenian tragedy constructed the Persian "other"; Yannis Hamilakis's The Nation and Its Ruins (2007) showed how northern Europe constructed Greece itself. Soyinka's Bacchae of Euripides (1973) and Walcott's Omeros (1990) turned the classics against empire.

Where sources disagree. Martin Bernal's Black Athena (1987–2006) argued that Greek culture had Egyptian and Phoenician roots suppressed by nineteenth-century racism; Lefkowitz and Rogers's Black Athena Revisited (1996) rejected his linguistic and historical evidence while largely conceding his point about the historiography. Marija Gimbutas's prehistoric Great Goddess (1974–91) is rejected by most archaeologists (Cynthia Eller, The Myth of Matriarchal Prehistory, 2000) but survives in popular feminist retellings.

The current wave: retellings, film and games

The present boom is literary first. Madeline Miller's The Song of Achilles (2011) makes the Achilles–Patroclus bond explicitly erotic; this has ancient warrant (Aeschylus' lost Myrmidons, Plato, Symposium 180a, Aeschines, Against Timarchus 142–50), though Homer leaves it unstated. Her Circe (2018) gives a character with perhaps sixty lines in Odyssey 10 an entire inner life; the invention is open and well made. Pat Barker's Silence of the Girls (2018) and Women of Troy (2021) retell the Iliad through Briseis, and are closer to Euripides' Trojan Women and Hecuba than to anything new. Natalie Haynes's A Thousand Ships (2019), Pandora's Jar (2020) and Stone Blind (2022) are the most polemical; Stone Blind builds Medusa's story on her rape by Poseidon, which comes from Ovid, Metamorphoses 4.790–803, not from Hesiod, Theogony 270–83, where she "lay with" the god in a soft meadow. Stephen Fry's Mythos (2017), Heroes (2018), Troy (2020) and Odyssey (2024) do what Bulfinch did, better and franker: they weld variant traditions into a single continuous narrative no ancient author ever told, and Fry, to his credit, usually says so in the notes.

Film distorts more systematically. Wolfgang Petersen's Troy (2004) removes the gods, compresses ten years into weeks, and kills Menelaus and Agamemnon at Troy, which no ancient source does. Zack Snyder's 300 (2006) follows Herodotus 7.204–33 in outline but makes the Persians monsters and the Ephors lepers, and it has been read as a straightforward war-on-terror allegory. Disney's Hercules (1997) inverts the myth: Hera becomes a loving mother and Hades a devil, a Christian overlay that no Greek would recognise, since Hades is grim but never evil. Clash of the Titans (1981, 2010) borrows a Norse Kraken. The honest exceptions are Cacoyannis's Euripides trilogy (1962–77), Pasolini's Edipo Re (1967) and Medea (1969), and Uberto Pasolini's The Return (2024), which plays the Odyssey's homecoming without gods and without apology.

Games are the newest channel and the mixed one. God of War (2005–18) invents its protagonist outright (the Kratos of Theogony 385 is an unrelated personification) and turns Olympus into a boss ladder. Supergiant's Hades (2020) is the opposite case: its Zagreus, Nyx, Chaos and Orphic Hymns are drawn from genuinely obscure material and it has sent many players to Hesiod. Assassin's Creed Odyssey (2018) puts Herodotus on a boat with the player, muddles its chronology freely.

What the whole wave shares, and what it distorts, is a drive to moralise and psychologise: gods become good or bad, heroes get interior motives, slavery and sacrifice get explained. The ancient material is more alien than that, and the reader who moves from Miller or Fry to Hesiod should expect the shock.

How to keep reading: five reference books

  1. Timothy Gantz, Early Greek Myth: A Guide to Literary and Artistic Sources (1993, two volumes). The indispensable one. Every myth, every ancient source for it, every variant, down to vase paintings. Dry and irreplaceable.
  2. Robin Hard, The Routledge Handbook of Greek Mythology (8th edition, 2020), descended from H. J. Rose's 1928 handbook. The best single-volume narrative reference with source notes; buy this before Fry.
  3. Apollodorus, The Library of Greek Mythology, translated by Robin Hard (Oxford World's Classics). The nearest thing antiquity produced to a systematic handbook, with Hard's notes keeping Ovid and Apollodorus apart.
  4. Walter Burkert, Greek Religion (English edition 1985). Still the standard account of cult, festival and belief; to be read alongside Robert Parker's On Greek Religion (2011) for the correctives.
  5. The Oxford Classical Dictionary (4th edition, 2012). For everything else: people, places, texts, institutions. For the reception history itself, add Hardwick and Stray's A Companion to Classical Receptions (2008).

Self-test

  1. Why did Roman names for the Greek gods prevail in Western Europe?
    Answer

    Greek was effectively lost in the Latin West from roughly the sixth to the late fourteenth century, so Ovid, Virgil and Seneca were the only route to the myths, and Latin remained the language of schooling long after Greek returned.

  2. Which Greek dramas survived as the Byzantine school "triads", and why does that matter?
    Answer

    Three plays each of Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides, chosen for teaching; it means our surviving tragedy is a schoolroom selection, not a representative sample, except for the nine "alphabetic" Euripides plays preserved by accident.

  3. What did the Arabic transmission carry, and what did it not?
    Answer

    Aristotle, Galen and Greek science and philosophy (Hunayn ibn Ishaq, Averroes, then Toledo); not Homer, poetry or myth, for which there was no interest and no theatre to make sense of tragedy.

  4. Name two Palaeologan scholars and their contributions.
    Answer

    Maximus Planudes (Greek Anthology; Greek translation of Ovid) and Demetrius Triclinius (metrically informed editions of Pindar and the tragedians).

  5. Why is Winckelmann's "noble simplicity and quiet grandeur" built on a misreading?
    Answer

    He never saw Greece, judged from Roman copies, took the Laocoön and Apollo Belvedere as Greek originals and assumed the marble was meant to be white.

  6. What was Bulfinch's lasting effect on popular mythology?
    Answer

    The Age of Fable (1855) fixed Latin names, Ovidian plots and a desexualised, wholesome tone as the default for English-speaking readers for a century.

  7. Why can the "Mask of Agamemnon" not be Agamemnon's?
    Answer

    The shaft graves at Mycenae date to the sixteenth century BC, about three centuries before any plausible date for a Trojan War.

  8. What is Vernant's objection to Freud's Oedipus?
    Answer

    Sophocles' Oedipus does not knowingly desire his mother; the play concerns knowledge, fate and pollution, not repressed wish, so the complex is imposed on the text rather than found in it.

  9. How did Burkert and the Paris school differ in explaining myth?
    Answer

    Burkert looked diachronically to ritual and the biology of hunting and sacrifice; Vernant and Detienne read myth synchronically as the structured thought of the Greek city.

  10. Which ancient source does Natalie Haynes's Medusa rely on, and what is the alternative?
    Answer

    Ovid, Metamorphoses 4.790–803 (rape by Neptune in Athena's temple); Hesiod, Theogony 270–83 has Medusa lie with Poseidon in a meadow with no violence.

Carry forward

  1. Every version of Greek myth you meet has passed through a filter with its own purpose (Ovid's wit, Byzantine schooling, Bulfinch's decency, Freud's clinic, Miller's empathy); learn to name the filter before judging the story.
  2. The texts survive by selection and accident, not by merit: Byzantine triads, one Euripides manuscript, Arabic Aristotle without Homer. Treat the canon as a sample.
  3. Variants are the native condition of Greek myth. Any single smooth narrative, from Apollodorus to Fry, is a later construction; the ancient sources disagreed with each other, and that disagreement is the evidence.
