01Diogenes Laertius and the Socratic remains

Almost everything here reaches us through one compiler. Diogenes Laertius wrote his Lives of the Eminent Philosophers probably in the first half of the third century AD, some five and a half centuries after Diogenes of Sinope died. Book 2 holds the Socratics, among them Aristippus and the Cyrenaics (2.65–104) and Euclides and the Megarians (2.106–120). Book 6 holds the Cynics, from Antisthenes (6.1–19) and Diogenes (6.20–81) to Crates, Hipparchia and Menippus (6.85–101).

The order is an argument. His Ionian succession runs from Socrates through Antisthenes, Diogenes and Crates to Zeno (1.15), so the Cynics, set between the Socratics and the Stoics, become the Stoa's pedigree. His sources were Hellenistic compilers who thought in such lines (Sotion's Successions, Diocles of Magnesia, Hippobotus, Satyrus, Hermippus), and he sets their versions side by side without judging.

Most of book 6 is chreiai, pointed sayings or acts attached to a name, a form drilled in rhetorical exercise books such as Aelius Theon's Progymnasmata. Collections began early (Zeno compiled one, 6.91), and their contents drifted. In book 2 Diogenes, washing vegetables, mocks Aristippus for courting kings and Aristippus has the last word (2.68); in book 6 Plato mocks Diogenes over his lettuces and Diogenes has it (6.58). The retort belongs to whoever the chapter is about. The testimonia are gathered in Giannantoni's Socratis et Socraticorum Reliquiae (SSR, 1990): Aristippus is IV A, Antisthenes V A, Diogenes V B.

02Antisthenes

Module 6.5 gives Antisthenes' dates, his training under Gorgias and his preference for madness over pleasure; module 12.2 lists his Socratic writings; module 10.4 reads his Ajax and Odysseus. Xenophon's Symposium gives him a speech on wealth and poverty as states of the soul (4.34–44): owning almost nothing, he is the richest man present because he has enough. Diogenes Laertius adds that virtue is teachable and sufficient for happiness, needing only "Socratic strength" (6.10–11), that toil (ponos) is a good, as Heracles and Cyrus show (6.2), and that the wise man lives by the law of virtue, not the city's (6.11).

Aristotle knew a logician. Antisthenes, he says, held that nothing can be said of a thing but its own proper account, one for one, so that contradiction is impossible and error nearly so (Metaphysics 1024b32–34; Topics 104b20–21); "the Antistheneans and such uneducated people" denied that one can define what a thing is, only say what it is like, as silver is like tin (Metaphysics 1043b23–28).

This adds to the founder dispute that modules 6.5 and 7.2 weigh. Aristotle, whose life overlapped with both men's, cites the Antistheneans for logic and "the Dog" for a joke and never connects them. And the name Cynic was traced both to the Cynosarges gymnasium where Antisthenes taught and to his nickname, "plain dog" (6.13): two aetiologies pinning the name on him, as a succession written backwards would need.

03Diogenes of Sinope: what can be known

Two witnesses are Diogenes' contemporaries, though one reaches us only through book 6. Aristotle's Rhetoric quotes "the Dog" calling the taverns the common messes of Attica (1411a24–25; SSR V B 184), the oldest testimony to Diogenes and proof that the nickname was current in his lifetime. Theophrastus' Megarian said Diogenes found his way of life by watching a mouse that needed no bed, feared no dark and craved no delicacies (Diogenes Laertius 6.22).

The outline: son of Hicesias, a banker at Sinope on the Black Sea (6.20), he left after a scandal over the coinage, lived in Athens and Corinth, and died at Corinth nearly ninety (6.76–78). The three deaths on offer (raw octopus, held breath, a dog's bite: 6.76–77) and Demetrius' claim that he died on Alexander's day (6.79) are literature; so, probably, is his sale to Xeniades of Corinth (6.29–31, 6.74), told in Menippus' satire Sale of Diogenes. Whether he wrote was disputed: one list gives dialogues, a Republic and seven tragedies, while Sosicrates and Satyrus said he wrote nothing, and Satyrus gave the tragedies to his follower Philiscus (6.80). The Republic at least existed: Philodemus' On the Stoics rebukes Stoics who tried to disown it, and its doctrines (wives and children in common, the cosmos as the only true commonwealth) are summarised at 6.72.

Where sources disagree. Diocles says Diogenes' father adulterated the coinage and the son went into exile; a certain Eubulides says Diogenes did it himself; Diogenes is said to confess it in his Pordalus; another version makes it a misreading of Apollo's advice to alter the nomisma, custom as well as coin (6.20–21). Debased Sinopean coins of about 350–340 BC led Branham (1996) and Navia (2005) to give the story a historical core. If the coins date the exile, Diogenes reached Athens after Antisthenes' death, and those who keep the link must posit an earlier visit.

04The doctrine under the anecdotes

Strip out the stories and a position remains, stated most fully at 6.70–72. Training (askēsis) is twofold, of soul and of body, and nothing in life succeeds without it; one should choose the toils that accord with nature instead of useless ones, and once practised, contempt for pleasure is itself most pleasant (6.70–71). The body is drilled to need little so that the soul is free. To deface the currency is to give custom less authority than nature (6.71); what is not shameful is not shameful in public, so if eating is not wrong it is not wrong in the agora (6.69). The instrument is frank speech, parrhēsia, which Diogenes called the finest thing among men (6.69). A syllogism supplies a theology: all things belong to the gods; the wise are friends of the gods; friends share; so all things belong to the wise (6.72).

Where sources disagree. Diogenes Laertius treats Cynicism as a school but concedes that some call it only a way of life (enstasis biou, 6.103), and Hippobotus' list of schools omits it (1.19–20). The emperor Julian (Orations 6 and 7) answered that it is a philosophy, in some ways universal and the most natural, and Goulet-Cazé (L'ascèse cynique, 1986) found in 6.70–71 a reasoned theory of training, though whether the wording is Diogenes' or a later compiler's is uncertain.

05Crates and Hipparchia

Crates of Thebes flourished in the 113th Olympiad, 328–325 BC (6.87). A Hellenistic Antisthenes, author of Successions, says a beggared Telephus on the tragic stage converted him, and that he turned his estate into about two hundred talents and gave it to his fellow citizens (6.87). Hippobotus made him a pupil of Bryson the Achaean, not of Diogenes (6.85): even this link in the chain had a rival. He was called the Door-opener for walking into houses to admonish (6.86). Asked by Alexander whether he wanted Thebes rebuilt, he asked why, since another Alexander would raze it; his country was obscurity and poverty, and he a fellow citizen of Diogenes (6.93). His verse turned Homer's Crete "in the midst of the wine-dark sea" (Odyssey 19.172) into Pera, the beggar's wallet, a city in the midst of wine-dark smoke (6.85).

Hipparchia of Maroneia, sister of his pupil Metrocles, is the only woman given her own life by Diogenes Laertius (6.96–98). She threatened to kill herself unless she could marry Crates; he stripped before her and said this was the bridegroom and these his possessions (6.96). Sextus cites their open union as a custom flouted (Outlines 1.153). At a dinner she argued against Theodorus the atheist: what would not be unjust if Theodorus did it is not unjust if Hipparchia does it; Theodorus striking Theodorus is not unjust; so Hipparchia striking Theodorus is not. He pulled at her cloak and asked if this was the woman who had left her loom; she answered that time taken from the loom for education was not badly spent (6.97–98). The argument slides from striking oneself to striking another, deliberately: dialectic parodied, and turned on a Cyrenaic.

06Menippus and Cynic writing

Cynics distrusted books and wrote many. Menippus of Gadara, a Phoenician and once a slave, said by Hermippus to have lent money by the day and hanged himself when he lost it (6.99–100), left thirteen books, among them a Nekyia, Wills and Letters from the Gods (6.101). None survives. Strabo calls him the serio-comic (spoudogeloios, 16.2.29), and his mixture of prose and verse mocking philosophers and gods outlived him: in Varro's Menippean Satires, which imitated rather than translated him (Cicero, Academica 1.8), in Seneca's Apocolocyntosis (Rome module 7.1), and in Lucian (module 7.4), who sends Menippus to Hades and to the moon. Around him ran the diatribe of Bion of Borysthenes, whom Diogenes Laertius files with the Academy (4.46–58) and whose sermons survive in Teles' excerpts in Stobaeus; the meliambi of Cercidas (P.Oxy. 1082); Onesicritus' romance of Alexander's education (module 11.7). A philosophy without treatises spread through parody, anecdote and sermon, forms that attach to any name.

07Aristippus and the Cyrenaics

Aristippus of Cyrene came to Athens drawn by Socrates' fame (2.65) and was in Aegina when Socrates died (Phaedo 59c). He was the first Socratic to charge fees (2.65), and could suit himself to place, time and person, so that Diogenes called him the king's poodle (2.66). Xenophon has him refuse both to rule and to be ruled, a foreigner everywhere (Memorabilia 2.1.13); Aristotle counts him among the sophists who scorned mathematics for saying nothing of good and bad (Metaphysics 996a29–b1).

The school's doctrine (2.86–93) starts from two affections (pathē): pain, a rough motion, and pleasure, a smooth one. The end is the particular pleasure of the moment; happiness, the sum of such pleasures, is chosen for their sake, not they for its (2.87–88). Absence of pain is no pleasure but an intermediate state, like sleep (2.89–90). Bodily pleasures are the strongest (2.90), and pleasure is good even from a shameful source, though the wise man avoids wrongdoing for fear of penalty and opinion (2.88, 2.93). Beneath lies an epistemology: we grasp only our own affections, so the honest report is "I am being whitened", not "this is white" (Sextus, Against the Mathematicians 7.191–192; Plutarch, Against Colotes 1120d–e).

Where sources disagree. Diogenes Laertius says the founder made smooth motion the end (2.85). Aristocles of Messene, quoted by Eusebius (Preparation for the Gospel 14.18.31–32), says the elder Aristippus talked constantly of pleasure but never clearly defined the end, and that his grandson, "mother-taught" by Arete, first defined it as living pleasantly. Tsouna (Stanford Encyclopedia, "The Cyrenaics") concludes that the founder was no ethical hedonist and shows no interest in epistemology, against Rowe (2015), who takes him to be the source of the anonymous "subtler" thinkers of Plato's Theaetetus (156a; module 12.2); Mondolfo gave that role to the grandson.

08The later Cyrenaics: Hegesias, Anniceris, Theodorus

Around 300 BC the school split three ways. Hegesias, the Death-persuader (2.86), argued that happiness is impossible, since the body is full of suffering, the soul shares it and fortune thwarts hope; life and death are equally choiceworthy, and the wise man aims only at freedom from pain (2.94–96). Cicero says his book The Man Who Starves Himself drove so many to suicide that a King Ptolemy forbade him to lecture (Tusculan Disputations 1.83–84); Tsouna judges the story apocryphal. Anniceris kept pleasure as the end but made room for friendship, gratitude, honour to parents and service to country: a friend is chosen for goodwill, not only for use (2.96–97). Theodorus made the ends joy, from wisdom, and grief, from folly, dismissed friendship, and allowed theft, adultery and sacrilege on occasion, since none is shameful by nature (2.98–99). Cicero ranks him among the atheists (On the Nature of the Gods 1.2, 1.63) and has him tell Lysimachus, who threatened him with the cross, that it is all one to Theodorus whether he rots in the ground or in the air (Tusculan Disputations 1.102). Pessimism, sociability, amoralism: the school had no stable centre, and the Garden took its ground. Epicurus' differences from it (10.136–137) belong to module 12.6.

09The Megarians and dialectic

Module 6.5 gives Euclides at Socrates' death, his good that is one thing under many names, Eubulides' Liar and Heap, Stilpo's apatheia and Sedley's Dialectical school. Diogenes Laertius adds that Euclides' followers were called Megarians, then Eristics, then Dialecticians, from their question-and-answer method (2.106). Eubulides is credited with seven arguments: the Liar, the Disguised, the Electra, the Veiled, the Heap, the Horned and the Bald (2.108). The Veiled: you know your father; you do not know this veiled man; he is your father; so you know and do not know him. Identicals cannot always be swapped after "know". The Horned: what you have not lost you have; you have not lost horns; so you have horns. A presupposition is smuggled in. An invented epitaph has a "lying argument" killing Philitas of Cos (Athenaeus 9.401e), perhaps the Liar itself.

Diodorus Cronus was nicknamed "old fogey": Strabo says the name passed to him from his teacher Apollonius Cronus (14.2.21), Diogenes Laertius that Ptolemy I gave it when he failed to solve a puzzle of Stilpo's at the king's table (2.111–112). He argued that nothing is moving, though things have moved (Sextus, Against the Mathematicians 10), and, Boethius reports, defined the possible as what is or will be true. Against Philo, for whom a conditional is false only when it has a true antecedent and a false consequent, he required that it never could have one (Sextus, Outlines 2.110). His Master Argument (module 6.5) set three propositions against each other: every past truth is necessary; the impossible does not follow from the possible; something is possible which neither is nor will be true. One must go (Epictetus, Discourses 2.19.1).

Stilpo, asked by Demetrius Poliorcetes, who took Megara in 307 BC, whether he had lost anything, said he had seen no one carry off knowledge (2.115); Seneca makes it "all my goods are with me" (Letters 9.18–19). He denied that one thing can be predicated of another, so that only "man is man" and "good is good" are safe (Plutarch, Against Colotes 1119c–1120b). Phaedo's school at Elis, moved to Eretria by Menedemus (2.105, 2.125–144), was the Megarians' smaller cousin.

Where sources disagree. Epictetus (Discourses 2.19.2–5) reports three answers: Diodorus denied the third proposition, Cleanthes the first, Chrysippus the second. He does not say how the three clash, and modern reconstructions, from Prior ("Diodoran Modalities", 1955) to Gaskin (The Sea Battle and the Master Argument, 1995), supply the missing steps differently. The Stoic answers belong to module 12.7.

10The Cynic as social critic, and the road to Zeno

The Cynic criticised by example. Diogenes answered Plato's definition of man with a plucked chicken (6.40) and temple officials arresting a petty thief by calling them great thieves leading away a small one (6.45). Each target is a convention claiming nature's authority. Epictetus' ideal Cynic belongs to Rome module 13.6, and the imperial Cynics, Demetrius and Peregrinus, to Rome module 13.3. Foucault's last course at the Collège de France (1984; The Courage of Truth, 2011) read Cynic parrhēsia as truth told at the risk of one's life.

Where sources disagree. Diogenes applied to himself the tragic curse of the man without city or home (6.38), and Crates called his country obscurity and poverty (6.93): on this evidence "citizen of the world" (6.63) means a citizen of nowhere. Yet 6.72 also makes the cosmos the only right commonwealth, a positive claim the Stoics built on. Kleingeld and Brown (Stanford Encyclopedia, "Cosmopolitanism") find no unambiguous positive commitment and make the Cynic life itself the cosmopolitan example.

The road to the Stoa ran through Crates. Zeno of Citium, shipwrecked near Athens, read the second book of Xenophon's Memorabilia in a bookshop, asked where such men were to be found, and was told to follow Crates, who was passing (7.2–3); Crates cured his modesty with a pot of lentil soup carried through the Kerameikos and broken (7.3), and people joked that Zeno wrote his Republic on the dog's tail (7.4). Zeno also studied with Stilpo and in the Academy (7.2); his Stoa belongs to module 12.7. The Stoics never settled the debt: Apollodorus allowed that the sage might live as a Cynic, Cynicism being a short cut to virtue (7.121), while Cicero, probably following Panaetius, would reject the Cynic way entirely as an enemy of modesty (On Duties 1.148).

11Self-test

  1. Why does the arrangement of Diogenes Laertius' books 2, 6 and 7 bear on the question of who founded Cynicism?
    Answer

    He follows an Ionian succession from Socrates through Antisthenes, Diogenes and Crates to Zeno (1.15), so placing the Cynics between the Socratics and the Stoics makes them the Stoa's pedigree. The arrangement presupposes the founder claim that modules 6.5 and 7.2 question.

  2. Which contemporary evidence is older than book 6's compilers, and how does it bear on Antisthenes' claim to be the first Cynic?
    Answer

    Aristotle quotes "the Dog" on taverns (Rhetoric 1411a24–25) and Theophrastus' Megarian gives the mouse (Diogenes Laertius 6.22). Aristotle treats Antisthenes and "the Antistheneans" as logicians (Metaphysics 1024b32–34, 1043b23–28) and never links them with the Dog.

  3. How do the versions of the coinage scandal at 6.20–21 show the difference between anecdote and evidence?
    Answer

    Diocles blames the father, Eubulides blames Diogenes, Diogenes is said to confess it in his Pordalus, and the oracle version makes it a misread command to alter the nomisma. Debased Sinopean coins of about 350–340 BC are evidence of another kind: they led Branham (1996) and Navia (2005) to give the story a historical core.

  4. What does the lettuce retort at 2.68 and 6.58 show about chreiai?
    Answer

    That they migrate: the same exchange gives the last word to Aristippus in his chapter and to Diogenes, against Plato, in his. No single anecdote can be trusted as biography.

  5. State the syllogism at 6.72 that gives Cynic poverty a theology.
    Answer

    All things belong to the gods; the wise are friends of the gods; friends hold things in common; so all things belong to the wise. The Cynic who owns nothing claims everything.

  6. Reconstruct Hipparchia's argument against Theodorus and say where it fails.
    Answer

    Whatever would not be unjust if Theodorus did it is not unjust if Hipparchia does it; Theodorus striking himself is not unjust; so Hipparchia striking Theodorus is not (6.97). It slides from striking oneself to striking another, deliberately: a parody of dialectic.

  7. What does Aristocles say about the two Aristippi, and why does it matter?
    Answer

    That the elder talked of pleasure but never clearly defined the end, and that his grandson, "mother-taught" by Arete, defined it as living pleasantly (Eusebius, Preparation for the Gospel 14.18.31–32). If so, the Cyrenaic end was first defined by the founder's grandson, not the founder, and Tsouna denies the founder was an ethical hedonist at all.

  8. How do Hegesias, Anniceris and Theodorus each change Cyrenaic hedonism?
    Answer

    Hegesias: happiness is impossible, so the aim is freedom from pain and life and death are indifferent. Anniceris: friendship, gratitude and country count, and friends are chosen for goodwill. Theodorus: joy and grief, from wisdom and folly, are the ends, friendship is rejected and conventional crimes are sometimes allowed (2.94–99).

  9. What does Eubulides' Veiled argument show about the verb "know"?
    Answer

    You know your father and do not know the veiled man, who is your father, so substituting one name for another inside "know" can turn truth into falsehood.

  10. Give the three propositions of the Master Argument and the answers of Diodorus, Cleanthes and Chrysippus.
    Answer

    Every past truth is necessary; the impossible does not follow from the possible; something is possible which neither is nor will be true. Diodorus rejected the third, Cleanthes the first, Chrysippus the second (Epictetus, Discourses 2.19.1–5).

12Carry forward

  1. Separate the chreia from the doctrine and date every witness: Aristotle and Theophrastus are contemporaries, Diogenes Laertius writes five and a half centuries later from compilers who wanted successions, and anecdotes move from name to name.
  2. Socrates' followers divided his legacy into self-sufficient virtue (Antisthenes, Diogenes, Crates), pleasure and the private affection (Aristippus' school) and refutation turned into logic (the Megarians). The Stoics took the first and third and Epicurus answered the second, so each Hellenistic school begins as a choice among Socratic inheritances.
  3. Cynicism argued by living: poverty, shamelessness and frank speech performed in public to expose convention's claim to be nature. Whether that is a philosophy or a way of life was asked in antiquity and is asked again wherever the Cynic reappears, in the Stoic sage, Epictetus' ideal Cynic and Foucault's parrhēsia.
