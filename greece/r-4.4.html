The Nostoi: a lost epic and its witnesses

The Nostoi ("Returns") was a five-book epic of the Trojan Cycle attributed to Agias (Hagias) of Troezen. It bridged the Iliou Persis and the Odyssey, and it is lost. What survives is the plot summary in Proclus' Chrestomathy, a few fragments, and the parallel digest in Apollodorus, Epitome 6. Proclus' outline: Athena sets Agamemnon and Menelaus quarrelling over when to sail; Diomedes and Nestor leave first and arrive safely; Menelaus follows and is blown to Egypt with five ships; Calchas and others go overland to Colophon, where Calchas dies after losing a prophecy contest to Mopsus (Strabo 14.1.27); Agamemnon sails late, sees Achilles' ghost, survives the storm off the Capherean rocks in which Locrian Ajax drowns, and is murdered on arrival by Aegisthus and Clytemnestra; Neoptolemus, warned by Thetis, goes home by land; Orestes' revenge closes the poem, and Menelaus reaches Sparta last of all.

The Odyssey is the oldest witness to several of these returns. Nestor's narrative (Odyssey 3.130–312) and Proteus' report to Menelaus (4.499–569) are miniature Nostoi embedded in Homer, older than the Cyclic poem by at least a generation.

Where sources disagree. Proclus names Agias; Eustathius and the Suda mention a Colophonian poet, and some fragments were assigned to Eumelus of Corinth. Whether Proclus summarises the archaic poem or a later redaction is uncertain; M. L. West (Loeb Greek Epic Fragments, 2003) treats the attribution as conventional.

The returns, hero by hero

Menelaus in Egypt

Nestor tells it first (Odyssey 3.276–312): Menelaus lost his helmsman Phrontis off Sunium and stopped to bury him; a storm off Cape Malea sent part of the fleet to Crete and five ships to Egypt. He stayed eight years (4.82–89), gathering wealth in Cyprus, Phoenicia, Ethiopia and Libya. Becalmed on Pharos, he was helped by Eidothea to ambush her father Proteus, who told him to sacrifice in Egypt and revealed the deaths of Ajax and Agamemnon and Odysseus' captivity with Calypso (4.351–570). Proteus also promised him Elysium rather than Hades, as Zeus' son-in-law (4.561–569). He reached Sparta on the day Orestes held the funeral feast for Clytemnestra and Aegisthus (3.311).

Herodotus heard another story from the priests at Memphis (Histories 2.113–120): Paris was blown to Egypt, king Proteus confiscated Helen, the Greeks fought ten years for a woman never in Troy, and Menelaus, collecting her afterwards, sacrificed two Egyptian children for a wind and fled. Herodotus argues Homer knew this version (citing Odyssey 4.227–230) but rejected it as unsuited to epic. Stesichorus' Palinode (fr. 192 PMG, via Plato, Phaedrus 243a) had already denied Helen went to Troy, and Euripides' Helen (412 BC) built a play on the Egyptian Helen and the phantom at Troy.

Where sources disagree. Homer's Proteus is a shape-shifting sea-god; Herodotus' is a mortal king at Memphis. Whether the Egyptian version is local tradition or Greek rationalising reflected back by Herodotus' informants is disputed; Alan Lloyd's commentary leans to the latter.

Nestor

The one uncomplicated return (Odyssey 3.130–183). After the Atreidae quarrelled, half the army left with Menelaus; at Tenedos Odysseus turned back to Agamemnon, while Nestor and Diomedes pressed on, took the open-sea route from Lesbos to Geraestus, and Nestor reached Pylos "nor did a wind fail" (3.182–183). Proclus and Apollodorus (Epitome 6.1) agree. In Book 3 Nestor's piety becomes the standard against which every other return is measured.

Diomedes

Homer knows only that he came home (3.180–181). Later tradition made Argos unliveable: Nauplius, avenging Palamedes, corrupted the chiefs' wives, and Aegialeia took Cometes as lover (Apollodorus, Epitome 6.9); Mimnermus (fr. 22 West) blamed Aphrodite, avenging her wound in Iliad 5. Diomedes went to Italy, was received by Daunus and founded Argyripa (Arpi) in Apulia. Lycophron (Alexandra 592–632) and Virgil (Aeneid 11.243–295) tell of his companions turned into birds on the Diomedean islands (Strabo 6.3.9); in Virgil the Latin embassy finds a chastened Diomedes who refuses to fight Aeneas again. Pindar (Nemean 10.7) says Athena made him a god.

Idomeneus

Nestor says Idomeneus lost no man on the way home (3.191–192). Two later traditions undo this. In Apollodorus (Epitome 6.9–10) his foster son Leucus seduces and kills queen Meda, seizes ten cities and expels the returning king. Servius (on Aeneid 3.121) has Idomeneus vow in a storm to sacrifice the first living thing he meets, which is his own son; plague follows and the Cretans drive him out. Virgil settles him in the Sallentine plain of Calabria (Aeneid 3.121–122, 400–401).

Where sources disagree. Diodorus (5.79.4) says Idomeneus and Meriones died in Crete and were honoured with a tomb at Knossos, contradicting the exile. The child-sacrifice vow appears only in Servius and later mythographers; its antiquity is uncertain.

Ajax son of Oileus and the shipwreck

Locrian Ajax had dragged Cassandra from Athena's statue during the sack (Proclus, Iliou Persis), and the goddess's anger followed the fleet; in Euripides' Trojan Women (77–94) she and Poseidon agree the storm in advance. Homer's version (Odyssey 4.499–511) is pointed: Poseidon drove Ajax onto the rocks of Gyrae but saved him, and he "would have escaped his doom, hated though he was by Athena", had he not boasted he had survived against the gods' will; Poseidon split the rock and he drowned. The Nostoi set the wreck at the Capherean rocks of Euboea, where Apollodorus (Epitome 6.7–11) adds Nauplius lighting false beacons. Lycophron (Alexandra 1141–1173) links the crime to the historical Locrian tribute of maidens to Athena at Troy.

Neoptolemus

Odysseus tells Achilles in Hades that his son returned unharmed (11.505–537), and Book 4 opens with his wedding to Hermione. The Nostoi gave the route: warned by Thetis, he went overland through Thrace, met Odysseus at Maroneia, buried Phoenix, and reached the Molossians in Epirus, where Peleus recognised him. Apollodorus (Epitome 6.12–14) adds Andromache and their son Molossus, ancestor of the Epirote kings (Strabo 7.7.8); Virgil's Buthrotum (Aeneid 3.294–336) is this settlement. His death at Delphi is contested. Pindar, Paean 6.98–120, says Apollo killed him in the sanctuary for slaughtering Priam at an altar; in Nemean 7.34–47, apparently answering Aeginetan offence, Pindar makes it a quarrel over sacrificial meats and him the honoured hero of Delphic rites. Euripides' Andromache (1085–1165) has Orestes engineer the murder. Pausanias (10.24.6) saw his tomb.

Teucer

Telamon banished Ajax's half-brother from Salamis for failing to bring Ajax home or avenge him (Sophocles' lost Teucer; Pacuvius). He founded Salamis in Cyprus, stated as fact by Pindar (Nemean 4.46), used by Isocrates to flatter Evagoras (Evagoras 18), and turned by Horace into "nil desperandum Teucro duce" (Odes 1.7.21–32). Euripides opens Helen (68–163) with Teucer in Egypt on his way to Cyprus; Virgil has him visit Belus in Sidon (Aeneid 1.619–626). Justin (44.3.2–3) takes him on to Spain, a late foundation legend of no archaic authority.

Philoctetes in Italy

Homer has him home safe (Odyssey 3.190) and the only archer who outshot Odysseus at Troy (8.219–220). Later writers drive him from Thessalian Meliboea by revolt and settle him in southern Italy: Lycophron (Alexandra 911–929) names Crimissa, Petelia and Macalla, with his tomb by the Crathis; Strabo (6.1.3) credits him with Petelia, Crimissa and Chone near Croton; the pseudo-Aristotelian Mirabilia (107) says he dedicated Heracles' bow in the temple of Apollo Alaeus at Macalla. Virgil's "little Petelia, propped on Philoctetes' wall" (Aeneid 3.402) sits among the Italian Greek cities. With Diomedes in Apulia and Idomeneus in Calabria, the Nostoi gave Magna Graecia heroic founders and gave Rome's poets a map of displaced Greeks to set against Aeneas.

The Odyssey: structure, and why it starts in the middle

Three movements. The Telemachy (Books 1–4) opens on Olympus and in Ithaca and sends Telemachus to Pylos and Sparta, where the returns of Nestor and Menelaus are told and Odysseus is spoken of but absent. The Phaeacian narration (Books 5–12) brings Odysseus from Ogygia to Scheria, where in Books 9–12 (the Apologoi) he tells his wanderings in the first person. The return and vengeance (Books 13–24) runs from Eumaeus' hut to the palace, the bow, the slaughter, and the reunions with Penelope and Laertes. The scholia report that Aristophanes of Byzantium and Aristarchus set the poem's telos at 23.296, where Odysseus and Penelope go to bed.

The poem begins in the tenth year, with Odysseus seven years on Calypso's island, and lets him narrate the earlier adventures himself. Horace coined the phrase: Homer "hurries the hearer into the midst of things", in medias res (Ars Poetica 148–149). Aristotle (Poetics 1451a) praised the Odyssey for making one action, not one life. The gains are large. The fabulous material is put in Odysseus' mouth, so the poet does not vouch for it and his reliability becomes part of his character. Telemachus grows up while his father is offstage. The weight falls on homecoming, not adventure. And the opening council, where Zeus complains that mortals blame the gods for suffering they bring on themselves (1.32–43), frames the whole poem as a moral demonstration.

Where sources disagree. Analysts (Kirchhoff, Wilamowitz, Denys Page, The Homeric Odyssey, 1955) found Book 24's language, second Nekyia and Laertes scene late. Unitarians (Heubeck in the Oxford commentary; Stephanie West) answer that the plot needs the suitors' families dealt with and that the three-generation reunion is its goal. Current consensus leans to keeping Book 24, but it is not settled.

The wanderings in order

The Apologoi run from Troy to Ogygia as variations on hospitality, appetite and restraint. The Cicones at Ismarus (9.39–61) are an ordinary raid gone wrong because the crew will not leave the beach. A storm off Malea (9.80–81) pushes the fleet out of the known world. The Lotus-eaters (9.82–104) are the gentlest danger: whoever eats forgets his nostos. Polyphemus (9.105–566) is the inverted host who eats his guests; Odysseus beats him with wine, a stake and the name "Nobody" (Outis, punning on me tis, "no one", and metis, "cunning", 9.405–414), then undoes the victory by shouting his real name (9.502–505), giving the Cyclops something to curse. Poseidon's hostility dates from that boast. Aeolus (10.1–79) gives perfect hospitality and the winds in a bag; the crew's suspicion opens it in sight of Ithaca, and Aeolus refuses a man the gods evidently hate. The Laestrygonians (10.80–132) sink eleven of twelve ships. Circe (10.133–574) turns men to pigs; Hermes' moly and a drawn sword win Odysseus a year's comfort the crew must break for him. The Nekyia (Book 11) is Circe's errand: Tiresias gives the terms of return (11.100–137) and the dead set Odysseus' choice of life beside Achilles' regret (11.488–491). He passes the Sirens bound to the mast (12.165–200), chooses Scylla over Charybdis and loses six men (12.201–259), and is forced to land on Thrinacia, where the starving crew kill Helios' cattle (12.260–402). Zeus' bolt sinks the last ship; Odysseus alone survives a second Charybdis and drifts to Calypso (12.403–453), where seven years pass and immortality is offered and declined (5.203–224). Only then comes Phaeacia, the halfway house between fairyland and Ithaca, with its ideal palace and magic ships (Books 6–8, 13).

Where sources disagree. Strabo (1.2.9–19) took the geography seriously; Eratosthenes said you would find Odysseus' route when you found the cobbler who sewed the bag of winds. Sicily for the Cyclopes and Laestrygonians was conventional by Thucydides (6.2.1); most modern scholars treat the wanderings as deliberately off the map.

Recognition, the suitors and the bow

Aristotle (Poetics 1459b) called the Odyssey "complex" because anagnorisis runs through it. The scenes are graded. Telemachus (16.172–219) must be told and shown a transformation; the dog Argos (17.290–327) knows by scent and dies. Eurycleia (19.386–507) finds the boar's-tusk scar, and the poet suspends the moment for seventy lines to tell how Odysseus got it on Parnassus; Auerbach's "Odysseus' Scar" (Mimesis, ch. 1) made this the type-case of Homer's fully lit narrative. Eumaeus and Philoetius (21.188–225) are shown the scar deliberately. Penelope (23.85–230) refuses every proof and sets her own test: she orders the bed moved, and Odysseus' protest that he built it round a living olive trunk is the one secret no impostor could know. Laertes (24.315–348) is tested almost cruelly before the scar and the list of trees convince him.

Where sources disagree. Philip Harsh (1950) argued Penelope recognises Odysseus in Book 19 and sets the contest knowing who will win; Amory and Winkler developed an "intuitive" recognition. Most commentators (Heubeck, Russo) keep her ignorant until the bed test and read the contest as a surrender she cannot explain. The text is deliberately opaque.

The suitors number 108 (16.245–253). Antinous is violent and unrepentant, Eurymachus smooth and dishonest, Amphinomus decent enough that Odysseus warns him and Athena still refuses him escape (18.125–157). The bow (21.11–41) was a guest-gift from Iphitus, later murdered by Heracles, and never went to Troy; it belongs to the house. The contest is to string it and shoot through twelve axes. Telemachus nearly strings it and is checked by his father's glance (21.128–129); every suitor fails. Odysseus turns it over "as a man skilled in the lyre stretches a string", plucks it so it sings like a swallow, and shoots through the axes as Zeus thunders (21.404–423). Book 22 opens with Antinous killed at his wine-cup, the inversion of the feast he has stolen, and ends with the maids hanged and the hall fumigated.

Themes

Nostos. The first word is andra, but the organising noun is return. The other returns in Books 3, 4 and 11 define Odysseus' by contrast: Nestor's effortless, Menelaus' rich but late, Agamemnon's fatal, Achilles' never. Nostos is not arrival but restoration of a position: husband, father, son, king.

Xenia. Good hosts (Nestor, Menelaus, Alcinous, Eumaeus) ask the stranger's name only after feeding him; bad hosts eat the guest (Polyphemus, Laestrygonians) or eat the host out of house (the suitors). The disguises make the second half a theoxeny, and the poem says so: "the gods go about cities in the likeness of strangers" (17.485–487).

Metis. Odysseus' intelligence is practical and verbal: the pun, the Cretan lies, the bow. It is set against bie, force, which belongs to the Cyclops and to Achilles; Athena loves him for it (13.291–299). Penelope shares it (the shroud, the gifts, the bed), and the poem calls their marriage homophrosyne (6.181–185).

Identity. Odysseus is polytropos, and he gives his name with full weight only three times: to the Cyclops (disastrously), to the Phaeacians (9.19–20), and to the suitors (22.35). Each disguise shed is a stage in becoming himself; he refuses Calypso's immortality because it would be a life without a name.

The oikos. The household is the unit the poem defends: stores, servants, bed, land. The slaughter is not revenge but repair of an oikos the suitors are consuming (bioton katedousin, "they eat up the livelihood", 1.160 and often). Laertes in his orchard, Eumaeus with his pigs, Penelope at her loom and Telemachus coming of age are parts of one institution.

Against the Iliad, and the question of one poet

The Iliad is tragic: its subject is anger, its horizon death, and its hero chooses glory over return. The Odyssey is comic in the old sense: its subject is survival, its horizon home, and its hero chooses return over immortality and hears glory recanted by Achilles' ghost (11.488–491). The gods differ. In the Iliad they quarrel, deceive each other and fight on the field; justice is not their business. In the Odyssey Zeus opens the poem insisting men suffer by their own fault (1.32–43), Poseidon's hostility is provoked and bounded, and the plot is largely a partnership between Athena and one mortal. The Odyssey moralises: the suitors are warned, Eumaeus rewarded. The world differs too: the Iliad is aristocratic and martial on one plain; the Odyssey has swineherds, beggars, Phoenician traders, a farm dog and the kitchen, and it travels. Pseudo-Longinus (On the Sublime 9.11–15) read this as biography, the Odyssey as "the work of old age", a setting sun lapsing into fable. Aristotle (Poetics 1459b) put it structurally: the Iliad simple and pathetic, the Odyssey complex and ethical.

Authorship was contested in antiquity. The chorizontes ("separators"), Xenon and Hellanicus, assigned the poems to different poets on grounds of language and inconsistency; Aristarchus answered them, and the single Homer prevailed, composing the Iliad young and the Odyssey old. The modern debate begins with Wolf's Prolegomena ad Homerum (1795); Milman Parry's demonstration in the 1930s that both poems are built from oral formulaic diction changed its terms, since shared technique makes shared authorship unprovable by style alone.

Where sources disagree. Janko's linguistic statistics (Homer, Hesiod and the Hymns, 1982) put the Odyssey slightly later but within a generation, compatible with one poet. M. L. West (The Making of the Odyssey, 2014) argues for a different seventh-century poet with the Iliad before him. Nagy's evolutionary model dissolves the question: both texts crystallised over centuries of performance. Monro's Law, that the Odyssey never repeats an Iliad episode, is accepted by all and explained differently: one poet's design, or a successor's deference. Unresolved.

Self-test

  1. Who is the traditional author of the Nostoi, and how do we know its plot?
    Answer

    Agias (Hagias) of Troezen. The poem is lost; we have Proclus' summary in the Chrestomathy, a few fragments, and the parallel in Apollodorus, Epitome 6.

  2. Where does Homer's Menelaus learn the fate of Agamemnon and Ajax, and from whom?
    Answer

    On Pharos off Egypt, from the sea-god Proteus, ambushed with Eidothea's help (Odyssey 4.351–570).

  3. How does Herodotus' Egyptian version of Helen differ from Homer's?
    Answer

    In Histories 2.113–120 Helen never reaches Troy; king Proteus of Memphis keeps her, and Menelaus collects her after the war, then sacrifices two Egyptian children and flees.

  4. Why does Locrian Ajax drown, according to Proteus in Odyssey 4?
    Answer

    Poseidon saved him from the wreck at Gyrae, but Ajax boasted he had escaped against the gods' will; Poseidon split the rock and he drowned. Athena's anger over Cassandra lay behind the storm.

  5. Give Pindar's two incompatible accounts of Neoptolemus' death.
    Answer

    Paean 6: Apollo killed him at Delphi for slaughtering Priam at an altar. Nemean 7: he died in a quarrel over sacrificial meats and became the presiding hero of Delphi.

  6. Which Italian cities did tradition assign to Philoctetes, and what did he dedicate there?
    Answer

    Petelia, Crimissa, Chone and Macalla near Croton (Lycophron, Strabo); Heracles' bow and arrows in the temple of Apollo Alaeus (pseudo-Aristotle, Mirabilia 107).

  7. Name the three movements of the Odyssey and their books.
    Answer

    Telemachy, 1–4; Phaeacian narration (with the Apologoi, 9–12), 5–12; return and vengeance, 13–24.

  8. What is the pun in Odysseus' false name, and where does it pay off?
    Answer

    Outis, "Nobody", echoes me tis, "no one", and metis, "cunning". It pays off at 9.405–414 when the other Cyclopes hear that "no one" is hurting Polyphemus and leave.

  9. What is Penelope's test, and why can only Odysseus pass it?
    Answer

    She orders the bed moved out of the chamber (23.177–230). Odysseus built it round a living olive trunk, so it cannot be moved, and only the two of them know.

  10. Who were the chorizontes, and what is Monro's Law?
    Answer

    Hellenistic critics (Xenon, Hellanicus) who held the two epics had different authors. Monro's Law observes that the Odyssey never retells an Iliad episode; both sides accept it and explain it differently.

Carry forward

  1. The Nostoi are a system of contrasts, not a list: Nestor, Menelaus, Agamemnon and Achilles are placed round Odysseus so that his return defines the right way home. Later modules on Agamemnon and Orestes, and on Aeneas, build on this frame.
  2. The displaced heroes (Diomedes, Idomeneus, Philoctetes, Teucer) are the mythic charter of Greek settlement in Italy and Cyprus, and the material Virgil reworks for Aeneas. Keep the geography in mind for Magna Graecia and Roman transmission.
  3. The Odyssey is a poem about metis, xenia and the oikos, and its gods are more moral than the Iliad's. Zeus' speech on human responsibility is the hinge for later modules on Hesiod, Solon and tragedy.
