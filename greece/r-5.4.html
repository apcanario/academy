Messenia and the helots

Sparta's peculiarity begins with a land grab. Between roughly 735 and 715 the Spartans crossed Taygetus and took the Messenian plain, the richest farmland in the Peloponnese; a revolt in the mid-seventh century took a generation to crush. The only contemporary witness is the poet Tyrtaeus, who says the first war lasted "nineteen years" under "our fathers' fathers" until the enemy abandoned Ithome (fr. 5 West), and describes the defeated as "like asses worn down by great burdens", handing over half of all the land produced (frs. 6–7 West). Everything else, Aristomenes included, comes from Pausanias, Description of Greece book 4, written in the second century AD from Messenian traditions formed after liberation in 369.

The conquered became helots, a status the ancients themselves found hard to classify: Pollux (Onomasticon 3.83) puts them "between free men and slaves", Strabo (8.5.4) calls them state slaves. Bound to the land, they owed a fixed share to an individual Spartiate, lived in their own villages and were freed only by the state; Messenian helots, unlike Laconian ones, remained a conquered people. Thucydides says "most Spartan institutions have always been designed with a view to security against the helots" (4.80), recording in the same passage that about two thousand helots who claimed to have fought best were garlanded as free and then made to disappear. The treaty of 421 bound Athens to help Sparta "if the slaves revolt" (Thucydides 5.23). Each year the ephors declared war on the helots so that killing them carried no pollution (Plutarch, Lycurgus 28, citing Aristotle). The earthquake of 465/4 triggered the great revolt on Ithome (Thucydides 1.101–103).

Where sources disagree. Critias (fr. 37 DK) said that in Sparta "the slaves are most slaves and the free most free", and Jean Ducat's Les Hilotes (1990) treats helotage as a coherent system of institutionalised contempt. Nino Luraghi (The Ancient Messenians, 2008) argues that a "Messenian" ethnic identity was largely constructed after 369 and that the eighth-century conquest narrative is a retrojection. Whether Messenians existed as a people before liberation is genuinely uncertain.

Lycurgus and the Great Rhetra

Herodotus (1.65–66) says the Spartans were the worst-governed Greeks until Lycurgus gave them laws; Delphi hailed him as "god or man, I do not know which". Plutarch opens his Life admitting that "nothing whatsoever can be said about the lawgiver Lycurgus that is not disputed" (Lycurgus 1). Xenophon (Constitution of the Lacedaemonians 10.8) makes him contemporary with the Heraclids, Aristotle with the first Olympiad. Lycurgus had a hero-cult and sanctuary (Herodotus 1.66; Plutarch, Lycurgus 31). A lawgiver with a shrine and a biography of pure anecdote is almost certainly a culture hero credited after the fact with an evolved system; "Lycurgan" is a label, not a date.

The institutions were real. Two hereditary kings, Agiad and Eurypontid, descended from the twin sons of Aristodemus (Herodotus 6.52), were priests of Zeus Lacedaemon and Zeus Uranius and generals (Herodotus 6.56–59; Xenophon, Constitution 15); after 506 only one king could lead an army (Herodotus 5.75). The gerousia was twenty-eight men over sixty elected for life, plus the kings; it prepared business and tried capital cases. The apella, all Spartiates, voted by shouting without right of amendment; Thucydides 1.87 shows the ephor Sthenelaidas pretending he could not hear the result and forcing a division. The five ephors, elected annually, summoned the assembly, exchanged monthly oaths with the kings and could fine or imprison any magistrate (Xenophon, Constitution 8, 15); Aristotle thought them "like tyrants", poor men open to bribes (Politics 1270b).

The Great Rhetra (Plutarch, Lycurgus 6) is the earliest Greek constitutional text we have in roughly its own words: a dedication to Zeus Syllanios and Athena Syllania; the people "tribed into tribes and obed into obes"; a gerousia of thirty including the kings (archagetai); assemblies "season by season between Babyka and Knakion"; the elders to introduce and set aside business, the people to have the power of decision. A rider, attributed to kings Theopompus and Polydorus, lets elders and kings dissolve the assembly "if the people speak crookedly". Tyrtaeus' Eunomia (fr. 4 West) versifies the same scheme, anchoring it in the seventh century.

Where sources disagree. Plutarch (Lycurgus 7) and Aristotle (Politics 1313a) say Theopompus added the ephorate about 130 years after Lycurgus; Herodotus (1.65) credits Lycurgus with it. Forrest and Cartledge accept a seventh-century core for the Rhetra; others treat the whole text as a fourth-century or later reconstruction, and the clause giving the people power rests on Wade-Gery's emendation, not a manuscript reading.

The agoge, the syssitia and the krypteia

Xenophon, Constitution 2–4, and Plutarch, Lycurgus 16–18, five centuries apart, agree on a shape. A newborn boy was inspected by the tribal elders and, if weak, exposed at the Apothetae (Plutarch only). At seven he joined the agelai ("herds") under the paidonomos and his whip-bearers. Boys went barefoot, had one cloak a year, slept on reeds, were kept hungry, encouraged to steal and whipped if caught (Xenophon, Constitution 2.1–9; Plutarch, Lycurgus 17–18, with the stolen fox). Youths of about twenty, eirenes, commanded the younger; the age-grade vocabulary (rhobidas, pais, melleiren, eiren) survives only in Roman-era inscriptions. Both authors treat pederastic attachments as normal and educational, Xenophon (2.12–14) implausibly denying any sexual element. At twenty a youth stood for a mess; at thirty he was a full citizen. Those who failed became hypomeiones, "inferiors" (Xenophon, Hellenica 3.3.6). The word agoge is not attested for the system before the Hellenistic period.

The syssitia (Spartan pheiditia; Alcman fr. 98 and Aristotle know the older andreia) were groups of about fifteen who dined together every evening for life; admission needed a unanimous vote by bread pellets (Plutarch, Lycurgus 12). Each member contributed monthly a medimnos of barley, eight choes of wine, five minae of cheese, two and a half of figs and a little money. Aristotle saw the flaw: a man who could not pay lost his citizenship, so the egalitarian mess became a wealth filter (Politics 1271a). The messes were also the army's basic unit (Herodotus 1.65; Xenophon, Constitution 5).

The krypteia is described in two incompatible ways. Plato (Laws 633b–c) makes it an endurance test, young men roaming barefoot in winter without servants. Plutarch, quoting Aristotle (Lycurgus 28), makes it a death squad: youths with daggers hiding by day and killing helots on the roads by night.

Where sources disagree. Jeanmaire (1913) read the krypteia as an initiatory rite of passage; others see a secret police. Nigel Kennell (The Gymnasium of Virtue, 1995) argued that much of Plutarch's agoge, including the whipping contest at Artemis Orthia that Roman tourists watched from a purpose-built theatre (Cicero, Tusculan Disputations 2.34), is a third-century and Roman reconstruction; Jean Ducat (Spartan Education, 2006) finds more of a Classical core. Infant exposure rests on Plutarch alone.

Spartan women and property

Xenophon (Constitution 1.3–9) says Lycurgus prescribed physical training for girls to produce strong children and allowed an elderly husband to bring in a younger man to father children, or a man to borrow another's wife with consent. Plutarch (Lycurgus 14–15) adds processions, choral mockery of bachelors, naked exercise and a wedding in which the bride's head was shaved and she was dressed as a man before the groom came to her in the dark and returned to his mess. Girls married late, about eighteen, and Cynisca, sister of Agesilaus, won the Olympic chariot race in 396 and 392 (Pausanias 3.8.1).

Property is the key. The 9,000 equal, inalienable kleroi of Plutarch (Lycurgus 8, 16) are contradicted by Aristotle, who says land was unevenly held, women controlled nearly two-fifths of it through heiresses (patrouchoi) and large dowries, and this was ruining the state (Politics 1270a). Stephen Hodkinson (Property and Wealth in Classical Sparta, 2000) showed that partible inheritance including daughters, combined with marriage between wealthy houses, produces exactly this concentration. Aristotle also calls the women licentious and useless when Thebes invaded in 370/69 (Politics 1269b).

Where sources disagree. The equal-lots scheme is now widely seen as a third-century ideal projected back. Sarah Pomeroy (Spartan Women, 2002) accepts real female economic power; sceptics note the entire picture is drawn by male non-Spartans with an argument to make, and whether wife-sharing was an institution or occasional elite practice is uncertain.

The Peloponnesian League

"The Lacedaemonians and their allies" is the ancient name; the League is a modern term. It began in the sixth century when Sparta, having failed to helotise Tegea, instead bound her by treaty after bringing home the bones of Orestes (Herodotus 1.66–68); a clause preserved by Aristotle (fr. 592 Rose, in Plutarch, Greek Questions 5) required Tegea to expel Messenians. Allies paid no tribute and kept autonomy, but swore to have the same friends and enemies and to follow Sparta's lead; Sparta's method was to install congenial oligarchies (Thucydides 1.19). Each ally had one vote in a congress whose majority bound all, though Sparta first decided in her own assembly whether to put the question (Thucydides 1.87, 1.119–125). Corinth's refusal in 506 to attack Athens and her defeat of the proposal to restore Hippias (Herodotus 5.75, 5.91–93) are the first sign that allies could say no, and probably mark the congress's birth. The standard escape clause, "unless gods or heroes prevent", appears in the Argive treaty of 418 (Thucydides 5.30). The League dissolved after Leuctra in 371, when Epaminondas freed Messenia.

Where sources disagree. Larsen argued for a formal constitution with a congress from the late sixth century; de Ste Croix (The Origins of the Peloponnesian War, 1972) and Cartledge stress bilateral oaths, with the congress appearing only when a historian needs to explain a Spartan defeat.

Religion, festivals and the divine ancestors

Sparta's festivals repeatedly shaped her wars. The Hyacinthia at Amyclae, in early summer, honoured Apollo and the dead youth Hyacinthus, whose tomb lay beneath Apollo's colossal archaic statue (Pausanias 3.19.2–3); mourning gave way to choruses, chariot races, girls in wicker carts and a feast at which citizens entertained helots and foreigners (Polycrates in Athenaeus 4.139d–f). The Spartans delayed marching to Plataea for it (Herodotus 9.7), the Amyclaeans went home for it even on campaign (Xenophon, Hellenica 4.5.11). The Gymnopaedia, in midsummer, was choral dancing in the agora in full heat (Pausanias 3.11.9; Plato, Laws 633c); Demaratus was humiliated there (Herodotus 6.67), and when news of Leuctra arrived during the men's chorus the ephors let the dance finish (Xenophon, Hellenica 6.4.16). The Carneia, in late summer, honoured Apollo Carneius, ram-god of the Dorian migration: Demetrius of Scepsis (Athenaeus 4.141e–f) describes nine tents each housing nine men dining under command as in a camp, and the staphylodromoi who chased a garlanded runner for good luck. Its truce is why only Leonidas' advance guard reached Thermopylae (Herodotus 7.206), and the Argives twice manipulated their calendar around it (Thucydides 5.54, 5.75–76). Add Artemis Orthia at Limnae, with its thousands of lead votives and grotesque masks, and the Menelaion at Therapne, where Helen and Menelaus received cult (Herodotus 6.61).

The kings claimed Heracles. Herodotus traces Leonidas through Aristodemus to Hyllus and Heracles (7.204); Tyrtaeus sings that "Zeus himself gave this city to the Heraclidae" (fr. 2 West), making the Dorian conquest a recovery of the hero's inheritance. The Dioscuri, Laconian twins buried at Therapne (Pindar, Nemean 10.55–59), doubled the dual kingship; after 506 one Tyndarid, represented by the dokana of two beams and crossbars, went to war with the one king while the other stayed home (Herodotus 5.75; Plutarch, On Brotherly Love 478a). Sparta also annexed the pre-Dorian past: Menelaus and Helen, the bones of Orestes and later of Tisamenus (Pausanias 7.1.8), and Cleomenes' retort on the Athenian acropolis, "I am no Dorian but an Achaean" (Herodotus 5.72).

Where sources disagree. Pausanias ties the Gymnopaedia to Apollo; lexicographers tie it to the dead of Thyrea (Herodotus 1.82). The sixth-century "Achaean policy" of claiming pre-Dorian heroes to legitimise rule (Leahy, Boedeker, Malkin) is a modern inference, not a Spartan statement.

Tyrtaeus and Alcman: the only Spartan voices

Nearly everything written about Sparta was written by outsiders; the exceptions are two seventh-century poets. Tyrtaeus wrote elegiacs for the Messenian war: historical pieces (frs. 2, 4–7 West) and exhortations that became the canon of hoplite ethics: "it is a fine thing for a brave man to die, fallen in the front ranks" (fr. 10.1–2); the warrior who "plants his feet and stands fast, biting his lip" (fr. 11); excellence as courage above wealth, beauty or athletic victory (fr. 12). Spartans sang him on campaign at the king's tent (Lycurgus, Against Leocrates 106–107; Philochorus in Athenaeus 14.630f). Athenian tradition made him an Athenian, even a lame schoolmaster sent by Delphi (Plato, Laws 629a; Pausanias 4.15.6).

Alcman, a generation later, is the Sparta nobody expects. The Louvre Partheneion (fr. 1 PMGF, a papyrus found at Saqqara in 1855) is a girls' choral song naming the leaders Hagesichora and Agido, comparing them to racehorses, invoking Aotis, and teasing itself about its singing; fr. 3 praises Astymeloisa with "limb-loosening desire". Elsewhere he writes of sleeping mountains (fr. 89), of the old man who wishes to be a sea-bird (fr. 26), of porridge and honey cakes (fr. 17). This is a Sparta of luxury, humour and cosmopolitan taste, matching the sixth-century Laconian pottery and bronzes exported to Samos and Etruria. Austerity came later, if it ever fully came.

Where sources disagree. Ancient scholars already argued whether Alcman was Lydian from Sardis or Spartan from Messoa (Suda; Palatine Anthology 7.18, 7.709). Calame (1977) reads the Partheneion as initiatory ritual, others as a festival of Orthia or Helen; the occasion is unknown. On Tyrtaeus, Snodgrass saw a developed phalanx; van Wees and Krentz read fr. 11's mixed fighting as a looser order.

Oliganthropia

Demaratus tells Xerxes Sparta has about 8,000 men (Herodotus 7.234). At Leuctra about 1,200 served and 400 died in an afternoon (Xenophon, Hellenica 6.4.15). Aristotle says the land could support 1,500 cavalry and 30,000 hoplites but there were "not even a thousand" (Politics 1270a); Plutarch gives 700 for the 240s, about 100 with land (Agis 5). Aristotle's name for the disease is oliganthropia, and he blamed the property system. Earthquake, war and late marriage contributed, but the mechanism was social: men fell below the mess contribution and became hypomeiones, like Cinadon, whose conspiracy of 399 drew on helots, neodamodeis, inferiors and perioikoi who "would gladly eat the Spartiates raw" (Xenophon, Hellenica 3.3.4–11). Plutarch (Agis 5) blames a law of the ephor Epitadeus permitting gifts and bequests of land; the gold that flooded in after 404 (Plutarch, Lysander 17) did the rest.

Where sources disagree. Hodkinson doubts Epitadeus existed, since partible inheritance already explains the concentration; others accept a fourth-century change. Whether the Spartiate body shrank chiefly by downward mobility or by real population loss remains debated.

The Spartan mirage

François Ollier coined le mirage spartiate (1933–43) for the gap between Sparta and what others made of her. The admirers were Athenian from the start: Critias; Plato, whose Republic and Laws lean on the model; and Xenophon, who served under Agesilaus, put his sons through Spartan training as trophimoi (Plutarch, Agesilaus 20) and wrote the Constitution and the encomiastic Agesilaus. Yet Constitution 14 says bluntly that Spartans of his day no longer obey Lycurgus, hoard money and prefer governing abroad: the ideal is already a lost past. Plutarch drew on Aristotle, Dicaearchus, Phylarchus and the Stoic Sphaerus, adviser to Cleomenes III, whose revolution of the 220s revived "Lycurgan" institutions and partly invented them. Polybius (6.45–50) passed the model to Rome; Rousseau, Prussia, the Third Reich and the film 300 each found what they wanted.

Modern correction runs in three directions. Chronology: Finley's "sixth-century revolution" and the archaeology of Orthia and Laconian pottery show an Archaic Sparta that was wealthy and open, with austerity arriving late. Property: Hodkinson's demonstration that Sparta was a highly unequal society of landowners from the start, and that the homoioi, "the similars", shared a lifestyle, not wealth. Texts: Kennell's and Ducat's redating of the agoge and Luraghi's dismantling of the Messenian narrative show how far Hellenistic and Roman Sparta rebuilt itself for philosophers and tourists. Most of what we "know" about Sparta is what someone wanted her to be.

Where sources disagree. Xenophon is read by some (Humble, Lipka) as a quietly critical observer and by others as a committed Laconiser, and the original position of chapter 14 is disputed. Mogens Hansen (2009) argued Sparta was a normal polis; Hodkinson replied that in land tenure, helotage and the perioikoi it was structurally unlike any other.

Self-test

  1. What is the one contemporary source for the Messenian Wars, and what does it say the helots owed their masters?
    Answer

    Tyrtaeus (frs. 5–7 West): the war lasted nineteen years and the conquered paid half of all produce, "like asses worn down by great burdens".

  2. According to Thucydides 4.80, what governing principle lay behind most Spartan institutions?
    Answer

    Security against the helots; the same passage records the garlanding and disappearance of about 2,000 helots.

  3. Name the four organs of the Spartan constitution and the body of thirty named in the Great Rhetra.
    Answer

    Two kings, the gerousia (28 elders plus the kings), five ephors, and the apella; the Rhetra's gerousia of thirty includes the kings as archagetai.

  4. Give two reasons to doubt that Lycurgus was a historical person.
    Answer

    He received hero-cult; his dates vary by centuries between authors; Plutarch admits nothing about him is undisputed; Herodotus already knew rival accounts of the laws' origin.

  5. What were the monthly contributions to a Spartan mess, and why did Aristotle think the messes undermined equality?
    Answer

    A medimnos of barley, eight choes of wine, five minae of cheese, two and a half of figs, and some money; a man who could not pay lost citizenship, so the mess filtered by wealth (Politics 1271a).

  6. How do Plato and Plutarch (citing Aristotle) differ on the krypteia?
    Answer

    Plato (Laws 633b–c) describes a winter endurance test; Plutarch (Lycurgus 28) describes youths with daggers hiding by day and killing helots by night.

  7. What fraction of Spartan land did Aristotle say women controlled, and by what mechanism?
    Answer

    Nearly two-fifths, through heiresses (patrouchoi) and large dowries under partible inheritance (Politics 1270a).

  8. Which festival explains why only Leonidas' advance guard reached Thermopylae, and which explains the delay before Plataea?
    Answer

    The Carneia (Herodotus 7.206) and the Hyacinthia (Herodotus 9.7).

  9. Why did one of the Dioscuri accompany the Spartan army after 506?
    Answer

    Because a new law allowed only one king on campaign, so one Tyndarid (represented by the dokana) went with him and the other stayed with the king at home (Herodotus 5.75).

  10. Trace Spartiate numbers from 480 to the 240s with the sources.
    Answer

    About 8,000 (Herodotus 7.234); about 1,200 at Leuctra with 400 dead (Xenophon, Hellenica 6.4.15); "not even a thousand" (Aristotle, Politics 1270a); 700, about 100 with land (Plutarch, Agis 5).

Carry forward

  1. Sparta is a conquest state: helotage explains the agoge, the messes, the krypteia, the League and the final collapse. Read every Spartan institution as an answer to the Messenian problem.
  2. "Lycurgan" is a label for an evolved system, and much of what Plutarch describes was reconstructed in the third century and under Rome. Date the source before trusting the detail: Tyrtaeus, Alcman, Herodotus and Thucydides outrank Xenophon, who outranks Plutarch.
  3. Equality was a lifestyle ideology over a landowning oligarchy. Property, inherited through women as well as men, concentrated in few hands and produced oliganthropia; this thread connects Archaic Sparta to Leuctra, to Agis and Cleomenes, and to the Roman Sparta the admirers visited.
