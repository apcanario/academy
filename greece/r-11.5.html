01Two generations at one court

Alexander's court was Macedonian in structure. The hetairoi (Companions) dined, hunted and drank with the king and expected to speak freely at his table. The seven somatophylakes (Bodyguards) held a rank more than a duty, and the royal pages were noble sons serving their apprenticeship at his side. The politics of the expedition is the story of Alexander replacing his father's generation with his own, and then trying to fit Persians into a structure built for Macedonians.

Age is not the whole line. Craterus became the traditionalists' champion, and Plutarch (Alexander 47) gives Alexander's own verdict: Hephaestion loved Alexander, Craterus loved the king. The young men owed their careers to Alexander personally. Several had been exiled by Philip for siding with the prince in the Pixodarus affair (Plutarch, Alexander 10; Arrian, Anabasis 3.6.5). Parmenion's eminence came from Philip and from a network of kin: his son Philotas led the Companion cavalry, another son, Nicanor, led the hypaspists, and Coenus was his son-in-law.

The rear: Antipater and Olympias

Antipater governed Macedon and crushed Agis III at Megalopolis (331 or 330; uncertain). Olympias refused to accept his authority, and both bombarded Alexander with accusations (Arrian 7.12.5–7). His reply to one long complaint from Antipater was that a single tear of his mother outweighed ten thousand letters (Plutarch, Alexander 39). By the mid-320s Olympias had withdrawn to Epirus (Plutarch, Alexander 68). In 324 Alexander summoned Antipater to Babylon. He sent his son Cassander instead, whom Alexander treated with contempt (Plutarch, Alexander 74). Another son, Iollas, was royal cupbearer.

Where sources disagree. The story that Antipater poisoned Alexander through Iollas appears in Arrian 7.27 and Plutarch, Alexander 77, both of whom doubt it. Plutarch says Olympias promoted it. Most scholars see Successor-war propaganda, though the summons of 324 was a real threat to Antipater.

02Breaking the old guard, 330–327

Phrada: Philotas and Parmenion

After Darius' death Parmenion was left at Ecbatana with the treasury, detached from the main army. In autumn 330 at Phrada in Drangiana, a plot came to light. Curtius (6.7–7.2) is fullest; Arrian (3.26), Diodorus (17.79–80) and Plutarch (Alexander 48–49) are briefer. Dimnus, a minor officer, revealed a plan to kill the king to his lover Nicomachus. Nicomachus told his brother Cebalinus, who told Philotas. Philotas saw the king twice and said nothing, and Cebalinus eventually got word to Alexander through a page. Dimnus died resisting arrest. Philotas was charged with failing to report the plot, not with joining it.

The ground had been prepared. Since Egypt Craterus had been collecting reports from Philotas' captive mistress Antigone of his boasts that the victories were his and his father's. Curtius describes a night council where Craterus, Hephaestion and Coenus urged action. Philotas was tried before the army and condemned. Those three tortured him to extract a confession that implicated Parmenion, and he was then executed. Alexander Lyncestis, in custody since 333, was killed in the same sweep. Polydamas rode by racing camel to the generals at Ecbatana, Cleander, Sitalces and Menidas, and they stabbed Parmenion while he read his letters (Arrian 3.26.3–4). Arrian is candid that Alexander either thought him guilty or judged it unsafe to leave alive a father whose son he had executed.

Where sources disagree. Arrian, following Ptolemy and Aristobulus, says the proofs were clear. Ptolemy, made a Bodyguard in the aftermath (Arrian 3.27.5), was not neutral. Badian ("The Death of Parmenio", 1960) argued for a frame-up to break Parmenion's faction. Heckel accepts that Philotas' silence was real but its exploitation political. Whether trial before the army was ancestral custom (Curtius 6.8.25) is also disputed.

The consequences were structural. The Companion cavalry was split between Hephaestion and Cleitus (Arrian 3.27.4), soldiers' letters were opened, and grumblers were put in a separate disciplinary unit (Diodorus 17.80.4). This set the pattern: dissent was answered by dividing commands so that no subordinate could hold a following of his own. The last of Philip's men at court were then removed one at a time. Coenus, who had turned on his father-in-law in 330, voiced the army's refusal to go beyond the Hyphasis in 326 (Arrian 5.27) and died of illness a few weeks later (Arrian 6.2.1). Curtius (9.3.20) has Alexander remark that he had made a long speech for a few days' life. Foul play is suspected but unproven. Antigonus survived because he was never at court: from Celaenae he held Anatolia against the Persian counter-offensive after Issus and stayed out of the expedition's politics.

Maracanda: Cleitus and the symposium

The Macedonian symposium involved heavy, often unmixed, drinking and the performance of status. According to Hegesander (in Athenaeus 1.18a), a man could not recline until he had speared a boar without nets. The symposium was also where Companions exercised parrhesia, frank speech to the king. A drinking contest at Calanus' funeral in 324 killed forty-one men (Chares, in Plutarch, Alexander 70).

In autumn 328 Cleitus had just been named satrap of Bactria, which was both a promotion and a removal from court. At a feast in Maracanda flatterers set Alexander above the Dioscuri and Heracles and disparaged Philip (Arrian 4.8.2–3), and verses mocked officers beaten by the Sogdians (Plutarch, Alexander 50–51). Cleitus defended Philip, reminded Alexander that his hand had saved him at the Granicus, and quoted Euripides' Andromache:

Alas, how evil is the custom now in Greece.

The point of the lines is that soldiers do the work and the general takes the glory. Alexander shouted for the guard in Macedonian and ran Cleitus through with a spear, then lay three days without food. Anaxarchus consoled him with the doctrine that whatever a king does is just, since Justice sits beside Zeus (Arrian 4.9.7; Plutarch, Alexander 52).

Where sources disagree. Aristobulus (Arrian 4.8.9) blames Cleitus, who was hustled out by Ptolemy and came back to provoke the king. Curtius (8.1.19–52) and Plutarch make Alexander more culpable. Modern readers debate whether this was drunken temper or a real quarrel over Philip's legacy and Persian ways. It was probably both.

Bactra: proskynesis, the pages, Callisthenes

In 327 Alexander tried to extend proskynesis, the Persian gesture of obeisance, to Macedonians and Greeks. Chares (Plutarch, Alexander 54) describes the method: each guest drank, made the gesture and received the king's kiss. Callisthenes, Aristotle's relative and the official historian, omitted the obeisance and said he would leave the poorer by a kiss. Arrian's formal debate between Anaxarchus and Callisthenes (4.10.5–12.5) is a literary set piece.

Where sources disagree. Arrian (4.12.2) has Leonnatus mock a prostrating Persian; Curtius (8.5.22–24) names Polyperchon. Some scholars see a claim to divinity. Others, Briant among them, see an attempt at one court protocol, so that Persians did not perform alone a gesture their peers refused.

Soon afterwards the page Hermolaus was flogged for striking a boar before the king. With Sostratus and others he planned murder on a night they held the watch. Alexander drank until dawn and the plot leaked (Arrian 4.13–14; Curtius 8.6–8). Curtius gives Hermolaus a speech of grievances: Philotas, Parmenion, Cleitus, Persian dress, proskynesis. The speech is composed, but probably accurate in substance. Callisthenes, the pages' tutor, fell with them, though no source says they named him.

Where sources disagree. Ptolemy says Callisthenes was tortured and hanged; Aristobulus says he died in chains of illness (Arrian 4.14.3). Chares says he was held for trial before Aristotle and died in India, obese and lice-ridden (Plutarch, Alexander 55). Theophrastus' Callisthenes (Cicero, Tusculan Disputations 3.21) began a lasting Peripatetic portrait of Alexander as corrupted by fortune.

03The orientalising policy

From 330 Alexander wore the diadem, the white-striped tunic and the Persian girdle, but not the trousers, sleeved kandys or upright tiara (Plutarch, Alexander 45; Diodorus 17.77.4–7). The choice kept the symbols of Achaemenid kingship and avoided what Greeks found most effeminate. Curtius (6.6.6) says he sealed Asian letters with Darius' ring and European ones with his own. Asian subjects were received in an audience tent guarded by Macedonian hypaspists and Persian "apple-bearers" (Phylarchus, in Athenaeus 12.539). Chares bore the Achaemenid title eisangeleus, controller of access. In ceremony the monarchy was dual: a Macedonian king to Macedonians, a Great King to Asians.

Persians entered government early. Mazaeus became satrap of Babylon in 331, followed by Artabazus in Bactria and Atropates in Media, and Darius' brother Oxyathres was enrolled among the Companions. Native satraps, however, usually sat beside Macedonian garrison and finance officers. In 327 Alexander ordered 30,000 Iranian youths trained in Macedonian arms. They reached Susa in 324 as the Epigonoi, and Macedonians called them an antitagma, a counter-army (Arrian 7.6.1; Diodorus 17.108.1–3). Iranian cavalry were brigaded into the Companion hipparchies and nobles entered the royal squadron itself (Arrian 7.6.3–5).

At Susa in spring 324 Alexander married Stateira, Darius' daughter (Arrian 7.4.4 calls her Barsine), and Parysatis, daughter of Artaxerxes III, uniting himself with both Achaemenid lines. Hephaestion took Drypetis, so that their children would be cousins. Craterus married Amastris, Ptolemy and Eumenes married daughters of Artabazus, Nearchus a daughter of Barsine, and Seleucus Apame, daughter of Spitamenes. Arrian counts about eighty Companion brides. Some 10,000 soldiers with Asian partners received gifts, and the army's debts were paid.

Where sources disagree. The debt payment is 20,000 talents in Arrian (7.5.3), about 10,000 in Diodorus (17.109.2) and 9,870 in Plutarch (Alexander 70). Arrian notes that some Companions disliked the marriages (7.6.2). Only Seleucus is known to have kept his wife after 323.

04Opis, 324: mutiny and the prayer for homonoia

At Opis Alexander announced the discharge of the old and unfit. The army read it as replacement by Persians and shouted that he should campaign with his father Ammon (Arrian 7.8.3). He had thirteen ringleaders executed, spoke (Arrian 7.9–10, largely Arrian's own composition) and withdrew. On the third day he gave Persians commands and announced Persian Companions, Persian foot-companions and Persian Silver Shields (Arrian 7.11.1–4). The Macedonians broke. Their spokesman Callines complained that only Persians were called the king's kinsmen and allowed to kiss him. Alexander declared them all his kinsmen.

A banquet for 9,000 followed, with Macedonians seated nearest the king, then Persians, then others (Arrian 7.11.8–9). Greek seers and Magi officiated, and Alexander prayed for homonoia and partnership in rule between Macedonians and Persians. About 10,000 veterans then left under Craterus. Their Asian-born children stayed, to be raised as Macedonians.

Where sources disagree. Tarn (1933; Alexander the Great, 1948) read the prayer as the birth of the brotherhood of mankind. He drew on Plutarch's rhetorical On the Fortune of Alexander (Moralia 329), where peoples are mixed as in a loving-cup. Badian ("Alexander the Great and the Unity of Mankind", Historia 1958) showed that it concerns two peoples, not mankind. It was a reconciliation after mutiny, with a hierarchical seating plan, offering shared rule to conqueror and conquered aristocracy. Badian's reading has prevailed.

05After India: purge, Harpalus, Greeks, Hephaestion

Emerging from Gedrosia, Alexander executed Cleander, Sitalces and Heracon (whom Curtius names among Parmenion's killers) for temple-robbery and abuses (Arrian 6.27.3–5; Curtius 10.1.1–8). Astaspes, the pretender Baryaxes, Orxines of Persis and Abulites of Susiana with his son followed (Arrian 6.29–30; 7.4.1; Plutarch, Alexander 68). Satraps were ordered to disband their mercenaries (Diodorus 17.106.3). Thousands of them gathered at Taenarum and later fought in the Lamian War.

Where sources disagree. Badian ("Harpalus", JHS 1961) saw a "reign of terror" by a shaken, suspicious king. Curtius (10.1.22–38) supports it for Orxines, framed, he says, by the eunuch Bagoas. Arrian presents genuine misrule by men who expected the king to die in India. Higgins (1980) and Heckel stress the modest numbers and plausible charges. Bosworth takes a middle line of real abuses punished with exemplary severity. The question is uncertain.

Harpalus, an Elimiote noble unfit for war, was treasurer. He had deserted once before Issus and been forgiven (Arrian 3.6.4–7). At Babylon he lived lavishly, raising monuments to his dead mistress Pythionice (Theopompus, in Athenaeus 13.595a–596b). In 324 he fled with about 5,000 talents and 6,000 mercenaries (Diodorus 17.108.6). Athens admitted him with a smaller sum, and 700 talents were deposited on the Acropolis, of which half vanished. The Areopagus inquiry convicted Demosthenes, who was fined fifty talents and went into exile (Plutarch, Demosthenes 25–26; Hyperides and Dinarchus, Against Demosthenes). Whether he took bribes or a war chest is uncertain. Harpalus was murdered on Crete by Thibron.

The Greek allies were auxiliaries, not partners. Their League contingents were paid off at Ecbatana in 330 (Arrian 3.19.5), and many re-enlisted as mercenaries. Greeks filled technical posts. Eumenes of Cardia ran the chancery and feuded with Hephaestion (Arrian 7.13.1). The Exiles' Decree of 324 (Diodorus 18.8.2–5) overrode the League's guarantees, and the Greek colonists in Bactria revolted in 325 and 323 (Diodorus 17.99.5–6; 18.7).

Hephaestion, now chiliarch, an Achaemenid title for the chief minister, died at Ecbatana in autumn 324 after fever and drinking. Alexander executed the physician Glaucias, cut his hair, and, Diodorus says (17.114.4–5), extinguished the sacred fires, which was normally done only at a king's death. Ammon allowed Hephaestion heroic cult, not divine (Arrian 7.14.7). The pyre cost 10,000 talents (Plutarch, Alexander 72) or 12,000 (Diodorus 17.115), and the chiliarchy kept his name (Arrian 7.14.10). Arrian grades the grief stories and admits he cannot sift them. The political loss was the one intermediary able to carry the king's policies through a reluctant court.

06Women at court

Olympias alone had an independent Macedonian power base, and she later had Philip III killed before Cassander executed her in 316. Sisygambis, Darius' mother, was captured at Issus and bowed to Hephaestion by mistake; Alexander told her that he too was Alexander (Diodorus 17.37.5–6; Curtius 3.12.15–17). Curtius says (10.5.19–25) she starved herself when he died. Barsine, Artabazus' daughter and Memnon's widow, was his mistress from 333 (Aristobulus in Plutarch, Alexander 21). Her son Heracles was murdered in 309 (Diodorus 20.28). Tarn denied that he existed; Brunt (1975) and most since accept him. Roxane, daughter of the Bactrian Oxyartes, married Alexander in 327 to seal the Sogdian settlement (Curtius 8.4.21–30). She and Alexander IV were killed by Cassander around 310. Stateira and Parysatis brought Achaemenid legitimacy; Plutarch (Alexander 77) says Roxane had Stateira killed after 323. These women carried legitimacy, which is why the Successors eliminated those with claims.

07Integration or autocracy, and what the Successors kept

One view sees a Macedonian-Iranian ruling class under construction, built through the Susa marriages, the Epigonoi, the mixed cavalry and the Opis prayer. Briant calls Alexander "the last of the Achaemenids", running Persia's imperial machine with a new elite on top. The other view, associated with Badian and in modified form with Bosworth, treats the Persians as a counterweight that freed the king from his own people. The sequence supports a combined reading. Proskynesis followed Cleitus, the Epigonoi were ordered after the pages, and Persian commands answered Opis. Integration served autocracy every time it was applied; whether it also served a larger vision remains open.

Macedon gave its own verdict at Babylon in 323. The infantry acclaimed Arrhidaeus because he was fully Macedonian, and in Curtius (10.6.13–14) Ptolemy objects to an heir of conquered blood. Nearly every Susa bride was repudiated. Peucestas, who learned Persian and wore Persian dress in Persis (Arrian 6.30.3), stayed unique. Seleucus kept Apame, so the Seleucids were half-Iranian from Antiochus I. The Successors kept the diadem, the court of philoi, Bodyguards and pages, and the use of local elites. Eumenes' tent with Alexander's empty throne (Diodorus 18.60–61) shows ceremonial being turned into legitimacy. They dropped proskynesis for Greeks, the joint aristocracy and the partnership in rule. Hellenistic kingdoms were Graeco-Macedonian courts over native subjects: heirs to Alexander's autocracy, not his integration.

08Self-test

  1. What was Philotas formally accused of at Phrada in 330, and who conducted his torture?
    Answer

    Failing to report the Dimnus plot, which he had heard of through Cebalinus, rather than plotting himself. Curtius names Craterus, Hephaestion and Coenus (Parmenion's own son-in-law) as his torturers.

  2. How was Parmenion killed, and how does Arrian explain the decision?
    Answer

    Polydamas carried orders by racing camel to Cleander, Sitalces and Menidas at Ecbatana, who stabbed him while he read letters. Arrian (3.26.4) suggests either that Alexander believed him guilty or that a father whose son had just been executed was too dangerous to leave alive.

  3. Which institutional changes followed the Philotas affair?
    Answer

    The Companion cavalry was divided between Hephaestion and Cleitus, soldiers' letters were opened, and malcontents were put in a separate disciplinary unit (Arrian 3.27.4; Diodorus 17.80.4).

  4. What political quarrel sat underneath the drunken killing of Cleitus at Maracanda?
    Answer

    Philip's reputation against Alexander's claims to surpass him, court flattery, mockery of Macedonian officers, and the standing of the old Companions under an increasingly Persian kingship. The Euripides quotation made the point that soldiers did the work while the king took the glory.

  5. Give the three ancient versions of Callisthenes' death.
    Answer

    Ptolemy: tortured and hanged. Aristobulus: kept in chains and died of illness. Chares: held for trial before Aristotle and died in India, obese and lice-ridden (Arrian 4.14.3; Plutarch, Alexander 55).

  6. What were the Epigonoi, and what did the Macedonians call them?
    Answer

    Thirty thousand Iranian youths ordered in 327 to be trained in Macedonian arms, who arrived at Susa in 324. The Macedonians called them an antitagma, a counter-army (Diodorus 17.108.3).

  7. Why did Alexander marry both Stateira and Parysatis at Susa?
    Answer

    Stateira was Darius III's daughter and Parysatis the daughter of Artaxerxes III, so the two marriages joined him to both lines of the Achaemenid house.

  8. State Tarn's and Badian's readings of the Opis prayer.
    Answer

    Tarn: a statement of the brotherhood of mankind. Badian: a prayer for concord and shared rule between Macedonians and Persians only, a political settlement after a mutiny with a seating order that kept Macedonians first. Badian's view is now standard.

  9. What is the "reign of terror" thesis, and what is the main objection to it?
    Answer

    Badian's view that the executions after Gedrosia were the paranoid lashing-out of a shaken autocrat. Critics (Higgins, Heckel) argue that the numbers were modest and the charges of misrule plausible, against officials who had expected Alexander to die in India.

  10. Name two things the Successors kept from Alexander's court and two they dropped.
    Answer

    Kept: the diadem, the court of philoi with Bodyguards and pages, and royal ceremonial used for legitimacy (as in Eumenes' Alexander-tent). Dropped: proskynesis for Greeks, the mixed marriages (Seleucus excepted), and the joint Macedonian-Persian ruling class.

09Carry forward

  1. Alexander's court politics was a generational replacement. Philip's men, bound by kinship, were broken in 330–326, and Alexander's personal friends took the great commands, which were divided so that no subordinate could build an independent power base.
  2. Each measure of Macedonian-Persian integration followed a Macedonian crisis, so integration and autocracy were two faces of one policy. Whether Alexander held a vision beyond his own power is the open question, and Badian's reading of Opis has replaced Tarn's.
  3. The Successors inherited the autocratic monarchy and its ceremonial but rejected the joint elite and every royal woman and child who carried mixed legitimacy. The result was Graeco-Macedonian dynasties ruling over Asian subjects, not a fused aristocracy.

The prompt this answers

Give me an in-depth report on Alexander's court and the politics within the expedition. Cover: the old guard he inherited from Philip (Parmenion, Antipater, Antigonus, Cleitus, Coenus, Craterus) and the young men around him (Hephaestion, Ptolemy, Perdiccas, Leonnatus, Lysimachus, Seleucus, Peucestas, Nearchus); Antipater and Olympias feuding in Macedon; the Philotas affair in 330 in full and the judicial murder of Parmenion; the killing of Cleitus at Maracanda and the drinking culture behind it; the pages' conspiracy and the fall of Callisthenes; the orientalising policy: Persian dress, the mixed court ceremonial, the Persian nobles admitted, the 30,000 Epigonoi, the Susa mass marriage and the Macedonian reaction to it, the Opis mutiny and the reconciliation banquet with its prayer for homonoia (Tarn's brotherhood of man against Badian's reading); the purge of the satraps in 325-324 and the reign of terror thesis; Harpalus' flight with the treasury and the Athenian scandal; Hephaestion's death and the grief; the position of the Greek allies and mercenaries; women at court: Olympias, Sisygambis, Barsine, Roxane, Stateira and Parysatis. End with the question of whether the court was moving towards an integrated Macedonian-Persian elite or towards autocracy, and what the Successors kept of it. Format: about 2500 words, headed sections, name sources, flag disagreements, end with a 10-question self-test with answers and the three ideas I must carry forward.
