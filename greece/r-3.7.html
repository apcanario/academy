Ovid's method and agenda

The Metamorphoses is a Latin hexameter poem in fifteen books, finished around AD 8, the year Augustus exiled its author to Tomis. Its proem (1.1–4) promises a carmen perpetuum, a continuous song from the creation of the world to Ovid's own day, and some 250 stories are chained together by transitions of place, kinship, theme or verbal sleight, ending with the apotheosis of Julius Caesar (15.745–870). The form is epic; the sensibility is the love elegist's. Ovid narrates violence with a declaimer's detachment, gives his gods the manners of Roman aristocrats, and treats transformation as diagnosis: a body is made to show what the person already was. Lycaon becomes the wolf he behaved like (1.237–239); Niobe, who could not stop weeping, becomes a rock that weeps.

His sources were overwhelmingly Hellenistic and overwhelmingly lost. Nicander's Heteroioumena, Boios's Ornithogonia, Parthenius's Metamorphoses and Callimachus's Aetia supplied catalogues of change-tales; Antoninus Liberalis, who summarised Nicander and Boios in the second century AD, is our best control for what that tradition looked like without Ovid. Behind them stand Euripides, Hesiod's Catalogue of Women, and, for Orpheus, Virgil's Georgics. Ovid also uses embedded narrators: Book 10's Pygmalion, Hyacinthus, Ganymede and Adonis are all sung by Orpheus, a grieving man who has sworn off women, and his choice of subjects is characterisation, not neutral report.

Where sources disagree. Whether the poem is Augustan, anti-Augustan or indifferent is the longest quarrel in Ovidian scholarship. Brooks Otis (1966) read a poet failing to be serious; Karl Galinsky (1975) one who preferred play to politics. Since the 1990s Philip Hardie and Denis Feeney have stressed how the Lycaon council scene (1.163–252), which compares the gods' alarm to Rome's at a plot against "Augustus", invites a parallel between Jupiter's arbitrary power and the princeps's. Leo Curran (1978) and Amy Richlin (1992) asked whether Ovid's sympathy for the poem's roughly fifty rape victims is real or aesthetic. None of this is settled.

Love that fails: Orpheus and Eurydice

The version everyone knows is Virgil's. In Georgics 4.453–527 Orpheus descends, charms Hades, and loses Eurydice by looking back on the threshold; Ovid retells it in Metamorphoses 10.1–85 with a lighter touch and finishes it in 11.1–66 with his death at the hands of Thracian women. The backward glance and the double death are first attested in Virgil, around 29 BC.

The Greek evidence tells a different story. Euripides, Alcestis 357–362 (438 BC), has Admetus wish he had Orpheus's voice so that he too could sing his wife back, which only makes sense if Orpheus succeeded. Isocrates, Busiris 8, lists raising the dead among his deeds. Hermesianax (fr. 7 Powell) calls the wife Agriope and has him recover her; the name Eurydice first appears in the Lament for Bion 123–124, around 100 BC, again implying success. The one classical dissent is Plato, Symposium 179d: the gods showed Orpheus only a phantom and sent him away, because a mere cithara player lacked the courage to die for love as Alcestis did. Pausanias 9.30.6 rationalises: Orpheus thought he saw her ghost at the oracle of the dead and killed himself when it did not follow.

Where sources disagree. C. M. Bowra (1952) argued the failure version was a Hellenistic invention which Virgil perfected; others take Plato's phantom as evidence it was current in the fourth century. Apollodorus, Library 1.3.2, gives the looking-back condition in Greek, but writes after Virgil. Which version is older is uncertain; that the tragic Orpheus is the Roman one is not.

Love and the image: Narcissus and Echo, Pygmalion

Narcissus (Met. 3.339–510) was believed an Ovidian creation until 2004, when a papyrus of Parthenius (P.Oxy. 4711) showed a Greek telling about fifty years older in which the boy falls for his reflection and kills himself. Conon, Narrations 24, has him spurn a male lover, Ameinias, who kills himself and curses him; Pausanias 9.31.7–9 reports a rationalised version in which Narcissus gazed at his reflection to recall a dead twin sister. Ovid's innovations are the slow wasting-away instead of suicide, Tiresias's framing prophecy, and above all Echo. She is Greek (Longus, Daphnis and Chloe 3.23, has her torn apart at Pan's instigation), but her punishment by Juno, her reduction to repeating last words, and her pairing with Narcissus have no known source before Ovid. The match is too neat to be accidental: the boy who can only love himself meets the girl who can only say what she has heard.

Pygmalion (Met. 10.243–297) is the clearest case of Ovid transforming a Greek story beyond recognition. Philostephanus of Cyrene (third century BC), known from Clement, Protrepticus 4.57, told of a Cypriot king who fell in love with an ivory cult statue of Aphrodite and took it to bed; Christian writers cite it as a scandal of idolatry. Ovid makes him an artist, the statue his own work, and Venus the answerer of his prayer rather than its object, so the story becomes about art rather than fetish. The statue is never named in Ovid; "Galatea" is an eighteenth-century addition popularised by Rousseau.

Apollo's loves: Daphne and Hyacinthus

Daphne (Met. 1.452–567) is the poem's first love story and sets the pattern of pursuit, prayer and transformation. Ovid motivates Apollo's passion by Cupid's arrow, shot in revenge for mockery; this is his. Parthenius, Erotika Pathemata 15, makes Daphne a Laconian huntress courted by the youth Leucippus, who disguises himself as a girl to join her band; Apollo, jealous, inspires the girls to bathe, Leucippus is exposed and killed, and Daphne, fleeing Apollo, becomes the laurel. Pausanias 8.20.2–4 tells the same tale in Arcadia with Daphne as daughter of the river Ladon. Ovid moves her to Thessaly as daughter of Peneus, cuts Leucippus, and ends with the laurel on the oak-wreathed doors of Augustus's house (1.562–563), a pointedly Roman close.

Hyacinthus (Met. 10.162–219) is a Spartan cult figure older than any love story: the Hyacinthia at Amyclae appear in Euripides, Helen 1469–1475, and the -nth- in his name is pre-Greek, which led Nilsson and Burkert to see a pre-Dorian vegetation god absorbed by Apollo. Apollodorus 1.3.3 and 3.10.3 make him beloved of Thamyris and then of Apollo, who kills him accidentally with a discus. The jealous Zephyrus who deflects the discus appears in Palaephatus 46 and Lucian, Dialogues of the Gods 14, not in Ovid, who keeps the death an accident and has Apollo inscribe the flower with AI AI. Pausanias 3.19.3–5 saw Hyacinthus carried to heaven on the altar at Amyclae, a hint of resurrection the poets dropped.

Aphrodite's loves: Adonis and Psyche

Adonis is one of the few myths here with early, rich Greek evidence. Sappho fr. 140a (c. 600 BC) is a ritual lament: "Delicate Adonis is dying, Cytherea; what shall we do?" Panyassis (in Apollodorus 3.14.4) gives the incest of Smyrna with her father, the birth from the myrrh tree, and a quarrel between Aphrodite and Persephone over the infant settled by Zeus dividing the year. Aristophanes, Lysistrata 387–398, and Theocritus, Idyll 15, show the women's Adonia; Bion's Lament for Adonis is the Hellenistic pathetic treatment. Ovid, Met. 10.503–739, tells Myrrha's incest at length, then the boar, the anemone and Venus's grief, inserting the Atalanta race. He omits Persephone and the shared year, converting a seasonal cult myth into elegiac romance.

Where sources disagree. Frazer read Adonis as a dying-and-rising vegetation god kin to Tammuz. Marcel Detienne (1972) argued the reverse: the Adonia's quick-sprouting, quick-withering gardens celebrate sterile seduction, the opposite of Demeter's grain. Whether any Greek source before late antiquity has Adonis actually return to life is doubtful.

Eros and Psyche is Latin, late, and embedded: Apuleius, Metamorphoses 4.28–6.24, second century AD, told by an old woman to a kidnapped bride. The skeleton is a folk-type (the lost supernatural husband), and Hellenistic art shows Eros and Psyche embracing from the fourth century BC, but no Greek literary version survives, and the Greek Onos that shares Apuleius's donkey plot lacks Psyche entirely. Whether Apuleius invented the tale, adapted a lost Greek original, or dressed folklore in Platonic allegory is uncertain. It is the most influential ancient fairy tale and it is not, in any recoverable sense, a Greek myth.

Hubris and the gods' justice

Arachne (Met. 6.1–145) has no Greek narrative source. Virgil, Georgics 4.246, calls the spider "hated by Minerva", so a story existed, and a scholion on Nicander preserves an Attic tale of Arachne and her brother Phalanx punished for incest. The contest, the tapestries and the transformation as we know them are Ovid's. Arachne's tapestry depicts the rapes of the gods (6.103–128) while Minerva's depicts their majesty, and Arachne's is better; the goddess tears it and beats her, and the metamorphosis is a reprieve from suicide, not justice.

Marsyas is old. Herodotus 7.26 saw the satyr's skin hanging at Celaenae; Plato, Symposium 215b–c, has Alcibiades liken Socrates to him. Apollodorus 1.4.2 and Diodorus 3.59 give the contest with Apollo and the flaying. Ovid, Met. 6.382–400, skips the contest and spends nineteen lines on the skinning.

Niobe is Homeric. Achilles tells Priam (Iliad 24.602–617) that Niobe lost six sons and six daughters for comparing herself to Leto and now stands as rock on Sipylus, still weeping. Hesiod fr. 183 M-W gave her ten and ten; Aeschylus's Niobe had a long-silent heroine mocked in Frogs 911–920. Ovid, Met. 6.146–312, gives seven and seven, as does Apollodorus 3.5.6. Pausanias 1.21.3 climbed Sipylus and reports the rock looks like a weeping woman only from a distance.

Salmoneus of Elis drove a chariot dragging bronze cauldrons and threw torches, claiming to be Zeus; the real bolt killed him and his city (Apollodorus 1.9.7; Virgil, Aeneid 6.585–594). Capaneus, scaling the wall of Thebes, swore not even Zeus's fire would stop him and was struck from the ladder (Aeschylus, Seven Against Thebes 423–446; Apollodorus 3.6.7). Midas is the comic variant: a historical Phrygian king (Assyrian Mita, eighth century BC) whose dedications Herodotus 1.14 saw at Delphi. The golden touch is a proverb in Aristotle, Politics 1257b, but is told fully only in Ovid, Met. 11.85–145; the asses' ears are in Aristophanes, Plutus 287, and Ovid adds the barber and the whispering reeds (11.146–193).

What the pattern teaches is not that the gods are just but that they are jealous of a boundary. Niobe's children are innocent; Arachne's work is superior; Marsyas's crime is a music lesson. The offence is the refusal of the gap between mortal and immortal, and the punishment asserts the gap by force. Pindar's "do not seek to become Zeus" (Isthmian 5.14) is the sober version; Ovid's is to make Minerva lose the contest and win anyway.

Falling and burning: Phaethon and Icarus

Phaethon's fullest surviving telling is Ovid, Met. 1.750–2.400: the quest to prove paternity, the Sun's palace, the runaway chariot, the scorched earth, the thunderbolt, the Heliades turned to poplars weeping amber. Euripides wrote a Phaethon (c. 420 BC) of which substantial fragments survive; Plato, Timaeus 22c, has an Egyptian priest cite the fall as a memory of a real conflagration; Apollonius, Argonautica 4.597–611, has the Argonauts pass the smoking body in the Eridanus.

Icarus is told twice by Ovid, in Ars Amatoria 2.21–96 and Met. 8.183–235, with the ploughman and shepherd gaping at the flyers as if at gods. Greek literary attestation is thin before Apollodorus, Epitome 1.12–13, though vase painters knew a winged Icarus by the sixth century BC. Diodorus 4.77 rationalises: they fled Crete by ship and Icarus drowned landing carelessly; Pausanias 9.11.4–5 says Daedalus invented sails. Both myths concern a son's grasp at a father's power, and both end in water.

Zeus's loves and their victims: Callisto, Leda, Ganymede

Callisto (Met. 2.401–530) is an Arcadian nymph of Diana's band whom Jupiter rapes disguised as Diana; exposed at the bath, she is expelled, bears Arcas, and is turned into a bear by Juno; years later Jupiter stops Arcas from spearing his mother and sets both in the sky, where Juno persuades Ocean never to let them bathe. Apollodorus 3.8.2 lists Greek variants: Hesiod, Eumelus, Asius and Pherecydes each gave her a different father; Zeus took the form of Artemis or Apollo; Zeus or Hera made her a bear; Artemis shot her. Ovid's placing of the whole punishment in Juno's hands and his pity for the mute bear are his emphases.

Leda is a story Ovid barely tells (one line in Arachne's tapestry, 6.109), so its fame is not his doing. Homer knows Leda as mother of Castor and Polydeuces (Odyssey 11.298–304) and Helen as daughter of Zeus, but never joins them with a swan. The Cypria makes Nemesis Helen's mother; Apollodorus 3.10.7 reconciles the two by having a shepherd bring Nemesis's egg to Leda. Euripides, Helen 17–21, has Helen report the swan "if the story is true"; Pausanias 3.16.1 saw the egg hung in a Spartan temple.

Ganymede is Homeric and not overtly erotic there. Iliad 20.231–235 says the gods carried him off to be Zeus's cupbearer for his beauty; Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite 202–217 records the horses given to Tros in compensation. Theognis 1345–1348 makes the desire explicit and uses it to justify pederasty; Plato, Laws 636c–d, says the Cretans invented the tale to excuse theirs. The eagle is a fourth-century addition, famous from Leochares's bronze (Pliny, Natural History 34.79); Ovid, Met. 10.155–161, gives Orpheus seven lines on it.

Shape, sex and hospitality: Tiresias, Lycaon, Baucis and Philemon

Tiresias has two transformation stories, and Ovid chooses one. In Met. 3.316–338 he strikes two mating snakes, becomes a woman for seven years, returns to manhood by striking them again, is asked whether men or women get more pleasure from sex, agrees with Jupiter that women do, and is blinded by Juno, Jupiter compensating with prophecy. This is Hesiod's Melampodia (fr. 275 M-W, via Apollodorus 3.6.7), where the ratio is one part of pleasure to the man and nine to the woman. The rival version is Callimachus, Hymn 5.57–136: young Tiresias stumbles on Athena bathing with his mother Chariclo, is blinded by the sight, and receives prophecy in consolation. Ovid takes the sex-change because it poses the question of pleasure immediately before Narcissus and Pentheus, both stories about looking.

Lycaon in Ovid (Met. 1.163–252) tests Jupiter by serving him human flesh; Jupiter destroys the house and Lycaon runs howling into the fields as a wolf, the first human metamorphosis in the poem and the trigger for the flood. The Greek Lycaon is a cult founder. Pausanias 8.2.1–7 makes him founder of the games of Zeus Lycaeus who sacrificed an infant on the altar and became a wolf, and Pausanias, unusually, believes it. Apollodorus 3.8.1 blames his fifty sons, who mixed a child's entrails into Zeus's meal. Plato, Republic 565d–e, uses the Lycaean rite as his image for the making of a tyrant. The ash altar on Mount Lycaeus has been excavated; the 2016 find of an adolescent burial in it is suggestive and nothing more.

Baucis and Philemon (Met. 8.611–724) has no Greek source at all. An old Phrygian couple host disguised Jupiter and Mercury; the wine jug refills; the gods drown the inhospitable neighbours and turn the pair, at death, into an oak and a linden intertwined. The pattern is the theoxeny of Odyssey 17.485–487 and Callimachus's Hecale, in which an old woman hosts Theseus; Acts 14:11–12, where Lycaonians take Paul and Barnabas for Zeus and Hermes, suggests the Anatolian setting carried real belief. Whether Ovid drew on a lost Hellenistic tale or composed it himself is uncertain.

Three stories (Arachne, Baucis and Philemon, the living statue of Pygmalion) and one that is not Ovid's (Psyche) have no recoverable Greek telling. Several more (Daphne's motive, Echo, Narcissus's slow death, Phaethon's full arc, Midas's gold) are Greek in outline and Ovidian in everything a reader remembers. How Roman does that make them? In naming, entirely. In tone, considerably: the declamatory speeches, the Augustan laurel, the gods as a senate. In substance, less than it looks, because the loss of Nicander, Boios and Parthenius means absence of a Greek source is usually absence of evidence. The 2004 Narcissus papyrus demoted Ovid's most celebrated invention to adaptation in an afternoon; Arachne or Baucis could go the same way. The honest position is that this group of myths reached Europe through a Roman poet who improved almost everything he touched and told us nothing about what he had changed.

Self-test

  1. Which fifth-century Greek text implies that Orpheus successfully brought his wife back from Hades?
    Answer

    Euripides, Alcestis 357–362: Admetus wishes he had Orpheus's voice to recover his wife, which presupposes Orpheus succeeded.

  2. What did the 2004 publication of P.Oxy. 4711 change about Narcissus?
    Answer

    It showed Parthenius had told the reflection story roughly fifty years before Ovid, so Narcissus is no longer an Ovidian invention; Echo's pairing with him still is.

  3. How did Philostephanus's Pygmalion differ from Ovid's?
    Answer

    He was a Cypriot king in love with an ivory cult statue of Aphrodite; Ovid made him a sculptor whose own statue Venus brings to life.

  4. Who is Leucippus and what does Ovid do with him?
    Answer

    In Parthenius 15 and Pausanias 8.20 he is Daphne's mortal suitor, disguised as a girl and exposed through Apollo's jealousy. Ovid cuts him and makes Cupid's arrow the motive for Apollo's passion.

  5. How many children does Niobe lose in Homer, Hesiod and Ovid?
    Answer

    Twelve in Iliad 24; twenty in Hesiod fr. 183; fourteen in Ovid and Apollodorus.

  6. Which two transformation stories are told of Tiresias, and whose are they?
    Answer

    The snake-striking sex change from Hesiod's Melampodia, used by Ovid; and the blinding for seeing Athena bathe, from Callimachus, Hymn 5.

  7. What literary evidence before Ovid is there for the golden touch and for the asses' ears of Midas?
    Answer

    The golden touch is a proverb in Aristotle, Politics 1257b; the asses' ears appear in Aristophanes, Plutus 287. Only Ovid tells the touch fully, and he adds the barber and the whispering reeds to the ears.

  8. Where does the Eros and Psyche story come from, and is it Greek?
    Answer

    Apuleius, Metamorphoses 4.28–6.24, Latin, second century AD. No Greek literary version survives; the Greek Onos that shares the donkey plot lacks Psyche.

  9. Name two stories in this module with no known Greek narrative source before Ovid.
    Answer

    Arachne (only Virgil's hint) and Baucis and Philemon (none at all). Echo's pairing with Narcissus is a third candidate.

  10. What does Ovid make the Lycaon episode do structurally, and what political reading does it invite?
    Answer

    It is the first human metamorphosis and the cause of the flood; Ovid compares the gods' alarm to Rome's at a plot against Augustus, inviting a parallel between Jupiter's power and the princeps's.

Carry forward

  1. Absence of a Greek source is usually absence of evidence: Nicander, Boios and Parthenius are lost, and Narcissus has already moved from Ovid's column to Greece's.
  2. Metamorphosis in Ovid is diagnosis, not reward or punishment: the body is made to show what the person already was, and the gods who impose it are jealous of a boundary, not just.
  3. Embedded narrators matter: Orpheus's Book 10 and Arachne's tapestry are characterised speech, and reading them as Ovid's own view flattens the poem.
