01Crete before the Greeks: the shape of the period

Bronze Age Crete is organised by two chronologies, both still in use: Arthur Evans's ceramic phases (Early, Middle, Late Minoan) and Nikolaos Platon's four palatial phases (Prepalatial, Protopalatial, Neopalatial, Postpalatial), to which some later variants add a Final Palatial phase between the last two. Absolute dates remain approximate.

Palatial phaseEvans phaseApproximate dates BCWhat happens

"Minoan" is a modern label taken from the mythical king Minos, popularised by Evans though used earlier by Karl Hoeck, whose Kreta (1823–29) headed its second volume "Das Minoische Kreta" (1828). We do not know what the islanders called themselves. Egyptian texts refer to Keftiu, probably Crete, and Odyssey 19.172–7 lists Eteocretans ("true Cretans") among the island's later peoples, which may preserve a memory that a pre-Greek population survived.

02The palaces, and what a "palace" was

Four large court-centred complexes dominate the picture. Knossos, near Heraklion, is the largest at roughly 13,000 square metres of ground floor, excavated by Evans from 1900. Phaistos, overlooking the Mesara plain, was dug by the Italian mission under Halbherr and Pernier; its Protopalatial phase is unusually well preserved because the Neopalatial builders shifted the plan rather than building directly on top. Malia, on the north coast, retains much of its Protopalatial character and has a town around it studied more fully than most. Zakros, at the far east end, was found by Platon in 1961 and is the only palace never looted after its LM IB destruction, so its storerooms, workshops and treasury were recovered intact, including the rock-crystal rhyton.

What the four share is a plan: a long central court aligned roughly north–south; a west court with raised walkways and stone-lined pits (kouloures) of disputed purpose; long magazines holding storage jars (pithoi); sunken rooms Evans called "lustral basins"; dark "pillar crypts"; stepped "theatral areas"; light-wells and pier-and-door partitions. There are no obvious throne rooms before LM II, no fortification walls, and no temple as a separate building type.

Function is contested. Evans saw royal residences, the seat of a "Priest-King". Colin Renfrew, in The Emergence of Civilisation (1972), reframed them as hubs of a redistributive economy: surplus flowed in, was recorded by scribes and flowed out as rations and craft goods. Since the 1990s Jan Driessen, Ilse Schoep and others have argued that the central court was the point of the design, that the buildings were made for gatherings and ritual, and that "palace" smuggles in a monarchy for which there is no direct evidence. Driessen prefers "court compound". The honest position is that these complexes combined storage, administration, craft production and ceremony, and that we cannot say who, or what kind of body, sat at the top.

Where sources disagree. Evans's "Priest-King" rests on a composite fresco and the Minos myth, not on any text or burial. Renfrew's model underestimates private exchange; Driessen's explains the courts but struggles with the scale of storage. "Heterarchy" and faction models now compete with the older palace-state model; there is no consensus on Minoan political organisation.

03Linear A and why it is unread

Three scripts were used on Bronze Age Crete. Cretan Hieroglyphic appears on seals and a few tablets from MM I to MM II. Linear A runs from about MM II to LM IB (c. 1800–1450) and is the administrative script of the Neopalatial period. Linear B, which adapted Linear A to write Greek, replaced it at Knossos after 1450.

Linear A survives on roughly 1,400 documents, mostly small clay tablets, roundels and nodules, totalling only about 7,400 signs. The largest archive, around 150 tablets, comes from the villa at Hagia Triada. Non-administrative texts are rarer but important: a recurring sequence conventionally read (j)a-sa-sa-ra-me appears on stone libation tables from peak sanctuaries and caves, and is generally taken as a religious formula or divine name. Because Linear B borrowed most of its signs, approximate phonetic values can be assigned to perhaps three-quarters of Linear A. The word ku-ro at the foot of lists can be shown by arithmetic to mean "total", and ki-ro probably "deficit". That is close to the limit of secure meaning.

The script is unread for structural reasons. The corpus is small and repetitive. There is no bilingual text. The documents are lists, not sentences, so grammar barely shows. And the language is unknown: no later Cretan language can be shown to descend from it, and the Eteocretan inscriptions of the first millennium (Praisos, Dreros) are too few and too late to help. Proposals have identified it as Luwian (Leonard Palmer), as Semitic (Cyrus Gordon, Jan Best), as a form of early Greek or Indo-European (Gareth Owens), and as an isolate. None produces readings that work across the corpus.

Where sources disagree. Whether Linear A sign values can be carried over from Linear B is itself disputed; John Younger accepts it as a working tool, sceptics note that Linear B may have reassigned values. Treat every published "translation" of Linear A as a hypothesis.

04Religion as reconstructed from images and places

Minoan religion must be rebuilt from frescoes, seals and rings, figurines, cult vessels and the places where they were deposited. There are no readable texts and no temples in the Greek sense. The central institution of the Protopalatial period was the peak sanctuary: open-air sites on summits such as Juktas above Knossos, Petsophas above Palaikastro, Kophinas and Atsipades, where worshippers left clay figurines of men, women, animals and detached human limbs, the last presumably for healing. Alan Peatfield's survey showed that many were abandoned or absorbed into palace control in the Neopalatial period. Caves were the second category: Psychro on Lasithi (later the Dictaean Cave), Kamares, and Arkalochori, where a hoard of bronze and precious-metal double axes was found.

Four symbols recur. The bull dominates: bull-leaping on seals and on the Knossos "Taureador" frescoes (which most now date to LM II–IIIA, after the Mycenaean takeover), stone bull's-head rhyta from Knossos and Zakros, and the sacrifice scene on the Hagia Triada sarcophagus. The double axe (Greek labrys) appears as a votive, on pottery and incised on pillars, often with the "horns of consecration" that crown buildings and altars; Evans read it as the goddess's emblem, but it may simply mark sacrifice. The snake goddess is known from two faience figurines from the Temple Repositories at Knossos (c. 1600), both heavily restored: the smaller figure's head, and the cat on it, are modern. Whether they show a goddess, a priestess or a votary is open. The labyrinth is not a Minoan image at all; it is a Greek word and story, discussed below, though Linear B tablet KN Gg 702 records offerings to da-pu2-ri-to-jo po-ti-ni-ja, "the Lady of the Labyrinth", at Knossos around 1375 BC.

Gold rings add epiphany scenes: a small figure descending from the sky, worshippers shaking a tree or embracing a boulder. Nanno Marinatos reads these as a coherent theology centred on a goddess and her consort; Martin Nilsson insisted on a plurality of deities. Evans's single Mother Goddess has been largely abandoned, but the sources cannot settle how many gods there were.

Where sources disagree. At Anemospilia on Juktas, Yannis and Efi Sakellarakis excavated a small building destroyed by earthquake c. 1700 with a young man bound on a platform and a bronze blade on his body, which they read as human sacrifice interrupted by the quake. Peter Warren's "Room of the Children's Bones" at Knossos (LM IB) produced cut-marked juvenile bones he interpreted as ritual cannibalism. Both readings remain controversial and sit awkwardly with the gentle Minoans of Evans's imagination.

05The Thera eruption and its dating problem

Some time in LM IA the volcano of Thera (Santorini) erupted, burying the town of Akrotiri, excavated by Spyridon Marinatos from 1967, under pumice and ash. Akrotiri is culturally Minoanised, with frescoes, lustral basins and Linear A, and its destruction gives a fixed horizon across the Aegean.

The date is one of the sharpest disputes in Aegean archaeology. Radiocarbon, above all on an olive branch buried alive in the pumice (Friedrich et al. 2006) and on short-lived seeds from Akrotiri, points to c. 1620–1600 BC. The conventional chronology, built on Egyptian synchronisms for LM IA and LM IB pottery and on Theran pumice at Tell el-Dab'a in Egypt, puts it around 1530–1500. The gap is a century. Work since 2018 on fluctuations in atmospheric radiocarbon (Pearson et al.) shows the scientific date could fall in the later sixteenth century, which narrows but does not close the gap.

What is no longer believed is Marinatos's 1939 thesis that the eruption destroyed Minoan civilisation. The palaces were burnt at the end of LM IB, two or three generations after the ash fell. Driessen and Macdonald in The Troubled Island (1997) argue that LM IB Crete shows signs of a society under stress, but the link between volcano and collapse is indirect at best.

Where sources disagree. The "high" (c. 1620–1600) and "low" (c. 1530–1500) chronologies for Thera remain unreconciled. Adopting the high date stretches LM IA and LM IB and alters every synchronism with Egypt. Most handbooks now give a range rather than a date.

06The Mycenaean takeover

At the end of LM IB, c. 1450, almost every major site on Crete was destroyed by fire: Phaistos, Malia, Zakros, Hagia Triada, Gournia, Mochlos, Palaikastro and the villas. Knossos was damaged but continued, and in LM II it acquires mainland Greek features: "warrior graves" with swords and boar's-tusk helmets; mainland pottery shapes; a throne room whose final form, with griffin frescoes, most now date to LM II–IIIA; and above all administration in Linear B, which writes Greek. The Knossos archive records a kingdom controlling central and western Crete, with place names such as Phaistos, Amnisos and Kydonia, roughly 100,000 sheep, and a ruler styled wanax.

Whether this was a conquest is debated. The old view was an invasion from the Argolid. Driessen and Macdonald argue that Crete was already weakened and that the LM IB fires may be partly internal. Others reply that a Greek-writing bureaucracy at exactly one surviving centre is hard to explain without new rulers from outside. The date of the Knossos tablets was itself a famous quarrel: Evans dated the palace's final destruction to c. 1400; Leonard Palmer argued in the 1960s that the tablets belonged to c. 1200 like those at Pylos; John Boardman defended Evans's stratigraphy. The current view places most tablets in LM IIIA2, c. 1375–1350.

Where sources disagree. Evans, Palmer and Boardman could not agree on when Knossos fell within a range of two centuries, and the excavation records of 1900–1904 were never good enough to settle it. The LM IIIA2 compromise is widely held but not universal.

07Arthur Evans and the making of "Minoan"

Evans began excavating the Kephala hill at Knossos, where Minos Kalokairinos had already dug in 1878, in March 1900, and published The Palace of Minos in four volumes (1921–35). His achievements are real: he recognised a pre-Greek civilisation, distinguished the three scripts, built the relative chronology still in use, and recorded a vast site before it decayed.

Much of what visitors see, however, is his. In the 1920s he rebuilt large parts of the palace in reinforced concrete, with the architects Christian Doll and Piet de Jong, inventing upper storeys, columns and staircases. The frescoes were restored by Émile Gilliéron father and son from fragments that were sometimes very small: the "Prince of the Lilies" combines pieces that may belong to two or three figures; the "Saffron Gatherer" was restored as a boy and later shown to be a monkey. Several "Minoan" ivory goddesses in museums outside Greece are now suspected forgeries from the Gilliérons' circle. Beyond objects, Evans supplied a story: a peaceful, goddess-worshipping thalassocracy, a "Pax Minoica" ruled by a Priest-King. That picture drew on Frazer and on the Minos legend, and fitted a post-1918 longing for a civilisation without war.

The critique is now mature: J. A. MacGillivray's Minotaur (2000), the essays edited by Yannis Hamilakis as Labyrinth Revisited (2002) and Cathy Gere's Knossos and the Prophets of Modernism (2009) trace how far the Minoans are a modernist artefact. On the specific point of peace, swords from Malia and Arkalochori and fortifications at sites such as Petras make the "unarmed Minoans" hard to sustain; Barry Molloy argues that violence was simply underrepresented in the art Evans privileged. The chronology, the scripts and the architecture are Evans's permanent contribution; the Priest-King, the Mother Goddess and the restored frescoes are early twentieth-century interpretations.

08The myths later Greeks attached to Crete

Minos is in Homer already a king of Knossos who "reigned in nine-year periods" and was the confidant of Zeus (Odyssey 19.178–9), the son of Zeus and grandfather of the Cretan leader Idomeneus (Iliad 13.450–3), and a judge of the dead (Odyssey 11.568–71). Thucydides 1.4 makes him the first ruler to build a navy, colonise the Cyclades and suppress piracy; Herodotus 3.122 pointedly refuses to count him among human rulers, and at 7.170–1 reports that he died at Camicus in Sicily pursuing Daedalus. Plato's Laws opens by crediting Minos with Crete's laws; Athenian tragedy made him a tyrant exacting tribute of youths.

Europa, daughter of Phoenix in Iliad 14.321–2 and of Agenor in Apollodorus, Library 3.1.1, was carried from Tyre to Crete by Zeus as a bull and bore Minos, Rhadamanthys and Sarpedon (Moschus, Europa; Ovid, Metamorphoses 2.833–75). Pasiphae, Minos's wife, conceived a passion for a bull Poseidon sent when Minos failed to sacrifice it; Daedalus built her a wooden cow and the child was the Minotaur, properly named Asterios (Apollodorus 3.1.3–4; Euripides' lost Cretans). Daedalus appears in Homer only as the maker of a dancing-floor for Ariadne at Knossos (Iliad 18.590–2); his labyrinth, his flight with Icarus and the boy's fall come from Apollodorus, Epitome 1.12–15, Diodorus 4.76–9 and Ovid, Metamorphoses 8.183–235. Theseus and the Minotaur is fullest in Plutarch, Theseus 15–19, who also records Philochorus's rationalising version in which the labyrinth was a prison and "Taurus" a Cretan general; Odyssey 11.321–5 has an older version in which Artemis kills Ariadne on Dia. Talos, the bronze man who circled Crete three times a day and died when Medea opened the vein in his ankle, is in Apollonius, Argonautica 4.1638–88 and Apollodorus 1.9.26. The birth of Zeus is Cretan from Hesiod onwards: Rhea bears him at Lyktos and hides him in a cave on Mount Aigaion (Theogony 477–84); later writers divide the honour between Dicte (Apollodorus 1.1.6) and Ida (Diodorus 5.70), with the Kouretes clashing their shields to drown his cries. Callimachus, Hymn to Zeus 8–9, mocks the Cretans as liars for showing a tomb of Zeus.

What, if anything, do they preserve?

The myths are Greek, reshaped between the eighth and first centuries BC, and most of their detail serves Greek concerns: Athenian politics in the Theseus story, Hellenistic learning in Callimachus, Roman taste in Ovid. But a cautious case for residue can be made on four points.

  1. Knossos was remembered as the great centre. Homer's "great city Knossos", Minos's seat, matches the fact that Knossos outlasted every other palace and was the capital of the Linear B kingdom. Mycenaean Greeks ruled there for two centuries and could carry the memory forward, as Nilsson argued in The Mycenaean Origin of Greek Mythology (1932).
  2. Specific cult sites are continuous. Linear B tablet KN Fp 1 records offerings to di-ka-ta-jo di-we, "Dictaean Zeus"; KN Gg 705 records offerings to Eleuthia at Amnisos, and Odyssey 19.188 places the cave of Eileithyia at Amnisos; the "Lady of the Labyrinth" has been noted. The Idaean and Psychro caves show cult from Minoan through Roman times. Here the chain from Bronze Age to Homer is documented.
  3. The bull and the craftsman. The island whose art is saturated with bulls became the island of the bull-god, the bull-lover and the bull-man; the island with the most elaborate architecture in the Aegean became the home of the archetypal engineer. This is probably later reinterpretation of visible ruins and images, but of real things.
  4. Thalassocracy. Minoan material at Kythera, Thera, Melos, Kea, Rhodes and Miletus shows real overseas presence behind Thucydides' sea-empire, though whether it was political control, trade or settlement cannot be decided from pottery.

Against these, the central figures resist. "Minos" may be a title rather than a name (Evans's suggestion, unprovable), or a Greek invention to give the island a founder. The labyrinth is probably not derived from labrys, as Maximilian Mayer proposed in 1892; both words are pre-Greek and the link is folk etymology. No Minoan image shows a bull-headed man. The sober conclusion is that Greek myth preserves the importance of Knossos, a few cult names, and an impression of bulls and ingenious buildings, and invents nearly everything else.

Where sources disagree. Ancient writers themselves split: Thucydides and Plato treat Minos as a historical lawgiver and admiral; Herodotus removes him from human history; Philochorus and Plutarch rationalise the Minotaur into a man. Among moderns, Nilsson's view that the myths carry Mycenaean memory is accepted in outline, but how much of the Cretan cycle is pre-Greek rather than Mycenaean or Archaic remains uncertain.

09Self-test

  1. Name the two chronological schemes used for Bronze Age Crete and who proposed each.
    Answer

    Evans's tripartite ceramic scheme (Early, Middle, Late Minoan) and Platon's palatial scheme (Prepalatial, Protopalatial, Neopalatial, Postpalatial).

  2. Which palace was never looted after its destruction, and who excavated it?
    Answer

    Zakros, found by Nikolaos Platon in 1961.

  3. Give two architectural features common to all four palaces.
    Answer

    Any two of: a north–south central court, a west court with raised walkways, long magazines with pithoi, lustral basins, pillar crypts, light-wells and pier-and-door partitions, no fortification walls.

  4. Why does Jan Driessen prefer the term "court compound" to "palace"?
    Answer

    Because the central court appears to be the organising purpose of the design, suggesting gathering and ritual, and "palace" presupposes a monarch for whom there is no direct evidence.

  5. State three reasons Linear A remains undeciphered.
    Answer

    A small corpus (c. 1,400 documents, c. 7,400 signs); no bilingual text; texts are lists rather than sentences; the underlying language is unknown and has no proven descendant.

  6. What do the two dating methods say about the Thera eruption, and roughly how far apart are they?
    Answer

    Radiocarbon (olive branch, seeds) gives c. 1620–1600 BC; Egyptian-based archaeological synchronisms give c. 1530–1500 BC; a gap of about a century, narrowed but not closed by recent radiocarbon recalibration.

  7. What changed at Knossos in LM II that points to Mycenaean rule?
    Answer

    Warrior graves with mainland weapons, the final form of the throne room, mainland pottery shapes, and administration in Linear B, which writes Greek.

  8. Name two elements of the Knossos visitors see today that are Evans's rather than Minoan.
    Answer

    Any two of: the reinforced-concrete reconstructions of upper storeys and staircases; the Gilliéron fresco restorations such as the Prince of the Lilies; the restored head and cat of the smaller snake goddess figurine.

  9. Which Linear B tablet names a "Lady of the Labyrinth", and why does it matter?
    Answer

    KN Gg 702 from Knossos, c. 1375 BC. It shows the word "labyrinth" in use at Knossos as a cult name centuries before Homer, a documented link between Bronze Age Crete and the later myth.

  10. In Homer, what is Daedalus said to have made, and where?
    Answer

    A dancing-floor for Ariadne at Knossos (Iliad 18.590–2); the labyrinth and the flight with Icarus are later additions.

10Carry forward

  1. Minoan Crete is a non-Greek civilisation whose language is unread; after c. 1450 BC Knossos was ruled by Greek-speaking Mycenaeans using Linear B, and this two-century Mycenaean Crete is the bridge through which anything Cretan reached later Greek myth.
  2. Much of the popular "Minoan" image, the Priest-King, the peaceful Mother Goddess civilisation, the restored frescoes and concrete palace, is Arthur Evans's early twentieth-century interpretation; separate his permanent contributions (chronology, scripts, the site) from his reconstructions.
  3. Greek myths about Crete preserve the importance of Knossos, a handful of cult names and places (Dictaean Zeus, Eileithyia at Amnisos, the Lady of the Labyrinth) and the island's bull imagery, but invent almost all narrative detail; use them as evidence for Archaic and Classical Greek thinking about the past, not as records of Minoan history.
