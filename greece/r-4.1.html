The lost poems and what Proclus preserved

Almost everything that happens before the Iliad opens was told in poems that no longer exist. The Iliad covers some fifty days of the tenth year and assumes its audience knows the rest. The rest was supplied by the Epic Cycle, hexameter poems of probably the seventh and sixth centuries BC, later arranged into a sequence running from the marriage of Earth and Sky to the death of Odysseus. The Trojan section comprised six:

What survives is a few dozen quoted lines, scattered testimonia, and the prose summaries in Proclus' Chrestomathy, preserved in Photius, Library cod. 239 and in the Venetus A manuscript of the Iliad. Proclus' page on the Cypria is the backbone of every modern account of the war's causes. It is also a summary of a poem as edited to dovetail with Homer, so episodes that originally overlapped the Iliad may have been trimmed. The standard editions are M. L. West, Greek Epic Fragments (2003) and The Epic Cycle (2013).

Where sources disagree. The Suda makes this Proclus the fifth-century Neoplatonist; most scholars since Wilamowitz prefer a second-century AD grammarian. The Cypria's authorship was contested in antiquity: Herodotus 2.117 denies it to Homer because the Cypria had Paris reach Troy from Sparta in three days, whereas Iliad 6.289–292 sends him via Sidon. Proclus' summary includes the Sidon detour, so either Herodotus knew a different text or the summary has been harmonised with Homer.

One fragment matters for everything that follows. The scholia to Iliad 1.5 quote the poem's opening (fr. 1 West): Earth, weighed down by the countless tribes of men, appealed to Zeus, who resolved to lighten her by means of the Trojan War, planning it with Themis. The war is a divine design, and Helen, Paris and the apple are its instruments. Hesiod, Catalogue of Women fr. 204.95–119 M–W has a related version: after Helen's marriage Zeus plans strife to bring the race of heroes to an end.

Troy before Priam

Aeneas gives the Trojan pedigree at Iliad 20.215–241, the longest in Homer; Apollodorus, Library 3.12.1–3 expands it. Dardanus, son of Zeus and the Pleiad Electra, founded Dardania on the slopes of Ida before Ilios existed on the plain; Apollodorus brings him from Samothrace after a flood to marry Bateia, daughter of the local king Teucer, whence the poets' name Teucrians. His son Erichthonius owned three thousand mares, on which Boreas as a stallion fathered twelve foals that ran over standing corn without breaking it. Erichthonius' son Tros named the people and had three sons: Ilus, Assaracus and Ganymedes.

Ganymedes became Zeus's cup-bearer, and Zeus paid Tros with the immortal horses Laomedon later owned (Iliad 5.265–267). Ilus won a spotted cow as a wrestling prize in Phrygia and was told to found a city where she lay down; she lay down on the hill of Ate, and the Palladium fell from heaven as confirmation (Apollodorus 3.12.3). Hence two lines: Ilus–Laomedon–Priam ruling the city, and Assaracus–Capys–Anchises–Aeneas in Dardania, which Poseidon at Iliad 20.302–308 prophesies will outlive the city.

Laomedon is the pivot because he cheats everyone. Apollo and Poseidon built the walls for him and were refused their wages (Iliad 21.441–457). Poseidon sent a sea-monster; an oracle demanded Laomedon's daughter Hesione. Heracles killed the monster for the promised horses of Zeus, was cheated too, and came back with six ships (Iliad 5.638–651), sacked the city and killed Laomedon and all his sons but one. Hesione, given to Telamon, ransomed her brother Podarces with her veil, hence Priam, as if from priamai, I buy (Apollodorus 2.6.4). The etymology is folk, but the point stands: Troy had fallen once already within its king's lifetime.

Where sources disagree. Dardanus' origin shifts with politics: Arcadian or Samothracian in Homer's tradition and Apollodorus, an emigrant from Italian Corythus in Dionysius of Halicarnassus 1.61 and Virgil, Aeneid 3.167–168, so that Aeneas is going home. Laomedon's wife is Strymo in Apollodorus, Placia or Leucippe elsewhere; Homer never names her.

The house of Priam

Priam had fifty sons, nineteen by Hecuba (Iliad 24.495–497), and twelve married daughters (6.248). Hecuba is daughter of Dymas the Phrygian in Homer (16.718), of Cisseus in Euripides (Hecuba 3), of the river Sangarius in a view Apollodorus 3.12.5 records. The children who matter for the war:

The Paris birth-legend is the one the war needs. Hecuba dreamt she bore a torch that set the city ablaze; the seer Aesacus read it; the infant was exposed on Ida, suckled by a bear, raised by the herdsman Agelaus, and earned the name Alexander, defender of men, by driving off cattle-thieves (Apollodorus 3.12.5). He lived with the nymph Oenone until the goddesses came. His recognition at Priam's funeral games is the plot of Euripides' lost Alexandros (415 BC) and Hyginus, Fabulae 91. Homer knows none of this, though the herding is implied at Iliad 24.29, where the goddesses come to Paris' farmyard.

The wedding on Pelion and the Judgement of Paris

Zeus and Poseidon pursued the Nereid Thetis until Themis revealed that her son would be greater than his father (Pindar, Isthmian 8.26–48; Apollodorus 3.13.5 credits Prometheus). So she was married down to a mortal, Peleus of Phthia, who caught her on the shore at Sepias as she changed shapes. Homer knows the wedding as a feast of all the gods, Apollo playing the lyre (Iliad 24.59–63), and knows Thetis went unwillingly (18.432–434). The gifts were Chiron's ash spear and Poseidon's horses Xanthus and Balius (16.140–144, 16.866–867).

Proclus says only that at the wedding Eris provoked a quarrel about beauty among Athena, Hera and Aphrodite, and that Zeus had Hermes lead them to Alexander on Ida. Paris, "excited by the prospect of marriage with Helen", chose Aphrodite. The bribes are spelled out by Helen herself at Euripides, Trojan Women 924–931: Athena offered conquest of Greece, Hera kingship over Asia and Europe, Aphrodite Helen. Apollodorus, Epitome 3.2 is the earliest surviving text to make the quarrel's object an apple thrown by Eris; Lucian, Dialogues of the Gods 20 gives the inscription "to the fairest". The Judgement is on the Chigi vase (c. 640 BC) with Alexandros labelled, so it is at least as old as the Cycle.

Where sources disagree. The apple is absent from Homer, from Proclus and from every quoted fragment of the Cypria; whether the poem had it is uncertain. Homer's one allusion to the Judgement, Iliad 24.25–30, was athetised by Aristarchus as foreign to Homer; Reinhardt's 1938 essay argued the reverse, that the whole Iliad presupposes it. Whether Eris was uninvited (Apollodorus; Hyginus 92) or merely present and quarrelsome (Proclus) is also open.

The consequences were immediate. Aphrodite told Paris to build ships; Helenus and Cassandra prophesied; Aphrodite ordered Aeneas to sail with him. Hera and Athena became Troy's enemies, which is the fixed condition of the Iliad.

Helen: birth, suitors, oath

Helen is Zeus's daughter throughout Homer (Iliad 3.199), and Leda is mother of Castor and Polydeuces by Tyndareus (Odyssey 11.298–300). There is no swan in Homer. The Cypria (fr. 10 West, in Athenaeus 8.334b–d) made her daughter of Zeus and Nemesis, who fled him in animal shapes until he caught her as a goose; Apollodorus 3.10.7 adds the egg found by a shepherd and given to Leda to hatch. Euripides, Helen 17–21 brings the swan to Leda herself, with Helen's own "if the story is true". Hesiod fr. 24 M–W gave her an Oceanid mother. Of her twin brothers Polydeuces is Zeus's and Castor mortal; in the Cypria they die fighting Idas and Lynceus during Paris' visit to Sparta and share immortality on alternate days (Proclus; Pindar, Nemean 10.55–90). At Iliad 3.236–244 Helen scans the plain for them, not knowing.

Before the suitors came Theseus. Alcman (fr. 21), Hellanicus and Stesichorus knew that Theseus and Peirithous carried off the child Helen to Aphidna and that the Dioscuri recovered her, taking Theseus' mother Aethra as her servant; Aethra is still with Helen at Iliad 3.144. Stesichorus and Euphorion, cited by Pausanias 2.22.6, made Iphigenia Helen's daughter by Theseus, given to Clytemnestra to raise.

The wooing is the climax of Hesiod's Catalogue (frr. 196–204 M–W): a roll of suitors with their gifts; Menelaus bid through Agamemnon and offered most; Odysseus sent nothing, knowing Menelaus would win; Achilles was still a boy with Chiron (fr. 204.87–92). The oath is in Apollodorus 3.10.9: Tyndareus, fearing the losers would turn on the winner, took Odysseus' advice to make all suitors swear to defend whoever was chosen, and in return Odysseus got help in winning Penelope. Euripides, Iphigenia at Aulis 49–71 has the oath sworn over a burnt sacrifice and Helen allowed to choose. Their one child was Hermione (Odyssey 4.12–14).

Where sources disagree. Thucydides 1.9 rejects the oath as the reason the Greeks followed Agamemnon, preferring his superior power and fleet. Homer never mentions the oath: his Greeks fight for Menelaus' honour and for plunder. The Hesiodic fragment that preserves it (204.78–85) is damaged exactly where its wording would be.

The abduction, and the Helen who never went

Proclus gives the Cypria's sequence. Paris was entertained by the Dioscuri and then by Menelaus in Sparta and gave Helen gifts. Menelaus sailed to Crete (for his grandfather Catreus' funeral, Apollodorus, Epitome 3.3), telling Helen to look after the guests. Aphrodite brought them together; they loaded most of the household property and sailed by night; Hera's storm drove them to Sidon, which Paris captured; at Troy they celebrated the marriage. Homer keeps the pieces: Paris left "with his wife and much property" (Iliad 13.626–627); they first lay together on the islet of Cranae (3.443–445); Sidonian women came on that voyage (6.289–292); Paris later offers to return the property but not Helen (7.362–364). Iris told Menelaus, who went to Agamemnon and then to Nestor, and Nestor delayed proceedings with four digressions.

Herodotus heard another story from the priests at Memphis (2.113–120). Paris was blown to Egypt; King Proteus kept Helen and the goods and expelled him; the Greeks besieged Troy for ten years disbelieving the Trojans' truthful denial; Menelaus collected Helen on his way home. Herodotus adds his own argument: Priam would never have let his city burn to keep Paris' mistress.

The poetic form is older. Stesichorus (fr. 192 PMG, at Plato, Phaedrus 243a) recanted a poem that had blamed Helen:

That story is not true; you did not go in the well-benched ships.

The ancients said Helen blinded him for slander and restored his sight when he retracted (Isocrates, Helen 64; Pausanias 3.19.11–13). The device was an eidolon, a phantom of cloud that went to Troy while the woman stayed in Egypt. Euripides staged it in Helen (412 BC): Hera, cheated at the Judgement, made the image; Hermes carried Helen to Proteus; Menelaus washes up with the phantom, which dissolves into air. At Electra 1280–1283 Zeus sends the phantom "so that strife and slaughter might come upon mortals": the Cypria's depopulation plan in a new key.

Where sources disagree. Whether Stesichorus wrote one Palinode or two is argued from a papyrus commentary (P.Oxy. 2506) that seems to mention two recantations. Whether Helen went willingly is the oldest dispute of all: Homer's Helen blames herself and Aphrodite by turns (Iliad 3.173–176, 3.399–412); Gorgias' Encomium acquits her under every heading; Herodotus' priests assume force. The Cypria, as summarised, makes Aphrodite the agent and says nothing of Helen's will.

Mustering the Greeks

Agamemnon and Menelaus went round the kings invoking the oath. Odysseus, warned by an oracle (Hyginus 95) that he would return after twenty years alone and destitute, feigned madness. In the Cypria and Apollodorus, Epitome 3.7, Palamedes exposed him by snatching the infant Telemachus and drawing his sword; Odysseus dropped the act. The ox and horse yoked together and the salt sown in the furrows are late (Hyginus 95; Lucian, On the Dance 46). The episode founded the enmity that killed Palamedes: by the Cypria (fr. 27 West, via Pausanias 10.31.2) Odysseus and Diomedes drowned him while fishing; in Euripides' lost Palamedes, Hyginus 105 and Apollodorus, Epitome 3.8 he was framed with a forged letter and buried gold, and stoned.

Achilles' recruitment has incompatible shapes. Homer's is plain: Nestor and Odysseus came to Phthia, and Achilles and Patroclus were sent off openly with their fathers' advice (Iliad 11.765–790; 9.252–259). The Cyclic version puts Achilles on Scyros only after the first abortive expedition, when a storm drove him there and he married Lycomedes' daughter Deidameia (Proclus; cf. Iliad 19.326, Neoptolemus growing up on Scyros). The version everyone remembers is later still: Calchas foretold that Troy would not fall without Achilles; Thetis hid him, aged nine, among Lycomedes' daughters under the name Pyrrha; Odysseus laid weapons among women's gifts and sounded a trumpet, and the boy seized the arms (Apollodorus 3.13.8; Hyginus 96; Ovid, Metamorphoses 13.162–170; Statius, Achilleid). Pausanias 1.22.6 saw Polygnotus' painting of Achilles among the maidens in the Propylaea, so the disguise was known by the mid-fifth century.

Where sources disagree. The Scyros stories cannot be reconciled: Homer has Achilles conquer Scyros as a warrior (9.668), the Cypria has him land there grown and blown off course, the later tradition hides him there as a child. Apollodorus, Epitome 3.16 makes him fifteen when he sailed; the Iliad's Achilles, with a son old enough to fight by the tenth year, must be older. The chronology was never made consistent.

The host gathered at Aulis. Homer's Catalogue of Ships (Iliad 2.494–759) totals some 1,186 vessels; Thucydides 1.10 rounds to 1,200; Apollodorus (Epitome 3.11–14) gives 1,013. A serpent devoured a sparrow and her eight chicks at the altar and was turned to stone; Calchas read nine years of war and the city taken in the tenth (Iliad 2.299–332).

Telephus and the false start

The Cypria has the fleet sail, miss Troy and sack Teuthrania in Mysia believing it was Troy. The king, Telephus, son of Heracles and Auge, killed Thersander son of Polyneices and drove them to the ships until Dionysus tripped him in a vine and Achilles wounded his thigh (Pindar, Isthmian 8.49–50; Apollodorus, Epitome 3.17). A storm scattered the returning fleet; this is when Achilles reached Scyros. Telephus' wound would not heal; Apollo's oracle said the wounder would heal it. He came to Argos in rags and, in Euripides' Telephus (438 BC, parodied in Aristophanes' Acharnians), seized the infant Orestes as hostage until he was heard. Achilles cured him with rust from the spear (Apollodorus, Epitome 3.20; Hyginus 101), and Telephus, married to Priam's sister Astyoche, agreed to guide the fleet but not to fight. His son Eurypylus would come to Troy's aid in the last year (Odyssey 11.519–521).

Where sources disagree. Apollodorus (Epitome 3.18) separates the Mysian fiasco from the second muster by eight years, so that the whole affair lasts twenty, matching Helen at Iliad 24.765–766, "this is now the twentieth year". The Iliad elsewhere counts nine years of war (2.134, 2.295). Most scholars treat the twenty-year scheme as a later harmonisation of Helen's loose phrase; Proclus gives no interval.

Aulis again, Tenedos, the landing, and nine years

Iphigenia

At the second gathering Agamemnon shot a deer and boasted that Artemis herself could not have done better. She sent storms; Calchas declared only Agamemnon's daughter would appease her; Iphigenia was fetched on the pretext of marriage to Achilles. So far Proclus. He continues: Artemis snatched her away, set a deer on the altar, carried her to the Taurians and made her immortal. Hesiod, Catalogue fr. 23a M–W agrees under another name, Iphimede made immortal as Artemis Einodia, that is Hecate; Stesichorus' Oresteia likewise made her Hecate. Euripides built two plays on the rescue: Iphigenia among the Taurians (c. 414), where she is Artemis' priestess until Orestes arrives, and Iphigenia at Aulis (405), where a messenger reports the deer on the altar. In the Taurians (20–24) the offence differs: Agamemnon had vowed Artemis the fairest thing the year produced, which turned out to be his daughter. Apollodorus, Epitome 3.21 records the boast and a third cause, Atreus' undelivered golden lamb.

Against the rescue stands the sacrifice. Aeschylus, Agamemnon 184–247 has contrary winds from the Strymon rotting ships and men, Agamemnon putting on "the yoke of necessity", and the girl lifted over the altar like a goat, gagged so she cannot curse the house; then the chorus stops: what followed it did not see. Pindar, Pythian 11.22–23 has her "slaughtered at the Euripus"; Sophocles' and Euripides' Electra plays assume she died; Lucretius 1.84–101 makes her death the exemplum of religious crime. Homer knows neither version: at Iliad 9.144–145 Agamemnon offers Achilles any of three living daughters, Chrysothemis, Laodice and Iphianassa.

Where sources disagree. Whether Iphianassa is Iphigenia under another name (so the scholiasts) or a different sister (Sophocles, Electra 157 names both) is undecided. The deer ending of Iphigenia at Aulis (1532–1629) is widely held to be a later addition, its final lines certainly Byzantine; the substitution is thus secure for the Cypria and Hesiod but not for Euripides' last play. And the causes of Artemis' anger are three incompatible stories, none adopted by Aeschylus, whose portent is eagles devouring a pregnant hare (Agamemnon 114–138).

Tenedos and Lemnos

At Tenedos Achilles killed its king Tennes despite Thetis' warning that Tennes' killer would die by Apollo's hand (Apollodorus, Epitome 3.26). At a feast Philoctetes was bitten by a water-snake and, because of the stench of the wound, was left on Lemnos (Proclus). Homer agrees on the island and the snake and adds that the Argives "would soon remember" him (Iliad 2.721–725). Sophocles' Philoctetes (409 BC) puts the bite at the shrine of the nymph Chryse (1326–1328) and makes Odysseus the abandoner (5–11). Achilles also quarrelled with Agamemnon at Tenedos over a late invitation to the feast, the first rehearsal of the Iliad's opening.

Where sources disagree. Sophocles' Lemnos is uninhabited, dramatically essential and historically absurd: in Homer it has a king, Euneus son of Jason, who buys Lycaon from Patroclus (Iliad 21.40–41, 23.746–747). Hyginus 102 replaces the snake with one of Heracles' poisoned arrows falling on Philoctetes' foot as punishment for revealing the site of Heracles' pyre.

Protesilaus and the landing

An oracle said the first man ashore would be the first to die, and Thetis warned Achilles (Apollodorus, Epitome 3.29). Protesilaus of Phylace leapt first and was killed; his brother Podarces took his forty ships (Iliad 2.695–710). Homer leaves his killer "a Dardanian man" and lingers on the wife tearing her cheeks and the house left half-built; the Cypria named Hector. Achilles killed Cycnus, son of Poseidon, whom Ovid (Metamorphoses 12.72–145) later made invulnerable, and routed the Trojans. The Greeks sent Menelaus and Odysseus into the city to demand Helen and the property; Antenor protected them, Antimachus urged the Trojans to kill Menelaus, and the demand was refused (Iliad 3.205–224, 11.138–142). Protesilaus' widow Laodamia and his three hours' leave from the dead are the matter of Euripides' lost Protesilaus and Hyginus 103–104; his tomb at Elaeus was a working cult site when Xerxes' governor plundered it (Herodotus 7.33, 9.116–120).

The first nine years

Proclus compresses nine years into a paragraph. The Greeks besieged the city and ravaged the countryside and neighbouring towns. Achilles asked to see Helen, and Aphrodite and Thetis arranged a meeting, found nowhere else. When the army wanted to go home Achilles held it. He raided Aeneas' cattle on Ida (Iliad 20.89–96), sacked Lyrnessus, Pedasus and twenty-three cities in all by his own count (9.328–329), and killed Troilus, whom Apollodorus (Epitome 3.32) has ambushed in the sanctuary of Thymbraean Apollo, a scene Attic pots show at a fountain-house. Patroclus sold Lycaon on Lemnos. From the spoils Achilles received Briseis of Lyrnessus and Agamemnon Chryseis of Thebe (Iliad 1.366–369, 2.690–693), the fuel of the Iliad's quarrel. Palamedes died. And Zeus, Proclus ends, planned to relieve the Trojans by detaching Achilles from the alliance; there followed a catalogue of Trojan allies. The Cypria closes exactly where Iliad 1 opens.

Thucydides 1.11 rationalised the nine years: the Greeks lacked supplies, farmed the Chersonese and raided for food, and never brought full strength against the walls. That is a man who had watched sieges, not a poet; but it shows that by the fifth century the long delay had itself become a problem the story had to explain.

Self-test

  1. Who preserved the summaries of the Epic Cycle, and in what two places do they survive?
    Answer

    Proclus, in the Chrestomathy; in Photius, Library cod. 239 and in the Venetus A manuscript of the Iliad. His identity (Neoplatonist or second-century grammarian) is disputed.

  2. According to Cypria fr. 1, why did Zeus bring about the Trojan War?
    Answer

    To relieve Earth, weighed down by the multitude of men; he planned it with Themis.

  3. Trace the line from Zeus to Priam in Homer's genealogy.
    Answer

    Zeus and Electra, Dardanus, Erichthonius, Tros, Ilus, Laomedon, Priam (Iliad 20.215–241). Aeneas descends from Tros through Assaracus, Capys and Anchises.

  4. Why was Podarces renamed Priam, and who ransomed him?
    Answer

    Heracles sacked Troy after Laomedon cheated him; Hesione ransomed her brother with her veil, and the name was explained from priamai, I buy (Apollodorus 2.6.4).

  5. Which three goddesses did Paris judge, and what did each offer according to Euripides?
    Answer

    Hera, Athena and Aphrodite; Hera offered rule over Asia and Europe, Athena conquest of Greece, Aphrodite Helen (Trojan Women 924–931).

  6. What is Helen's parentage in the Cypria, and how does it differ from Euripides' Helen?
    Answer

    In the Cypria she is daughter of Zeus and Nemesis, caught as a goose, with Leda hatching the egg; in Euripides Zeus came to Leda herself as a swan.

  7. What was the oath of Tyndareus, who proposed it, and which historian rejected it as the cause of the expedition?
    Answer

    All suitors swore to defend whichever man Helen married; Odysseus proposed it in exchange for help winning Penelope (Apollodorus 3.10.9); Thucydides 1.9 said Agamemnon's power, not the oath, assembled the fleet.

  8. What is the eidolon of Helen, and which three authors transmit the Egyptian story?
    Answer

    A phantom of cloud that went to Troy while the real Helen stayed in Egypt: Stesichorus' Palinode, Herodotus 2.113–120 (Helen in Egypt, without the phantom) and Euripides' Helen.

  9. How does Aeschylus' account of Iphigenia at Aulis differ from the Cypria's?
    Answer

    Aeschylus (Agamemnon 184–247) has her gagged and sacrificed, with contrary winds as the cause and no rescue; the Cypria has Artemis substitute a deer and carry her to the Taurians, immortal, after Agamemnon's boast over a deer.

  10. Who killed Protesilaus, and why was Philoctetes left on Lemnos?
    Answer

    Homer says only "a Dardanian man"; the Cypria and Apollodorus name Hector. Philoctetes was bitten by a water-snake at Tenedos (or at Chryse's shrine, in Sophocles) and abandoned because of the stench of the wound.

Carry forward

  1. The war is a divine plan before it is a human quarrel: Zeus's decision to depopulate the earth (Cypria fr. 1; Hesiod fr. 204) underlies the Judgement, the oath and Helen's flight, and the "will of Zeus" in the Iliad's fifth line inherits it.
  2. Knowledge of the pre-Iliadic story rests on summaries of lost poems read through fifth-century tragedy and Roman-era handbooks; nearly every episode has an early (Cyclic) and a late (Apollodorus, Hyginus) form, and the familiar version is usually the late one.
  3. Troy had already been sacked once, by Heracles, for Laomedon's broken faith; the Trojan War is a second reckoning, and Aeneas' Dardanian line is marked from the start as the one that will survive it.
