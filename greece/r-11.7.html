A structural problem, not a gap

Every narrative of Alexander that survives was written at least 270 years after his death in Babylon in June 323 BC. The men who marched with him did write, and some wrote at length, but their books are lost. What remains are fragments quoted by later authors, mostly Strabo, Plutarch, Arrian and Athenaeus, collected by Felix Jacoby in Die Fragmente der griechischen Historiker (FGrH II B, beginning at no. 117). So the Alexander historian works at two removes. First, what did Ptolemy or Cleitarchus actually say? Second, why did they say it? A third hangs over both: how far did the surviving author, writing under Augustus, Claudius or Hadrian, reshape what he took over? There is no neutral base text.

Callisthenes and his death

Callisthenes is the paradox of the tradition: the most contemporary narrator and among the most flattering. His surviving fragments make Alexander the son of Zeus at Siwa and have the waves of the Pamphylian sea do obeisance. Yet in 327 at Bactra he publicly refused proskynesis, the Persian prostration, and was soon implicated in the Pages' Conspiracy (Arrian 4.10–14; Plutarch, Alexander 53–55). His death made him a Peripatetic martyr, and a hostile picture of Alexander as a tyrant corrupted by fortune is often traced to that school's grievance, though how coherent this "Peripatetic portrait" was is debated: Tarn (Alexander the Great II, 1948) built a hostile Peripatetic school tradition out of it; Badian ("The Eunuch Bagoas", 1958) denied that any such school line can be shown.

Where sources disagree. Arrian 4.14.3 sets the versions side by side: Ptolemy says Callisthenes was tortured and hanged; Aristobulus that he was carried in chains with the army and died of illness. Chares (Plutarch, Alexander 55.9) says he was held in fetters for seven months and died of obesity and lice before he could be tried before Aristotle. Arrian admits that even the eyewitnesses could not agree.

Ptolemy and Aristobulus

Arrian's preface explains why he chose these two: both campaigned with Alexander, and Ptolemy, being a king, would find lying more disgraceful than other men. That is a weak argument. Ptolemy wrote as ruler of an Egypt whose legitimacy rested partly on Alexander's body, which he had hijacked in 321. R. M. Errington ("Bias in Ptolemy's History of Alexander", 1969) showed how consistently Perdiccas, Ptolemy's great enemy, is diminished: at Thebes in 335 Ptolemy has Perdiccas attack without orders (Arrian 1.8.1), where Diodorus 17.12 has Alexander direct the assault. Ptolemy's date of writing is uncertain: most put it late in his reign (after 301, perhaps after 285), but some argue for an early date as propaganda in the Successor wars.

Aristobulus is the gentler apologist. He claims Alexander drank long for companionship, not wine (Arrian 7.29.4). Lucian (How to Write History 12) has Alexander throw Aristobulus's book into the Hydaspes for inventing a single combat with Porus, a story probably itself invented.

Where sources disagree. At the Malli town in 325, Cleitarchus and Timagenes had Ptolemy saving Alexander's life. Curtius 9.5.21 notes that Ptolemy himself wrote he was absent elsewhere. Here the self-interested source declines credit: bias does not always run one way.

The Ephemerides and the forgery debate

Plutarch (Alexander 76–77) and Arrian (7.25–26) both quote the "Royal Diaries" for Alexander's final fever: day after day of bathing, sacrificing, sleeping in the bathhouse, briefing officers, then losing his voice. The quotation is tightly dated and sober, and it rules out poison.

Where sources disagree. Lionel Pearson ("The Diary and the Letters of Alexander the Great", 1955) argued the diaries were a later literary forgery. A. E. Samuel ("Alexander's 'Royal Journal'", 1965) and N. G. L. Hammond ("The Royal Journal of Alexander", 1988) defended a genuine court journal, perhaps on the Achaemenid model. A. B. Bosworth ("The Death of Alexander the Great: Rumour and Propaganda", 1971) took a middle line: a real-sounding document was produced or doctored soon after 323 to rebut the rumour that Antipater's sons poisoned the king. That nothing survives from it except the last days is itself suspicious. Uncertain.

Cleitarchus and the Alexandrian romantic tradition

Cleitarchus wrote at Alexandria, from participants' accounts and oral tradition, for a readership that wanted drama. Thaïs leading the drunken burning of Persepolis (Athenaeus 13.576d–e), the Amazon queen's visit, and huge Persian casualty figures are his signatures. Ancient critics judged him harshly. Quintilian (10.1.74) put it in five words:

Clitarchi probatur ingenium, fides infamatur.

"His talent is approved, his reliability condemned." Yet he was the most widely read Alexander historian in the Hellenistic and Roman world, which is why so much of him survives in derivative form.

Where sources disagree. The traditional date is c. 310 BC. W. W. Tarn pushed it after 280 to discredit him. A papyrus list of scholars (P. Oxy. 4808, published 2007) calls Cleitarchus tutor of Ptolemy IV Philopator, which would put him in the later third century. Some accept this; others, Luisa Prandi (2012) among them, think the papyrus has confused him with someone else. If late, he used Ptolemy and Aristobulus rather than preceding them. Unresolved.

Diodorus is the earliest of the five and the least mediated: book 17 follows one main source closely enough that scholars reconstruct Cleitarchus largely from it, and its single large gap (the lacuna after 17.83, covering Bactria and Sogdiana in 329 to 327) is the biggest hole in the Vulgate. Justin is the last and the least reliable: Trogus wrote a full Augustan history in forty-four books, Justin cut it to anecdote and moral, and where he can be checked against Diodorus and Curtius the compression has often garbled sequence and names (the two Darius battles merged, officers confused), so a detail found only in Justin needs another witness.

Curtius: the rhetorician as historian

Curtius has suffered from his talent. His speeches are free compositions, and his Alexander follows a Roman tyrant-narrative: great gifts undone by success. Elizabeth Baynham's Alexander the Great: The Unique History of Quintus Curtius (1998) argued that he must be read as a literary artist with his own design, not mined as a conduit for Cleitarchus. Still, he preserves details no one else has, including on the Persian side, and he knows Ptolemy's account well enough to correct Cleitarchus by it.

Where sources disagree. Curtius' date rests mainly on 10.9.1–6, which praises a new emperor who saved Rome from civil strife after a night of turmoil, "like a new star". Most, with Elizabeth Baynham (Alexander the Great: The Unique History of Quintus Curtius, 1998), read Claudius after Caligula's murder in AD 41; others (J. R. Hamilton, "The Date of Quintus Curtius Rufus", 1988) argue for Vespasian after 69, and a minority for Severus Alexander or later.

Plutarch: lives, not histories

Plutarch is explicit about his aim (Alexander 1.2):

For it is not histories that we are writing, but lives.

A jest, he says, can reveal more than battles. He keeps anecdotes historians discard and names his authorities, which makes him invaluable for reconstructing lost sources. The letters he quotes are a problem: J. R. Hamilton's Plutarch, Alexander: A Commentary (1969) accepted some; others think most are rhetorical exercises. The two early speeches On the Fortune or Virtue of Alexander (Moralia 326d–345b) are display pieces arguing that Alexander was a philosopher in arms, who won by virtue rather than luck. They show the Alexander of the schools, not of the campaign.

Where sources disagree. On the Amazon queen, Plutarch (Alexander 46) lists those who told the story (Cleitarchus, Polyclitus, Onesicritus and others) and those who called it fiction (Aristobulus, Chares, Ptolemy and others). He adds that when Onesicritus read the episode to Lysimachus, the old king smiled and asked where he himself had been at the time.

Arrian: method and Xenophontic pose

L. Flavius Arrianus of Nicomedia, pupil of Epictetus, consul and governor of Cappadocia in the 130s, styled himself a new Xenophon. The title Anabasis, the seven books, the plain Attic prose and the soldier-philosopher persona all imitate Xenophon. His stated method (preface) is to record as true what Ptolemy and Aristobulus agree on, to choose the more credible when they differ, and to mark other material as legomena, "things said". In a second preface (1.12) he presents himself as the worthy chronicler Alexander lacked, a prose Homer for a new Achilles. The Indica, written in Ionic in imitation of Herodotus, paraphrases Nearchus' voyage (Indica 18–42) and Megasthenes on India.

Arrian is no neutral editor. He condemns the killing of Cleitus and the Persian dress (4.7–14) and closes with a eulogy excusing faults as youth and fortune (7.28–30). Bosworth (From Arrian to Alexander, 1988) showed him misreading, conflating and embellishing his sources. His date is uncertain: Bosworth argued for early composition, others for after the Cappadocian command.

The Vulgate versus Arrian

"Vulgate" is a modern label for the shared material in Diodorus, Curtius, Justin and the Metz Epitome, traced in large part to Cleitarchus. The older view, still common in popular books, treats Arrian as the good source and the Vulgate as romance. That ranking does not hold.

  • Arrian's sources were participants with interests, writing decades later. Arrian prefers them because they are respectable, not because he tested them.
  • The Vulgate preserves uncomfortable truths. Curtius on the Philotas affair or the dragging of Batis at Gaza (4.6.25–29) may be embellished, but Arrian's silence is not proof of innocence.
  • Both sides are processed. Arrian's Xenophontic restraint is as much a literary choice as Curtius' melodrama.
Where sources disagree. Persepolis, 330. Arrian 3.18.11–12 presents the burning of the palace as deliberate policy, revenge for Xerxes' sack of Athens, and adds his disapproval. Diodorus 17.72, Curtius 5.7 and Plutarch, Alexander 38 have a drunken revel at which the courtesan Thaïs throws the first torch. Many scholars suspect both: a policy decision staged as ritual, which Cleitarchus turned into a party and Ptolemy into statecraft. Excavation confirms deliberate destruction, not motive.

The Metz Epitome and the Liber de Morte

The Metz Epitome is a late-antique Latin summary covering 330 to 325, closely related to Curtius and Diodorus, which makes it a Vulgate control text. It survived in a single manuscript at Metz destroyed in 1944, so editions rest on earlier transcriptions. In the same manuscript stood the Liber de Morte Alexandri, a pamphlet narrating a poisoning plot by Antipater's family and a forged testament distributing the empire. A version of it is also embedded in the Alexander Romance (3.30–33).

Where sources disagree. Waldemar Heckel (The Last Days and Testament of Alexander the Great, 1988) traced the pamphlet to the circle of Polyperchon and Olympias, c. 317, to blacken Cassander. Bosworth ("Ptolemy and the Will of Alexander", 2000) placed it c. 309 in Ptolemy's interest, since the will flatters Ptolemy. Both agree it is Successor propaganda; the beneficiary is disputed.

Documents, coins and images

Non-literary evidence rarely resolves famous episodes, but it is free of narrative shaping and shows Alexander the administrator.

  • Babylonian astronomical diaries. The diary for 331 records the lunar eclipse of 20 September and Gaugamela on 1 October, with Darius' troops deserting him. The diary for 323 records, without comment, "the king died" on 29 Aiaru, 10 or 11 June, fixing the date independently of Greek tradition.
  • The Priene inscription. The temple of Athena Polias carries the dedication: "King Alexander dedicated the temple to Athena Polias", with an edict on Priene's status and taxes, now in the British Museum. It contrasts with Strabo 14.1.22, where Ephesus politely declined Alexander's offer to pay for its Artemision.
  • The letter to Chios and the Mytilene decree. Alexander's letter to Chios (c. 332) orders the return of exiles and a democratic constitution, with a garrison. Mytilene's reconciliation decree regulates returning exiles' property. Both show "liberation" of Greek cities working as direct royal intervention.
  • The Exiles' Decree inscriptions. Diodorus 18.8 reports the decree proclaimed at Olympia in 324; the Tegea inscription shows a city working through its consequences in property law.
  • Coins and the Porus medallions. Beyond the standard Heracles/Zeus tetradrachms, the "elephant medallions" (a horseman attacking an elephant; on the reverse, Alexander with a thunderbolt, crowned by Victory) are the only near-contemporary image of Alexander with divine attributes. Their mint (Babylon is likely) and date (323 or a little later) are debated (Frank Holt, Alexander the Great and the Mystery of the Elephant Medallions, 2003).
  • The Alexander Sarcophagus. Found at Sidon in 1887, now in Istanbul, probably made for Abdalonymus, the king Alexander installed there. Macedonians and Persians hunting together show the regime's image from a client king's side.
  • The Alexander Mosaic. From the House of the Faun at Pompeii (c. 100 BC), copying an early Hellenistic painting: evidence for how Alexander and Darius were imagined around 300, not for the battle.
Where sources disagree. The mosaic's original is often identified with the battle painting Philoxenus of Eretria made for Cassander (Pliny, Natural History 35.110); others suggest Apelles or Helen of Egypt. Whether it shows Issus or Gaugamela is uncertain.

The Alexander Romance

The Alexander Romance is falsely attributed to Callisthenes, so its author is called Pseudo-Callisthenes. The earliest recension (alpha) took shape in Alexandria around the third century AD from older materials: a novel in letters, Egyptian propaganda making Alexander son of the last pharaoh Nectanebo II, Indian wonder-tales, and the testament pamphlet. Its value for Alexander is almost nil; for how later cultures adopted him, enormous.

  • Latin and medieval Europe: Julius Valerius translated it in the early fourth century; Leo of Naples' tenth-century version (the Historia de Preliis) fed the Old French Roman d'Alexandre, whose twelve-syllable line named the "alexandrine". Walter of Châtillon's Alexandreis (c. 1180) follows Curtius instead.
  • Armenian and Syriac: an Armenian version by the fifth century; the Syriac Alexander Legend (c. 630) adds the gates against Gog and Magog.
  • Persian: Zoroastrian texts call him "the accursed", but Ferdowsi's Shahnameh (c. 1010) makes Sikandar Darius' half-brother and a legitimate Iranian king; Nizami (12th century) makes him sage and prophet.
  • Arabic: identification with Dhu'l-Qarnayn of Qur'an 18.83–98, traditional but disputed, carried him into Islamic romance.
  • Ethiopic: a Ge'ez version, via Arabic, makes him a chaste Christian ascetic.
Where sources disagree. The older view that the Syriac Romance was translated from a lost Middle Persian version, implying an early Iranian adoption, (Theodor Nöldeke, Beiträge zur Geschichte des Alexanderromans, 1890) has been challenged; Claudia Ciancaglini (2001) derives it directly from Greek. The route into Persian tradition remains disputed.

Modern Quellenforschung

Source criticism ("Quellenforschung") tries to assign each passage of the surviving authors to its lost origin. Hermann Strasburger's Ptolemaios und Alexander (1934) reconstructed Ptolemy from Arrian and set the agenda. Tarn (Alexander the Great, 1948) was partisan, dismissing anything unflattering as Vulgate distortion. Lionel Pearson's The Lost Histories of Alexander the Great (1960) gave cautious portraits of each lost author. Hammond (Three Historians of Alexander the Great, 1983; Sources for Alexander the Great, 1993) assigned passages to sources with a confidence many found excessive. Bosworth's Historical Commentary on Arrian's History of Alexander (vols. I–II, 1980 and 1995, unfinished) is the indispensable tool and treats Arrian as an author with his own errors; Baynham did the same for Curtius. The shift since the 1980s runs from naming the lost source behind a sentence to reading each surviving author as a writer with purposes.

Reading an anecdote: a method

  1. Find every version in Arrian, Plutarch, Curtius, Diodorus, Justin and the Metz Epitome. A story in both families outweighs one in a single Vulgate author.
  2. Look for a named source. A named eyewitness raises the stakes, not necessarily the reliability: ask what that witness gained.
  3. Ask who benefits: Ptolemy against Perdiccas, Aristobulus for the king, Cleitarchus for drama, Successors for legitimacy, Romans for a tyrant lesson.
  4. Strip the genre. Speeches, omens, encounters with philosophers and Amazons are literary forms. The bare act may survive; the words rarely do.
  5. Test against documents. Where inscriptions, diaries or coins overlap with narrative, they outweigh it.
  6. Watch for legend. Romance motifs (divine birth, wonder-voyages, poisoning plots) entered even sober texts early.
  7. Grade the result: secure, plausible, doubtful or legendary, and say which.

Take the Gordian knot. Arrian 2.3.7 gives both versions: most said Alexander cut it; Aristobulus said he drew the pin from the yoke-pole. The duller version comes from an apologist with no reason to invent it. The visit and oracle are plausible; the sword cut is probably legend.

Self-test

  1. Why did Arrian say he trusted Ptolemy and Aristobulus, and what is wrong with his reasoning?
    Answer

    Both campaigned with Alexander, and Ptolemy as a king would find lying especially shameful (Anabasis, preface). But both wrote decades later with clear interests: Ptolemy dynastic legitimacy and the diminishing of rivals such as Perdiccas, Aristobulus a defence of Alexander's character.

  2. Give the three versions of Callisthenes' death and their sources.
    Answer

    Ptolemy: tortured and hanged. Aristobulus: carried in chains and died of illness (both in Arrian 4.14.3). Chares: held in fetters for seven months and died of obesity and lice (Plutarch, Alexander 55).

  3. What is the Vulgate, and which surviving authors belong to it?
    Answer

    A modern label for the shared tradition traced mainly to Cleitarchus, found in Diodorus 17, Curtius, Justin's epitome of Trogus and the Metz Epitome. Plutarch draws on both it and the Arrian tradition.

  4. How does the Thebes episode of 335 illustrate Ptolemy's bias?
    Answer

    Ptolemy (Arrian 1.8.1) has Perdiccas attack without orders, which shifts blame for the slaughter to Ptolemy's later enemy. Diodorus 17.12 has Alexander directing the assault.

  5. What are the main positions in the Ephemerides debate?
    Answer

    Pearson: a later forgery. Samuel and Hammond: a genuine court journal. Bosworth: a document produced or doctored soon after 323 to rebut poisoning rumours. It is quoted only for the last illness, which is suspicious.

  6. Why does the date of Cleitarchus matter, and what new evidence complicated it?
    Answer

    An early date (c. 310) makes him independent of Ptolemy and Aristobulus; a late one means he may have used them. P. Oxy. 4808 calls him tutor of Ptolemy IV, implying a later third-century date, but some think the papyrus is mistaken.

  7. What passage is the main evidence for Curtius' date, and what are the options?
    Answer

    Curtius 10.9.1–6, praising a new emperor who ended civil turmoil. Most read Claudius (AD 41); alternatives are Vespasian (AD 69/70) or a later emperor.

  8. What does the Babylonian astronomical diary add to the record of Alexander's death?
    Answer

    An independent, contemporary cuneiform entry, "the king died", dated 29 Aiaru, 10 or 11 June 323, unaffected by Greek narrative shaping.

  9. How does Arrian present the Gordian knot, and how should we grade the versions?
    Answer

    Arrian 2.3.7: most said Alexander cut it; Aristobulus said he pulled the pin from the pole. The undramatic version from an apologist is more plausible; the sword cut is likely legend, though the visit and oracle are plausible.

  10. Name two ways the Alexander Romance was adapted outside Greek, and what each made of Alexander.
    Answer

    Any two, for example: Ferdowsi's Shahnameh made Sikandar half-brother of Darius and a legitimate Iranian king; Nizami made him sage and prophet; Arabic tradition linked him to Dhu'l-Qarnayn; the Ethiopic version made him a Christian ascetic.

Carry forward

  1. There is no neutral base text for Alexander: both the Arrian tradition and the Vulgate descend from interested, lost writers, and each surviving author reshaped them for his own age.
  2. Bias does not run in one direction. Eyewitnesses can omit and flatter, while sensational writers can preserve what eyewitnesses suppressed; judge each episode, not each author wholesale.
  3. Documents such as the Babylonian diaries, city inscriptions and coins are the only evidence free of narrative shaping, though not of self-presentation; use them to anchor narratives and to see Alexander the administrator behind Alexander the hero.
