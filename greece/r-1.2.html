01Schliemann and the shaft graves

Heinrich Schliemann came to Mycenae in 1876 fresh from Troy, carrying a literal reading of Pausanias (2.16.6), who says the graves of Agamemnon and his companions lay inside the walls. Everyone else had assumed the tholos tombs outside the citadel were meant. Schliemann dug just inside the Lion Gate and found a circular enclosure of upright slabs, Grave Circle A, with five shaft graves (a sixth was cleared by the Greek ephor Stamatakis in 1877): nineteen bodies with gold death-masks, diadems, inlaid daggers, silver and gold vessels, Baltic amber and ostrich-egg rhyta. The telegram "I have gazed upon the face of Agamemnon" is probably a later embellishment, but it captures the claim.

The claim was wrong by three or four centuries. The shaft graves date to Late Helladic I–IIA, roughly 1600–1450 BC, well before any plausible Trojan War horizon. An earlier circle, Grave Circle B (c. 1650–1550 BC), was excavated by Papadimitriou and Mylonas in 1951–54 outside the later walls. Together they show a warrior elite emerging from the modest Middle Helladic world and suddenly rich in exotic goods, Minoan craftsmanship and weapons. Why is still argued: mercenary service in Egypt against the Hyksos, control of metal routes to the north and west, or competitive display among chiefdoms consolidating into states. Circle A was deliberately re-enclosed when the citadel wall was extended around 1250 BC, so the thirteenth-century rulers already treated these graves as ancestral monuments.

Where sources disagree. Schliemann's reliability (Calder and Traill against Easton on the Troy treasure, module 10.10; the Mask of Agamemnon, module 10.2) bears on the findspots, not on the Mycenaean dates. Absolute dating also hangs on the Thera eruption: radiocarbon favours c. 1620–1600 BC, Egyptian synchronisms c. 1530–1500 BC. The gap is not closed.

03The palace centres and citadel architecture

By the fourteenth century the mainland was organised around a handful of palatial states, each controlling a hinterland of perhaps 2,000 square kilometres. The core building is the megaron: columned porch, vestibule, and a main hall with a great circular hearth ringed by four columns, a throne against the right-hand wall, and painted floors and walls. This is the room Homer's heroes feast in.

  • Mycenae sits on a hill between two ravines in the Argolid. Its Cyclopean walls were built in three stages; the Lion Gate with its relieving triangle belongs to the second, c. 1250 BC. The final extension (c. 1200) enclosed an underground cistern, a sign of siege anxiety. Nine tholos tombs lie outside; the Treasury of Atreus (c. 1250) has a corbelled dome 13.5 m high, unmatched until the Pantheon.
  • Tiryns, near the Argive shore, has walls up to 8 m thick with corbelled galleries, and the best preserved megaron plan, with a boar-hunt fresco.
  • Pylos in Messenia, excavated by Carl Blegen from 1939, is unwalled and burned in a single fire that baked its 1,100 tablets. Blegen dated the fire c. 1200; pottery re-study (Mountjoy) puts it at the LH IIIB–IIIC transition, c. 1180. In 2015 Davis and Stocker found the undisturbed Griffin Warrior grave of c. 1450, with the Pylos Combat Agate.
  • Thebes lies under the modern town on the Kadmeia. Tablets recovered since 1964, especially the Pelopidou Street archive of 1993–95, show a palace controlling much of Boeotia, perhaps including the fortress of Gla and the drained Copais basin.
  • Knossos was Minoan, but after the destructions of c. 1450 it was run by Greek-speakers keeping Linear B archives, the only Mycenaean administration on Crete of this scale.
Where sources disagree. The Knossos tablets' date was the Palmer–Boardman controversy of the 1960s: Leonard Palmer argued Evans had mis-stratified them and they belonged c. 1200, contemporary with Pylos; John Boardman defended c. 1400. Consensus now puts most in LM IIIA2, c. 1375–1350, with the Room of the Chariot Tablets earlier, though Hallager and Niemeier have kept a later date for part of the archive.

04Linear B and its decipherment

Arthur Evans found the first tablets at Knossos in 1900, named the script Linear B to distinguish it from the earlier, still undeciphered Linear A, believed it recorded a non-Greek language, and sat on most of the material until his death in 1941. Alice Kober showed in the 1940s that the language was inflected and worked out which signs shared consonants or vowels without knowing what either was. Michael Ventris, an architect, built on Kober's grids, tested the hypothesis that place-names such as Amnisos and Knossos were spelt syllabically, and found the result read as archaic Greek. He announced it on the BBC on 1 July 1952, then published with John Chadwick, "Evidence for Greek Dialect in the Mycenaean Archives", Journal of Hellenic Studies 73 (1953). Confirmation came when Blegen read the new Pylos tablet Ta 641: ti-ri-po-de stood beside a drawing of a tripod, and qe-to-ro-we ("four-eared") beside a four-handled jar. Ventris died in a car crash in 1956, weeks before Documents in Mycenaean Greek appeared.

The script has about 87 syllabic signs plus ideograms and numerals, and fits Greek badly: no voiced/unvoiced distinction, no final consonants. The tablets are not literature but single-year accounts on unbaked clay, surviving only where fire baked them.

What the tablets reveal

  • The wanax (wa-na-ka) stands at the top, with a temenos (estate) three times anyone else's (Pylos Er 312), his own "royal" craftsmen, and a role in cult. Whether each palace had its own wanax or one at Mycenae stood over all is unknown; Homer's anax andrōn Agamemnon preserves the word.
  • The lawagetas (ra-wa-ke-ta, "leader of the people") is second, with a temenos one-third the size. Older scholarship made him a war-leader; the tablets never show him commanding anything, and the title vanishes from later Greek.
  • Below them: telestai (landholders with obligations), hequetai ("followers", with chariots and distinctive cloaks), a qa-si-re-u (basileus) who is merely a local headman, often of bronzesmiths, and the damos, the village community, which holds land collectively and in one case (Pylos Ep 704) disputes a priestess's claim.
  • Slaves (do-e-ro, do-e-ra) are common; many are "slaves of the god" who nevertheless hold land. The Pylos Aa/Ab/Ad series lists some 750 women with children, rationed in wheat and figs, many labelled by origin on the Anatolian coast and islands: Milesian, Knidian, Lemnian, "women of Asia". One group is ra-wi-ja-ja, "captives". These look like the war-booty women of the Iliad.
  • Textiles were the chief industry. Knossos counted some 100,000 sheep, set wool targets per flock and tracked cloth through workshops; Pylos ran flax. A planned, quota-driven economy on the Near Eastern model, though less total than once thought.
  • Chariots: the Knossos Sc series issues chariots, corslets and horses to named men; Sd/Se/Sf inventory chariot bodies with and without wheels; Pylos Sa lists wheels. Knossos could field well over a hundred.
  • Defence: the Pylos "o-ka" tablets (An 657 and related) post coast-watchers under named officers, and Jn 829 collects "temple bronze" for spear-points. Whether this is the emergency before the destruction is debated.

Gods attested by name

The surest attestations are: Zeus (di-we, dative), Hera (e-ra), Poseidon (po-se-da-o, the dominant god at Pylos, which fits his later Messenian and Arcadian cults), Hermes (e-ma-a2), Artemis (a-te-mi-to, genitive, at Pylos) and Eileithyia (e-re-u-ti-ja, receiving honey at Amnisos, exactly as Odyssey 19.188 remembers). Ares appears both as a-re and as Enyalios; Paiawon is the later Apolline epithet Paean; Dionysus (di-wo-nu-so) occurs at Pylos and Khania, overturning the old idea that he was a late import. "Potnia" (Mistress) is the most frequent divine title, often qualified: a-ta-na po-ti-ni-ja at Knossos is probably "Mistress of Athana", and may or may not be Athena. There are female doublets, Diwia and Posidaeia, who later disappear. Apollo and Aphrodite are absent; Demeter is uncertain.

Where sources disagree. Pylos Tn 316, written hastily shortly before the fire, lists gold vessels and human beings (po-re-na) brought to Potnia, Zeus, Hera, Hermes and others. Chadwick and Palaima read the people as cult attendants or dedicated servants; others, following earlier suggestions, see human sacrifice in extremity. The tablet does not settle it. Likewise di-wo-nu-so was long treated as a personal name, not the god, until the Khania tablet paired it with Zeus in a context of offerings.

05Religion and burial

Mycenaean religion left no clear temples before the thirteenth century. The exception is the Cult Centre at Mycenae: small shrines below the palace with a fresco of goddesses, and a "Temple" holding grotesque terracotta figures over half a metre tall, coiled clay snakes and a dais. Comparable shrines stand at Phylakopi on Melos and at Methana. Elsewhere the evidence is small "Phi", "Psi" and "Tau" figurines in houses and tombs, and processions and libations on palace walls. Iconography borrows heavily from Crete: double axes, horns of consecration, griffins flanking the throne at Pylos and Knossos. The tablets add offerings of oil, honey, wine and animals at monthly festivals, and a priesthood of both sexes holding land and slaves. What is missing is myth. No tablet names a hero; the Linear B men called a-ki-re-u (Achilleus) and e-ko-to (Hektor) are ordinary people, the latter a "slave of the god".

Burial changed. The shaft graves were a brief Argolid phenomenon. From the late sixteenth century the mainland adopted the tholos, a corbelled dome under a mound entered by a dromos, first in Messenia and then for ruling families everywhere, and the rock-cut chamber tomb for the broader elite. Both were reopened over generations, earlier skeletons swept aside, which shows the grave goods mattered at the funeral rather than for eternity. Inhumation was the rule; cremation, Homer's universal rite, is rare until the twelfth century. There is no cult at tombs in the palatial period; the hero-cult offerings at Mycenaean tombs belong to the eighth century, when Greeks rediscovered them.

06Ahhiyawa, Wilusa and a historical Troy

The Hittite archives at Hattusa contain about 28 texts naming a western power called Ahhiyawa (earlier Ahhiya), collected in Beckman, Bryce and Cline, The Ahhiyawa Texts (2011). Emil Forrer proposed in 1924 that the name is Achaia; Ferdinand Sommer's objections held the field for decades, but the equation is now generally accepted. The key documents:

  • The Indictment of Madduwatta (fifteenth century): a man of Ahhiya, Attarissiya, raids Anatolia and Cyprus with chariots. Forrer saw Atreus; almost nobody follows him.
  • The Annals of Tudhaliya I/II list among the rebel lands of Assuwa both Wilusiya and Taruisa, matching Greek (W)ilios and Troia, Homer's two names for one city.
  • The Alaksandu Treaty (Muwatalli II, c. 1280) binds a king of Wilusa named Alaksandu, phonetically Alexandros, Paris's other name. Among the oath-gods is a damaged [A]ppaliunas, which some read as Apollo, Troy's protector in the Iliad.
  • The Manapa-Tarhunta Letter reports a freebooter, Piyamaradu, attacking Wilusa and Lazpa (Lesbos).
  • The Tawagalawa Letter (Hattusili III, c. 1250) addresses the king of Ahhiyawa as "Great King" and "my brother", the rank of Egypt and Babylon, complains that Piyamaradu operates from Ahhiyawan Millawanda (Miletus), and calls "the matter of Wilusa over which we were at enmity" settled. Tawagalawa, the king's brother, is plausibly Etewoklewes, Eteokles.
  • The Shaushgamuwa Treaty (Tudhaliya IV, c. 1220) lists the Great Kings and then erases Ahhiyawa, suggesting its standing had collapsed.

The implication is modest but real: a Greek-speaking kingdom treated as an equal by the Hittite king, holding Miletus, active on the Anatolian coast, and at some point in the thirteenth century in dispute with the Hittites over Wilusa. It does not supply a siege or a ten-year expedition.

Where sources disagree. Mycenae is the default capital of Ahhiyawa; Joachim Latacz argued for Thebes on the strength of its tablets and Near Eastern seals; earlier scholars favoured Rhodes. At Hisarlık, Troy VI fell c. 1300 to what Blegen judged an earthquake, Troy VIIa to fire c. 1180 with arrowheads and unburied bodies; which, if either, is "Homer's Troy" is open. The Korfmann–Kolb quarrel over the Lower Town is set out in module 10.10; here it is enough that a ditch and settlement outside the citadel are now accepted.

07Trade with Egypt and the Levant

Egyptian records know the Aegean as Keftiu (Crete), whose envoys carry Minoan-style vessels in the Theban tombs of Senenmut and Rekhmire, and Tanaja (Danaoi?), whose towns appear on a statue base of Amenhotep III at Kom el-Hetan (c. 1380): the names have been read as Mycenae, Nauplion, Kythera, Messenia and Thebes alongside Knossos, Amnisos and Phaistos, like the itinerary of a diplomatic voyage. Faience plaques stamped with Amenhotep III's name have been found at Mycenae. In return, LH IIIA2 pottery turns up at Amarna, at Ugarit, Tell Abu Hawam and Beth Shan, and across Cyprus, where local imitations followed.

The Uluburun shipwreck off Kaş (c. 1320) shows the mechanism: ten tonnes of Cypriot copper ingots and a tonne of tin, glass ingots, Canaanite jars of terebinth resin, ebony, ivory, Baltic amber, a gold scarab of Nefertiti and, among the personal possessions, Mycenaean swords and seals, probably those of two Mycenaean envoys travelling west on a Levantine ship. The Kadmeia at Thebes yielded Kassite Babylonian lapis lazuli cylinder seals, and Linear B records men called a3-ku-pi-ti-jo (Egyptian) and ku-pi-ri-jo (Cypriot). The exchange was asymmetric: Mycenaeans needed copper and tin, which they lacked entirely, and exported perfumed oil in stirrup jars, textiles, slaves and perhaps mercenaries. Whether this was royal gift-exchange, private enterprise or both is unresolved; the tablets never mention foreign trade.

08How much Homer remembers

The Iliad and Odyssey took their shape in the eighth century, four hundred years after the palaces burned, in a society of small chiefdoms and iron tools. The question is what survived the gap in the oral tradition.

Genuine Bronze Age memories:

  • The boar's-tusk helmet Meriones lends Odysseus (Iliad 10.261–65), leather with rows of sliced tusk, is exactly the helmet from the shaft graves, Dendra and the Pylos and Thera frescoes, obsolete by the twelfth century. No poet after 1100 could have seen one.
  • Nestor's cup (Iliad 11.632–37), heavy, gold-studded, four-handled with two doves at each handle, resembles the gold cup from Shaft Grave IV with two dove-topped handles. The match is inexact, but the type is Bronze Age.
  • Bronze weaponry throughout; silver-studded swords; the tower shield of Ajax and the body-covering shield that strikes Hector's ankles and neck (Iliad 6.117), a sixteenth-century form.
  • The title anax, the god-list, Eileithyia's cave at Amnisos, and the Catalogue of Ships (Iliad 2.494–759), whose geography ignores Ionia and names places such as Pylos, Dorion and Eutresis that mattered in the Bronze Age and not later.
  • Names: Achilleus, Hektor and Alexandra all occur in Linear B, so the onomastic stock is old.

Iron Age reality or invention:

  • Universal cremation; iron as a familiar metal; paired throwing spears rather than the single thrusting spear; chariots used as battlefield taxis by men who dismount to fight, because the poets no longer knew how chariotry worked.
  • Basileus as "king", Phoenician traders, a temple of Athena at Troy, the hero's house with a hearth-room rather than a palace with archive and workshops, and a politics of assemblies and quarrelsome peers rather than the wanax's scribal bureaucracy. Nothing in Homer suggests writing or taxation.
  • Agamemnon's Mycenae is a petty kingship; the real Mycenae counted sheep by the tens of thousands.
Where sources disagree. Moses Finley (The World of Odysseus, 1954) held that Homer's society is essentially the tenth and ninth centuries with a Bronze Age veneer. Anthony Snodgrass called it an amalgam matching no single period. Susan Sherratt (1990) proposed layers that entered the tradition at different dates and froze. Latacz treats the Catalogue and the Hittite texts as evidence of a real coalition war; most Anglophone scholars think that exceeds the evidence. The honest answer is that Homer remembers objects, names, places and a few institutions, and invents or updates almost everything about how people lived and fought.

09Self-test

  1. Which ancient author led Schliemann to dig inside the walls of Mycenae, and what did he find?
    Answer

    Pausanias 2.16.6, who placed the royal graves inside the walls. Schliemann found Grave Circle A with its gold-rich shaft graves of c. 1600–1450 BC.

  2. Why can the shaft-grave burials not be Agamemnon and his companions?
    Answer

    They date to Late Helladic I–IIA, three to four centuries before any plausible Trojan War horizon.

  3. Describe the megaron.
    Answer

    Porch, vestibule and main hall with a large central hearth ringed by four columns, a throne on the right wall and painted decoration; the core of every Mycenaean palace.

  4. Who, besides Ventris, made the decipherment of Linear B possible, and what did Pylos Ta 641 prove?
    Answer

    Alice Kober's grids of inflected sign groups and John Chadwick's collaboration. Ta 641 showed the word ti-ri-po-de beside a drawn tripod, confirming the readings were Greek.

  5. Rank the wanax, the lawagetas and the basileus as the tablets show them.
    Answer

    The wanax is supreme with the largest estate; the lawagetas second with one-third of that; the qa-si-re-u is a mere local headman, often over bronzesmiths.

  6. Name five Olympian gods attested by name in Linear B and two who are absent.
    Answer

    Attested: Zeus, Hera, Poseidon, Hermes, Artemis (also Ares, Dionysus). Absent: Apollo (unless Paiawon counts) and Aphrodite.

  7. What did the Pylos palace mainly produce and administer?
    Answer

    Textiles and flax, with women workers rationed and counted; also bronze, chariots, land tenure and offerings to the gods.

  8. What is the Tawagalawa Letter and what does it say about Wilusa?
    Answer

    A letter of Hattusili III (c. 1250) to the king of Ahhiyawa, addressed as Great King and brother, mentioning Piyamaradu's raids from Miletus and a past dispute over Wilusa now settled.

  9. What does the Uluburun wreck tell us about Mycenaean trade?
    Answer

    Bulk copper and tin, glass, resin and luxury goods moved westward on a Levantine ship c. 1320, apparently with Mycenaean passengers; the Aegean imported metals and exotica and exported oil, cloth and people.

  10. Give one object Homer remembers accurately from the Bronze Age and one practice he gets from his own time.
    Answer

    The boar's-tusk helmet (Iliad 10.261–65) is genuinely Mycenaean; universal cremation is an Iron Age practice.

10Carry forward

  1. The Mycenaean world was a literate, bureaucratic palace economy ruled by a wanax, and it ended c. 1200–1180 BC; everything in later Greece, including Homer, grew up after a four-century gap in which writing, palaces and palace kingship were lost.
  2. Most of the Olympian pantheon, Zeus, Hera, Poseidon, Hermes, Artemis, Ares, Dionysus, was already worshipped by name in the Bronze Age, so Greek religion has a continuous spine even where myth and cult practice changed.
  3. Hittite texts prove an Achaean great power active on the Anatolian coast and in dispute over Wilusa/Ilios; they make a Trojan war plausible as a type of event without confirming the one Homer tells, and that distinction governs how to read every later "historical" use of myth.
