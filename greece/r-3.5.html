The founding: Cadmus, Europa and the Sown Men

The Theban saga begins outside Greece. Europa, a princess of Tyre, is carried off by Zeus in the form of a bull to Crete, where she bears Minos, Rhadamanthys and Sarpedon (Hesiod, Catalogue of Women fr. 140–141 M-W; Ovid, Metamorphoses 2.833–875; Apollodorus, Library 3.1.1). Her brother Cadmus is sent to find her with orders not to return without her. He never does; the search is what moves a Phoenician house into Boeotia.

Where sources disagree. Europa's father is Agenor in Apollodorus and most later writers, but Homer (Iliad 14.321–322) and Hesiod make her the daughter of Phoenix. Later genealogists reconciled the two by making Phoenix a son of Agenor. Herodotus (1.2) treats the abduction as a historical Cretan raid.

Delphi tells Cadmus to abandon the search, follow a cow and found a city where she lies down (Pausanias 9.12.1–2; Apollodorus 3.4.1). She halts at the site of Thebes. Companions sent for water to the spring of Ares are killed by the dragon guarding it; Cadmus kills the dragon and, on Athena's instruction, sows its teeth. Armed men, the Spartoi, spring up and fight until five survive: Echion, Oudaios, Chthonios, Hyperenor and Pelorus (Apollodorus 3.4.1; Euripides, Phoenician Women 657–675, 931–944; Ovid, Met. 3.1–130). They are the Theban aristocracy: Echion will marry Agave and father Pentheus; Creon's line will be reckoned descended from the Spartoi. Because the dragon was sacred to Ares, Cadmus serves the god for a "great year" and is then given Harmonia, daughter of Ares and Aphrodite, with the gods attending the wedding (Hesiod, Theogony 937, 975–978; Apollodorus 3.4.2).

Among the gifts is a necklace made by Hephaestus; Apollodorus (3.4.2) adds a variant in which Zeus had given it to Europa and Cadmus passed it on. Statius (Thebaid 2.265–305) makes it a thing forged in malice. It will bribe Eriphyle, kill Alcmaeon and end in the Delphic treasury. Herodotus (5.58–59) credits Cadmus with bringing letters to Greece and claims to have seen "Cadmeian" script on tripods in the Theban Ismenion. The children are Semele, Agave, Autonoe, Ino and Polydorus. In old age Cadmus and Harmonia go to Illyria and are turned into serpents (Euripides, Bacchae 1330–1339; Ovid, Met. 4.563–603).

The daughters: Semele, Pentheus, Actaeon

Three of Cadmus's daughters lose a son, and the deaths are the foundation myths of Dionysiac Thebes. Semele, loved by Zeus, is tricked by Hera into asking him to come as he comes to his wife; the thunderbolt kills her, and Zeus sews the unborn child into his thigh until term (Hesiod, Theogony 940–942; Euripides, Bacchae 1–42, 88–104; Ovid, Met. 3.253–315). Her sisters said she had lied about a divine lover, and that slander is the reason Dionysus comes to Thebes (Bacchae 26–31).

Where sources disagree. Homeric Hymn 1 (1–9) explicitly rejects Thebes as the birthplace in favour of Nysa. Diodorus (3.62–64) distinguishes an older Dionysus from the Theban one. Tragedy made the Theban version canonical.

Pentheus, son of Agave and Echion, rules while the aged Cadmus stands aside. In Euripides's Bacchae (405 BC) he denies the god, imprisons the stranger, is lured into maenad dress to spy on the women of Cithaeron, is perched in a fir and torn apart by his mother and aunts, who take him for a lion; Agave carries the head home and Cadmus leads her into recognition (1216–1300). Aeschylus wrote a lost Pentheus; Theocritus (Idyll 26) and Ovid (Met. 3.511–733) retell it. Apollodorus (3.5.5) says Labdacus later died "in the same way" for the same impiety, giving the resistance to Dionysus a dynastic pattern.

Actaeon, son of Autonoe, is eaten by his own hounds. In the version that became standard he sees Artemis bathing and is turned into a stag (Callimachus, Hymn 5.107–118; Ovid, Met. 3.138–252), but that is not the oldest motive.

Where sources disagree. Apollodorus (3.4.4) says Acusilaus blamed Zeus's anger at Actaeon's courting of Semele and calls the bathing story merely "the more common". Stesichorus (in Pausanias 9.2.3) had Artemis wrap him in a deerskin to stop him marrying Semele. Euripides (Bacchae 337–340) has him boast of outhunting the goddess. The voyeur motif owes its prominence to Hellenistic poetry.

The second founding: Amphion, Zethus and Niobe

Polydorus dies while Labdacus is a child; Nycteus and then his brother Lycus rule as regents. Nycteus's daughter Antiope is impregnated by Zeus and bears twins on Cithaeron who are exposed and raised by a herdsman. Lycus and his wife Dirce keep Antiope a slave until she escapes and finds her sons, who kill Lycus and tie Dirce to a bull; the spring where she dies carries her name (Apollodorus 3.5.5; Pausanias 9.5.5–9; Hyginus, Fabulae 7–8; Euripides's lost Antiope). The brothers wall the city, Zethus carrying stones, Amphion moving them with the lyre Hermes gave him.

Where sources disagree. Homer makes the twins the founders: Antiope's sons "first founded the seat of seven-gated Thebes" (Odyssey 11.260–265), which cannot be squared with Cadmus. Pausanias (9.5.6) gave Cadmus the acropolis and the twins the lower town. Modern scholars see two independent foundation legends spliced, with the Labdacids written out of the city for exactly the interval the twins rule.

Amphion marries Niobe, daughter of Tantalus, who boasts herself more worthy of worship than Leto; Apollo and Artemis kill the children in a day. Homer is the first witness (Iliad 24.602–617): six sons and six daughters lie nine days unburied, and Niobe is turned to stone on Sipylus. Aeschylus and Sophocles each wrote a lost Niobe; Ovid gives the fullest version (Met. 6.146–312). Apollodorus (3.5.6) records the disagreement over numbers himself: Homer six and six, Hesiod ten and ten, Herodorus two and three, his own source seven and seven, with one daughter, Chloris, surviving to marry Neleus. Amphion dies by Apollo's arrows (Hyginus 9) or his own hand (Ovid 6.271); Zethus dies of grief (Pausanias 9.5.9). The throne reverts to Laius, who had been sheltered in exile by Pelops.

Laius and Chrysippus: two origins for the curse

The curse on the house has two rival explanations, and the choice determines the moral shape of everything that follows. The first is Pelopid: as Pelops's guest, Laius falls in love with his son Chrysippus and abducts him in his chariot; the boy dies, by suicide or killed by his half-brothers Atreus and Thyestes, and Pelops curses Laius never to have a son, or to die by him if he does (Hyginus 85; Apollodorus 3.5.5; schol. Euripides, Phoenician Women 1760, citing "Peisander", where Hera sends the Sphinx to punish Thebes for not avenging the crime). Euripides wrote a lost Chrysippus, and Plato (Laws 836c) and Athenaeus (13.602f) treat Laius as the inventor of pederasty.

The second is Apolline. In Aeschylus, Seven against Thebes 742–757, Apollo "thrice" told Laius at Delphi that he would save the city only by dying childless, and Laius, "mastered by folly", begot Oedipus anyway. No Pelops is needed; the fault is disobedience.

Where sources disagree. Whether Chrysippus is pre-Euripidean is contested. Lloyd-Jones argued that Aeschylus's lost Laius (467 BC) used the abduction and that the "ancient transgression" of Seven 742 refers to it; Hutchinson and most recent commentators find no trace of Chrysippus before Euripides. Sophocles's Oedipus Tyrannus is silent on both: the oracle to Laius there (711–714) is a prediction, not a prohibition.

Oedipus

The epic Oedipus is not the tragic one. In the Odyssey (11.271–280) his mother, called Epicaste, married him in ignorance; the gods made it known "at once"; she hanged herself and he went on ruling Thebes "in woes". The Iliad (23.679–680) knows funeral games for Oedipus at Thebes, so he died there. Hesiod (Works and Days 162–163) knows a war at Thebes "for the flocks of Oedipus". Nothing about blinding or exile. The lost Oedipodea (6,600 lines, attributed to Cinaethon) focused on the Sphinx and the marriage; its one secure fragment says the Sphinx killed Haemon, son of Creon, "most beautiful" of the Thebans, and Pausanias (9.5.10–11) reports that in it the four children were born of a second wife, Euryganeia. Pherecydes gave Oedipus three wives in sequence.

Where sources disagree. Epic denied that Eteocles, Polynices, Antigone and Ismene were children of incest. The incestuous brood is secure first in Aeschylus (Seven 752–757, the "field" that bore a "bloody root") and dominates Sophocles and Euripides. Whether Aeschylus invented it is uncertain.

The tragic plot is Sophocles, Oedipus Tyrannus (c. 429 BC), with Apollodorus 3.5.7–9 filling gaps. Laius has the infant's ankles pierced and pinned (OT 718, 1034) and exposed on Cithaeron; the Theban herdsman passes him to a Corinthian shepherd, and the childless Polybus and Merope raise him. Taunted as a bastard, he goes to Delphi and hears he will kill his father and marry his mother. Avoiding Corinth, he meets Laius where three roads meet in Phocis (OT 733–734; Pausanias 10.5.3–4 saw the graves at the Cleft Way) and kills him and all but one attendant in a quarrel over the road. The Sphinx, offspring of Echidna (Hesiod, Theogony 326–327, where she is "Phix"), kills Thebans who fail her riddle of the creature with one voice and two, three and four feet; Oedipus answers "man" and she leaps from the rock (Apollodorus 3.5.8). Creon has offered throne and widow to the liberator.

Sophocles then builds the discovery as an investigation of his own design: plague, Tiresias's refusal and accusation (316–462), Jocasta's attempt to discredit oracles that only tightens the net (707–770), the Corinthian messenger revealing the adoption, the herdsman forced to speak. Jocasta hangs herself; Oedipus blinds himself with her brooches (1268–1279). He asks for exile; the play ends with Creon undecided. In Oedipus at Colonus (401 BC) the blind man, led by Antigone, reaches the grove of the Eumenides at the poet's own deme. Creon tries to seize him; Polynices begs a blessing and receives the curse that the brothers will die by each other's hands (1370–1396); summoned by thunder, Oedipus goes into the grove, and only Theseus knows the grave that will protect Athens against Thebes (1518–1534).

Where sources disagree. Homer implies burial at Thebes. Lysimachus (schol. OC 91) placed the grave at Eteonos in Boeotia after Thebes refused the body. Pausanias (1.28.7) saw a tomb on the Areopagus and was told the bones came from Thebes. Colonus is an Athenian claim, probably Sophocles's own shaping of a local cult.

Eteocles, Polynices and the Seven

The sons' quarrel has its own curse. The Thebais (fr. 2–3 West) told how Polynices set before his blind father the silver table and gold cup of Cadmus which Laius had used, and Oedipus cursed the sons to divide their inheritance "with the sword"; and how they later sent him the haunch of the sacrifice instead of the shoulder, and he prayed they die at each other's hands. Euripides (Phoen. 63–68) simplifies: the sons locked their father away, and he cursed them. They agree to rule in alternate years; Eteocles refuses to yield; Polynices goes to Argos (Apollodorus 3.6.1). A Stesichorus fragment (P. Lille 76) shows a sixth-century version in which the queen, still alive, proposes that one son keep the kingdom and the other take the flocks and gold, after Tiresias has foretold the fratricide.

At Argos Polynices and Tydeus, exiled from Calydon, brawl at Adrastus's door; he recalls an oracle to yoke his daughters to a boar and a lion, recognises the beasts on their shields, and marries Argeia to Polynices and Deipyle to Tydeus (Euripides, Suppliants 131–150). The seer Amphiaraus, married to Adrastus's sister Eriphyle, foresees that every leader but Adrastus will die and refuses to go; bound by oath to let Eriphyle settle disputes with Adrastus, he is undone when Polynices bribes her with Harmonia's necklace. He goes, charging his sons Alcmaeon and Amphilochus to kill their mother and march on Thebes (Apollodorus 3.6.2; Pindar, Nemean 9.13–27; Odyssey 15.244–247 already knows he died "because of a woman's gifts"). At Nemea the infant Opheltes dies while his nurse Hypsipyle shows the army water; Amphiaraus renames him Archemorus, "beginner of doom", and the Nemean games are founded (Bacchylides 9.10–24; Euripides, Hypsipyle). Tydeus goes ahead as envoy and kills all but one of the fifty Thebans sent to ambush him (Iliad 4.382–398).

Where sources disagree. The list of Seven is unstable. Aeschylus (Seven 375–652), Sophocles (OC 1313–1325) and Euripides's Suppliants put Eteoclus at a gate and keep Adrastus out of the seven; Phoenician Women (1104–1140) includes Adrastus and omits Eteoclus. Apollodorus (3.6.3) notes that "some" exclude Tydeus and Polynices as non-Argives and count Eteoclus and Mecisteus.

In Euripides, Tiresias declares that only the death of a pure-born Spartos can save the city, and Creon's son Menoeceus kills himself on the walls against his father's wish (Phoen. 911–1018); no earlier source knows this, and it is probably Euripides's invention on the model of Macaria in his Children of Heracles. The assault fails at every gate; the brothers meet in single combat and kill each other, and in Euripides Jocasta stabs herself over the bodies (1427–1459).

Antigone and Creon

Creon takes power and, in Sophocles's Antigone (c. 442 BC), decrees that Polynices lie unburied on pain of death. Antigone buries him and, caught, defends the unwritten laws of the gods against the city's decree (450–470). Ismene refuses to help and is later refused a share of the guilt. Haemon, Antigone's betrothed, argues with his father and fails; she is walled into a rock tomb. Tiresias warns Creon of pollution; he relents too late, finds Antigone hanged and Haemon dead by his own sword, and his wife Eurydice kills herself cursing him (1192–1316).

Where sources disagree. This ending is Sophoclean, not traditional. In Euripides's lost Antigone (per the ancient hypothesis to Sophocles's play) she is married to Haemon and bears a son, Maeon. In Phoenician Women 1627–1766 she refuses Haemon and goes into exile with her father. Euripides's Suppliants and the Athenian funeral orations (Herodotus 9.27.3; Lysias 2.7–10) say Theseus recovered the Argive dead and buried them at Eleusis; Pindar (Olympian 6.15) and Pausanias (9.18.3) have seven pyres at Thebes. The Antigone scene at the end of Aeschylus's Seven (1005–1078) is regarded by most editors as a fourth-century addition made under Sophocles's influence.

The Epigoni, the sack and the necklace

Ten years later the sons of the Seven return. Apollodorus (3.7.2) names Alcmaeon and Amphilochus, Aegialeus son of Adrastus, Diomedes son of Tydeus, Promachus son of Parthenopaeus, Sthenelus son of Capaneus, Thersander son of Polynices and Euryalus son of Mecisteus. Delphi promises victory if Alcmaeon leads; he agrees only after Eriphyle, bribed a second time by Thersander with Harmonia's robe, presses him (3.7.5). At Glisas the Theban king Laodamas, son of Eteocles, kills Aegialeus and falls to Alcmaeon. Tiresias advises the Thebans to parley while they slip away by night, and dies on the road drinking from the spring Tilphussa (Apollodorus 3.7.3; Pausanias 9.33.1). The Epigoni sack the empty city, send Tiresias's daughter Manto to Delphi as first-fruits, and install Thersander. Adrastus dies of grief at Megara. Sthenelus's boast in the Iliad (4.405–410), "we took the seat of seven-gated Thebes", is the oldest allusion.

Where sources disagree. Herodotus (5.61) and Pausanias (9.5.13) have Laodamas survive and lead the Thebans to Illyria. Herodotus (4.32) doubted Homer wrote the Epigoni, which was also credited to Antimachus of Teos.

Alcmaeon kills Eriphyle as ordered and is driven mad by her Erinys. Purified by Phegeus at Psophis, he marries Phegeus's daughter Arsinoe (Alphesiboea in Pausanias 8.24.8) and gives her the necklace and robe. The land withers; Delphi sends him to ground that did not exist when his mother cursed him, the new silt at the mouth of the Achelous, where he marries the river's daughter Callirhoe. She demands the heirlooms; Alcmaeon tells Phegeus he must dedicate them at Delphi to be cured, a servant betrays the lie, and Phegeus's sons ambush and kill him (Apollodorus 3.7.5–6; Pausanias 8.24.8–10). Callirhoe prays that her infant sons Acarnan and Amphoterus grow up in a day; they kill Phegeus and his sons and dedicate necklace and robe at Delphi. Ephorus (in Athenaeus 6.232d–e) closes the circuit: in the Third Sacred War the Phocian Phayllus plundered the necklace for his mistress, who went mad, and whose son burnt the house down. Long after Harmonia's wedding the necklace was still destroying its owners.

The lost epics and the three tragedians

Three Theban epics circulated under Homer's name or those of shadowy early poets. The Oedipodea (6,600 lines) covered the Sphinx and the marriage; the Thebais (7,000 lines), opening "Sing, goddess, of thirsty Argos", the first war, and Pausanias (9.9.5) ranks it just after the Iliad and Odyssey; the Epigoni (7,000 lines), opening "Now, Muses, let us begin on the younger men", the second; an Alcmaeonis followed. About two dozen lines survive from all of them. They establish that the epic Oedipus kept his throne and remarried, that the curse on the sons was provoked by concrete dishonour (the cup, the haunch), and that Adrastus's horse, Tydeus's cannibalism and Amphiaraus's descent were fixed before tragedy.

Aeschylus, Seven against Thebes (467 BC), third play of a connected trilogy (Laius, Oedipus, Seven, with the satyr-play Sphinx), compresses the war into one day inside the walls. Polynices never appears. The centrepiece is the Scout's description of each Argive champion's shield, answered by Eteocles's assignment of a defender (375–676), until the seventh gate pairs him with his brother and he chooses to go. Aeschylus's shaping is structural: an Erinys working through three generations, Eteocles as sympathetic defender rather than usurper, and the Delphic prohibition rather than Chrysippus as root fault. The original ending was almost certainly the lament over the two corpses.

Sophocles wrote his three Theban plays across forty years and never as a trilogy; they contradict one another. In Antigone Ismene says Oedipus died "hated and infamous" (49–52); in Colonus he dies a blessed hero of Attica. Creon is humane in Oedipus Tyrannus, a tyrant in Antigone, a thug in Colonus. Sophocles's lasting contributions are the self-blinding with the brooches, the plague and investigative structure, the Corinthian upbringing, the exile and the Athenian death. The Tyrannus is also the play that made incestuous parentage the centre of the story rather than a variant.

Euripides, Phoenician Women (c. 409 BC), is deliberately encyclopaedic. Jocasta is alive and speaks the prologue; blind Oedipus is shut inside the palace; Antigone views the Argive army from the roof in a scene modelled on the Iliad's view from the walls (88–201); the brothers meet under truce and fail to agree (446–637), Eteocles declaring he would go to the stars for Tyranny; Menoeceus dies for the city; Jocasta dies over her sons; Creon issues the edict and the exile order; Antigone chooses her father over Haemon. The chorus is Phoenician, kin of Cadmus, which lets the play open on the whole Tyrian prehistory. The ancient hypothesis already called it "overfull", and the authenticity of much of the exodos after 1582 is disputed: Fraenkel excised heavily, Mastronarde retains most.

Where sources disagree. How much of the canonical Oedipus is Sophoclean invention is uncertain. Some argue the self-blinding and exile were already in Aeschylus's lost Oedipus, reading the "twin pains" of Seven 782–783 as the eyes; others find that phrase too ambiguous to carry the weight. Nothing before 467 BC shows Oedipus blind.

Self-test

  1. Why does Cadmus come to Boeotia, and what does Delphi tell him to do instead?
    Answer

    He is searching for his abducted sister Europa under orders not to return without her. Delphi tells him to abandon the search, follow a cow and found a city where she lies down (Pausanias 9.12.1–2; Apollodorus 3.4.1).

  2. Who are the Spartoi, and which later figures descend from them?
    Answer

    The armed men who sprang from the sown dragon's teeth; five survived. Echion fathered Pentheus by Agave, and Creon's line, including Menoeceus and Haemon, was counted descended from the Spartoi, which is why Tiresias demands a Spartos in Phoenician Women.

  3. Trace the necklace of Harmonia from its making to its last recorded owner.
    Answer

    Made by Hephaestus; given by Cadmus (or Zeus via Europa) to Harmonia; used by Polynices to bribe Eriphyle; given by Alcmaeon to Arsinoe; sought back by deceit for Callirhoe; dedicated at Delphi by Acarnan and Amphoterus; plundered by the Phocian Phayllus for his mistress, who went mad (Ephorus in Athenaeus 6.232d–e).

  4. Give three different motives for Actaeon's death and a source for each.
    Answer

    He courted Semele and angered Zeus (Acusilaus in Apollodorus 3.4.4; cf. Stesichorus in Pausanias 9.2.3); he boasted of outhunting Artemis (Euripides, Bacchae 337–340); he saw Artemis bathing (Callimachus, Hymn 5; Ovid, Met. 3).

  5. How does Homer's account of Amphion and Zethus conflict with the Cadmus story, and how did Pausanias reconcile them?
    Answer

    Odyssey 11.260–265 calls the twins the first founders of Thebes. Pausanias 9.5.6 assigned Cadmus the acropolis and the twins the lower city.

  6. State the two rival explanations for the curse on Laius's house and name a source for each.
    Answer

    Pelops's curse after Laius abducted Chrysippus (Euripides's lost Chrysippus; schol. Phoen. 1760; Hyginus 85); Laius's disobedience of Apollo's thrice-repeated command to die childless (Aeschylus, Seven 742–757).

  7. In what three ways does the epic Oedipus differ from the Sophoclean one?
    Answer

    He keeps ruling Thebes and dies there (funeral games, Iliad 23.679); he is not said to be blinded; his four children are by a second wife, Euryganeia, not by his mother (Oedipodea, via Pausanias 9.5.10–11).

  8. Which champion does Aeschylus place at each gate, and whom does he omit who appears in Phoenician Women?
    Answer

    Tydeus (Proetid), Capaneus (Electran), Eteoclus (Neistan), Hippomedon (Onca), Parthenopaeus (Northern), Amphiaraus (Homoloid), Polynices (Seventh). He omits Adrastus, whom Euripides includes in place of Eteoclus.

  9. Why does Athena withhold immortality from Tydeus, and how does Amphiaraus die?
    Answer

    Tydeus, dying, split open Melanippus's head and ate his brains, so Athena withheld the immortality she was bringing (Apollodorus 3.6.8; Thebais fr. 9). Amphiaraus, fleeing Periclymenus, was swallowed alive with his chariot when Zeus split the earth (Pindar, Nemean 9.24–27).

  10. Give two points on which Sophocles's three Theban plays are inconsistent.
    Answer

    Oedipus dies disgraced (Antigone 49–52) but blessed at Colonus; Creon is reasonable in Oedipus Tyrannus, tyrannical in Antigone, violent in Colonus. They were written over forty years and never planned as a trilogy.

Carry forward

  1. Epic and tragedy are different layers: the epic Oedipus rules on, remarries and dies at home; the blinding, exile, incestuous children and Athenian death are fifth-century shaping, much of it Sophoclean. Always ask which layer a detail belongs to.
  2. A house curse needs a mechanism, and the mechanism is an object or a vow: Harmonia's necklace, Cadmus's cup, the oath to Eriphyle, the Delphic prohibition. Follow the object and you follow the curse; the same pattern governs the Pelopids and the Trojan cycle.
  3. Thebes is the city Athenian drama uses to think about itself from outside: autochthony gone wrong (the Spartoi), tyranny against law (Creon and Antigone), state against family. Theseus's role in Colonus and Suppliants shows Theban myth being rewritten as Athenian political claim; read every later "Athenian" ending with that in mind.

Cover: Cadmus, Europa, the dragon's teeth and the Spartoi, Harmonia and the necklace; Semele and the birth of Dionysus; Pentheus and the Bacchae; Actaeon; Amphion and Zethus, Niobe; Laius, Chrysippus and the origin of the curse; Oedipus (the exposure, Corinth, the oracle, the crossroads, the Sphinx, Jocasta, the discovery, the blinding, Colonus); Eteocles and Polynices, the Seven against Thebes by name with their fates, Amphiaraus; Antigone and Creon; the Epigoni and the sack of Thebes; and Alcmaeon and the necklace. Treat the lost epics (Oedipodea, Thebais, Epigoni) and show how Aeschylus' Seven, Sophocles' three Theban plays and Euripides' Phoenician Women each reshape the material. Format: about 3000 words, headed sections, name sources, flag disagreements, end with a 10-question self-test with answers and the three ideas I must carry forward.
