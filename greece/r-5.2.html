What a polis was

The word polis is usually translated "city-state", and the translation misleads in both halves. A polis was not primarily a built-up place and not quite a state. It was a community of citizens (politai) who together owned a territory (chora) with a settlement at its centre, governed themselves through shared laws and offices, and worshipped shared gods. Thucydides puts it in the mouth of Nicias to the beaten Athenians in Sicily:

men are the city, not walls or empty ships.
(Thucydides 7.77.7).

The physical polis nonetheless had recognisable parts. The acropolis, the fortified height, was the oldest element and often the Bronze Age core; in the Archaic period it generally shed its palace function and became the seat of the principal cult, as at Athens. The agora, the open gathering place, was the real innovation: a space that belonged to no one household and where the assembly met, justice was done and trade happened. Homer already knows it. The Phaeacian city in the Odyssey has walls, harbours, temples and an agora paved with set stones (Homer, Odyssey 6.262–272), and the shield of Achilles shows elders judging a blood-price case in the agora (Iliad 18.497–508).

Laws were the third element. The earliest surviving polis law is from Dreros in Crete, inscribed around 650–600 BC, limiting the chief magistrate (kosmos) to one term in ten years and binding the whole citizen body to the rule. Named lawgivers (Zaleucus of Locri, Charondas of Catana, Draco and Solon) mark the period, and two of the earliest are from western colonies. Cults completed the structure: a polis had a tutelary deity, civic festivals, and a sacred calendar, and the great stone temples that begin around 700 (Isthmia, Samos, Corinth) are the first monumental architecture of the Greek world since the Bronze Age.

A polis differed from a mere city in that the people remained the polis wherever they were. When the Phocaeans sailed away from Persian rule in 545 the polis went with them (Herodotus 1.164–165); when the Athenians evacuated Attica in 480 the polis was on the ships at Salamis. A city is a place; a polis is a membership. Most were tiny: Mogens Hansen's Copenhagen Polis Centre catalogued 1,035 poleis, most with territories under 100 square kilometres.

Where sources disagree. When did the polis emerge? Ehrenberg argued for a sudden crystallisation around 800–700; Snodgrass tied it to the eighth-century population surge, Morris to a change in who received formal burial (the argument is in module 5.1). The current consensus is a long process, 900–600, with no single birth moment.

Aristotle's definition

Aristotle's Politics opens by deriving the polis genetically. Man and woman form the household (oikos); households form the village (kome); villages form the polis, which is the first community that is self-sufficient (autarkes) and which exists "for the sake of living well" (Politics 1252b27–1253a1). Hence the famous dictum that man is by nature a politikon zoon, a polis-dwelling animal, and that whoever lives outside a polis is either a beast or a god (1253a2–29). In Book 3 he defines the polis by the citizen rather than the territory: a citizen is one who shares in judgement and office (1275a22–23), and the polis is a sufficient multitude of such citizens (1275b20–21). The polis is not its walls: one could wall in the Peloponnese without making it a polis (1276a26–30).

Two cautions. Aristotle writes in the 330s, and his definition is normative and Athens-shaped. His household-village-polis sequence is logical, not historical, though it maps well onto the synoecism of villages archaeologists see in the 8th century.

Oikos, phratry, tribe and deme

Beneath the polis sat older units. The oikos was the household: the kin group, its land, livestock, dependants and slaves, under a male head. Hesiod's advice to acquire a house, a woman and a plough-ox first (Works and Days 405) shows the oikos as the unit of survival.

The phratry ("brotherhood") was a group of oikoi claiming common descent and sharing cults, especially of Zeus Phratrios and Athena Phratria. In Athens phratry membership was the practical proof of legitimate birth and hence citizenship. Homer's Nestor advises Agamemnon to marshal the army by tribes and phratries (Iliad 2.362–363), the earliest literary attestation of both.

The tribe (phyle) was the largest kin unit. Ionian poleis, Athens included, had four (Geleontes, Hopletes, Argadeis, Aigikoreis); Dorian poleis had three (Hylleis, Dymanes, Pamphyloi), and Tyrtaeus names them fighting as units at Sparta (Tyrtaeus fr. 19 West).

The deme was a village or ward, a territorial not a kin unit. Its moment came in 508/7, when Cleisthenes reorganised Athens into ten new tribes built from 139 demes drawn from three regions, deliberately cutting across the old kin loyalties (Herodotus 5.66–69; Aristotle, Constitution of the Athenians 21). The reform lies just after our period but is where the process was heading: the polis overriding the clan.

Where sources disagree. The older view (Fustel de Coulanges, and still in many textbooks) treated phratry and tribe as primordial clan structures the polis gradually dissolved. Denis Roussel (1976) and Félix Bourriot argued the reverse: the genos and phratry as we see them are products of the polis, formalised to regulate citizenship, not survivals from a tribal past.

Citizens, metics and slaves in outline

Citizenship in the Archaic polis normally required descent from a citizen father, legitimate birth and, often, a property or hoplite qualification for full participation. Solon in 594/3 graded Athenians into four property classes with different access to office (Aristotle, Constitution of the Athenians 7). Women transmitted citizen status but held no political rights.

Resident foreigners, metoikoi, were a product of the polis becoming attractive. Metics paid a tax, needed a citizen sponsor, could not own land, and served in the army. Slavery was ubiquitous and took two forms. Chattel slaves, bought and foreign, concentrated in households and workshops; Theopompus claimed Chios was the first Greek polis to buy barbarian slaves (FGrH 115 F122). Dependent populations such as the Spartan helots and Thessalian penestai were conquered peoples bound to the land. Solon's abolition of debt-bondage for Athenians (Constitution of the Athenians 6) marks the moment when the line between citizen and slave hardened: a citizen could no longer become one.

How a colony was founded

The Greek term was apoikia, "a home away", and the standard mechanics, as reconstructed from Herodotus, Thucydides and inscriptions, ran as follows. The mother-city (metropolis) decided, or a faction within it was pushed, to send out settlers. An oikistes (founder) was appointed, almost always an aristocrat, with near-absolute authority over the voyage and the first settlement; he laid out the site, divided the land into lots (kleroi), founded the cults, and on death was buried in the agora and worshipped as a hero.

Delphi's role was to sanction. Herodotus's account of Cyrene has the Theraeans consult Apollo repeatedly, ignore the first instruction, suffer seven years of drought, and finally comply (Herodotus 4.150–159). The oracle's authorisation gave the venture legitimacy and the oikist his mandate. Whether Delphi directed settlement or ratified choices already made is unresolvable; many foundation oracles were composed after the fact. Thucydides records that at Naxos in Sicily, the first Greek colony there, the settlers raised an altar to Apollo Archegetes ("Founder-Leader") on which all later sacred envoys from Sicily sacrificed before sailing to Greece (Thucydides 6.3.1).

Mother-city ties were real but loose. The colony took fire from the metropolis's hearth, its cults and calendar, its dialect and alphabet, and often its constitution. It was nonetheless an independent polis from day one. The 4th-century Cyrene foundation decree (SEG 9.3, Meiggs and Lewis 5), which claims to transcribe the original 7th-century Theran oath, specifies that one son per household was to be sent on pain of death, that settlers would retain Theran citizenship if the venture failed within five years, and curses those who broke the agreement. Corinth was unusual in trying to hold its colonies close (Thucydides 1.24–55).

Where sources disagree. Robin Osborne (1998) attacked the whole state-sponsored model as a 5th-century retrojection: the 8th-century ventures, he argued, were private, piecemeal and mixed, and only later did poleis invent oikists and foundation dates to own them. Malkin and others accept a messier reality but note that oikist cults are archaeologically early. Thucydides' Sicilian dates (6.3–5), probably from Antiochus of Syracuse, remain the backbone of the chronology, and the pottery broadly supports them.

The main waves and sites

The first move was Euboean. Chalcis and Eretria, trading at Al Mina in Syria from around 800, planted Pithecusae on Ischia in the Bay of Naples by about 770, a mixed emporium with metalworking and Phoenician and Etruscan finds. Cumae on the mainland opposite followed around 750 (Strabo 5.4.4; Livy 8.22.5–6), the first true Greek polis in Italy, and the one from which the Etruscans and ultimately the Romans took their alphabet.

Sicily and Italy. From 734 the Chalcidians and Corinthians divided eastern Sicily: Naxos, Leontini and Catana to Chalcis, Syracuse to Corinth (Thucydides 6.3–4). Syracuse became the greatest Greek city of the west and reduced the native Sicels to a serf class, the Kyllyrioi. On the Italian toe and instep, Achaean settlers from the northern Peloponnese founded Sybaris and Croton, both on rich coastal plains; Sybaris became a byword for luxury before Croton destroyed it in 510 (Herodotus 5.44–45). Taras (Tarentum) was Sparta's only colony, founded, the tradition says, by the Partheniai, sons born of irregular unions during the First Messenian War and denied full citizenship (Strabo 6.3.2, citing Antiochus and Ephorus). The story is a stasis story, and that is its point.

The Black Sea. Megara and Miletus led here. Megara founded Chalcedon around 685 and Byzantium opposite around 660; Herodotus has Megabazus call the Chalcedonians blind for missing the better site (Herodotus 4.144). Pliny credits Miletus with ninety Black Sea colonies (Natural History 5.112), an exaggeration, but Sinope, Olbia at the Bug-Dnieper mouth and Panticapaeum in the Crimea were real, trading grain, fish, slaves and metals for wine, oil and pottery. Herodotus describes the Scythian king Scyles, who kept a Greek house at Olbia and was killed for it (Herodotus 4.78–80).

Cyrene. Herodotus gives Theran and Cyrenaean versions (4.150–158): drought, the stammering oikist Battus, a failed first settlement on the island of Platea, then the mainland. Cyrene was ruled by Battiad kings for eight generations and grew rich on horses, grain and silphium.

Massalia. The Phocaeans founded Marseille around 600, beating the Carthaginians at sea to do it (Thucydides 1.13.6). Justin preserves the legend of the Gallic princess Gyptis choosing the Greek Protis as husband (Justin 43.3.8–12), a myth of consensual settlement. Massalia pushed wine, writing and coinage up the Rhône and founded Emporion in Spain.

Naucratis. This was not a colony but an emporion, a licensed trading port in the Nile Delta. Herodotus says Amasis (570–526) granted it to the Greeks, with a shared sanctuary, the Hellenion, funded by nine Ionian, Dorian and Aeolian cities, plus separate temples of Aegina, Samos and Miletus (Herodotus 2.178–179). Archaeology shows Greek presence from about 620, under Psammetichus I, so Amasis probably regularised what already existed.

Why they went

Three motives are given by the sources and all three were usually in play. The first was land. Stenochoria, "narrowness of land", is the term Greeks used, and the Cyrene story opens with drought and the Taras story with men who had none. Plato lists famine, overpopulation and civil strife as the standard causes of colonisation (Laws 708b, 740e). Archilochus, a settler on Thasos, complained that the island stood "like the backbone of an ass, crowned with wild wood" (Archilochus fr. 21 West), the voice of a man who went for land and found rock.

The second was trade. Al Mina and Pithecusae were emporia before they were poleis, placed for metals rather than fields; Massalia, Naucratis and the Black Sea sites were commercial in placement if not always in motive. The third was stasis, internal conflict. The Partheniai of Taras and the Phocaeans fleeing Persia are explicit cases; Theognis of Megara is full of the bitterness of displaced aristocrats. A colony exported the losers of a civil quarrel.

Where sources disagree. Older scholarship split colonies into "agrarian" and "commercial" types; Graham's Colony and Mother City (1964) stressed land hunger, Boardman's The Greeks Overseas the commercial side. The present view is that the dichotomy is false: land was the goal, but sites were chosen by people who already knew the sea lanes. Population pressure is easier to assert than to prove.

Colonisation and Greek identity

In 750 there was no word for "Greek" in the sense Herodotus would later use. Homer speaks of Achaeans, Argives and Danaans; Hellenes in the Iliad are one small people in Thessaly (Iliad 2.684). By 500 the term covered everyone from Massalia to the Crimea, and the catalyst was distance. Confronted with Sicels, Scythians and Etruscans, settlers discovered what they shared with the Megarian in the next bay: language, gods, the agora, the symposium. Herodotus's eventual definition of Greekness as common blood, language, shrines and sacrifices, and customs (Herodotus 8.144.2) is the end-point of a process that colonisation set going. The Panhellenic sanctuaries are the institutional expression: Olympia (traditionally from 776), Delphi, the Hellenion at Naucratis, the altar of Apollo Archegetes. Jonathan Hall argues that Hellenicity was first aggregative, built from linking genealogies, and only became oppositional after the Persian Wars.

Myth travelled with the settlers and was replanted. Odysseus's wanderings were pinned to the west: Scylla and Charybdis at the Straits of Messina, the Cyclopes in Sicily, Circe at Monte Circeo, the Phaeacians at Corcyra. Heracles was made to drive Geryon's cattle through Italy and Sicily, giving every city a founding visit from the hero. The Black Sea became the Argonauts' sea; Eumelus of Corinth's Corinthiaca gave Corinth a claim on Medea; Olbia worshipped Achilles as Pontarches on the island of Leuce (Arrian, Periplus 21–23); the Crimea became Iphigenia's Tauris. Cyrene received a nymph of that name, carried off by Apollo from Thessaly (Pindar, Pythian 9.5–70). Carol Dougherty has shown that foundation narratives follow a pattern (crisis, oracle, marriage or murder, new city) that made each colony's story legible to every other Greek.

Relations with the other seafarers were mixed. The Phoenicians were in the west first, at Carthage (traditionally 814), Motya in western Sicily, Gadir and Sardinia; Thucydides says they held headlands round Sicily and withdrew westwards when the Greeks arrived in numbers (Thucydides 6.2.6). Greeks took the alphabet from them and shared Pithecusae and Naucratis with them, but by the 6th century competed. The Etruscans were the Greeks' best customers for pottery and learned writing, the symposium and hoplite arms from Cumae; yet around 540–535 an Etruscan-Carthaginian fleet met the Phocaeans of Alalia in Corsica, and the Greeks, though victorious, had to abandon the island (Herodotus 1.166–167). By 550 the frontier had hardened into spheres: Greeks in eastern Sicily, southern Italy, the Black Sea and Provence; Phoenicians in the far west; Etruscans in the Tyrrhenian.

Where sources disagree. Was the Greek-native encounter colonial in the modern sense? The old model of Hellenisation, Greeks civilising passive natives, has given way to Malkin's "middle ground" and attention to intermarriage (the Gyptis story, mixed burials at Pithecusae). Critics reply that Syracuse enslaved the Sicels; violence was real. The archaeology supports both, in different places.

Self-test

  1. In what sense is "city-state" a misleading translation of polis?
    Answer

    A polis was a community of citizens, not a place: "men are the city" (Thucydides 7.77.7). It could move with its people, as the Phocaeans and the Athenians at Salamis showed, and most were far too small to be called states.

  2. How does Aristotle define a citizen, and where?
    Answer

    One who shares in judgement and office (Politics 1275a22–23); the polis is a sufficient body of such people.

  3. What is the earliest surviving polis law, and what does it do?
    Answer

    The Dreros law from Crete, c. 650–600 BC, forbidding the chief magistrate (kosmos) from holding office again within ten years.

  4. Distinguish phratry, tribe and deme.
    Answer

    Phratry: a kin-based brotherhood controlling proof of legitimate birth. Tribe (phyle): the largest kin unit, four in Ionian and three in Dorian cities. Deme: a territorial village or ward, made the basis of Athenian citizenship by Cleisthenes in 508/7.

  5. What were the duties of an oikistes?
    Answer

    Lead the expedition, choose and lay out the site, divide land into lots, found the cults; after death he was buried in the agora and received heroic cult.

  6. Which document preserves the terms of the Cyrene foundation, and what did it stipulate?
    Answer

    The 4th-century Cyrene foundation decree (SEG 9.3), claiming to quote the 7th-century Theran oath: one son per household must go; settlers could return with citizenship if the colony failed within five years; curses on breakers of the oath.

  7. Name the mother-cities of Syracuse, Taras, Byzantium, Olbia and Massalia.
    Answer

    Corinth, Sparta, Megara, Miletus, Phocaea.

  8. Why is Naucratis not a colony in the usual sense?
    Answer

    It was an emporion, a trading port in Egypt under Pharaonic control, shared by nine Greek cities with a common Hellenion; the Greeks had no territory or independent polis there (Herodotus 2.178–179).

  9. What does the Taras foundation story reveal about motives for colonisation?
    Answer

    The Partheniai were Spartans of irregular birth denied full citizenship: colonisation as a safety valve for stasis, exporting the losers of an internal dispute.

  10. What happened at Alalia, and why does it matter?
    Answer

    Around 540–535 an Etruscan-Carthaginian fleet fought the Phocaean settlers of Corsica (Herodotus 1.166); the Greeks won but had to leave. It marks the hardening of the western Mediterranean into Greek, Phoenician and Etruscan spheres.

Carry forward

  1. The polis is a body of citizens sharing laws, land and cults, not a walled town; every later argument about democracy, tyranny and citizenship in this course is an argument about who belongs to that body.
  2. Colonies were independent poleis from birth, authorised by Delphi and led by an oikist, and they doubled the Greek world between 750 and 550; the west and the Black Sea are where lawgivers, philosophers and tyrants will keep appearing.
  3. "Greekness" was forged at the edges: contact with Sicels, Scythians, Phoenicians and Etruscans produced the shared identity that Olympia, Delphi and Herodotus would later define, and myth was redrawn onto the new map to make it Greek.
