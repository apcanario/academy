01Protagoras: the measure tested in the Theaetetus

Module 6.5 gives Protagoras' three fragments (DK 80 B 1, B 4, B 6a) and the Great Speech of Plato's Protagoras, whose myth module 2.4 reads against Hesiod. Of the book one sentence survives. Plato calls it Truth (Theaetetus 161c); Sextus says the measure sentence opened the Overthrowing Arguments (Against the Mathematicians 7.60). Its word hōs can mean "that" or "how": man measures that things are, or how they are. The fullest interpretation is Plato's.

The Theaetetus takes it up because Theaetetus' definition, knowledge is perception (151e), is the same claim: as each thing appears to me, so it is for me, and the wind is cold for the man who shivers and not for the other (152a–c). To make perception infallible, Socrates attaches a "secret doctrine" that Protagoras supposedly told only his pupils (152c–e): nothing is anything in itself, all things come to be from motion, and perception and its object are twin offspring of meeting motions, so that no two perceptions share an object (156a–157c).

After jibes he withdraws as unfair (why not a pig as the measure; why charge fees if no one is wiser, 161c–e), Socrates composes a Defence of Protagoras (166a–168c), the most generous Sophist in Plato. The wise man replaces not false beliefs, since none are false, but worse appearances with better, as a doctor alters a sick man's taste with drugs; whatever seems just to a city is just for it while it holds to it (167c). Then the reversal (170a–171c): most people think Protagoras wrong; on his principle their belief is true for them; so he must grant his own doctrine false. Protagoras, Socrates jokes, would pop up from the ground to the neck to protest (171d). The decisive objection is the future (177c–179b): whether a thing will prove healthy or advantageous is judged better by doctor or legislator than by layman, so not everyone is a measure. Last, extreme flux would make all speech impossible (181c–183c). The Cratylus (385e–386a) compresses the case: if things are for each as they appear, no one is wiser or worse than another, so things must have a stable being of their own (386d–e).

Where sources disagree. Plato's Theaetetus relativises truth to each judge; Sextus (Against the Mathematicians 7.389–390, crediting the reversal to Democritus and Plato) and Aristotle (Metaphysics 4.5, 1009a6–15) read Protagoras as saying every appearance is simply true. Burnyeat (Philosophical Review, 1976) argued that the later tradition flattened Plato's relativist into an infallibilist, and that the reversal still catches a relativist who asserts his doctrine to others. Fine (1998) reads Plato's Protagoras as an infallibilist throughout, since the qualifiers Socrates drops at 171a–c would otherwise make the refutation a cheap win.

02Gorgias: On Not Being and the Helen

Gorgias' treatise survives in two paraphrases. Sextus (Against the Mathematicians 7.65–87; DK 82 B 3) titles it On Not Being, or On Nature and casts it as an attack on the criterion of truth. The pseudo-Aristotelian On Melissus, Xenophanes and Gorgias (MXG 979a11–980b21), badly transmitted, adds critical comment and says Gorgias borrowed from Melissus and Zeno (979b). Behind module 6.5's three theses lie these arguments.

  1. Nothing is. If anything is, it is what-is, or what-is-not, or both. What-is-not is not, and nothing can be both (7.75–76). What-is is eternal or generated: if eternal it has no beginning, so is unlimited, and the unlimited is nowhere; if generated, it came from what-is or what-is-not, and neither is possible (Sextus 7.66–76).
  2. If anything is, it cannot be known. If things thought were things that are, a chariot racing on the sea would exist because someone thinks of it; and Scylla and Chimaera, which are not, are thought (Sextus 7.79–80; MXG 980a9–12).
  3. If known, it cannot be communicated. Speech is not the things it signifies; as sight does not hear sounds, speech cannot convey colours (Sextus 7.83–86). The MXG adds that one thing cannot be in two minds at once (980b).

This is Eleatic logic turned on Eleatic conclusions: Parmenides (module 12.1) exhausted the alternatives to reach one changeless being; Gorgias exhausts them to reach none.

Where sources disagree. Isocrates (Helen 3) treats "nothing exists" as a thesis Gorgias dared to maintain. Heinrich Gomperz (1912) read the treatise as a rhetorical jest, a parody of Eleatic dialectic with no place in the history of philosophy; since Calogero (1932) most readers take it as a serious argument about being, thought and language. Which paraphrase is closer is also argued: Caston (2002) holds that Sextus recasts the work as epistemology and misreads its logical form, which the MXG keeps, while granting Sextus' versions of single arguments their value.

The Encomium of Helen (DK 82 B 11) is the other half. Helen went to Troy by the will of gods and Necessity, or by force, or persuaded by speech, or conquered by love (6); on each hypothesis she is blameless. Speech acts on the soul as drugs act on the body, some curing, some killing (14), so persuasion is a form of compulsion and the persuaded are as innocent as the forced. The speech ends by calling itself Gorgias' plaything (paignion, 21). Plutarch preserves the matching view of tragedy: a deception in which the deceiver is more just than the non-deceiver, and the deceived wiser than the undeceived (On the Glory of the Athenians 5, 348c; DK 82 B 23). Beside On Not Being the position is coherent: if speech does not convey things, it works by effects, and the orator makes states of soul. Plato's Gorgias attacks exactly this: rhetoric produces conviction without teaching (454e–455a), and Gorgias, pressed, concedes he will teach justice to a pupil who lacks it (460a).

03Prodicus, Hippias and Thrasymachus

Prodicus of Ceos sold the correctness of names. Plato's Socrates says he could afford only the one-drachma lecture, not the fifty-drachma course (Cratylus 384b). In the Protagoras he is called in to split "to become" from "to be" in Simonides' poem (340b–c), and Socrates, teasing, gets him to gloss Simonides' "hard" as "bad" (341a–c); the Laches calls him the best of the Sophists at distinguishing names (197d). The chorus of the Clouds heeds no sky-theorist but Prodicus, for his wisdom, and Socrates, for his swagger (360–363): the audience of 423 classed the two together.

Hippias of Elis came to Olympia, in Plato's telling, wearing nothing he had not made himself, from ring to belt, with epics and tragedies in his baggage (Hippias Minor 368b–d). His Collection (DK 86 B 6) gathered sayings of poets and prose writers by theme, perhaps the first anthology of earlier opinions. Xenophon gives him his best argument: how can laws be serious when their makers repeal them (Memorabilia 4.4.14)? Socrates answers with unwritten laws, such as honouring gods and parents, that hold everywhere and punish their breakers by themselves (4.4.19–24), adding divine law to module 6.5's nomos and physis.

Thrasymachus of Chalcedon was for Aristotle a stylist of rhythm and pity (Rhetoric 3.1, 1404a14; 3.8, 1409a2). Republic 1 makes him a theorist whose thesis hardens under pressure: the ruler, strictly speaking, never errs about his advantage (340d–341a); the shepherd fattens sheep for himself (343b); the perfectly unjust tyrant is happiest (344a–c). Socrates answers with craft analogies: every craft serves its object (341c–342e), and even thieves need justice among themselves (351c–352a). Thrasymachus blushes (350d), but Glaucon thinks him charmed rather than refuted (358b), which is why the Republic continues (module 12.3).

04Antiphon's On Truth and the Dissoi Logoi

Antiphon's ethical argument survives in his own words on Oxyrhynchus papyri (P.Oxy. 1364 and 1797, with P.Oxy. 3647): DK 87 B 44. Module 6.5 states the central claim and the identity dispute; the argument is more careful than the slogan. Justice is defined conventionally, as not transgressing the laws of one's city. Law's demands are agreed, nature's necessary. Break a law unseen and you escape shame and penalty; strain against nature and the harm is the same seen or unseen, for one is harmed "not through opinion but through truth". Hence the title. The later columns turn to the victim: the man who only defends himself, or treats bad parents well, suffers more, and the law neither prevents the wrong nor gives him more persuasive power in court than his injurer. P.Oxy. 1797 makes a paradox of it: if justice is not wronging one who has not wronged you, the truthful witness against a harmless neighbour does injustice by obeying the law. Gagarin (Antiphon the Athenian, 2002) reads the whole as an exploration of problems in nomos, not a recommendation of crime; the immoralist Antiphon, kin to Plato's Callicles, rests on the first column alone.

The Dissoi Logoi (DK 90), "Twofold Arguments", is an anonymous Doric treatise preserved at the end of manuscripts of Sextus Empiricus. It cites the Spartan victory over Athens and her allies (1.8), so it is later than 404. It argues both sides of paired terms (good and bad, seemly and shameful, just and unjust, true and false), then asks whether virtue can be taught and attacks election by lot: Protagoras' two opposed arguments in classroom form. Death is bad for the dying and good for undertakers (1.3). If every people brought its shameful customs to one heap and took away what it thought seemly, nothing would be left (2.18), an argument out of Herodotus 3.38. It shows Sophistic method written by someone other than Plato.

05Which Socrates? The four witnesses weighed

Module 6.5 introduces the four witnesses; here each is weighed. Of the four, Aristophanes alone wrote in Socrates' lifetime. The Clouds (423, later partly revised; plot in module 6.4) gives him a Thinkery, a basket in which he walks on air and contemplates the sun (225), experiments on the flea's leap (144–152) and the god Vortex in place of Zeus (380–381). Plato's Apology answers this picture by name (19c), denying that Socrates studied the heavens or took fees (19c–20c). Yet Plato's own Socrates admits a youthful keenness for natural inquiry (Phaedo 96a–99d). Dover's edition of the Clouds (1968) argued that Aristophanes built a composite intellectual from natural philosophers, Sophists and ascetics and gave it Socrates' face; the Frogs (1491–1499) still calls it graceful not to sit chattering beside Socrates and cast music aside.

Xenophon, born about 430, was in Asia with Cyrus' army at the trial, wrote decades later in exile, and claims to have attended conversations he was too young for (Symposium 1.1, set in 422). His Socrates is pious, practical and self-mastered. Russell's quip that a stupid man's report of a clever man is never accurate (A History of Western Philosophy, 1945) is the old prejudice; the better objection is that Xenophon writes apologetic and selects. Plato, about twenty-eight in 399, gave Socrates views that change between dialogues. Aristotle, born in 384, adds to module 6.5's universal definitions the inductive arguments (Metaphysics 13.4, 1078b27–29), the intellectualism (virtues are forms of knowledge, Nicomachean Ethics 6.13, 1144b17–30) and the questioning without answers, "for he confessed he did not know" (Sophistical Refutations 34, 183b7–8). But his "Socrates" in Politics 2.1 (1261a6) is explicitly the speaker of Plato's Republic: Aristotle too sometimes reports a character.

Where sources disagree. Burnet and A. E. Taylor held that Plato's Socrates, even the Socrates of the Phaedo and Republic with his Forms, was the historical man; Gigon (Sokrates, 1947) answered that every Socratic writing is fiction and the man irrecoverable. Schleiermacher's criterion stands between: ask what Socrates must have been, beyond Xenophon's portrait, to make Plato's possible. Aristotle's statement that the separated Forms were Plato's, not Socrates' (Metaphysics 1.6, 987b1–10; 13.4, 1078b30–32), weighs against Burnet and Taylor.

06Elenchus, paradoxes, irony and the sign

The elenchus has rules. The answerer must say what he really believes (Crito 49c–d; Gorgias 495a–b), because a life is under examination, not a thesis; and one witness suffices, the opponent himself (Gorgias 472b–c). To the problem of the elenchus that module 6.5 notes, Vlastos (Oxford Studies in Ancient Philosophy, 1983) gave his own answer: Socrates assumes that anyone holding a false moral belief also holds true beliefs entailing its negation.

The paradoxes are conclusions of arguments. No one does wrong willingly: no one wants to be miserable, so whoever pursues the bad mistakes it for good (Meno 77b–78b). The Protagoras (352a–358d) argues it against the many: grant that the pleasant is the good, and "overcome by pleasure" means doing bad knowingly because overcome by the good, which is absurd; the real fault is mismeasuring near and distant pleasures, cured by an art of measurement (356c–357b). Irwin (Plato's Moral Theory, 1977) took the hedonism as Socrates' own; the Gorgias (495a–499b), refuting Callicles' identification of pleasure and good, is the main text against. Virtue is knowledge: health and wealth benefit only when wisdom uses them, so virtue, which always benefits, is wisdom (Meno 87c–89a). Doing injustice is worse than suffering it: Polus grants it is more shameful; the shameful is so by pain or harm; it is not more painful; so it is more harmful (Gorgias 474c–475e). Aristotle named the weak joint: the denial of weakness of will conflicts with what plainly appears (Nicomachean Ethics 7.2, 1145b21–28).

Irony. Attic eirōneia meant sly dissembling; eirōn is among the rogue's names Strepsiades will answer to (Clouds 449). Thrasymachus calls Socrates' ignorance his habitual irony (Republic 337a); Alcibiades says he spends his life being ironic (Symposium 216e). Aristotle makes him the graceful self-deprecator (Nicomachean Ethics 4.7, 1127b22–26). Vlastos (1991) called it complex irony: the disavowal is true of certain knowledge and false of the tested beliefs he lives by.

The sign. Plato's Socrates has a voice that always turns him back and never urges him on (Apology 31c–d), and which was silent on the day of the trial, proof that death is no evil (40a–c). Euthyphro (3b) and Xenophon (Memorabilia 1.1.2) link it to the "new divinities" of the charge.

Where sources disagree. Plato's sign only forbids. Xenophon's also commands: it told Socrates what to do and what not to do, and friends who followed its advice profited (Memorabilia 1.1.4). In Xenophon it forbids him to prepare a defence (Apology 4; Memorabilia 4.8.5), where Plato's is silent. Xenophon's sign is a private oracle; Plato's is a check on a reasoner.

07The trial continued: Polycrates, Xenophon and the two Apologies

Module 6.5 gives the indictment, the vote and the amnesty argument. The fight over Socrates' memory continued in pamphlets. The rhetorician Polycrates published an Accusation of Socrates, now lost. Its charges are recovered from Xenophon's replies to "the accuser" (Memorabilia 1.2.9–64) and from Isocrates, who mocks Polycrates for making Alcibiades Socrates' pupil when no one had noticed (Busiris 5). They were political: contempt for election by lot (1.2.9), Hesiod's line that no work is a disgrace read as licence for crime (1.2.56), and the training of Critias and Alcibiades (1.2.12).

Where sources disagree. Hermippus, quoted by Diogenes Laertius (2.38), says Polycrates wrote the prosecution speech for the trial; others, Diogenes adds, gave it to Anytus. Favorinus (2.39) showed it was later, since it mentions Conon's rebuilding of the walls six years after Socrates' death. Most moderns follow Favorinus and take Polycrates as Xenophon's accuser; how far his charges echo what was said in 399 is uncertain.

Xenophon's answer on Critias and Alcibiades (1.2.12–48) concedes the facts: Critias was the greediest and most violent of the oligarchs, Alcibiades the most insolent of the democrats. Both, he argues, came to Socrates for power, behaved well while with him, and left once they thought themselves his superiors (1.2.14–16, 1.2.39). Socrates called Critias' pursuit of Euthydemus swinish (1.2.29–30); Critias, as one of the Thirty, banned the teaching of the art of words and with Charicles forbade Socrates to talk with the young (1.2.31–38). Alcibiades, not yet twenty, questioned Pericles until he admitted that what the majority imposes on the rich by force is violence, not law (1.2.40–46): he had learned argument and used it like a Sophist.

The two Apologies differ in kind. Plato's is a three-part speech by a writer who places himself in court (34a, 38b). Xenophon's is a short memoir from Hermogenes' report (Apology 2), written to explain the boastful tone: Socrates had decided that death now was better than old age (5–9). Plato's oracle says no one is wiser than Socrates (21a), launching a mission of examination; Xenophon's that no one is more free, more just or more sensible (Apology 14), a verdict on character. Plato's Socrates proposes free meals, then a fine; Xenophon's refuses any counter-penalty (23). Plato alone gives the argument that death is dreamless sleep or a journey to the dead, either a gain (40c–41c).

08The Crito and the Phaedo: Plato's portrait of the death

The Crito opens before dawn, the ship from Delos sighted off Sunium (43c–d). Socrates answers Crito's pleas from two principles: heed the expert, not the many (47a–48a), and never return wrong for wrong, a view few hold (49a–e). Then the Laws speak (50a–54d): they begot and educated him; he must persuade them or obey (51b–c); by staying seventy years he agreed to their terms (52b–53a); escape would confirm the verdict (53b–c). The Apology's promise to disobey an order to stop philosophising (29c–d) sits uneasily with this; Kraut (Socrates and the State, 1984) reconciles them by reading "persuade" as leaving room for disobedience by one who argues his case before the city.

The Phaedo is Phaedo's report to Echecrates at Phlius (57a). Among the absent Phaedo notes that Plato, he thinks, was ill (59b), one of three places where Plato names himself. The proofs of immortality belong to module 12.3, the closing myth to module 8.4. The death scene (115b–118a) enacts the argument that philosophy is practice for dying (64a). Asked how he wants to be buried, Socrates says however they like, if they can catch him (115c); the jailer weeps (116b–d); he declines to wait for sunset (116e–117a) and rebukes his friends' tears (117d). Phaedo calls him the best, wisest and most just man of his time (118a). Nietzsche read the last words as a confession that life is a sickness (The Gay Science 340); Most (Classical Quarterly, 1993) tied the cock for Asclepius to Plato's illness.

Where sources disagree. Gill ("The Death of Socrates", Classical Quarterly, 1973) argued that Plato idealised the death, since hemlock poisoning is normally more violent than the quiet, rising numbness of the Phaedo. Bloch (2001) answered that Conium maculatum produces exactly the peripheral paralysis Plato describes. Accurate or not, the scene is chosen for its meaning: the body dies while the reasoning part talks to the end.

09The Socratic dialogue as a genre

Aristotle names Socratic conversations (Sōkratikoi logoi) as a kind of prose imitation beside the mimes of Sophron and Xenarchus (Poetics 1, 1447b9–11). Diogenes Laertius records Socratic dialogues by Phaedo, Euclides, Crito, Simon the shoemaker and others (2.105–124). Aeschines of Sphettus, present at the trial and the death (Apology 33e; Phaedo 59b), wrote seven (Diogenes Laertius 2.61). In his Alcibiades Socrates says he could help the young man by no art, only through love and a divine dispensation (theia moira); in his Aspasia, Aspasia questions Xenophon and his wife by induction (Cicero, On Invention 1.51–53). Antisthenes wrote Socratic works on Heracles, Cyrus and Aspasia among many (Diogenes Laertius 6.15–18); only his speeches Ajax and Odysseus survive. Xenophon's four Socratic books span memoir, banquet, household dialogue and defence.

The genre is the key to the Socratic problem. Its authors shared scenes, interlocutors (Alcibiades, Aspasia, Callias) and themes, and invented freely within them, as Plato does in setting conversations in his own childhood. Kahn (Plato and the Socratic Dialogue, 1996), whose reply to Vlastos module 6.5 gives, built on this: the dialogues are a literary form, not minutes. The schools that Antisthenes, Euclides and Aristippus founded are the subject of module 12.5.

10Self-test

  1. How does the Theaetetus make Protagoras' measure doctrine the same claim as Theaetetus' definition of knowledge, and what extra theory does Socrates need to sustain it?
    Answer

    If things are for each person as they appear, perception is always of what is and cannot be false, which is what "knowledge is perception" requires (151e–152c). To give each perception its own object, Socrates adds the "secret doctrine" of universal motion and the twin-offspring theory of perception (152c–e, 156a–157c).

  2. Why is the argument from the future (Theaetetus 177c–179b) harder for Protagoras to escape than the reversal at 170a–171c?
    Answer

    Against a relativist the reversal seems to need the qualifier "true for him" dropped at 171a–c, a move Burnyeat defends and Fine takes as a sign that Plato's Protagoras is an infallibilist. Judgements about what will prove healthy or advantageous are made better by doctor or legislator than by layman, so not everyone is a measure.

  3. State Gorgias' chariot argument and explain how it uses Eleatic method against Eleatic conclusions.
    Answer

    If what is thought were what is, a chariot racing on the sea would exist because someone thinks of it, so thought does not reach being (Sextus, Against the Mathematicians 7.79–80; MXG 980a). Parmenides had argued from the link between thought and being to one changeless being; Gorgias cuts the link and, by the same exhaustive disjunctions, reaches nothing.

  4. In what sense is the Helen the practical counterpart of On Not Being?
    Answer

    If speech cannot convey things, it acts on souls by its effects, as drugs act on bodies (Helen 14); persuasion is a kind of compulsion, so Helen is blameless. The orator manages states of soul rather than truths, which is what Plato's Gorgias attacks.

  5. What does Antiphon mean by saying one is harmed through truth, not opinion, and why does Gagarin deny that On Truth preaches immoralism?
    Answer

    Breaches of law harm you only if seen, through reputation and penalty; breaches of nature harm you whether seen or not, so nature is what holds in truth. Gagarin (2002) argues that the later columns, on the defenceless victim and the truthful witness, probe the incoherence of conventional justice rather than recommend crime.

  6. What evidence suggests that Aristophanes' natural-philosopher Socrates is not pure invention, and what caution did Dover add?
    Answer

    Plato's own Socrates admits a youthful keenness for natural inquiry (Phaedo 96a–99d), and the Frogs (1491–1499) still calls it graceful not to sit chattering beside Socrates and cast music aside. Dover (1968) argued that the Clouds fuses traits of natural philosophers, Sophists and ascetics into one figure with Socrates' face.

  7. Reconstruct the Protagoras' argument that weakness of will is ignorance, and give Aristotle's objection.
    Answer

    If the pleasant is the good, "overcome by pleasure" means doing bad knowingly because overcome by the good, which is absurd; so the error is mismeasuring near and distant pleasures, curable by an art of measurement (352a–358d). Aristotle replies that the denial conflicts with what plainly appears (Nicomachean Ethics 7.2, 1145b21–28).

  8. How do Plato's and Xenophon's accounts of Socrates' sign differ, and how does each relate it to the charge?
    Answer

    Plato's sign only turns Socrates back and was silent on the day of the trial (Apology 31c–d, 40a–c); Xenophon's also gives positive advice to him and his friends and forbade him to prepare a defence (Memorabilia 1.1.4; Apology 4). Both Euthyphro (3b) and Xenophon (Memorabilia 1.1.2) connect it with the charge of new divinities.

  9. Whom is Xenophon answering in Memorabilia 1.2, and how does he defend Socrates over Critias and Alcibiades?
    Answer

    "The accuser", usually identified with Polycrates, whose pamphlet postdates Conon's walls (Diogenes Laertius 2.39). Xenophon argues that both men sought Socrates for political advantage, behaved well while with him, and left once they thought themselves his superiors; Critias as one of the Thirty even banned him from talking to the young (1.2.12–48).

  10. Where do the Apology and the Crito appear to conflict, and how does Kraut reconcile them?
    Answer

    In the Apology (29c–d) Socrates says he will disobey an order to stop philosophising; in the Crito (51b–c) the Laws say he must persuade or obey. Kraut (1984) reads "persuade" as permitting disobedience by one who argues his case before the city, so the two are consistent.

11Carry forward

  1. The Sophists' own texts (Gorgias' two treatises, Antiphon's papyrus, the Dissoi Logoi) are tougher philosophy than Plato's portraits suggest; when a Sophist reaches us through Plato, separate the fragment from the reconstruction and the reconstruction from the refutation.
  2. Socratic ethics is intellectualist: virtue is knowledge and wrongdoing is error. Plato's divided soul, Aristotle's account of weakness of will and the Stoic theory of passions as false judgements each define themselves by what they keep or reject of it.
  3. After 399 the trial became a literary war over whether Socrates made Critias and Alcibiades, fought by Polycrates, Xenophon and Plato in a genre of free invention; a Socratic text is a move in that war before it is evidence for the man.
