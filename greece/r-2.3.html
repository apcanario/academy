Hades the god and Hades the place

In Homer "Hades" is nearly always a person. The dead go "to the house of Hades"; using the god's name for his realm is a later shorthand. Greeks heard the name as "the Unseen" (a-ides), an etymology Plato toys with in Cratylus 403a; modern linguists reject it, and the origin is uncertain.

His position is fixed in Iliad 15.187–193: the three sons of Cronus drew lots, and Zeus took the sky, Poseidon the sea, Hades "the murky darkness", with earth and Olympus held in common. Hesiod, Theogony 453–506, gives the brothers' birth, and at 767–774 the "echoing halls of the strong god" guarded by the hound. Hades is Zeus's near double: Homer calls him Zeus Katachthonios, "Zeus of the Underworld" (Iliad 9.457), and his epithets Pylartes "gate-fastener" (Iliad 8.367) and Polydegmon "receiver of many" (Homeric Hymn to Demeter 17) describe a jailer, not a judge. Agamemnon calls him "unyielding and inexorable, and therefore most hateful of gods to mortals" (Iliad 9.158–159). He has little mythology beyond the abduction of Persephone and the moments when heroes force his doors; Heracles wounds him with an arrow "at Pylos among the dead" (Iliad 5.395–404).

Cult reflects this remoteness. Pausanias 6.25.2 knew only one true temple of Hades, at Elis, opened once a year. Elsewhere he was approached under euphemisms: Plouton, "the Wealthy One", which from the fifth century (Sophocles, Antigone 1200) softens him into giver of the harvest; Klymenos at Hermione (Pausanias 2.35.9); Eubouleus in Eleusinian and Orphic texts. The place itself has no stable location in Homer. Odysseus sails to it across Oceanus (Odyssey 10.508–515, 11.13–22); yet Patroclus's ghost goes beneath the earth "like smoke" (Iliad 23.100), and Hades fears Poseidon's earthquake will split the ground above him (Iliad 20.61–65).

Where sources disagree. Whether Homer's Hades lies beyond the western Ocean or below the earth has troubled readers since the Alexandrian critics. Most modern scholars (Sourvinou-Inwood, "Reading" Greek Death, 1995) treat the two as alternative traditional images rather than parts of one map.

The abduction of Persephone and the Homeric Hymn to Demeter

Hesiod disposes of the story in two lines: Zeus gave Persephone to Hades, who "snatched her from her mother" (Theogony 912–914). The full narrative is the Homeric Hymn to Demeter, 495 hexameters usually dated between about 650 and 550 BC and preserved in a single Moscow manuscript found in 1777.

The narrative

Persephone is gathering flowers on the Nysian plain when the earth opens at a narcissus Zeus has grown as a lure; Hades carries her off in his chariot (1–20). Only Hecate and Helios hear her cry. Demeter searches nine days with torches, refusing food; on the tenth Hecate joins her and Helios names the thief, adding that Hades is "no unworthy son-in-law" (62–89). Demeter leaves Olympus disguised as an old woman. At Eleusis she sits by the Maiden Well, is found by the daughters of King Celeus, and becomes nurse to the infant Demophoön (98–183). She refuses wine and asks for the kykeon, barley meal in water with pennyroyal (206–211); the servant Iambe makes her laugh (202–205). Each night she lays the child in the fire to make him immortal, until Metaneira spies and shrieks; Demeter reveals herself and orders a temple built above the Kallichoron spring (231–274). Then she withholds the harvest for a year, until Zeus relents (305–313). Hades releases Persephone but "secretly gave her a honey-sweet pomegranate seed to eat" (371–374). Because she has eaten below she must return: one third of the year with Hades, two thirds with her mother (398–403, 445–447). Demeter restores the grain, teaches her rites to Triptolemus, Diocles, Eumolpus and Celeus (473–479), and the hymn closes on the initiate's reward:

Blessed is he among men on earth who has seen these things.

The uninitiated "never has a share in such things once dead, down in the mouldering gloom" (480–482).

Where sources disagree. The hymn tells the pomegranate twice: the narrator says Hades gave it "secretly" (372), but Persephone tells her mother he "forced me against my will" (411–413). On the division of the year the hymn says one third below; Ovid (Metamorphoses 5.564–567; Fasti 4.613–614) says half. The abduction site also moves: Nysa in the hymn, Enna in Sicily for Diodorus 5.3–4 and Ovid, Eleusis itself in the local version in Pausanias 1.38.5.

Eleusis

Almost every element of the Mysteries has its aition in the hymn: the Kallichoron, where initiates danced; the kykeon drunk after fasting; the ritual obscenity of the procession, echoing Iambe; the torchlight; the ancestors of the priestly clans, the Eumolpidae and Kerykes. Demophoön has been read as the shadow of the child initiated "from the hearth", a known Eleusinian office. The hymn omits Athens, one argument for dating it before Athenian control of the sanctuary (Richardson, 1974). Clinton (Myth and Cult, 1992) argues instead that the hymn reflects the Thesmophoria more than the Mysteries, whose own myth centred on Demeter's gift of grain.

The seasons

The hymn ties Persephone's return to spring, "when the earth blooms with every kind of fragrant spring flower" (401–403). But in Greece grain is sown in October and harvested in May and June; the dead season is the parched summer. Nilsson (1940) therefore argued that Persephone's months below are those the seed-corn spends in underground storage jars from harvest to sowing. The hymn's text resists this, since it names spring, and Demeter's famine is a withholding of germination, not a winter. Most recent commentators (Foley, 1994) read the hymn less as seasonal allegory than as a story about marriage, loss and mortality, to which seasonal meaning was later attached.

The geography of the underworld

Circe's sailing directions (Odyssey 10.508–515) are the first map: walk from Ocean's shore to where "into Acheron flow Pyriphlegethon and Cocytus, which is a branch of Styx". Hesiod adds the house of Styx (Theogony 775–806) and the chasm of Tartarus (720–819). Plato systematises four rivers in Phaedo 112e–113c. Virgil assembles everything.

Charon and Cerberus

Charon is absent from Homer and Hesiod. He first appears in the lost epic Minyas, quoted by Pausanias 10.28.2, and is a stock figure by the fifth century: Euripides, Alcestis 252–257; Aristophanes, Frogs 180–270, where he charges two obols. Virgil's filthy, fire-eyed ferryman (Aeneid 6.298–304) is the one Dante inherited. Cerberus is in Homer only as "the hound of Hades" (Iliad 8.368). Hesiod names him, gives him fifty heads and explains his job: he fawns on those who enter and devours any who try to leave (Theogony 310–312, 769–774). Three heads become standard in sixth-century art.

The judges

In Homer only Minos sits "holding a golden sceptre, giving judgements to the dead" (Odyssey 11.568–571); the dead come to him as litigants, and he settles disputes rather than weighing souls. Rhadamanthys in Homer lives in Elysium (Odyssey 4.564). The tribunal of three is Plato's: in Gorgias 523e–524a Rhadamanthys judges the dead of Asia, Aeacus those of Europe, Minos hears appeals. Virgil keeps Minos shaking the urn of lots (Aeneid 6.432) and makes Rhadamanthys inquisitor of Tartarus (6.566–569).

Where sources disagree. Whether Homer's Minos judges the dead at all is contested: Sourvinou-Inwood reads him as continuing his earthly kingship, and sees the moralised afterlife as a sixth- to fifth-century development. Aeacus is a judge in Plato, but the doorkeeper of Hades in the manuscripts of Aristophanes' Frogs and in Lucian, Dialogues of the Dead 20.

Tartarus, the asphodel meadow, Elysium

Tartarus in Homer is a prison for gods, "as far beneath Hades as heaven is above earth" (Iliad 8.13–16, 478–481). Hesiod makes it a primordial being (Theogony 119) and a chasm so deep that a bronze anvil falls nine days to reach it (720–725). It holds the Titans; no mortal is punished there. The ordinary dead occupy the asphodel meadow (Odyssey 11.539, 573; 24.13). Elysium in Homer is not in the underworld at all: Menelaus will be sent alive to "the Elysian plain at the ends of the earth", where Rhadamanthys lives (Odyssey 4.561–569). Hesiod's Isles of the Blessed house the heroes of the fourth race (Works and Days 167–173). Pindar, Olympian 2.56–80, gives the earliest full statement of posthumous judgement, the good passing to the Isle of the Blessed.

Where sources disagree. Reece (2007) has argued that the "asphodel meadow" is a misdivided "ashy meadow" (spodos), refitted by folk etymology to the plant; the matter is open.

From Homer to Virgil

Homer's underworld is flat and undifferentiated. The dead are witless shadows who need blood to speak (Odyssey 11.23–50, 147–149); three sinners are punished and one man judges, but the ordinary dead are neither rewarded nor penalised. Between Homer and Virgil the picture is moralised and mapped. Hesiod and the Hymn to Demeter introduce a blessed fate for the few. The Orphic gold tablets, from Hipponion (c. 400 BC) onward, give the initiate a route: avoid the spring by the white cypress and drink from the Lake of Memory. Aristophanes, Frogs 145–158, shows a popular version, with a slough of mud for perjurers and parricides and myrtle groves for initiates. Plato builds the eschatological myths of Gorgias 523a–527a, Phaedo 107d–115a and Republic 614b–621d: judgement at a meadow where roads fork, Tartarus for the incurable, purgation and rebirth for the rest, and a plain of Lethe where souls drink and forget.

Virgil, Aeneid 6, fuses these layers into the itinerary that became the Western default. The vestibule holds personified Grief, Disease, Old Age, Fear, Hunger, Death and Sleep (273–281). Acheron, Cocytus and Styx are one tangled flood (295–297). Charon refuses the unburied, who wait a hundred years (325–330). Beyond Cerberus, drugged with a honeyed cake (417–423), lie the infants, the falsely condemned under Minos, the suicides and the Fields of Mourning (426–476). The road forks (540–543): left to Tartarus, ringed by Phlegethon, where Tisiphone guards the gate and Rhadamanthys interrogates (548–627); right to Elysium, now underground with its own sun (637–678). Anchises explains that souls are purged for a thousand years, drink Lethe and return to bodies (713–751). None of this is Homeric; it is Platonic, Orphic-Pythagorean and Stoic.

Where sources disagree. Virgil's Theseus, "sitting and destined to sit for ever" in Tartarus (6.617–618), contradicts the Greek tradition that Heracles freed him (Apollodorus 2.5.12). Virgil is also at odds with himself: Elysium is both final home and a waiting-room before rebirth.

Odysseus sees three sinners together (Odyssey 11.576–600). What they share is offence against the gods in person, which is why they are the exception to Homer's indifferent afterlife.

The katabasis tradition

Odysseus. Odyssey 11 is strictly a nekyia, a summoning of the dead at a pit on Ocean's shore; yet from 11.568 Odysseus sees Minos, Tityos and Heracles as though inside. Analysts since Wilamowitz have treated 11.565–627 as an interpolation; unitarians reply that Homer moves the camera without caring.

Heracles. The twelfth labour is already in Iliad 8.367–369. Apollodorus 2.5.12 gives the full form: initiated first at Eleusis, he descends at Taenarum, frees Theseus, fails to free Pirithous, and drags Cerberus up with Persephone's consent. Bacchylides 5.56–175 has him meet Meleager's ghost and ask for Deianira in marriage, with terrible irony. The Eleusinian initiation (also Euripides, Heracles 610–619) recasts the labour as an initiate's rehearsal of death.

Orpheus. The earliest allusion is Euripides, Alcestis 357–362, where Admetus wishes he had Orpheus' voice to bring Alcestis back; the wish implies success. Plato, Symposium 179d, says the gods showed Orpheus only a phantom and sent him away for cowardice. The named Eurydice and the backward look first appear together in Virgil, Georgics 4.453–527, followed by Ovid, Metamorphoses 10.1–85.

Theseus and Pirithous. The friends descend to carry off Persephone for Pirithous; Hades seats them on the Chair of Forgetfulness, where they grow fast to the rock (Apollodorus, Epitome 1.23–24; Pausanias 10.29.9). Heracles frees Theseus; when he reaches for Pirithous the earth quakes. Odyssey 11.631 names the pair, but Hereas of Megara (in Plutarch, Theseus 20.2) charged that Pisistratus inserted the line to flatter Athens.

Where sources disagree. Bowra (1952) argued that Orpheus' failure is Virgil's invention; Heath (1994) and most since reply that Plato's Symposium and the Attic relief of Orpheus, Eurydice and Hermes (c. 410 BC) show a failed return a century before Virgil, though not the backward glance. Hermesianax (fr. 7 Powell) calls the wife Agriope; the name Eurydice is not secure before the first century BC.

Hecate, the Erinyes, Thanatos and Hypnos

Hecate. Hesiod's hymn (Theogony 411–452) makes her daughter of Perses and Asteria, honoured by Zeus above all, with nothing chthonic about her; West (1966) defends the passage as Hesiod's own. In the Hymn to Demeter she hears the cry, carries a torch in the search, and ends as Persephone's attendant (24–25, 52–62, 438–440). By the fifth century she is goddess of crossroads, ghosts and sorcery: Medea's mistress (Euripides, Medea 395–397), recipient of monthly suppers at crossroads (Aristophanes, Plutus 594–597), invoked with howling dogs in Theocritus, Idyll 2.

The Erinyes. In Homer they avenge wrongs against parents and oaths (Iliad 9.454, 571; 19.259–260). Hesiod makes them daughters of Gaia from the blood of Uranus (Theogony 185); Aeschylus makes them daughters of Night, black-robed, dripping from the eyes (Eumenides 46–56, 321–322), and settles them in Athens as the Semnai Theai. Three in Euripides (Orestes 408), they are named Allecto, Tisiphone and Megaera in Virgil and Apollodorus 1.1.4. Demeter was worshipped as Demeter Erinys at Thelpusa (Pausanias 8.25.4–7), a hint that the fury was once an earth-goddess.

Thanatos and Hypnos. Night bore Death and Sleep fatherless (Theogony 211–212); Sleep is gentle, Death has "a heart of iron, pitiless as bronze" (758–766). In Iliad 16.671–683 the twins carry Sarpedon's body to Lycia, the scene on the Euphronios krater. Only Euripides puts Death on stage, black-robed and armed with a sword, wrestled into submission by Heracles (Alcestis 24–76, 843–849). Sisyphus's binding of Thanatos, so that no one could die until Ares freed him, is Pherecydes'.

Olympian and chthonic in ritual

Greek ritual expresses the distinction between upper and lower powers through orientation. To the Olympians one sacrificed (thuein) on a raised altar (bomos) a white victim, head pulled back so the throat faced the sky; the meat was shared, the god receiving thigh-bones and fat in smoke. To the powers below one sacrificed into a pit (bothros) or on a low hearth (eschara) a black victim with head pressed down so the blood ran into the earth, often at night, the victim burnt whole, the celebrant turned away. Homer knows the code: in the oath of Iliad 3.103–104 a white lamb is brought for Helios and a black one for Earth; for the dead Odysseus digs a pit, promises Tiresias an all-black ram and lets the blood run into the trench (Odyssey 11.23–36), turning his face away as Circe instructed (10.527–529). The verb for offerings to heroes and the dead was enagizein; Herodotus 2.44 notes that some Greeks sacrifice to Heracles as an immortal and others make offerings to him as a hero. Zeus Meilichios received pigs burnt entire (Xenophon, Anabasis 7.8.4–5).

Where sources disagree. How systematic this dichotomy was is the central modern argument. Rohde, Harrison and Nilsson built on it a two-tier religion of sky-gods and earth-powers. Schlesier (1991–92) attacked the dichotomy as a nineteenth-century construct; Scullion (1994) defended a modified version; Ekroth (2002) showed from inscriptions and bone deposits that heroes, and often underworld gods, received ordinary thysia with a shared meal, holocausts being rare. Burkert's compromise, that the categories mark two poles of a spectrum, is now the usual position.

Self-test

  1. Which passage fixes Hades' share of the cosmos, and how was it allotted?
    Answer

    Iliad 15.187–193: the three sons of Cronus drew lots. Zeus took the sky, Poseidon the sea, Hades the darkness; earth and Olympus were common.

  2. Which two deities hear Persephone's cry in the Homeric Hymn to Demeter?
    Answer

    Hecate, in her cave, and Helios. Hecate later joins the search and Helios names Hades as the abductor.

  3. What fraction of the year does Persephone spend below according to the hymn, and who gives a different figure?
    Answer

    One third (398–403, 445–447). Ovid gives half the year in Metamorphoses 5 and Fasti 4.

  4. Why does Nilsson's seed-corn interpretation sit awkwardly with the hymn's own text?
    Answer

    Nilsson placed Persephone's absence in summer, when seed-corn is stored underground, with her ascent at autumn sowing. The hymn says she returns when the earth blooms in spring (401–403).

  5. Is Charon in Homer? Where does he first appear?
    Answer

    No. He first appears in the lost epic Minyas, quoted by Pausanias 10.28.2, and is a fixture by the fifth century (Euripides, Aristophanes).

  6. What is Minos doing in Odyssey 11, and who turned him into one of three judges?
    Answer

    He is giving judgements to the dead who bring disputes to him, continuing his kingship, not weighing lives. Plato, Gorgias 523e–524a, makes him, Rhadamanthys and Aeacus a tribunal.

  7. Where is Elysium in Homer, and where in Virgil?
    Answer

    In Odyssey 4.561–569 it is a plain at the ends of the earth, reached alive by Menelaus. In Aeneid 6 it is underground, the right-hand branch of the fork beyond Charon and Cerberus.

  8. What is wrong with saying "the Danaids carry water in leaking jars in the Greek underworld"?
    Answer

    The image is Roman (Ovid, Horace, Hyginus). In Plato's Gorgias and Polygnotus' Delphic painting the leaking jar punishes the uninitiated in general, not the daughters of Danaus.

  9. What is the earliest text to say that Orpheus failed, and when is the name Eurydice first secure?
    Answer

    Plato, Symposium 179d, where the gods show him only a phantom. The name Eurydice is not secure before the first century BC; Hermesianax calls her Agriope.

  10. List four ritual markers that separate a chthonic from an Olympian sacrifice.
    Answer

    Low pit or hearth rather than raised altar; black rather than white victim; head pressed down so blood enters the earth rather than throat to the sky; victim burnt whole rather than shared and eaten. Night-time and the averted face are further markers.

Carry forward

  1. Homer's underworld is not moral. Reward, punishment and judgement for ordinary mortals enter between the sixth and fourth centuries through Eleusis, Orphic and Pythagorean teaching and Plato, and Virgil fixes the result; read every later afterlife against that sequence.
  2. The Persephone myth is first a cult myth of Eleusis and a story about marriage and loss; the seasonal allegory is secondary and does not fit the Greek farming year cleanly. Keep the hymn's own promise, a better lot after death for the initiate, in view for the modules on mystery cult and Plato.
  3. The Olympian and chthonic distinction is a real grammar of ritual gesture, not two religions. Treat it as a spectrum with heroes and figures such as Zeus Meilichios and Hecate in the middle, and expect it to recur in hero cult, oath-ritual and tragedy.
