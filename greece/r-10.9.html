The plan of Zeus

The Iliad's proem names a cause before it names a single action: the wrath of Achilles sent many souls to Hades, "and the plan of Zeus was being accomplished" (Dios d' eteleieto boulē, Iliad 1.5). The clause is grammatically parenthetical and thematically enormous. It places everything that follows under a divine design without saying what the design is.

Ancient readers gave two answers. The narrow one identifies the plan with the one Zeus forms in Book 1: to honour Achilles by giving the Trojans the upper hand until the Achaeans pay for Agamemnon's insult (1.508–10; 2.1–4, where Zeus "pondered how he might honour Achilles"). The broad one comes from the scholia on 1.5, which quote the Cypria (fr. 1 West). Earth, weighed down by the countless tribes of men, appealed to Zeus, who pitied her and resolved to lighten her load through "the great strife of the Iliac war". The same scholion reports that Momus advised Zeus against thunderbolts and floods and suggested instead that he marry Thetis to a mortal and father a beautiful daughter. Achilles and Helen become the two instruments of depopulation. Proclus' summary of the Cypria opens with Zeus planning the war with Themis. The idea had a long life: in Hesiod's Catalogue of Women (fr. 204.95–123 M–W) Zeus resolves to end the race of demigods, and Euripides uses the overpopulation motive at Helen 36–41 and Orestes 1639–42.

Where sources disagree. Nobody has settled whether the Iliad poet knew the Cypria plan, or meant it. Neoanalysts such as Wolfgang Kullmann argue that the Iliad presupposes a cyclic story of this kind, which is why 1.5 needs no gloss. The other side is Aristarchus' own, preserved in the scholia on 1.5: explain Homer from Homer, read the plan as the Thetis plan only, and note that the Iliad never mentions a burdened Earth. The Cypria as a poem is later than the Iliad, although its material may be older. Kirk (The Iliad: A Commentary, vol. 1, 1985) on 1.5 takes a middle line: the phrase is deliberately open, and the local plan (Achilles' honour) is one phase of the larger one (the fall of Troy and the end of the heroes).

The consequence is what matters. If the war is Zeus' population control, the Iliad is a poem about mass death administered from above, and the causes the heroes argue over (Helen, Briseis, the insult) are surface. If the plan is only the Thetis plan, Zeus is a god who acts because a mother asked him to. The poem supports both readings, and much of its theology lives in the gap between them.

Zeus, Thetis and the scales of fate

Thetis' supplication (1.495–530) follows the ritual form exactly. She clasps Zeus' knees with one hand and his chin with the other, and she calls in a debt: Achilles has already told how she once saved Zeus, when Hera, Poseidon and Athena tried to bind him, by summoning the hundred-handed Briareus (1.396–406). Zeus' first reply is anxious, because Hera will scold. His second is absolute: the nod of his head cannot be revoked (1.524–30). From here on Zeus is bound by his own promise, and the middle books carry it out: the false dream sent to Agamemnon (2.1–40), the ban on divine intervention (8.1–27), the Trojan advance to the ships, and the death of Patroclus, which Zeus forecasts at 8.473–7 and 15.59–71.

Alongside the promise runs the language of fate (moira, aisa) and the golden scales. At 8.68–74 Zeus weighs the two armies; the Achaean fates sink, and he thunders against them. At 16.658 Hector, turning to flee, "recognised the sacred scales of Zeus", a metaphor that the earlier scene has made literal. At 22.208–13 Zeus weighs the death-fates of Achilles and Hector. Hector's sinks toward Hades, and Apollo leaves him. Odysseus' remark that Zeus tips the scales in battle (19.223–4) shows that the image was proverbial. The device probably comes from the Memnon story of the Aethiopis, in which Thetis and Eos watched the souls of their sons weighed. Aeschylus staged that weighing in his lost Psychostasia (Plutarch, How the Young Man Should Listen to Poems 17a).

The test cases are Sarpedon and Hector. At 16.431–61 Zeus, grieving for his son, asks whether he should snatch him from death. Hera answers that he may: "do it; but we other gods will not all approve" (16.443). Then she warns that every god will want to save a favourite. Zeus gives way and sheds a rain of blood. At 22.168–85 the exchange is replayed with Athena over Hector. Zeus yields again, and adds that he was not speaking in earnest.

Where sources disagree. Is Zeus subject to fate? Three positions have long competed. (1) Fate is an impersonal power above Zeus, and the scales are something he reads, not something he sets. This is the older view, found in W. C. Greene's Moira (1944). (2) Fate and the will of Zeus coincide. Homer speaks of "the aisa of Zeus" (17.321) and pairs Zeus with Moira (19.87), so the scales are a ritual of decision, not discovery (Lloyd-Jones, The Justice of Zeus, 1971; B. C. Dietrich, Death, Fate and the Gods, 1965, traces a more complex development). (3) The question is partly misplaced. Zeus could act "beyond fate" (hyper moron; cf. 2.155, 16.780, 20.30), but doing so would break the order the gods share, which is exactly Hera's argument at 16.443–9. Narratological readers add that fate is the shape of the tradition: Zeus "could" save Sarpedon in the way the poet "could" have the Achaeans sail home in Book 2, and these near misses are dramatic tension, not metaphysics (Janko, The Iliad: A Commentary, vol. 4, 1992, on 16.431–61; J. V. Morrison, Homeric Misdirection, 1992). No single answer fits every passage, and that is probably the honest conclusion.

The partisans and their reasons

Iliad 20.33–40 gives the formal line-up. Hera, Athena, Poseidon, Hermes and Hephaestus side with the Achaeans; Ares, Apollo, Artemis, Leto, Xanthus and Aphrodite with the Trojans. Their motives are uneven: some come from myth, some from cult, some are purely personal.

Two of the partisans are worth a sentence beyond the table. Aphrodite is the only god the poem shows failing in battle: she carries Paris out of his duel in a mist (3.373–82) and is wounded in the wrist by Diomedes while rescuing Aeneas, so that Zeus tells her war is not her work (5.330–51, 5.426–30). Xanthus is the one partisan with no myth behind him, only a grievance of the present: Achilles chokes his streams with corpses, the river rises against him, and Hephaestus has to burn the water back (21.211–382), the Iliad's one fight between a man and a landscape.

Three points are worth pressing. First, the Judgement of Paris is the obvious explanation for Hera's and Athena's hatred, yet the Iliad names it only at 24.25–30. There Paris is said to have insulted the goddesses who came to his farmstead, and to have praised the one who gave him grievous lust. Everywhere else the poem treats their hatred as a given, and at 4.31–5 Zeus himself asks what Priam has done to deserve it.

Where sources disagree. Aristarchus athetised 24.25–30, partly because he held that Homer did not know the Judgement and partly because the word machlosynē ("lust") seemed un-Homeric. Karl Reinhardt (Das Parisurteil, 1938) argued the reverse: the poet knew the story and held it back until the end, when the gods' partisanship has to be accounted for before Hector is ransomed. Most scholars now accept the lines and treat the silence as deliberate. A minority still regards them as an intrusion.

Second, Poseidon's motive falls apart if pressed. Apollo suffered the same fraud from Laomedon and still defends Troy, and Poseidon himself rescues Aeneas at 20.293–308 because the line of Dardanus has to survive. Grudges in the Iliad are real, but they give way to destiny and to the gods' private attachments. Third, Ares is a war god without a cause. Zeus calls him the most hateful of the Olympians to him (5.890), and Athena wounds him twice: through Diomedes' spear in Book 5, and with a boundary stone thrown by her own hand at 21.403–8. The poem's sympathy runs with Athena's disciplined violence, not with violence for its own sake.

Double motivation: whose decision?

At 1.188–222 Achilles is divided in his heart: draw his sword and kill Agamemnon, or check his anger. As he draws, Athena seizes him by the hair. Only he can see her, and she tells him she has come to stop his fury, "if you will obey" (1.207). He obeys, saying that a man should listen to the gods. So whose restraint is it, his or hers?

The classic answer is Albin Lesky's "double motivation" (Göttliche und menschliche Motivation im homerischen Epos, 1961): Homer routinely gives an act both a human and a divine cause and sees no contradiction. The text supports him. Achilles' deliberation is narrated before Athena arrives. Her speech gives reasons, since gifts three times as great will come to him (1.213–14), and she makes her intervention depend on his consent. The goddess does not replace the man's decision. She makes it visible and gives it dignity.

Where sources disagree. Bruno Snell (Die Entdeckung des Geistes, 1946) argued that Homeric man lacks a unified self, so decisive turns of mind can only be represented as divine interventions. E. R. Dodds (The Greeks and the Irrational, 1951, ch. 1) read Agamemnon's apology, "I am not to blame, but Zeus and Moira and the Erinys" (19.86–7), as evidence of a shame culture that projected unwelcome impulses onto the gods. Bernard Williams (Shame and Necessity, 1993) attacked both. Homeric characters deliberate, decide and accept responsibility, and only the vocabulary differs from ours. Agamemnon, after all, still pays the compensation (19.137–8). In Homer, divine causation explains an act; it does not excuse it.

Ancient readers asked the same question in different terms. Heraclitus the Allegorist (Homeric Problems 17–20) read Athena's grip on Achilles' hair as reason taking hold of the head, the seat of thought. That is an allegorical version of the psychological reading Lesky would later give.

Thetis, pity and the limits of immortality

Thetis is the most important mother in the poem and arguably its most important god. Laura Slatkin (The Power of Thetis, 1991) showed how much the Iliad compresses into her. The courtship, Themis' prophecy and the marriage to Peleus are told in module 4.1; what matters here is the consequence: her son's mortality is the price of Zeus' continued rule. Her rescue of Zeus with Briareus hints at a cosmic power the poem otherwise keeps offstage, and it is the reason Zeus owes her. Whether that story is old is disputed: M. M. Willcock ("Mythological Paradeigma in the Iliad", 1964) argued that Achilles' tale of Briareus is the poet's ad hoc invention, made for the moment of the plea and attested nowhere else, which would make the "debt" a rhetorical fiction; Slatkin takes it as a compressed trace of a real cosmogonic tradition about Thetis. The poem's theology reads differently on each view.

Her scenes mark the poem's structure: the first plea (1.357–427, 1.495–530); her lament among the Nereids as "unhappy mother of the best of men" (dysaristotokeia, 18.54); her request for new armour (18.369–617); and her last errand, telling Achilles that Zeus wants Hector's body returned (24.77–140). She knows the choice her son faces, a long life without glory or a short one with it (9.410–16), and every intervention she makes brings closer the outcome she mourns. A goddess who cannot die watches the one thing she cares for become mortal.

Divine pity in the Iliad follows the same pattern: deep feeling with no power to undo. Zeus' rain of blood for Sarpedon (16.459–61) is the strongest case. At 17.198–208 he pities Hector as he puts on Achilles' armour, knowing that his death is near. At 17.441–7 he pities the immortal horses of Achilles (not Hector's), who weep for Patroclus, and regrets giving them to a mortal, since nothing is more wretched than man of all that breathes and creeps on the earth. Apollo, declining to fight Poseidon, states the theology behind this. Men are like leaves that flourish and then perish (21.462–7), and their fate is not worth a quarrel between gods.

Olympian comedy: deception, laughter, theomachy

The Deception of Zeus

The Dios apatē (14.153–353) is the poem's longest divine episode. To distract Zeus from the battle, Hera bathes and dresses, borrows Aphrodite's embroidered band (kestos himas, 14.214–17) under a false pretext, and bribes Sleep with the promise of the Grace Pasithea (14.267–9). Sleep is nervous, because the last time he helped her Zeus nearly threw him into the sea (14.249–62). On Ida, Zeus assures Hera that he has never desired anyone so much and then lists his previous lovers (14.315–28). The earth sends up flowers beneath them, and a golden cloud covers them (14.346–51). When he wakes in Book 15, Zeus reminds Hera of the time he hung her from the sky with anvils tied to her feet (15.18–24).

Where sources disagree. Is the scene comedy or cult? The flowers and cloud recall the sacred marriage (hieros gamos) of Sky and Earth. Jasper Griffin (Homer on Life and Death, 1980) reads the scene as comedy whose point is the contrast with the men dying below; Janko sets out the sacred-marriage parallels and takes the demonstration that desire can overpower even Zeus seriously. Aristarchus athetised the list of lovers (14.317–27) as unfitting. The episode also contains a theogony that is not Hesiod's, naming Ocean and Tethys as the origin of the gods (14.201, 14.246), which Plato noticed (Theaetetus 152e). Janko (vol. 4, 1992) reads the episode as both comic and cosmological.

Laughter

Book 1 ends with Hephaestus bustling round Olympus pouring nectar to defuse a quarrel between Zeus and Hera, and "unquenchable laughter rose among the blessed gods" (1.599). The joke depends on the gods' freedom from consequence: they can quarrel and laugh because nothing that happens among them is final. Demodocus' song of Ares and Aphrodite caught in Hephaestus' net (Odyssey 8.266–366) repeats the pattern, with the same laughter at 8.326.

The theomachy

In Book 20 Zeus lifts his ban and the gods go to war with one another. The opening is cosmic. Zeus thunders, Poseidon shakes the earth, and Hades leaps from his throne in fear that the ground will split and expose his house (20.54–66). Then the battle is postponed. When it resumes (21.385–513) it is farce. Athena fells Ares with a stone and knocks down Aphrodite. Apollo declines to fight Poseidon. Hera boxes Artemis' ears with her own bow, and Artemis runs weeping to her father's lap (21.489–513). Hermes concedes to Leto without striking a blow. Zeus watches, and his heart laughs with joy (21.389–90).

The meaning lies in the contrast with the plain below. On Olympus nobody dies: a wound heals and a defeat ends in a sulk. The theomachy sits between Achilles' fight with the river and his killing of Hector, so the gods' war reads as a parody of the human war around it. Pseudo-Longinus (On the Sublime 9.6–8) put the problem precisely: Homer makes his men gods and his gods men, and the battle of the gods is terrifying but, unless taken as allegory, wholly godless.

The Odyssey's different theology

The Odyssey opens on Olympus with a theodicy. Zeus complains that mortals blame the gods for their troubles, when "they themselves, through their own recklessness, suffer beyond their portion" (1.32–4). His example is Aegisthus, whom Hermes warned and who went ahead anyway (1.35–43). The proem has already said that Odysseus' companions died "by their own recklessness" (atasthaliai, 1.7) when they ate the cattle of the Sun. The suitors' destruction makes the same point at length, and Laertes draws the conclusion: the gods still exist on Olympus, if the suitors have paid (24.351–2). Gods visit men in the guise of strangers to watch their violence and their good order (17.485–7).

Athena's patronage is different in kind as well. In the Iliad she favours heroes. In the Odyssey she has a sustained, almost collegial bond with Odysseus, founded on shared intelligence: she cannot abandon him, she says, because he is so smooth-spoken, quick-witted and shrewd (13.330–2). Against this stands Poseidon's grudge over the blinding of Polyphemus (1.68–75, with the curse at 9.526–36). That grudge is a personal vendetta, not justice, and Zeus indulges it by letting Poseidon turn the Phaeacian ship to stone (13.125–87).

Where sources disagree. How different is the Odyssey's theology? Kullmann ("Gods and Men in the Iliad and the Odyssey", 1985) argued for a real shift from a capricious Zeus to an ethical one. Lloyd-Jones (The Justice of Zeus, 1971), whom Kullmann was answering, had argued that the Iliad's Zeus already punishes crooked judgements (16.384–92) and broken oaths (4.160–8), so the difference is one of emphasis. Poseidon's grudge shows that the Odyssey has not fully moralised its gods either.

Critics and allegorists

Xenophanes and Plato

The philosophical attack begins with Xenophanes of Colophon in the late sixth century. Homer and Hesiod, he wrote, gave the gods everything that is shameful among men: theft, adultery and deceiving one another (fr. B11 DK; cf. B12). He added that men make gods in their own image (B15–16) and proposed instead a single god who does not move (B23–6). Plato's Republic (2.377d–383c, 3.386a–392c) turns the criticism into censorship. It bans Hephaestus' binding of Hera and the battles of the gods (378d), the two jars of Zeus (379d, quoting 24.527–33), Athena's prompting of Pandarus to break the truce (379e), the lying dream of Book 2 (383a), the gods' unquenchable laughter (389a) and the Deception (390b–c). Plato also rejects the defence that these stories carry hidden meanings (hyponoiai), because the young cannot tell allegory from literal sense (378d).

The Alexandrians

The Hellenistic editors (Zenodotus, Aristophanes of Byzantium and above all Aristarchus) dealt with the problem in the text itself. Lines judged unfitting (aprepes) for gods or heroes were marked with the obelos as suspect, among them the Judgement at 24.25–30 and Zeus' list of lovers at 14.317–27. Aristotle had already given a more relaxed answer: some things said about the gods are neither better nor true, "but that is what people say" (Poetics 25, 1460b35–1461a1, with a reference to Xenophanes).

Allegory

The other defence was allegory. Theagenes of Rhegium (c. 525 BC), the first Homeric critic we know of, read the theomachy in two ways, according to a scholion on Iliad 20.67 that goes back to Porphyry. Physically, it is a war of elements, with Apollo and Hephaestus as fire against Poseidon as water. Morally, it is a war of qualities, with Athena as wisdom against Ares as folly and Aphrodite as desire. Metrodorus of Lampsacus went further and turned the heroes into parts of the cosmos. The Stoics systematised allegory through etymology. Heraclitus' Homeric Problems (first century AD) states the stakes in its opening: if Homer meant nothing allegorically, he was impious throughout. Heraclitus reads the plague of Book 1 as summer sickness sent by the sun, and Hera hung between the anvils as the ordering of the elements. By the time of Porphyry's On the Cave of the Nymphs, the Neoplatonists had made Homer a theologian.

If the gods made it happen, who is responsible?

The poems ask this question before any critic does. On the wall Priam tells Helen, "you are not to blame in my eyes; the gods are to blame" (3.164). Helen blames herself (3.173–80, 6.344–8) and then gives the poem's most startling answer. Zeus laid an evil fate on her and Paris, she says, so that they might be a song for people yet to come (6.357–8). In the Odyssey Alcinous says the same of Troy (8.579–80). Agamemnon blames Zeus, Moira and the Erinys, and still pays.

Later writers kept pressing. Gorgias' Encomium of Helen argues that if the gods compelled her, she is blameless. In Euripides' Trojan Women Hecuba answers that "Aphrodite" is only a name for Helen's own folly: her mind saw Paris and became Cypris (987–90). Homer's position is neither of these. Divine causation and human responsibility run together, and the gods give suffering a purpose by making it worth singing about. That theodicy of song is the Iliad's answer, and its most disturbing one.

Self-test

  1. What does Iliad 1.5 assert, and what two identifications of the "plan of Zeus" did ancient readers offer?
    Answer

    That the plan (boulē) of Zeus was being accomplished through Achilles' wrath. The narrow reading identifies it with Zeus' promise to Thetis to honour Achilles (1.508–10, 2.1–4); the broad reading, from the scholia quoting the Cypria (fr. 1 West), identifies it with Zeus' plan to relieve the overburdened Earth by means of the Trojan War.

  2. In the scholion quoting the Cypria, what alternative methods did Momus advise against, and what did he propose instead?
    Answer

    He advised against thunderbolts and floods, and proposed marrying Thetis to a mortal and fathering a beautiful daughter, Helen. Achilles and Helen together would bring about the war that thins out mankind.

  3. Name the three passages in which Zeus' scales appear in the Iliad narrative, and what each decides.
    Answer

    8.68–74: the Achaean fates sink and Zeus thunders against them. 16.658: Hector recognises the turning of Zeus' sacred scales and flees. 22.208–13: the death-fates of Achilles and Hector are weighed; Hector's sinks and Apollo leaves him.

  4. What argument does Hera use at 16.443–9 to stop Zeus saving Sarpedon, and why does it matter to the question of fate?
    Answer

    She says he may do it, but the other gods will not approve, and every god will then want to save a favourite son. It suggests that Zeus can act "beyond fate" but refrains because doing so would destroy the shared divine order. Fate is binding as a social and cosmic constraint, not as a physical impossibility.

  5. Where is the Judgement of Paris mentioned in the Iliad, and how did Aristarchus and Reinhardt respond?
    Answer

    Only at 24.25–30. Aristarchus athetised those six lines, partly because he thought Homer did not know the story and partly because the word machlosynē seemed un-Homeric. Reinhardt (1938) argued the poet knew it and deliberately withheld it until the end.

  6. Why does Poseidon hate Troy, and what makes the motive awkward?
    Answer

    Laomedon cheated him and Apollo of their wages for building the wall and herding cattle (21.441–60). The awkwardness: Apollo suffered the same fraud yet defends Troy, and Poseidon himself rescues the Trojan Aeneas (20.293–308) because Dardanus' line must survive.

  7. What features of 1.188–222 support Lesky's double motivation over the view that Athena removes Achilles' agency?
    Answer

    Achilles' deliberation is narrated before she arrives; she gives him reasons (threefold gifts, 1.213–14); and she makes her intervention conditional, "if you will obey" (1.207). He then chooses to comply.

  8. How does the theomachy of Book 21 differ in tone from its opening in Book 20, and what was pseudo-Longinus' verdict?
    Answer

    Book 20 opens cosmically, with Hades leaping from his throne in fear that the earth will split (20.54–66); Book 21 is farce, with Hera boxing Artemis' ears with her own bow and Hermes conceding to Leto. Pseudo-Longinus (On the Sublime 9.6–8) said Homer makes gods into men, and the theomachy, unless taken as allegory, is wholly godless.

  9. What is the key claim of Zeus' speech at Odyssey 1.32–43, and what element of the poem resists a fully moral reading?
    Answer

    That mortals suffer beyond their portion through their own recklessness (atasthaliai), with Aegisthus as the example, warned by Hermes and ignoring him. Poseidon's personal grudge over Polyphemus, which Zeus indulges by letting him petrify the Phaeacian ship (13.125–87), is vendetta rather than justice.

  10. What did Theagenes of Rhegium and Heraclitus the Allegorist each do with Homer's gods?
    Answer

    Theagenes (c. 525 BC; scholion on Iliad 20.67) read the theomachy physically as elements in conflict and morally as qualities in conflict, Athena wisdom against Ares folly. Heraclitus (Homeric Problems, 1st century AD) argued that if Homer meant nothing allegorically he was impious throughout, and read, for example, Book 1's plague as sun-sent sickness and Athena's grip on Achilles' hair as reason.

Carry forward

  1. In Homer, divine and human causation are not alternatives. The gods explain an act without excusing it, and those who invoke them, like Agamemnon, still pay.
  2. Fate is the shape of the story. Zeus could overturn it but will not, because the cosmic order and the tradition both depend on it, and the gods' pity is real feeling without the power to undo mortality.
  3. Every later Greek answer to Homer's gods is one of three moves: moralise them (the Odyssey, Plato), correct the text (the Alexandrians), or allegorise them (Theagenes to Heraclitus). The same three moves recur throughout the rest of the course.
