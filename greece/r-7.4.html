01Achaea: a province of monuments and a town of lecture halls

Mainland Greece had been Roman since Mummius sacked Corinth in 146 BC; what changed in 27 BC was administrative. Augustus detached Achaea from Macedonia and made it a senatorial province governed by a proconsul of praetorian rank, seated at Corinth, which Caesar had refounded in 44 BC as the colony Laus Iulia Corinthiensis. Tiberius merged Achaea back into a large imperial command with Macedonia and Moesia in AD 15; Claudius returned it to the senate in 44 (Tacitus, Annals 1.76; Suetonius, Claudius 25). Nero's "liberation" of Greece in 67 lasted until Vespasian revoked it (Pausanias 7.17.3–4).

Augustus founded Nicopolis opposite Actium and settled veterans at Patrae; Strabo describes much of the Peloponnese as depopulated (8.6.20–23). The old cult centres survived on Roman money.

Athens had no political weight and immense cultural capital. Cicero's son and Horace (Epistles 2.2.45) had studied there; under the Empire this became institutional. Marcus Aurelius in 176 endowed salaried chairs at Athens for the four philosophical schools, Platonic, Peripatetic, Stoic and Epicurean, and for rhetoric (Dio Cassius 72(71).31.3, the Loeb's book with the traditional book in brackets; Philostratus, Lives of the Sophists 566). The ephebate became a gentlemen's gymnasium for Athenian and foreign youths. The Herulian sack of 267 wrecked the lower city, yet the schools recovered and still drew Basil, Gregory of Nazianzus and the future emperor Julian in the 350s.

02Hadrian and the Panhellenion

Hadrian (117–138) held the Athenian archonship in 112 and visited three times as emperor (124/5, 128/9, 131/2). He completed the Olympieion begun six centuries before, built the library north of the Roman Agora, reformed the olive-oil law (IG II² 1100) and was initiated at Eleusis. The arch near the Olympieion carries two inscriptions: "This is Athens, the former city of Theseus" on one face and "This is the city of Hadrian, not of Theseus" on the other.

In 131/2 he founded the Panhellenion, a league of cities that could prove Greek descent, with its council and temple at Athens and its own quadrennial games. Inscriptions show cities from Asia Minor, Crete and Cyrene submitting foundation myths as evidence; many Hellenistic foundations could not qualify. It lasted about a century and mattered less politically than as a formal audit of Greek identity under imperial patronage.

Where sources disagree. Spawforth and Walker (1985–86), who reconstructed the Panhellenion from epigraphy, read it as Hadrian's own design to bind the Greek East to Rome through a shared antiquarian ideal. Romeo (2002) and others have since argued that the initiative came from Greek cities, with Hadrian ratifying rather than inventing it. The literary sources (Dio Cassius 69.16; Pausanias 1.18.9) say only that Hadrian built the temple and founded the festival.

03The Second Sophistic

The phrase is Philostratus' own. In his Lives of the Sophists (481), written in the 230s, he distinguishes the old sophistic of Gorgias from a "second" one he traces back to Aeschines, and which he treats as flourishing in his own age. Its practitioners were wealthy public speakers who declaimed on historical and mythical themes in Attic Greek, served as ambassadors to emperors and spent fortunes on buildings.

Dio of Prusa, "Chrysostom", exiled under Domitian and restored under Trajan, wrote the Olympic Oration (Or. 12), a theology of Phidias' Zeus, and the Euboean (Or. 7), idealising rural poverty. Aelius Aristides of Smyrna (117–after 181) wrote the Panathenaicus (Or. 1), a vast praise of Athens, To Rome (Or. 26) praising the empire, and the Sacred Tales (Or. 47–52), a diary of illness and dreams sent by Asclepius at Pergamum, our most intimate record of a pagan's religious life. Herodes Atticus (101–177), consul in 143 and tutor to Marcus Aurelius, rebuilt the Panathenaic stadium in marble and built the Odeion below the Acropolis; Philostratus (VS 545–566) gives him his longest life, including his trial before Marcus at Sirmium in 174. Lucian of Samosata (c. 120–after 180), a Syrian who learnt Greek as a second language, turned the same training to satire: Dialogues of the Gods, Zeus Rants, Alexander the False Prophet, On the Death of Peregrinus and A True Story mock gods, oracle-mongers and philosophers from inside their own idiom.

Where sources disagree. Bowersock (1969) treated the sophists chiefly as power brokers between cities and court. Bowie (1970) read their archaism as compensation for political loss. Swain (1996) and Whitmarsh (2001) see a confident negotiation of Greek identity within Rome rather than nostalgia. Brunt (1994) questioned whether "Second Sophistic" names a real movement at all or only Philostratus' canon.

04Plutarch and Pausanias: the great witnesses

Plutarch of Chaeronea (c. 45–after 120), Roman citizen, Platonist and priest of Apollo at Delphi, is the single most important bridge between classical Greek religion and us. The Parallel Lives, twenty-three surviving pairs, preserve otherwise lost traditions on Theseus, Lycurgus and Numa; the preface to Theseus openly marks the boundary where history gives way to "the mythical and the tragic". The Moralia contains the Delphic dialogues (On the E at Delphi, On the Pythian Oracles, On the Obsolescence of Oracles), debating why the oracle now answers in prose and why shrines have fallen silent, and On Isis and Osiris, our fullest Greek account of Egyptian myth, read allegorically. The death of Pan announced to a ship off Paxi (De defectu 419b–d) and the Greek Questions show a man collecting a world he felt thinning.

Pausanias, probably from Lydia, travelled mainland Greece between about 150 and 180 and wrote the ten books of the Description of Greece, from Attica (1) through Olympia (5–6) to Delphi (10). He records sanctuaries, cult images, rituals and local myths told by priests and guides, with the Olympian Zeus of Phidias (5.11) and the Arcadian cults of Lycosura (8.37–38) among his most valuable passages. At 8.8.3 he says that he once dismissed Greek legends as foolishness but, at the Arcadian stage of his work, came to think them riddles of ancient wisdom.

Where sources disagree. Wilamowitz and much nineteenth-century scholarship treated Pausanias as an armchair compiler copying Hellenistic guidebooks. Habicht (1985) rehabilitated him as an autoptic traveller, and this is now the consensus, though how far his religious reflections are his own rather than conventional remains debated (Elsner, Hutton).

05Galen and Ptolemy

Galen of Pergamum (129–c. 216) trained at Smyrna and Alexandria and from 169 served Marcus Aurelius and his successors in Rome. His output, roughly a tenth of all surviving Greek literature before AD 300, includes On Anatomical Procedures, On the Natural Faculties, On the Usefulness of the Parts and the autobiographical On My Own Books. He welded Hippocratic humoral theory, Platonic teleology and Aristotelian logic into a system that governed medicine until Vesalius and Harvey. He dissected apes and pigs, not humans, so his anatomy errs in ways his authority long concealed. His death date is uncertain: the Suda gives seventy years, Arabic sources eighty-seven.

Claudius Ptolemy (c. 100–170) worked at Alexandria. The Mathematical Syntaxis, known as the Almagest from its Arabic title, sets out in thirteen books a geocentric model with epicycles and the equant that predicted planetary positions well enough to stand for fourteen centuries. The Tetrabiblos systematised astrology; the Geography gave coordinates for eight thousand places; the Harmonics and Optics treated sound and refraction mathematically. Both men were Greek in language and Roman in patronage, and neither saw a tension.

Where sources disagree. R. R. Newton (1977) accused Ptolemy of fabricating observations to fit Hipparchus' parameters. Most historians of astronomy (Toomer, Jones, Swerdlow) reject fraud, reading the discrepancies as ancient practices of selecting and rounding data rather than modern dishonesty.

06The religious marketplace

Civic cult continued, funded by the same families who paid for the sophists' buildings, with the imperial cult housed alongside the old gods. But the Empire made movement easy, and with movement came choice. Eleusis initiated Augustus, Hadrian, Lucius Verus and Marcus Aurelius (176), and Pausanias refuses to describe its interior rites (1.38.7). Isis had temples across the Greek world (Apuleius, Metamorphoses 11). Asclepius flourished at Epidaurus and Pergamum, where Aristides lived in the sanctuary for two years. Mithras barely appears in Greece itself. Delphi declined while Claros and Didyma answered theological questions about the highest god, and the Chaldaean Oracles of the late second century provided the Neoplatonists with scripture. Lucian's Alexander shows a new oracle of the snake-god Glycon being founded at Abonoteichus and acquiring Roman senatorial clients.

Where sources disagree. Nock (1933) and Cumont saw the "oriental" cults as a spiritual stepping stone toward Christianity. Burkert (1987) argued that the mysteries were options within polytheism, not religions of salvation competing with the Church. The "marketplace" model itself (North 1992) has been criticised for importing modern consumer assumptions into a world where civic cult was never optional.

07Neoplatonism

Plotinus (204/5–270), an Egyptian who studied with Ammonius Saccas in Alexandria and taught at Rome from 244, reorganised Plato into a hierarchy: the One beyond being, from which Intellect and Soul emanate down to matter; the philosopher's task is return by contemplation. His treatises were edited after his death by Porphyry into six groups of nine, the Enneads, with a Life of Plotinus prefixed; Ennead I.6 On Beauty and VI.9 On the Good or the One are the best entry points. Porphyry of Tyre (c. 234–305) wrote the Isagoge that became the medieval logic primer, On the Cave of the Nymphs allegorising Odyssey 13, and fifteen books Against the Christians, burned in 448.

Iamblichus of Chalcis (c. 245–325), teaching at Apamea, answered Porphyry's sceptical Letter to Anebo with On the Mysteries, a defence of theurgy: ritual acts that draw the gods down rather than the soul up alone. The Athenian school, refounded by Plutarch of Athens (died 432) and led by Syrianus and then Proclus (412–485), made this the official philosophy of the Academy's last century. Proclus' Elements of Theology, Platonic Theology and commentaries on the Timaeus, Parmenides and Republic are systematic to the point of architecture; Marinus' Life of Proclus 30 shows him praying to Athena as her image left the Parthenon. The emperor Julian (361–363), pupil of Maximus of Ephesus, tried to make this tradition the state religion and died in Persia before it could take.

Where sources disagree. Dodds (1951) and most earlier scholarship read theurgy as Platonism's decline into magic. Shaw (1995) and Addey (2014) argue it is a coherent ritual theology grounded in the Timaeus. Whether Plotinus himself practised any rite remains uncertain: Porphyry reports a séance in the Iseum at Rome (Life 10) but also his master's indifference to civic cult.

08Christianity in the Greek cities

Paul reached Greece around AD 49–51. Acts 17:16–34 places him at Athens disputing with Epicureans and Stoics, taken to the Areopagus, citing the altar "to an unknown god" and quoting Greek poetry (the line "in him we live and move and have our being" echoes Epimenides; "we are his offspring" is Aratus, Phaenomena 5). The response is polite indifference. At Corinth he stayed eighteen months, and the Delphi inscription dating Gallio's proconsulship to 51/52 fixes the chronology (Acts 18:12).

The Church's intellectuals were trained in the same schools as the sophists. Justin Martyr (Dialogue with Trypho 2–8) tried Stoic, Peripatetic and Platonist teachers before conversion, and identified John's Logos with the Stoics'. Clement of Alexandria's Stromateis treats Greek philosophy as a tutor that led the Greeks to Christ as the Law led the Jews. Origen's Against Celsus preserves most of a Platonist's attack of around 178 by answering it. In the 350s Basil of Caesarea and Gregory of Nazianzus studied rhetoric at Athens (Gregory, Or. 43); Basil's Address to Young Men tells Christians to read Homer and Plato selectively, as bees take from flowers. In the early sixth century an anonymous Syrian writing as Dionysius the Areopagite recast Proclus' hierarchies as angelic orders; Koch and Stiglmayr demonstrated the dependence in 1895. Tertullian's "What has Athens to do with Jerusalem?" (De praescriptione haereticorum 7) is the Latin exception, not the Greek rule.

Where sources disagree. Harnack's thesis of the "Hellenisation of Christianity", a Greek metaphysical overlay on a Semitic gospel, dominated a century of scholarship. Recent work (Edwards, Boys-Stones) stresses that Second Temple Judaism was already Greek-speaking and philosophically engaged, so there was no un-Hellenised Christianity to corrupt.

09The long ending, 391 to 529

The conventional endpoints are legal and symbolic rather than a single moment.

Theodosius' laws of 391 and 392 forbade sacrifice, divination and temple entry. Enforcement varied; Libanius (Or. 30) pleads against monks demolishing Syrian shrines, and Athens, with a pagan elite still in office, kept its festivals longer than most. Alaric burned Eleusis in 396 and the mysteries never resumed. Phidias' Zeus was taken to Constantinople and perished in the Lausus palace fire of 475.

At Alexandria, Hypatia, a Neoplatonist mathematician and daughter of Theon, was killed by a Christian mob in March 415 during the conflict between the prefect Orestes and the bishop Cyril. Socrates Scholasticus, a Christian historian, calls it a disgrace to the Church; Damascius (Life of Isidore) blames Cyril directly; the seventh-century John of Nikiu calls her a witch.

The Parthenon lost the statue of Athena before Proclus' death in 485, and at some point between the late fifth and late sixth century an apse was cut into its east end, the entrance reversed to the west, and it was dedicated to the Theotokos, the Mother of God. It served as a church for nearly a thousand years. In 529 Justinian forbade pagans to teach, and Malalas records an order to Athens that no one teach philosophy. Damascius, the last head of the Academy, and Simplicius went to the Persian court of Khusro I and returned under the peace of 532.

Where sources disagree. The 393 Olympic date rests on Cedrenus, who says only that the games ceased under Theodosius I; a bronze victor list found at Olympia in 1994 runs to 385, and some scholars (Lee, Remijsen) think the games continued until the Zeus temple burned under Theodosius II around 426. Alan Cameron (1969) argued the Academy was not actually closed in 529 and teaching persisted; Watts (2006) answers that the ban ended the school as an institution even if individuals lingered. Tardieu's thesis that Simplicius then settled at Harran, in Mesopotamia, is widely cited and widely doubted. The Parthenon's conversion date is uncertain by a century.

10How the Byzantine scribes saved it

Everything in this course that is not inscription or papyrus reached us through Byzantine copyists. The shift from papyrus roll to parchment codex in the second to fourth centuries forced a first selection: what was not recopied was lost. Schooling narrowed the canon to seven plays each of Aeschylus and Sophocles, ten of Euripides, Homer, the orators and school prose. A second filter came in the ninth century when minuscule script replaced uncial; texts transliterated into the new hand in the next two centuries are the ancestors of our editions. Photius' Bibliotheca summarised 279 volumes he had read, many now lost. Arethas of Caesarea commissioned the Clarke Plato (Bodleian E. D. Clarke 39) from John the Calligrapher in 895; the Venetus A manuscript of the Iliad with its ancient scholia was written in the tenth century; Constantine Cephalas compiled the Palatine Anthology around 900; the Suda lexicon preserved thousands of fragments. The nine "alphabetic" plays of Euripides survive in a single manuscript, Laurentianus 32.2, copied about 1300. After 1204 and again before 1453 Byzantine scholars carried books to Italy, where Aldus Manutius printed the first editions of Aristotle, Aristophanes and Plato between 1495 and 1513. The classical world is, in a literal sense, what medieval Christian copyists chose to write out.

11Self-test

  1. In which year did Augustus make Achaea a separate senatorial province, and what was its capital?
    Answer

    27 BC; Corinth, refounded by Caesar in 44 BC as Colonia Laus Iulia Corinthiensis.

  2. What did Marcus Aurelius do for Athenian education in 176?
    Answer

    He endowed salaried chairs for the four philosophical schools (Platonic, Peripatetic, Stoic, Epicurean) and for rhetoric (Dio Cassius 72(71).31.3; Philostratus, VS 566).

  3. What was the Panhellenion, and what qualified a city to join?
    Answer

    A league of Greek cities founded by Hadrian in 131/2 with its temple and council at Athens; cities had to demonstrate Greek descent, often through foundation myths.

  4. Who coined the term "Second Sophistic", and in which work?
    Answer

    Philostratus, in the Lives of the Sophists (481), written in the 230s.

  5. Which work of Aelius Aristides records his dreams and cures from Asclepius?
    Answer

    The Sacred Tales (Orations 47–52).

  6. In which Plutarchan dialogue does the death of Pan story appear, and in which do his friends discuss why oracles have fallen silent?
    Answer

    Both are in On the Obsolescence of Oracles (De defectu oraculorum, the Pan story at 419b–d).

  7. Where does Pausanias say he changed his mind about the value of Greek myths?
    Answer

    In Book 8 on Arcadia (8.8.3), where he says he once thought the legends foolish but came to see them as riddles of ancient wisdom.

  8. What is the Isagoge, who wrote it, and why does it matter?
    Answer

    A logic work by Porphyry of Tyre that became the medieval logic primer.

  9. Which two Theodosian laws, with dates, outlawed pagan sacrifice?
    Answer

    Codex Theodosianus 16.10.10 of February 391 and 16.10.12 of November 392, the latter extending the ban to domestic cult.

  10. Why is 393 as the date of the last Olympic Games disputed?
    Answer

    Cedrenus says only that the games ceased under Theodosius I; a victor list from Olympia runs to 385, and some scholars argue they continued until the temple of Zeus burned around 426.

12Carry forward

  1. Roman Greece was not a decline but a reorganisation: political power left, cultural authority stayed, and the people who tell us most about classical cult and myth (Plutarch, Pausanias, the sophists) were Roman citizens writing under Roman peace.
  2. Christianity's Greek theologians were trained by the same schools, in the same texts, as the pagan philosophers they argued with; the Christian doctrine of God and the Neoplatonic One were built from one toolkit, which is why Proclus survives inside Pseudo-Dionysius.
  3. The ending was a process, not an event: laws in 391–392, a sack in 396, a murder in 415, a church in the Parthenon, a school closed in 529. And every text in this course exists only because Byzantine Christian scribes chose to copy it.
