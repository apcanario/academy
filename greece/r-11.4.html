Two lines of descent: Heracles and Achilles

Alexander inherited two heroic genealogies and used them differently. On his father's side the Argead kings of Macedon claimed descent from Temenus of Argos, a great-great-grandson of Heracles. The claim had been tested in public: Herodotus (5.22) reports that Alexander I was admitted to the Olympic games after proving his Argive ancestry. Heracles was the dynasty's god, and Alexander's tetradrachms carried his head in the lion-scalp across the empire. He was the mortal who earned Olympus by labour, and that was the point.

On his mother's side Olympias was a Molossian princess of the Aeacid house, which traced itself through Neoptolemus to Achilles and beyond him to Aeacus, a son of Zeus. Plutarch opens his life with both lines (Alexander 2.1). The Achilles line was literary and personal: he kept an Iliad annotated by Aristotle in a casket taken from Darius (8.2, 26.1–2).

Heracles was the ancestor he invoked to claim things: Tyre, where he demanded to sacrifice to Melqart, the Tyrian Heracles (Arrian, Anabasis 2.15.7–16.7), and Aornos, which Heracles had reputedly failed to take (4.28.1–4). Achilles was the model he invoked to explain himself: the brief glorious life, the beloved companion, the grief.

Troy, 334

The crossing was staged as religious drama. Alexander sacrificed at the tomb of Protesilaus, the first Greek to die at Troy, so that his own landing would be luckier (Arrian 1.11.5), and Diodorus (17.17.2) has him hurl a spear into Asian soil, claiming it as spear-won land.

At Ilium he sacrificed to Athena, dedicated his own armour in her temple and took in exchange arms said to survive from the Trojan War (Arrian 1.11.7–8). One of these, the sacred shield, was carried before him in battle, and in India in 325 Peucestas held it over him as he lay wounded in the Mallian town (6.10.2). He also sacrificed to Priam at the altar of Zeus Herkeios, where Neoptolemus had killed the old king, to avert Priam's anger from Neoptolemus' descendants (1.11.8). This is the Molossian genealogy taken literally: he carried the blood-guilt of his ancestor and paid it off.

He then crowned the tomb of Achilles and called him fortunate to have had Homer as herald of his fame (Arrian 1.12.1).

Where sources disagree. Arrian reports, with a "some say", that Hephaestion crowned the tomb of Patroclus. Plutarch (Alexander 15.8) instead has Alexander anoint himself and run naked round the tomb-stele with his companions, Hephaestion unnamed. Aelian (Varia Historia 12.7) pairs the two men explicitly. The famous image of the two lovers racing round the tombs of Achilles and Patroclus is a modern composite of these accounts; how far the Patroclus parallel was a public statement in 334, rather than a reading back from Hephaestion's death in 324, is uncertain.

Piety in practice: sacrifice, seers and omens

Whatever he believed about himself, his religious conduct was conventional and constant. Macedonian kings were their people's chief priests, and Alexander sacrificed daily; the Royal Journals extracted by Arrian (7.25.2–6) and Plutarch (76) have him carried out on a litter to sacrifice as he lay dying. Their authenticity is disputed, but nothing contradicts the habit.

His chief seer was Aristander of Telmessus, from a Lycian city famous for diviners. Aristander read Philip's dream of sealing Olympias' womb with a lion seal (Plutarch 2.5), the bird that dropped a stone on Alexander at Gaza (Arrian 2.26.4–27.2), the birds eating the barley meal that marked out Alexandria (3.2.1–2), and the eclipse before Gaugamela (3.7.6). On the Jaxartes in 329 he declared the omens bad and held to it, and Alexander was wounded as predicted (4.4.3–9).

Divination was a tool of command: at Tyre, Plutarch (25.1–2) says, Alexander altered the calendar so that Aristander's prophecy came true "this month". But the dependence was real and grew. After Hephaestion's death Plutarch (75.1–2) describes a palace full of sacrificers and purifiers. The Chaldaean warning not to enter Babylon (Arrian 7.16.5–17.6) and the stranger found on the throne (7.24.1–3), which some read as a garbled Babylonian substitute-king ritual (attractive but uncertain), belong here.

Egypt, Siwa and Babylon

The journey

In the winter of 332/1, after the foundation of Alexandria, Alexander went west along the coast to Paraetonium and then south across some 250 kilometres of desert to the oasis of Siwa. Arrian (3.3.1–2) gives the motive as a pothos, a longing, and adds two reasons: the oracle was reputed infallible, and Perseus and Heracles had consulted it, and Alexander "traced part of his birth to Ammon" as the myths traced theirs to Zeus. Ammon, the Egyptian Amun, had long been equated with Zeus through Cyrene; Pindar wrote him a hymn and Lysander consulted him.

Where sources disagree. On the desert crossing, water ran out and the guides lost the way. Rain fell. Then, according to Ptolemy, two talking snakes led the army; according to Aristobulus, two ravens (Arrian 3.3.4–6). Callisthenes also had birds. On the return, Aristobulus says Alexander went back the same way, Ptolemy that he struck straight across to Memphis (3.4.5). Ptolemy, whose dynasty later made the serpent a sign of Alexander's divinity, had his own reasons for snakes.

What the oracle said

The earliest account was Callisthenes', preserved in Strabo (17.1.43). Only Alexander was allowed into the temple in his own dress; the oracle usually answered by nods and tokens, not words as at Delphi; but the prophet told the king in so many words that he was the son of Zeus. Callisthenes added confirmations from Didyma and the Erythraean prophetess Athenais. Strabo calls this flattery, but it was the official version, written for the court within a year or two.

Arrian is strikingly reticent: Alexander asked his question and "received the answer his heart desired, as he said" (3.4.5). We are never told the question. Plutarch (27) offers the fuller tradition: the prophet greeted him as son of Zeus; Alexander asked whether any of his father's murderers had escaped and was told to mind his words, for his father was no mortal; he asked whether he would rule all mankind and was told yes. Plutarch also preserves the rationalising story that the prophet meant to say o paidion, "my child", and mispronounced it o pai Dios, "son of Zeus". Diodorus (17.51) and Curtius are similar. Alexander himself wrote to Olympias that he had received secret answers he would tell to her alone (Plutarch 27.8). He died without telling her.

Where sources disagree. The questions about Philip's murderers and world rule appear only in the later vulgate and look like elaboration of Callisthenes' core. What is secure is the greeting, which any Egyptian priest would give a pharaoh. Whether Alexander heard it in an Egyptian, a Greek or a deliberately ambiguous sense cannot be settled.

Olympias' snake

Siwa connected with stories already circulating. Plutarch (2–3) reports Olympias' dream of a thunderbolt striking her womb, Philip seeing a serpent beside her, and Delphi telling Philip to honour Ammon above all gods. Eratosthenes said Olympias told Alexander the secret of his birth; others had her protest, "Will Alexander never stop slandering me to Hera?" (3.3–4). The claim was argued over while people who knew the family were alive.

Pharaoh and king of Babylon

In Egypt divine sonship was a constitutional fact: every pharaoh was son of Ra and of Amun. Alexander's hieroglyphic titulary gave him the throne name Setepenre Meryamun, "chosen of Ra, beloved of Amun", and the barque shrine built in his name in Luxor temple shows him as pharaoh before Amun.

Where sources disagree. A formal coronation at Memphis appears only in the Alexander Romance (Pseudo-Callisthenes 1.34). Arrian, Plutarch, Diodorus and Curtius do not mention one, and several modern scholars doubt it took place. The titulary shows that the priesthood treated him as pharaoh; it does not prove a ceremony.

At Babylon in 331 he sacrificed to Bel-Marduk on the instructions of the Chaldaeans and ordered the rebuilding of Esagila, which he believed Xerxes had destroyed (Arrian 3.16.4–5, 7.17.1–3). Babylonian documents, including the astronomical diaries, style him "king of the world", the old Mesopotamian royal title. Babylonian kingship was not divine, but the king held office from Marduk and stood at the centre of temple ritual. In both places Alexander found subjects ready to grant a sacred status that Macedonians and Greeks would not.

Proskynesis at Bactra

In 327, at Bactra, Alexander tried to extend Persian court ceremonial to his Macedonians and Greeks. Arrian (4.10.5–12.5) gives a set debate in which Anaxarchus argues that Alexander will be honoured as a god after death, so better now, and Callisthenes replies that honours to men and gods must be kept distinct; Curtius (8.5.5–8.6.1) has a similar one. These speeches are largely literary. The better evidence is the eyewitness chamberlain Chares of Mytilene, used by Plutarch (54.3–6) and Arrian (4.12.3–5). At a drinking party a cup was passed round; each guest drank, performed proskynesis, and received a kiss from the king. Callisthenes drank but skipped the bow, and was refused the kiss. "I go away the poorer by a kiss," he said. The experiment was dropped for Macedonians.

What the gesture meant

To a Persian, proskynesis was social. Herodotus (1.134) describes the scale: equals kissed on the lips, near-equals on the cheek, inferiors prostrated themselves. The Great King received it as the summit of a social hierarchy, not as a god; Achaemenid kings did not claim divinity. To a Greek the same act was what one did before a god. When the Spartan envoys Sperthias and Bulis refused to prostrate themselves before Xerxes, they said it was not their custom to worship a man (Herodotus 7.136). Isocrates (Panegyricus 151) mocks Persians for doing obeisance to a mortal and calling him a divinity. Callisthenes could therefore refuse in the name of Greek piety, and Alexander could deny that he was asking for worship, and both could be sincere.

Where sources disagree. Balsdon (1950) argued the attempt was about unifying court protocol and implied no claim to divinity. Bosworth and others reply that Alexander knew how Greeks would read the gesture. Callisthenes was arrested after the Pages' Conspiracy later in 327 and either hanged after torture (Ptolemy) or died in chains (Aristobulus) (Arrian 4.14.3). The same man had earlier reported the Siwa greeting and Alexander's prayer at Gaugamela to the gods "if he were really sprung from Zeus" (Plutarch 33.1). The court historian had promoted the sonship; he drew the line at its consequences.

Dionysus as model

From Bactria onward Dionysus joins Heracles, and the connection began with guilt. Thebes, Dionysus' birthplace, was razed in 335, and Plutarch (13.4) says Alexander later blamed the god's anger for the murder of Cleitus, killed in 328 on a day sacred to Dionysus when Alexander had sacrificed to the Dioscuri instead (Arrian 4.8.1–2, 4.9.5).

In 326, at Nysa, envoys led by Acuphis claimed their city had been founded by Dionysus. Ivy grew on the nearby mountain Meros, "thigh", recalling the god's birth from Zeus' thigh. Alexander spared the city and his officers held a Bacchic revel in ivy wreaths (Arrian 5.1.1–2.7). Arrian cites Eratosthenes' view (5.3.1–4) that the Macedonians invented such discoveries, like Heracles' failure at Aornos, to flatter Alexander. By 323 Arrian (7.20.1) reports him thinking himself worthy to be a third god of the Arabs beside Ouranos and Dionysus.

Where sources disagree. On the return through Carmania in 325, Plutarch (67) and Curtius (9.10.24–29) describe a seven-day drunken procession in imitation of Dionysus' triumph. Arrian (6.28.1–2) disbelieves it, since neither Ptolemy nor Aristobulus mentions it. Worse, Dionysus' conquest of India is not clearly attested before Alexander: Euripides' Bacchae (13–20) brings the god from Bactria and Persia. Probably, though not certainly, Alexander imitated a myth his own campaign helped create.

Bosworth ("Alexander, Euripides and Dionysos", 1996) made the Dionysus model central to the drive for divinity: a son of Zeus who wins recognition by conquering the East and punishing deniers. Others see a court theme pushed by flatterers.

324: divine honours and Hephaestion

In the summer of 324 Nicanor of Stagira read out at Olympia a royal letter ordering the Greek cities to take back their exiles (Diodorus 18.8.2–5; 17.109.1). This broke the League of Corinth's terms and threatened Athens' hold on Samos. In the same months the Greek cities debated granting Alexander divine honours, and in 323 sacred envoys came to him at Babylon wearing crowns, "as though to honour a god" (Arrian 7.23.2).

The responses survive as anecdote. At Sparta, Damis proposed: "Since Alexander wishes to be a god, let him be a god" (Plutarch, Moralia 219e; Aelian, Varia Historia 2.19). At Athens, Demades proposed the honours and was later fined for it (Athenaeus 6.251b; Aelian, Varia Historia 5.12; the sum varies). Lycurgus asked what kind of god one had to purify oneself after visiting (Plutarch, Moralia 842d). Demosthenes, who first opposed the honours, is quoted by his prosecutors as conceding that Alexander could be son of Zeus, and of Poseidon too if he liked (Hyperides, Against Demosthenes; Dinarchus, Against Demosthenes 1.94). Greeks found the honours absurd, conceded them anyway, and reversed them once he was dead.

Where sources disagree. No ancient source quotes a request from Alexander. Tarn (1948) argued he demanded deification as a constitutional device, since a god could issue orders like the Exiles' Decree that a League hegemon could not. Balsdon (1950) found no evidence of a request. Habicht argued the cities offered cult spontaneously, as to earlier benefactors. Something prompted the debates of 324; whether a royal letter, an informal signal or civic initiative is uncertain.

Hephaestion's heroisation

Hephaestion died at Ecbatana in autumn 324. Alexander cut his hair like Achilles over Patroclus (Arrian 7.14.4), planned a pyre at Babylon said to cost ten or twelve thousand talents (Diodorus 17.115; Plutarch 72), and sent to Siwa to ask what honours were due. Arrian (7.14.7, 7.23.6) says Ammon permitted sacrifice to Hephaestion as a hero, and quotes a letter to Cleomenes in Egypt ordering hero-shrines at Alexandria and on Pharos. A votive relief from Pella dedicated to Hephaestion as hero suggests the cult was actually practised.

Where sources disagree. Diodorus (17.115.6) says Ammon allowed sacrifice to Hephaestion as a god. Arrian's "hero" is more cautious and fits the Pella evidence. Either way, Alexander treated Ammon as the authority who could raise a mortal above the human, a power he could hardly deny himself.

Did he believe it?

Ancient writers already argued the point. Plutarch (28) says he used the belief to dominate barbarians but was restrained with Greeks; wounded, he quoted Homer: this is blood, not the ichor of the gods (Iliad 5.340). Arrian's verdict (7.29.3) is cautious: referring his birth to a god was perhaps a device to impress his subjects, and not a great fault. Yet Arrian also says that a man so unlike others was born "not without the divine" (7.30.2).

The modern positions:

  • Tarn (Alexander the Great, 1948): a rationalist who used deification as a political instrument without literal belief.
  • Balsdon ("The 'Divinity' of Alexander", Historia 1950): deflationary; the evidence for official claims is thin and much is later invention.
  • Badian (from "Alexander the Great and the Loneliness of Power", 1962): the belief grew as power isolated him; a symptom of autocracy, not a calculation.
  • Fredricksmeyer ("Alexander, Zeus Ammon, and the Conquest of Asia", 1991): sincere conviction, rooted in Macedonian religion and Philip's precedent, that the sonship was real and divinity could be won by achievement.
  • Bosworth (Conquest and Empire, 1988): he came to believe himself son of Zeus and sought recognition as a god in his lifetime.

The choice between belief and policy is probably false. For a Greek, divine sonship did not exclude a mortal father, and divine honours could be earned by deeds, as Heracles earned them. A king could believe this and also see its political value. What the evidence shows is growth: Siwa in 331, Bactra in 327, the cult debates of 324.

Ruler cult and the new idea of a king

Alexander did not invent cult for the living. Lysander received altars, sacrifices and paeans at Samos (Plutarch, Lysander 18, citing Duris), and Philip had his statue carried as a thirteenth beside the twelve gods at Aegae on the day of his murder (Diodorus 16.92.5) and built the Philippeion at Olympia (Pausanias 5.20.9–10). Fredricksmeyer stresses these precedents. But Alexander made the practice general and permanent. Ptolemy hijacked his corpse and founded a state cult of Alexander at Alexandria; Lysimachus' coins showed him with Ammon's ram's horns. Within a generation the Athenians sang to Demetrius Poliorcetes, who was present and visible, that:

the other gods are far away, or have no ears

(Athenaeus 6.253d–f, from Duris). Seleucid and Ptolemaic dynastic cult, and in time the Roman imperial cult, descend from this.

Three things changed in the Greek idea of a king. First, legitimacy moved from ancestry to achievement. Homeric kings were "Zeus-nurtured" and the Argeads had their Temenid pedigree, but the Hellenistic king was Soter or Euergetes, saviour or benefactor, his divinity proved by victory and benefaction. Second, cult became a language between a city and a power it could not control: "godlike honours" let a free city acknowledge a master without formally surrendering autonomy, exactly as the Greeks handled Alexander in 324. Third, Greek kingship absorbed the sacred kingships of Egypt and Mesopotamia; pharaoh, king of the world and son of Zeus became one office. Alexander's empire broke up within a generation; his religious model of kingship lasted far longer.

Self-test

  1. Why did Alexander sacrifice to Priam at the altar of Zeus Herkeios at Troy, and which genealogy does this act take literally?
    Answer

    To avert Priam's anger from the descendants of Neoptolemus, who had killed Priam at that altar. It treats his maternal Molossian (Aeacid) descent from Achilles through Neoptolemus as real, including the inherited blood-guilt (Arrian 1.11.8).

  2. What happened to the sacred shield Alexander took from the temple of Athena at Ilium?
    Answer

    It was carried before him in battle, and in 325, in the Mallian town in India, Peucestas held it over him as he lay badly wounded (Arrian 6.10.2).

  3. Name three omens interpreted by Aristander of Telmessus.
    Answer

    Any three of: Philip's dream of the lion seal on Olympias' womb; the sweating statue of Orpheus; the bird dropping a stone at Gaza; the birds eating the barley meal at Alexandria; the lunar eclipse before Gaugamela; the eagle at Gaugamela; the unfavourable sacrifices at the Jaxartes.

  4. On the desert route to Siwa, what guided the army according to Ptolemy, and according to Aristobulus?
    Answer

    Ptolemy said two talking snakes; Aristobulus said two ravens (Arrian 3.3.4–6). Callisthenes also had birds.

  5. What does Callisthenes' account (in Strabo 17.1.43) say about how the Siwa oracle normally answered, and what exception did it make for Alexander?
    Answer

    It normally answered by nods and tokens rather than words, as at Delphi, but the prophet explicitly told Alexander in words that he was the son of Zeus.

  6. What is the o paidion story in Plutarch, and why does the Egyptian context make the greeting less surprising?
    Answer

    Plutarch reports that the prophet meant to say "my child" (o paidion) in Greek and mispronounced it as "son of Zeus" (o pai Dios). In Egypt any pharaoh was by definition son of Amun-Ra, so a priest greeting the ruler of Egypt as son of the god was following standard usage.

  7. Why could Callisthenes refuse proskynesis on grounds of piety while Alexander denied asking for worship?
    Answer

    For Persians, proskynesis was a social gesture of deference to a superior, and the Great King was not a god (Herodotus 1.134). For Greeks, prostration was an act of worship due only to gods (Herodotus 7.136; Isocrates, Panegyricus 151). The same act meant different things to the two peoples.

  8. Why does Arrian doubt the Carmanian revel, and what did Eratosthenes say about the Dionysus discoveries at Nysa?
    Answer

    Arrian notes that neither Ptolemy nor Aristobulus mentions the revel, so he does not believe it (6.28.1–2). Eratosthenes held that the Macedonians invented the traces of Dionysus, like Prometheus' cave and Heracles at Aornos, to flatter Alexander (Arrian 5.3.1–4).

  9. Give the Spartan and Demosthenic responses to the question of divine honours in 324.
    Answer

    Damis at Sparta: since Alexander wishes to be a god, let him be a god. Demosthenes, as his prosecutors quote him: let him be son of Zeus, and of Poseidon too if he likes. Both concede the honours while mocking them.

  10. How do Arrian and Diodorus differ on Ammon's reply about Hephaestion, and which version is better supported?
    Answer

    Arrian (7.14.7, 7.23.6) says Ammon allowed sacrifice to him as a hero; Diodorus (17.115.6) says as a god. Arrian's version is better supported by the letter to Cleomenes ordering hero-shrines and by the votive relief from Pella to Hephaestion as hero.

Carry forward

  1. Alexander's divinity was built in stages from inherited material: Heracleid and Aeacid descent, the Siwa greeting of 331, which was also standard pharaonic address, the proskynesis experiment of 327, and the cult debates of 324. Treat any single episode as a point on a curve, not as the whole claim.
  2. The same ritual meant different things to different peoples. Proskynesis, divine sonship and sacred kingship were ordinary in Persia, Egypt and Babylon and offensive or comic to Greeks, and much of the conflict came from that gap.
  3. Ruler cult was Alexander's most durable political invention. It moved Greek kingship from pedigree to achievement and benefaction, and gave cities a way to acknowledge masters they could not resist. Hellenistic and Roman monarchy grew directly from it.
