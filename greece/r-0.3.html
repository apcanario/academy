The nine periods and their boundaries

Every date in Greek history before about 500 BC is a convention agreed by modern scholars, and most of the period names are modern too. "Minoan" was coined by Arthur Evans after 1900, "Mycenaean" came into use after Schliemann's excavations of 1876, "Hellenistic" was Droysen's invention of 1836, and "Dark Age" is a label that many archaeologists now avoid. The boundaries below are the ones used in most current handbooks, with the Bronze Age collapse given a row of its own as the transition between the Mycenaean period and the Early Iron Age; where a boundary is genuinely contested, the table says so.

Two boundaries deserve a note. The Archaic period has no agreed start: some books begin it at 800 with the alphabet, others at the traditional first Olympiad in 776, others at 750 with the first colonies. And "Roman Greece" has two possible beginnings, because Rome destroyed Corinth and took effective control in 146 BC but did not organise the province of Achaea until Augustus in 27 BC.

Where sources disagree. The term "Dark Age" comes from Anthony Snodgrass's 1971 book and reflects the loss of writing, monumental building and figurative art between c. 1100 and 800. Since the discovery of the Lefkandi "heroön" on Euboea (c. 950 BC, with gold, Near Eastern imports and horse burials), many scholars argue the darkness was uneven and prefer the neutral "Early Iron Age". Both terms remain in use.

Bronze Age: Crete and the mainland

Minoan Crete is the first literate, urban civilisation on European soil. Evans divided its pottery into Early, Middle and Late Minoan (EM, MM, LM), each with subdivisions; Nikolaos Platon later proposed an alternative scheme based on the palaces (Prepalatial, Protopalatial, Neopalatial, Postpalatial). Both systems are still in use, and the relationship between them is approximate. The first palaces at Knossos, Phaistos and Malia rise around 1950 BC; after a destruction around 1700 they are rebuilt larger, and the Neopalatial period (c. 1700–1450) is the age of the frescoes, the bull-leaping, Linear A, and the Theran town at Akrotiri buried by the volcano. Around 1450 almost every Cretan site except Knossos is destroyed, and Knossos itself begins producing documents in Linear B, which Michael Ventris showed in 1952 to be Greek. The simplest explanation is a mainland takeover.

The mainland Mycenaeans first appear as a wealthy warrior elite in the Shaft Graves at Mycenae (c. 1600 BC), whose gold masks Schliemann wrongly attributed to Agamemnon. Palaces follow at Mycenae, Tiryns, Pylos, Thebes and elsewhere around 1400. The Linear B tablets are bureaucratic inventories, not literature, but they give us the king (wanax), the gods (Zeus, Hera, Poseidon, Dionysus, Athena all appear) and the Pylos coast-watchers of the final year. Hittite letters refer to a kingdom of Ahhiyawa, which most scholars take to be Achaea, a Mycenaean power active in western Anatolia.

Where sources disagree. Whether Ahhiyawa is Mycenaean Greece is argued from the Tawagalawa Letter and related Hittite texts. The identification is accepted by most Hittitologists and Aegean prehistorians but remains an inference; no Mycenaean text names a kingdom at all.

Collapse and the Early Iron Age

Between about 1225 (Thebes) and 1180 (Pylos) the palaces burn. Mycenae and Tiryns fall around 1200, are partly reoccupied, and decline. Linear B stops. Settlement numbers across the Peloponnese fall by something like two thirds over the next century. The pottery phase LH IIIC (c. 1190–1050) is a long, uneven afterglow, with some sites (Tiryns, Lefkandi, Perati) thriving briefly, and then Submycenaean and Protogeometric wares mark the Early Iron Age proper.

The Greeks remembered this as migration. Thucydides 1.12 says that after Troy the Boeotians settled Boeotia in the sixtieth year and the Dorians took the Peloponnese in the eightieth; Herodotus 1.56 traces the Dorians through Pindus and Doris. The "Dorian invasion" dominated nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century explanations and is now almost universally dropped as a cause, because there is no archaeological signature of a new population. What replaced it is discussed below.

Recovery is visible from about 950 (Lefkandi), accelerates after 900 (Geometric pottery, the Athenian Kerameikos), and by 800–750 the Greeks are trading with Phoenicians at Al Mina and on Euboea, adopting the Phoenician alphabet, founding Pithekoussai on Ischia (c. 770) and Cumae, and then Naxos, Syracuse (734/3) and the rest. Thucydides 6.3–5 gives foundation dates for the Sicilian colonies counted backwards from their later history; they are the earliest Greek dates with any claim to precision.

Archaic and Classical

The Archaic centuries build every institution the Classical world inherits. Hesiod, writing around 700, mentions competing at funeral games for Amphidamas of Chalcis (Works and Days 654–659), which ties him to the Lelantine War, the first war Greeks remembered. Lawgivers (Zaleucus at Locri, Draco at Athens c. 621, Solon in 594/3), tyrants (Cypselus at Corinth c. 657, Peisistratus at Athens 546–527), hoplite warfare, temples in stone, and the Panhellenic sanctuaries at Olympia, Delphi, Isthmia and Nemea all belong here. Sparta's peculiar constitution forms in the seventh and sixth centuries; the Athenian democracy is created in 508/7.

The Classical period is bracketed by Persia and Macedon. Herodotus is the source for 490–479; Thucydides for 431–411; Xenophon's Hellenica carries the story to 362. Within it: the Athenian empire and Pericles (Parthenon begun 447), the plague of 430, Sicily 415–413, the oligarchic coups of 411 and 404, the Theban ascendancy after Leuctra (371), and Philip's victory at Chaeronea in 338. Alexander crosses to Asia in 334, wins at Granicus, Issus (333) and Gaugamela (331), reaches the Indus and dies at Babylon on 10 or 11 June 323; the date is fixed by a Babylonian astronomical diary, which makes it the most securely dated event in Greek history before the Roman period.

Hellenistic to Late Antique

From 323 the story is dynastic. The Wars of the Successors end at Ipsus (301) and Corupedium (281), leaving three major kingdoms: Antigonid Macedon, Seleucid Asia and Ptolemaic Egypt. Greek becomes the common language from the Adriatic to Afghanistan; Alexandria, with its Library, replaces Athens as the intellectual capital. Rome arrives through the Macedonian Wars (Cynoscephalae 197, Pydna 168) and destroys Corinth in 146, the same year it destroys Carthage. Athens is sacked by Sulla in 86 BC after backing Mithridates. Actium (31) and the deaths of Antony and Cleopatra (30) remove the last Greek monarchy.

Under Rome, Greece is a cultural province: Hadrian completes the Olympieion at Athens (AD 131/2), Pausanias writes his guidebook around 150–180, and the Second Sophistic revives Attic prose. The Herulian sack of Athens in AD 267 is the first serious blow. Constantine founds Constantinople in 330, Theodosius I bans pagan cult in 391–392 (the Olympic games are usually said to end around 393, though the last firmly attested victor is earlier), Alaric raids Greece in 396, and in 529 Justinian's law forbidding pagans to teach (Codex Justinianus 1.11.10) is applied at Athens, where the chronicler John Malalas records a ban on the teaching of philosophy. Damascius and Simplicius, the last heads of the Platonic school, travelled to the Persian court; Agathias, Histories 2.30–31, tells the story.

Where sources disagree. Whether anything was "closed" in 529 is argued between Cameron and Watts in module 7.4. Treat "the closing of the Academy" as a convenient endpoint, not a documented event on one day.

How the Greeks themselves dated things

No Greek state had an era. Each city named the year after its chief magistrate: at Athens the eponymous archon, at Sparta the senior ephor, at Argos the priestess of Hera. Because these lists ran independently and the civic years began at different seasons, a Panhellenic event had to be dated by several at once. Thucydides 2.2 does exactly that for the outbreak of the war: the forty-eighth year of Chrysis's priesthood at Argos, Aenesias ephor at Sparta, Pythodorus archon at Athens, in the spring. He then abandons magistrate dating and counts "by summers and winters", explaining at 5.20 that magistrate years are unreliable because an event can fall anywhere within one.

The Olympiad system was the solution. Hippias of Elis compiled a list of Olympic victors around 400 BC; Plutarch, Numa 1.4, notes that it rested on no firm authority. Timaeus of Tauromenium (c. 350–260 BC) was the first historian to date systematically by Olympiads, and Eratosthenes of Cyrene (c. 276–194) made the system the backbone of his Chronographiai, counting from the first Olympiad in what we call 776 BC. An Olympiad is a four-year span, so "the third year of the 75th Olympiad" is 478/7. Diodorus Siculus in the first century BC gives each year by Olympiad, Athenian archon and Roman consul together, which is why his history is so easy to synchronise.

For the deep past the Greeks had only genealogy, and they counted roughly three generations to a century (Herodotus 2.142). Eratosthenes fixed the fall of Troy at 1184/3 BC by counting backwards from the first Olympiad through the Return of the Heraclidae and the Ionian migration; the figure is preserved by Clement of Alexandria, Stromateis 1.21, and Censorinus, De die natali 21. It became canonical, but it was never the only answer: the Parian Marble (an inscribed chronicle of 264/3 BC) gives 1209/8, Herodotus 2.145 implies about 1250, Ephorus gave 1135 and Duris of Samos 1334. Apollodorus of Athens turned Eratosthenes's scheme into verse in the second century BC, and that is largely how it reached the Roman and Christian chronographers.

Where sources disagree. The date 776 is itself under pressure. Mallwitz's reading of the Olympia stratigraphy and Christesen's of the victor lists, both pushing the real start towards 700, are set out in module 5.1. The Greeks' zero point may be two or three generations too early.

The three big chronological debates

When did Thera erupt?

The eruption buried Akrotiri in the Late Minoan IA phase. Archaeologists working from Egyptian synchronisms (Minoan pottery in dated Egyptian contexts, Theran pumice at Tell el-Dab'a) placed it around 1530–1500 BC. Radiocarbon, including an olive branch buried in the tephra (Friedrich and others, 2006) and large sequences of short-lived samples analysed by Sturt Manning, points to 1620–1600. Recent refinements to the calibration curve (Pearson and others, 2018 onwards) narrow the gap and make a date around 1560–1540 possible, but the dispute has not closed. It matters because the whole Aegean Late Bronze Age sequence has to shift by up to a century depending on the answer.

Where sources disagree. Scientific dating favours the "high" chronology (c. 1600); Egyptian-based archaeology favours the "low" (c. 1500). Manning and Höflmayer have each moved toward the middle; Bietak has not. Say "c. 1600–1500, contested".

Why did the palaces fall around 1200?

Ancient explanation: migration, the Dorians. Modern candidates: raiders from the sea (the "Sea Peoples" whom Ramesses III fought in his eighth year, c. 1177, recorded at Medinet Habu); earthquake (Amos Nur's "earthquake storm" hypothesis); drought (pollen and isotope data from Cyprus and Syria, Kaniewski and Drake); internal revolt; and Colin Renfrew's "systems collapse", in which an over-specialised palace economy fails when any one input is interrupted. Eric Cline's synthesis (1177 B.C., 2014) argues for a cascade of several. There is no consensus beyond the view that no single cause is adequate, and that the destructions were spread over forty years rather than a single event.

When was Homer written down?

Milman Parry showed in the 1930s that the poems are products of oral formulaic composition, so "written down" and "composed" may be different moments. The Greeks themselves disagreed: Herodotus 2.53 puts Homer "four hundred years before my time", roughly 850; the Peisistratid recension story (Cicero, De oratore 3.137; pseudo-Plato, Hipparchus 228b) has Athens producing an official text in the sixth century. Modern positions: Richard Janko's linguistic statistics put the Iliad around 750–725; M. L. West argued on internal evidence for a seventh-century Iliad (c. 680–640) and a later Odyssey; Gregory Nagy's evolutionary model has no single moment, with the text crystallising only in the Panathenaic performances of the sixth century and fixed by Alexandrian editors. The earliest Greek alphabetic inscriptions in hexameter, the Dipylon oinochoe (c. 740) and Nestor's cup from Pithekoussai (c. 730), show that the metre and the writing system coexisted by the late eighth century, which is a floor, not a date.

Where sources disagree. Eighth century (Janko), seventh (West) or a sixth-century crystallisation (Nagy): the range is two hundred years, and the question is partly about what "the text of Homer" means.

Two rhythms help. The Classical period is almost exactly the span between two Persian-related events, 480 and 323, and its middle is the war of 431–404. And the two "falls of Greece", 338 (to Macedon) and 146 (to Rome), are separated by 192 years, close to the 200 years between the end of the collapse (c. 1150) and the first recovery at Lefkandi (c. 950) if you want a symmetry to hang the two dark passages on. The symmetry is a mnemonic, not a fact.

Self-test

  1. Which script records the earliest Greek, and roughly when does it first appear at Knossos?
    Answer

    Linear B, deciphered by Ventris in 1952; it appears at Knossos after the Mycenaean takeover around 1450 BC.

  2. Give the conventional date range of the Classical period and the two events that define its limits.
    Answer

    480–323 BC: Xerxes' invasion (Thermopylae and Salamis) to the death of Alexander at Babylon.

  3. Why is the Athenian date for Solon's archonship written 594/3 rather than 594?
    Answer

    The Attic civic year began in midsummer, so each archon year overlaps two Julian years.

  4. Who first used Olympiads systematically as a dating framework, and who made the system canonical?
    Answer

    Timaeus of Tauromenium first; Eratosthenes of Cyrene in his Chronographiai.

  5. What date did Eratosthenes give for the fall of Troy, and name one source that preserves it.
    Answer

    1184/3 BC; preserved by Clement of Alexandria, Stromateis 1.21 (also Censorinus, De die natali 21).

  6. How does Thucydides date the outbreak of the Peloponnesian War at 2.2?
    Answer

    By three magistrates at once: the 48th year of Chrysis as priestess of Hera at Argos, Aenesias as ephor at Sparta, Pythodorus as archon at Athens.

  7. State the "high" and "low" dates for the Thera eruption and which kind of evidence supports each.
    Answer

    High, c. 1620–1600 BC, from radiocarbon (the olive branch, Manning's sequences); low, c. 1530–1500, from Egyptian synchronisms and pottery.

  8. Name three modern explanations for the collapse around 1200 BC other than the Dorian invasion.
    Answer

    Any three of: Sea Peoples raids, earthquakes, drought, internal revolt, systems collapse (Renfrew), or Cline's multi-cause cascade.

  9. Which event is the most securely dated in Greek history before the Roman period, and why?
    Answer

    Alexander's death on 10/11 June 323 BC, fixed by a Babylonian astronomical diary.

  10. What did Justinian actually do in AD 529, and what is the scholarly caveat?
    Answer

    He forbade pagans to teach (Codex Justinianus 1.11.10), applied at Athens according to Malalas; whether anything was formally "closed" is disputed, so 529 is a conventional endpoint.

Carry forward

  1. Before about 500 BC every Greek date is a modern reconstruction or an ancient back-calculation; treat "776" and "1184" as traditions to be weighed, not facts to be trusted.
  2. The Bronze Age collapse of c. 1200 is the hinge of the whole story: Homer, the myths of the Heraclidae and the sense of a lost heroic age all look back across it.
  3. The Greeks dated by magistrates and Olympiads, and reconciling those lists is how Thucydides, Eratosthenes and Diodorus built chronology; every later date you meet rests on that scaffolding.
