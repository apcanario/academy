The fifty years and their sources

The Pentecontaetia is Thucydides' name for the roughly fifty years between the Persian retreat after Plataea and Mycale (479) and the outbreak of the Peloponnesian War (431). His account (Thucydides 1.89–118) is a compressed, undated digression explaining how Athens became powerful enough to frighten Sparta into war, and it is almost the only continuous narrative we have. The rest is patchwork: Plutarch's Lives of Aristides, Cimon and Pericles, written five centuries later from lost sources; Diodorus Siculus 11–12, who dates by archon year but often wrongly; the Athenaion Politeia attributed to Aristotle (23–28) for constitutional change; Old Comedy; and the stones, above all the Athenian Tribute Lists and building accounts.

Where sources disagree. Thucydides gives almost no absolute dates, so the chronology is reconstructed from Diodorus' archon years, Plutarch's asides and epigraphy. Scholars still argue whether the Egyptian expedition fell in 460–454 or 459–453 and whether the First Peloponnesian War began in 461 or 460. Treat dates below as approximate to a year or two unless an inscription fixes them.

From league to empire

Founding, 478/7

After the Spartan regent Pausanias disgraced himself at Byzantium the allies asked Athens to take over, and Sparta let the leadership go (Thucydides 1.95). Aristides assessed the first contributions and the allies swore oaths, sinking iron ingots to mark their permanence (Athenaion Politeia 23.5; Plutarch, Aristides 25). The treasury was placed on Delos and the synod met there. Members contributed ships or money (phoros); Thucydides 1.96 gives the first assessment as 460 talents, a figure many think too high for the early League. Ten Athenian Hellenotamiai handled the money from the start.

The slide to compulsion

Thucydides says the allies brought much of it on themselves: they preferred to pay rather than serve, so their fleets dwindled while Athens' grew (1.99). Naxos tried to leave around 470–468 and was besieged and "enslaved contrary to the established rule" (1.98.4), Thucydides' phrase for losing autonomy. Thasos revolted in 465–463 over its mainland mines; after a siege of over two years it surrendered walls, ships and mines (1.100–101). Sparta had secretly promised Thasos an invasion of Attica and was stopped only by the great earthquake and helot revolt. Cimon's victory at the Eurymedon (c. 467) and the destruction of an Athenian fleet in Egypt (c. 460–454) bracket this stage: one showed the League's purpose fulfilled, the other the cost of continuing it.

454 and after

In 454/3 the treasury moved from Delos to Athens, on a Samian proposal according to Plutarch (Pericles 12), and most historians connect it with the Egyptian disaster. From that year one-sixtieth of each city's tribute was dedicated to Athena and inscribed on stone; these quota lists are our best evidence for membership and payment. The synod is never heard of again. Other instruments multiplied: garrisons and Athenian officials; transfer of serious lawsuits to Athenian courts; imposed democracies, as at Erythrae (c. 453); and cleruchies, settlements of Athenian citizens on confiscated allied land that gave poorer Athenians a farm and the empire a garrison. They were planted on Naxos, Andros, Lemnos, the Chersonese and at Brea in Thrace; the Brea decree (IG I3 46) restricts settlers to the two lowest property classes.

The Peace of Callias with Persia, around 449, would have removed the League's reason for existing. Diodorus 12.4 and fourth-century orators affirm it; Thucydides never mentions it and Theopompus called it a forgery. The quota list for 449/8 is missing, which some read as a suspension of tribute while Athens debated what came next.

Samos, 440–439

Samos, one of the few remaining ship-contributing allies, revolted after Athens intervened in its quarrel with Miletus and imposed a democracy. Pericles commanded a nine-month siege (Thucydides 1.115–117); Sparta's allies debated intervention and, on Corinth's insistence, voted against (1.40). Samos lost fleet and walls and paid an indemnity. The empire could no longer be broken from inside.

Where sources disagree. Russell Meiggs (The Athenian Empire, 1972) traced a steady hardening of control from the 450s in the language of decrees. G. E. M. de Ste. Croix argued that the allied demos in most cities preferred Athenian rule to their own oligarchs, citing Thucydides 8.48 and Samian loyalty in 412–404. Others point to how fast allies revolted whenever Athens weakened. Both readings work because loyalty was divided along class lines within each city.

Cimon, Ephialtes and the Areopagus

Through the 470s and 460s the leading Athenian was Cimon, son of Miltiades: aristocratic, pro-Spartan, repeatedly elected general, victor at Eion, Scyros and the Eurymedon. His policy was the League as crusade against Persia and friendship with Sparta; his spoils funded public walks and open dinners (Plutarch, Cimon 10). His rivals were Themistocles, ostracised in the late 470s, and then Ephialtes son of Sophonides, about whom we know little.

The Areopagus, the council of ex-archons, had emerged from the Persian Wars with great prestige and, the Athenaion Politeia (23.1) believed, a general "guardianship of the laws". In 462/1 Ephialtes, with Pericles as junior associate, prosecuted individual Areopagites and then transferred most of the council's powers to the Council of 500, the Assembly and the popular courts, leaving it homicide trials and some religious functions (Athenaion Politeia 25; Plutarch, Cimon 15). The timing was political: Cimon had taken 4,000 hoplites to help Sparta against the rebels on Ithome, and the Spartans sent them home alone of all the allies (Thucydides 1.102). The insult destroyed his position; he opposed the reforms on his return and was ostracised in 461. Ephialtes was murdered soon after.

Where sources disagree. What the Areopagus lost in 462 is uncertain; Aristotle's text speaks only of "additional powers". Reconstructions range from the audit and vetting of magistrates to hearing eisangeliai for offences against the state. Hignett doubted the Areopagus ever held a formal guardianship and thought fourth-century tradition inflated the reform. Aeschylus' Eumenides (458), which stages the Areopagus' foundation as a homicide court, is read as comment on the reform, but whether approving or cautionary is disputed.

Pericles and the reforms of the 450s

Pericles son of Xanthippus inherited Ephialtes' faction and was the most influential Athenian from about 460 until his death in 429, elected general fifteen years running from 443. Thucydides' "in name a democracy, in fact rule by the first man" (2.65.9) is a flourish: Pericles held no office beyond the annual generalship and in 430 was fined and deposed.

Three reforms carry his name. Pay for jurors, two obols a day, introduced according to the Athenaion Politeia (27.3–4) to compete with Cimon's private generosity; it was the first state pay for civic service and opened the courts to men who could not afford a lost day's wages. The citizenship law of 451/0, requiring an Athenian father and mother (Athenaion Politeia 26.4; Plutarch, Pericles 37); its motive is debated between protecting the value of citizenship now that it carried pay and land, curbing aristocratic foreign marriages, and Aristotle's bare "because of the number of citizens". It lapsed during the war, when Pericles himself sought an exemption for his son by Aspasia, and was re-enacted in 403. And the opening of the archonship by lot to the zeugitai, the third property class, from 457 (Athenaion Politeia 26.2). His remaining rival, Thucydides son of Melesias, attacked the use of allied money on buildings and was ostracised in 443 (Plutarch, Pericles 11–14).

How the radical democracy worked

  • The Assembly (ekklesia): all adult male citizens, meeting on the Pnyx, perhaps 6,000 of a citizen body of 30,000–40,000 at a time. It decided war, peace, finance and legislation and elected generals; any citizen could speak.
  • The Council of 500 (boule): fifty from each of the ten Cleisthenic tribes, chosen by lot from citizens over thirty for one year, at most twice in a lifetime. It prepared the Assembly's agenda (every motion needed a probouleuma), supervised magistrates and finance and ran the navy; each tribal fifty presided for a tenth of the year.
  • The law courts (dikasteria): 6,000 sworn jurors a year, chosen by lot from volunteers over thirty, sitting in panels of 201, 501 or more. No judges, no advocates; litigants spoke for themselves against a water clock and the panel voted without deliberation. The courts heard private suits, public prosecutions, the audit of every outgoing magistrate and allied cases.
  • The lot: almost all of some 700 annual magistracies were filled by lot, on the principle that any citizen was competent and that election favoured the rich. Only offices needing expertise, chiefly the ten strategoi and the financial boards, were elected and repeatable.
  • The generals: one per tribe, elected annually, commanding by land and sea and, being re-electable, the only continuous leadership the system allowed. Pericles' tenure shows what this permitted; Arginusae in 406 shows the risk.
  • Liturgies: the richest 1,200 or so citizens and metics funded public services in rotation from their own pockets: a festival chorus (choregia) or, most expensively, a warship for a year (trierarchy). They were the main direct levy on wealth and a route to esteem.
  • Ostracism: each year the Assembly voted whether to hold one; if so, every citizen could scratch a name on a sherd, and provided 6,000 voted the man with most was exiled for ten years without loss of property. Used against Themistocles, Cimon and Thucydides son of Melesias.
  • The graphe paranomon: prosecution of a decree's proposer for proposing something contrary to law. First attested in 415 but generally thought to have replaced the Areopagus' guardianship after 462. Conviction annulled the decree and fined the proposer; it made the courts, not the Assembly, the final arbiter.

The system's core was lot, rotation, pay and accountability: every officeholder was vetted before office, could be deposed during it and was audited after it. Its weakness was that a persuasive speaker could move the Assembly fast, with nothing to slow it beyond the Council's agenda control and the threat of later prosecution.

Where sources disagree. Whether fifth-century Athens distinguished laws (nomoi) from decrees (psephismata), as the fourth century did, is unclear; if not, the graphe paranomon before 403 may have meant something looser. Mogens Hansen's standard reconstruction relies on fourth-century evidence and he concedes that the fifth-century Assembly may have been both more powerful and less regulated. The number of meetings a year and the date of Assembly pay are uncertain.

The building programme and who paid

The Acropolis had lain in ruins since 480; a tradition held that the Greeks had sworn at Plataea not to rebuild destroyed temples. The programme began in 447 with the Parthenon, Ictinus and Callicrates as architects and Phidias overseeing the sculpture; it was dedicated at the Panathenaea of 438 and the sculpture finished by 432. Mnesicles' Propylaea followed from 437 and was left incomplete when war came. The building accounts (IG I3 436–451) survive in part; Plutarch (Pericles 12–13) gives the only narrative.

Plutarch frames the programme as a controversy: Thucydides son of Melesias charged that Athens was adorning herself like a courtesan with money extorted from the allies for a war against Persia; Pericles replied that the allies paid for protection, received it, and had no claim on the surplus, and that the works spread wealth to every trade. Whether tribute actually paid for the Parthenon is less certain than Plutarch implies. The accounts show payments from the treasurers of Athena, the Hellenotamiai and the sale of spoils, and a transfer of a 5,000-talent reserve to Athena around 449 is usually inferred. Lisa Kallet-Marx and others argue that tribute's direct share was smaller than tradition holds and that Athens' own revenues, Laurium silver, harbour dues, court fees and public leases, covered much of it. The total cost is unknown; figures such as 470 talents for the building are modern estimates from incomplete accounts.

Where sources disagree. The Oath of Plataea is accepted by some as explaining the thirty-year delay and rejected by others, including Theopompus in antiquity, as a fourth-century fabrication. The Callias decrees, crucial for how League money reached Athena, are dated to 434/3 by tradition and to the late 440s by others. The Parthenon's function is itself debated: with no attested altar or priestess, some treat it as a treasury and votive monument rather than a cult temple.

The First Peloponnesian War, c. 460–446

The breach of 462 was followed by Athenian alliances with Argos and Thessaly and by the settlement of the Messenian rebels at Naupactus after Ithome fell (Thucydides 1.102–103). Megara, at war with Corinth, left the Peloponnesian League for Athens around 460, giving Athens the Isthmus roads and the port of Pegae; Corinth became Athens' bitterest enemy. The war was fought with Sparta's allies more than with Sparta. Athens beat Corinth at sea and at Megara (Myronides, 458), took Aegina into the League as a tributary (457) and finished its own Long Walls. Sparta marched into Boeotia and won at Tanagra (457); sixty-two days later Myronides crushed the Boeotians at Oenophyta and Athens dominated Boeotia and Phocis for a decade. The Erechtheid casualty list (IG I3 1147), with dead in Cyprus, Egypt, Phoenicia, Halieis, Aegina and Megara in one year, shows the scale of commitment.

Cimon, recalled, negotiated a five-year truce in 451 and died on Cyprus around 450. By the mid-440s overextension told. The Boeotian exiles beat Tolmides at Coronea in 447 and Athens evacuated Boeotia. In 446 Euboea revolted; Megara defected and killed its garrison; the Spartan king Pleistoanax invaded Attica and withdrew without fighting, for which he was exiled at home (Thucydides 1.113–114; Plutarch, Pericles 22–23, who says Pericles bribed him). Pericles reduced Euboea and planted a cleruchy at Histiaea. The Thirty Years' Peace of 446/5 gave up Nisaea, Pegae, Troezen and Achaea; each side kept its allies, neutrals might join either, and disputes were to go to arbitration (Thucydides 1.115.1). Athens lost its land empire and kept its sea empire. Thucydides' speakers in 432–431 refer back to this war constantly; it is why Megara and Corinth mattered so much in 431.

The critics

Criticism began in the democracy's lifetime; Thucydides' Cleon calls the demos "spectators of words" (3.38). The most sustained fifth-century critique is the Constitution of the Athenians preserved among Xenophon's works but not his; its unknown author, the "Old Oligarch", probably wrote in the 420s. His argument is a backhanded tribute: the system is bad because it gives power to the poor over the good, but every feature of it, pay, the lot, the courts' grip on the allies, the licence of slaves and metics, the naval empire, is perfectly designed to keep the poor in power, and he sees no way of changing it short of destroying it (Pseudo-Xenophon 1.1–9, 3.1–9).

Plato's hostility runs deeper. In the Gorgias (515e) Socrates says Pericles made the Athenians lazy, cowardly, talkative and greedy by introducing pay. In Republic 557a–564a democracy is the regime of licence, where the lot gives office to the unqualified and excess of freedom breeds tyranny; the ship whose sailors brawl over the helm (488a–489a) is the Assembly. Plato's objection is epistemic: government is a craft, and crafts belong to those who know. Aristotle classed Periclean Athens as the extreme democracy in which the demos rather than law is sovereign (Politics 1292a), yet conceded that the many judging together may judge better than the few (1281a–b).

Where sources disagree. Thucydides' own verdict is divided: the Funeral Oration (2.35–46) is the finest surviving defence of Athenian freedom, and 2.65 blames Pericles' successors rather than the system; yet the Mytilenean and Sicilian debates show an Assembly swayed by anger and greed, and 8.97 praises the moderate regime of 411 as the best government of his lifetime. Whether he was a critic of democracy or of demagogues remains open.

Was it democratic?

The modern debate turns on who was excluded. Women of citizen family counted for descent after 451 but had no political existence: no Assembly, no juries, no outright property, no voice in court except through a male kyrios. Metics, perhaps 10,000–20,000 adult males, paid a monthly tax, served in the army and performed liturgies, but could not vote, own land or marry citizens. Slaves are hardest to count, with estimates from 60,000 to 150,000 for Attica. On the widest estimates those with political rights were perhaps a tenth of the population and a third of adult males.

One school, from the Old Oligarch to Moses Finley (Democracy Ancient and Modern, 1973) and Josiah Ober (Mass and Elite in Democratic Athens, 1989), accepts the exclusions and insists that among the included the democracy was real and unprecedented: direct participation, lot, pay, the sovereignty of the Assembly over wealth and birth, ordinary citizens forcing the elite to speak their language. Ellen Meiksins Wood stressed that the achievement was the equality of the working peasant with the aristocrat, and that slavery, though real, was not the basis of citizen leisure since most citizens worked. Against this, others point out that tribute and slave labour made pay and public building possible, so that the democracy of the citizens was financed by the subjection of others, and feminist historians since the 1970s have argued that the 451 law made the exclusion of women constitutive: citizen mothers became necessary and received nothing.

The honest position is that "democracy" in Athens meant what the word says, power of the demos, and that the demos was a hereditary, male, free minority. Within it, the direct power exercised by ordinary men has no parallel before or since. Outside it, the regime looked like any other Greek city, and in its empire rather worse.

Self-test

  1. What is the main ancient source for the Pentecontaetia, and what is its chief limitation?
    Answer

    Thucydides 1.89–118, a digression explaining the growth of Athenian power. It is compressed, selective and almost undated, so the chronology must be reconstructed from Diodorus, Plutarch and inscriptions.

  2. Where was the League treasury first kept, when was it moved, and what evidence does the move produce?
    Answer

    On Delos. Moved to Athens in 454/3. From then one-sixtieth of each city's tribute was dedicated to Athena and inscribed, producing the Athenian Tribute Lists.

  3. Name the three major allied revolts of the period with approximate dates.
    Answer

    Naxos c. 470–468, Thasos 465–463, Samos 440–439. Euboea (446) and Byzantium (440) were lesser or concurrent revolts.

  4. What was a cleruchy and who went?
    Answer

    A settlement of Athenian citizens on confiscated allied land, giving the settlers a farm and Athens a garrison. The Brea decree restricts settlers to the two lowest property classes.

  5. Why did Cimon fall from power in 462/1?
    Answer

    He led 4,000 hoplites to help Sparta against the helot revolt at Ithome and the Spartans dismissed them alone of all allies. The humiliation discredited his pro-Spartan policy, Ephialtes carried the Areopagus reforms in his absence, and Cimon was ostracised on his return.

  6. What did the Areopagus retain after 462?
    Answer

    Homicide trials and certain religious functions. Its "additional" powers, probably the audit and vetting of magistrates and a general guardianship of the laws, went to the Council, Assembly and courts.

  7. What did the citizenship law of 451/0 require, and what happened to it?
    Answer

    Both parents had to be Athenian. It lapsed during the Peloponnesian War (Pericles obtained an exemption for his son by Aspasia) and was re-enacted in 403.

  8. Which offices were elected rather than filled by lot, and why?
    Answer

    The ten generals and the main financial boards, because they required expertise. They could be held repeatedly, which made the generalship the only route to continuous leadership.

  9. What were the terms of the Thirty Years' Peace of 446/5?
    Answer

    Athens gave up Nisaea, Pegae, Troezen and Achaea; each side kept its existing allies; neutrals could join either; disputes were to go to arbitration. Athens kept its sea empire and lost its land empire.

  10. What is the core of Plato's objection to Athenian democracy, as distinct from the Old Oligarch's?
    Answer

    Plato's objection is epistemic: government is a craft requiring knowledge, and the lot and Assembly hand it to the ignorant (Republic 488–489, Protagoras 319). The Old Oligarch's is one of class: the system gives power to the poor over the good, and does so with irritating efficiency.

Carry forward

  1. Empire and democracy grew together and funded each other: tribute, cleruchies and court fees paid for the jury pay and buildings that made the radical democracy work, and the demos that voted at home was the body that rowed the fleet holding the allies down. Keep this coupling in mind for the Peloponnesian War and the coups of 411 and 404.
  2. The First Peloponnesian War and the Thirty Years' Peace are the back-story of 431: Corinth's grievance, Megara's double defection, Sparta's fear of Athenian land power and the arbitration clause all return in Thucydides book 1.
  3. The constitutional machinery (lot, rotation, pay, the courts as final arbiter, the graphe paranomon) is what later modules will see tested under stress, from the Mytilenean debate to Arginusae and the trial of Socrates; and the exclusion of women, metics and slaves is the measure every later critic, ancient and modern, applies to the achievement.
