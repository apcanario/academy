The crown games and their cycle

Four festivals stood above all others because their only prize was a wreath: the stephanitic or crown games, together called the periodos (circuit). An athlete who won all four was a periodonikes. Olympia belonged to Zeus and gave a wreath of wild olive cut from the sacred tree behind his temple; Delphi's Pythian Games belonged to Apollo and gave bay laurel; the Isthmian Games near Corinth belonged to Poseidon and gave pine (dry wild celery for a period in the classical age, according to Plutarch, Table Talk 5.3); Nemea in the Argolid belonged to Zeus and gave fresh wild celery.

The cycle ran on a four-year frame, the Olympiad, which Greek historians from the late fourth century used as a common chronology. Olympia fell in year one, at the second full moon after the summer solstice; Nemea and Isthmia were biennial, held in years two and four (Isthmia in spring, Nemea in summer); the Pythian Games fell in late summer of year three. A serious competitor could therefore attend a crown festival every year and never clash.

Where sources disagree. The date 776 BC rests on the victor list compiled by Hippias of Elis around 400 BC, which Plutarch (Numa 1) already distrusted. Pausanias (5.8.6) says the stadion alone was run for the first thirteen Olympiads, which looks like back-projection to fill a gap. Excavation shows cult activity at Olympia from the eleventh century and a surge of dedications around 700, so most scholars now treat 776 as a rounded convention rather than a recorded event; some would lower the real start of regular games to c. 700.

Founding myths

Each site told more than one story. For Olympia, Pindar gives two in his own odes. Olympian 1 tells how Pelops won Hippodamia by beating her father Oenomaus in a chariot race, with horses from Poseidon; the Pelopion, a Bronze Age tumulus inside the Altis, was his tomb, and the games his memorial. Olympian 10 says instead that Heracles founded the festival and measured out the Altis after sacking Augeas of Elis. Pausanias (5.7.6–5.8.5) stacks up further layers: Cronus and Zeus wrestled there, the Idaean Heracles from Crete raced his brothers, and Iphitus of Elis revived lapsed games with the Spartan Lycurgus. The ancients themselves noticed the tension; Pausanias simply records each tradition.

Delphi's games commemorated Apollo's killing of the serpent Python, and began as a musical contest of hymns to the god (Pausanias 10.7.2); Strabo (9.3.10) describes the nomos Pythikos, a flute piece imitating the fight. Athletics arrived with the reorganisation after the First Sacred War. The Isthmian Games were funeral games for Melicertes, the son of Ino who was carried ashore by a dolphin and worshipped as Palaemon; Plutarch (Theseus 25) adds that Theseus refounded them in honour of Poseidon. Nemea's games were funeral games too, for the infant Opheltes (renamed Archemorus, "beginner of doom"), killed by a snake while his nurse showed the Seven against Thebes a spring; Apollodorus, Library 3.6.4 and Bacchylides 9 tell it, and the judges at Nemea wore black in mourning. A rival Nemean story credited Heracles after the lion. The pattern matters: Greek athletics was understood as a rite of death and memory, as in the games for Patroclus in Iliad 23, not as recreation.

The programme of events

Pausanias (5.8.6–5.9.2) supplies the traditional dates at which each event entered the Olympic programme. The core was footraces: the stadion (one length of the track, about 192 m at Olympia); the diaulos (two lengths, turning at a post); and the dolichos, a long race of between seven and twenty-four lengths, the figure uncertain and perhaps varying by site. Runners started standing, toes in grooves cut into a stone sill, held by a rope gate, the hysplex, that dropped at the signal; a false start earned a whipping.

The pentathlon (from 708 BC) combined stadion, long jump with hand-weights (halteres), discus, javelin thrown with a leather thong (ankyle) and wrestling. How the winner was decided remains the classic problem of Greek sport: a victor in three events won outright, but the procedure when no one managed three is nowhere stated clearly. The combat events were wrestling (three falls to win, 708 BC), boxing (688 BC; thongs of oxhide round the knuckles, no rounds, no weight classes, fought until a man was knocked out or raised a finger), and pankration (648 BC), an all-in fight in which only biting and gouging were forbidden, and the Spartans supposedly allowed even those at home (Philostratus, Gymnasticus 11). The hoplitodromos, a diaulos run in helmet and shield (greaves until the fifth century), was added in 520 BC and closed the athletic programme.

The hippodrome events were the aristocrat's domain: the four-horse chariot (tethrippon, 680 BC, twelve laps), the ridden horse (keles, 648 BC), the two-horse chariot (408 BC), races for foals, and for a time a mule-cart race, dropped in 444 BC as undignified. Crucially, the owner won, not the driver or jockey, which is how women and tyrants could be Olympic victors without competing in person. Boys' events ran alongside the men's from 632 BC.

Rules, truce, judges, nudity

Olympia was run by Elis. The Hellanodikai ("judges of the Greeks"), originally one, then two, then nine, ten and twelve, were chosen by lot from the Eleans, wore purple and trained for ten months in a special building at Elis before each festival (Pausanias 5.9.4–6, 6.24.1). Competitors arrived at Elis a month early, trained under the judges' eyes, and were sorted into men and boys at the judges' discretion, since no birth certificates existed. At Olympia, athletes, fathers, brothers and trainers swore over a boar's flesh before the statue of Zeus Horkios ("of the oath") that they would do nothing wrong (Pausanias 5.24.9). Cheating was fined and the fines paid for bronze Zeuses, the Zanes, lining the way into the stadium; Pausanias (5.21.2–18) read their inscriptions and names the bribers, beginning with Eupolus of Thessaly in 388 BC. Whip-bearing stewards enforced order on the spot, and a Hellanodikes could be appealed against to the Elean council, but his verdict stood.

The sacred truce (ekecheiria, "holding of hands") was announced by heralds and covered a month, later perhaps three, around the festival. Pausanias (5.20.1) saw the text on a bronze discus in the Temple of Hera, attributed to Iphitus and Lycurgus. It did not stop Greek wars; it protected pilgrims travelling to and from Olympia and placed Elis itself under protection. The clearest case is Thucydides 5.49–50: in 420 BC the Eleans fined Sparta two thousand minae for attacking Lepreum during the truce and barred Spartans from the festival; a Spartan who entered a chariot under another name was whipped.

Where sources disagree. Who first competed naked? Pausanias (1.44.1) names Orsippus of Megara, whose loincloth fell off in the stadion of 720 BC; Dionysius of Halicarnassus (7.72.3) names the Spartan Acanthus in the same year; Thucydides (1.6) says simply that the Spartans introduced it, "not many years ago" in his terms. Homer's wrestlers wear a zoma (Iliad 23.683, 710). Modern explanations range from ritual (Sansone) to a marker of citizen status distinguishing Greek from barbarian (Bonfante); the vase evidence suggests nudity was normal by the sixth century, whatever the origin.

Prizes, rewards at home, and famous athletes

At the crown games the prize was the wreath, a palm branch and a ribbon of wool. The real return came at home. Solon's Athens reportedly paid an Olympic victor 500 drachmas and an Isthmian victor 100 (Plutarch, Solon 23); a fifth-century decree (IG I3 131) gave victors in the crown games free meals in the Prytaneion for life. Cities granted front seats at the theatre, tax exemptions, statues in the agora and at Olympia, and odes. Croton kept a stable of champions; Astylus, who switched his allegiance from Croton to Syracuse to please Hieron, had his statue at home torn down and his house turned into a prison (Pausanias 6.13.1). Xenophanes (fr. 2 West) complained that a sprinter received honours above a wise man who did the city more good. Many lesser festivals were chrematitic, giving cash or goods; the Panathenaic prize list of the 380s (IG II2 2311) gives a hundred amphorae of oil to the winning stadion runner, a small fortune.

Three victors became legends. Milo of Croton won the wrestling at Olympia six times between about 540 and 516 BC (five as a man), and once more at each of the other crown games; stories tell of him carrying his own statue into the Altis, eating twenty pounds of meat a day, and dying when a tree trunk he was splitting closed on his hands and wolves found him (Pausanias 6.14.5–8; Strabo 6.1.12). Theagenes of Thasos won Olympic boxing in 480 and pankration in 476 and claimed 1,300 or 1,400 crowns across the circuit; after his death an enemy flogged his statue nightly until it fell on him, the statue was tried for murder and thrown in the sea, and after a famine the Delphic oracle ordered it fished out and worshipped as a healer (Pausanias 6.11.2–9; Dio Chrysostom 31.95). Diagoras of Rhodes, boxing champion of 464 BC, was celebrated in Pindar's Olympian 7, which the Rhodians inscribed in gold letters in the temple of Athena at Lindos; his sons Damagetus and Acusilaus won on the same day in 448 and carried him round the stadium while the crowd called out that he should die now, having nothing greater to live for; his third son Dorieus was a periodonikes three times in pankration (Pausanias 6.7.1–7).

Women: exclusion and the Heraia

Married women were barred from the Olympic festival on pain of being thrown from Mount Typaeon (Pausanias 5.6.7); the one exception was the priestess of Demeter Chamyne, who sat on an altar opposite the judges (Pausanias 6.20.9). Pausanias adds that unmarried girls were not forbidden to watch, a detail many scholars accept with caution, since it stands alone. The tale of Diagoras's daughter Callipateira (or Pherenice), who entered disguised as a trainer to see her son Peisirodus win, was caught and acquitted because her father, brothers and son were all victors, is told to explain why trainers thereafter attended naked (Pausanias 5.6.7–8). Women could nonetheless win as owners: the Spartan princess Cynisca took the four-horse chariot in 396 and 392 BC and set up a bronze chariot and a boastful epigram in the Altis (Pausanias 3.8.1, 6.1.6).

The Heraia, a separate festival for Hera at Olympia, is known almost entirely from Pausanias 5.16.2–8. A college of Sixteen Women of Elis wove a robe for the goddess every four years and organised footraces for unmarried girls in three age classes, run on the Olympic track shortened by one sixth, with hair loose, a short chiton above the knee and the right shoulder bared. Winners received an olive crown and a share of the cow sacrificed to Hera, and could dedicate painted portraits. Foundation was credited to Hippodamia; the bronze "Spartan girl runner" statuettes may show the costume.

Where sources disagree. Whether the Heraia was a pre-marital initiation rite (Scanlon, following Calame on girls' choruses) or simply a cult occasion with a race attached is undecided; its date is also unknown, and we cannot say if it was held before or after the men's games or in a different year. Pausanias's Elis and its Sixteen Women are second-century AD realities; how far they describe archaic practice is uncertain.

Olympia as a site, and Pindar's ode

The sanctuary grew around the Altis, a walled grove beside the Alpheios and Cladeos with the conical ash altar of Zeus, built up from centuries of burnt thighbones, at its centre. The Temple of Hera (c. 600 BC) is the oldest monumental building. Libon's Temple of Zeus (c. 470–457), paid for from Elis's spoils over Pisa, received Phidias's gold and ivory Zeus around 430; Phidias's workshop, with his named cup, was found in 1958 under a Byzantine church. Treasuries of colonial cities lined the northern terrace; the Bouleuterion held the oath statue; the Prytaneion housed the victors' feast. The stadium was moved three times, ending in the mid fourth century outside the Altis to the east, holding perhaps 40,000 standing spectators on earth banks with a single stone seat for the Demeter priestess. The Leonidaion guest house, the Philippeion of Philip II, the Palaestra and Gymnasium for training, and the Echo Colonnade followed in the fourth and third centuries; the hippodrome, with its ingenious staggered starting gate described by Pausanias (6.20.10–14), was long thought lost under the Alpheios but was traced by geophysical survey in 2008.

The epinikion, the choral victory ode, is the literary monument of the games. Simonides, Pindar and Bacchylides wrote them for pay, to be sung at the victor's homecoming or the festival itself. Pindar's forty-five surviving odes are grouped by festival: fourteen Olympians, twelve Pythians, eleven Nemeans, eight Isthmians. The standard shape names the victor, city and event, pivots to a myth tied to the family or city, dispenses maxims on mortality and divine favour, and returns to praise. Olympian 1, for Hieron of Syracuse's horse Pherenicus in 476, opens with water as the best of elements and gold as the brightest, then retells and corrects the Pelops story. Pindar insists victory is a gift of the gods and that the ode preserves it against oblivion: "fame is the one thing that lasts" is a fair summary of his creed, not a quotation. Whether the odes were sung by a chorus or performed solo by a kitharodos is disputed (Lefkowitz argued for solo performance; Carey and Burnett defended the choral tradition), and the question is open.

The Athenian festival year

Athens kept about 120 festival days a year. The Panathenaia (28 Hekatombaion, Athena's birthday, roughly August) was celebrated annually and, from 566/565 BC, as the Great Panathenaia every fourth year with games open to all Greeks. The peplos, a saffron and purple robe showing the Gigantomachy, was begun nine months earlier at the Chalkeia by the Arrephoroi and woven by girls called ergastinai; it was carried in the procession from the Dipylon gate to the Acropolis, in the Hellenistic age hoisted as a sail on a ship-cart, and draped on the ancient olive-wood statue of Athena Polias. The procession, with knights, hoplites, metics carrying trays, maidens with baskets, elders with olive branches and a hundred cattle for the hecatomb, is widely identified with the Parthenon frieze, though Connelly has argued the frieze shows the sacrifice of Erechtheus's daughters instead, a thesis most scholars reject. Events unique to the festival included the apobates (leaping on and off a moving chariot in armour), the Pyrrhic war dance, the euandria (manly beauty), torch race, boat race, and rhapsodic recitation of Homer in sequence (pseudo-Plato, Hipparchus 228b). Prizes were Panathenaic amphorae of olive oil.

The City Dionysia (10–14 Elaphebolion, March) began with the escort of Dionysus Eleuthereus's statue from the Academy and ended with the dramatic contests: three tragedians each with three tragedies and a satyr play, five comedies, and ten dithyrambic choruses from the tribes, all funded by wealthy choregoi and judged by ten citizens drawn by lot. The Rural Dionysia in Poseideon and the Lenaia in Gamelion completed the dramatic calendar. The Anthesteria (11–13 Anthesterion, February) was the oldest Dionysiac festival: Pithoigia (jar-opening of the new wine), Choes (pitchers, a drinking contest, children's first taste of wine, the archon basileus's wife "married" to Dionysus in the Boukoleion; pseudo-Demosthenes 59.73–78), and Chytroi (pots of grain boiled for Hermes Chthonios and the dead). It was an unlucky day; doors were tarred and buckthorn chewed against ghosts, and the proverb "Out, Keres, the Anthesteria is over" sent the spirits away.

Women dominated three festivals. The Thesmophoria (11–13 Pyanepsion, October) was for married citizen women only: Anodos (ascent to the Thesmophorion), Nesteia (fasting on the ground, with ribald abuse), Kalligeneia (fair birth). Women called antletriai had earlier thrown piglets and dough models into pits and now brought up the rotted remains to mix with seed-corn (scholiast to Lucian, Dialogues of the Courtesans 2.1); men were excluded, which Aristophanes's Thesmophoriazusae exploits. The Arrephoria in Skirophorion sent two girls aged seven to eleven, chosen from noble families and resident on the Acropolis, down a natural stair at night carrying covered objects they must not see to the sanctuary of Aphrodite in the Gardens, bringing other objects back (Pausanias 1.27.3); the underground passage survives. The Adonia was a private, mid-summer women's rite with no state funding: women, respectable and hetairai alike, lamented Adonis on the rooftops, planted "gardens of Adonis" in pots that sprouted and withered, and carried effigies to burial; Aristophanes (Lysistrata 387–398) and Plutarch (Nicias 13, Alcibiades 18) link its laments to the launch of the Sicilian expedition in 415.

The Apatouria (Pyanepsion) belonged to the phratries, the hereditary brotherhoods through which citizenship was recognised. On Dorpia men feasted; on Anarrhysis they sacrificed to Zeus Phratrios and Athena Phratria; on Koureotis fathers presented infants (meion) and adolescents cutting their hair (koureion) and swore to their legitimacy. The name was explained by the apate, trick, by which the Athenian Melanthus killed the Boeotian Xanthus.

Spartan festivals

Sparta's three great Apolline festivals shaped its military calendar. The Carneia, in the month Karneios (August–September), honoured Apollo Karneios with a camp-like feast in nine tents and the staphylodromoi, youths who chased a man wreathed in wool; catching him was a good omen for the harvest. Herodotus (6.106, 7.206) reports the Carneia as the reason the Spartans arrived late at Marathon and sent only Leonidas's three hundred to Thermopylae. The Hyacinthia at Amyclae, early summer, mourned the youth Hyacinthus whom Apollo loved and accidentally killed, with a day of grief and no bread or garlands, then days of boys' lyre-songs, girls' processions in wicker carts, and a feast for citizens and slaves (Athenaeus 4.139d–f, quoting Polycrates); Xenophon (Hellenica 4.5.11) records Amyclaeans leaving an army mid-campaign to attend. The Gymnopaidiai, in high summer, were dances by choruses of naked boys and men in the agora, an endurance test in the heat (Plato, Laws 633c); Xenophon (Hellenica 6.4.16) describes the ephors receiving news of Leuctra during the men's chorus and letting it finish. At the sanctuary of Artemis Orthia the diamastigosis, flogging of boys at the altar, grew in Roman times into a spectacle with a stone theatre; the classical form was a cheese-stealing contest at the altar (Xenophon, Constitution of the Lacedaemonians 2.9; Pausanias 3.16.9–11).

Hellenistic and Roman afterlife

From the third century onwards new festivals multiplied, many declared isolympic or isopythian (equal in status) by decree: the Ptolemaia at Alexandria (279/278 BC), the Soteria at Delphi, the Nikephoria at Pergamum, and scores of civic games across Asia Minor. Athletes became professionals organised in guilds under the patronage of Heracles, with imperial charters by the second century AD; the four old games remained the summit of prestige. Rome absorbed rather than suppressed this culture. Sulla moved the Olympic Games of 80 BC to Rome (Appian, Civil Wars 1.99), leaving only the boys' stadion at Olympia. Tiberius and Germanicus won chariot victories as owners. Nero had the games of AD 65 postponed to 67 so that he could compete, added musical contests, fell from a ten-horse chariot and was crowned anyway; the Eleans later annulled his Olympiad (Suetonius, Nero 24; Pausanias 10.7.1 for Delphi). Augustus founded the Actia at Nicopolis and the Sebasta at Naples (AD 2); Domitian the Capitoline Games (AD 86). Herodes Atticus gave Olympia its first aqueduct and nymphaeum in the 150s, and Pausanias, who saw the site intact, remains the best guide.

The end was slow. The last victor in the literary record is the Armenian prince Varazdates, boxing champion in AD 369. Theodosius I's laws of 391–392 (Theodosian Code 16.10.10–12) banned sacrifice and pagan cult, and the chronicler Cedrenus, writing in the eleventh century, says the games ceased after the Olympiad of 393. A bronze plaque from Olympia, however, lists victors to 385, and the workshop of Phidias was still in use. Theodosius II's order of 426 to destroy pagan temples, the fires that gutted the Temple of Zeus, Christian conversion of the site and the earthquakes of 522 and 551 completed the ruin; the Alpheios and Cladeos buried it under metres of silt until 1875.

Where sources disagree. No ancient text records an edict abolishing the Olympic Games. Cedrenus's 393 date is late and inferential; the Theodosian laws forbid sacrifice, not athletics. Recent work on the Olympia inscriptions (Ebert, Sinn) suggests contests of some kind continued into the fifth century, perhaps without the sacrifice to Zeus, and that the Antioch "Olympics" ran until 520. Whether the games "ended in 393" is therefore a modern convenience, not a fact.

Self-test

  1. Name the four crown games with their god and wreath.
    Answer

    Olympia, Zeus, wild olive; Pythia at Delphi, Apollo, laurel; Isthmia, Poseidon, pine (celery for a time); Nemea, Zeus, wild celery.

  2. How did the four fit into one Olympiad?
    Answer

    Olympia in year one, Nemea and Isthmia in years two and four, Pythia in year three, so a crown festival every year.

  3. What two different foundation myths for Olympia does Pindar himself give?
    Answer

    Pelops's chariot victory over Oenomaus (Olympian 1) and Heracles's foundation after defeating Augeas (Olympian 10).

  4. What was the hoplitodromos, and when was it added?
    Answer

    A race, usually a diaulos, run in helmet and shield (originally greaves too); added in 520 BC and closing the athletic programme.

  5. Who actually received the victory in the chariot race, and why did that matter for women?
    Answer

    The owner, not the driver; so Cynisca of Sparta could be Olympic victor in 396 and 392 BC without being present.

  6. What were the Zanes?
    Answer

    Bronze statues of Zeus paid for by fines on cheating athletes, lining the way into the stadium, inscribed with warnings; Pausanias names the offenders.

  7. Give one concrete reward an Athenian Olympic victor received at home.
    Answer

    500 drachmas under Solon's law (Plutarch, Solon 23), or free meals for life in the Prytaneion (IG I3 131).

  8. Describe the Heraia races.
    Answer

    Footraces for unmarried girls in three age groups on a track shortened by one sixth, hair loose, short chiton, right shoulder bare; olive crown and a share of the cow; organised by the Sixteen Women of Elis (Pausanias 5.16).

  9. What happened on the three days of the Thesmophoria, and who attended?
    Answer

    Anodos (ascent), Nesteia (fasting), Kalligeneia (fair birth); married citizen women only, with pig remains retrieved from pits to mix with seed-corn.

  10. Why is "the Olympics ended in 393" an oversimplification?
    Answer

    No edict abolishing the games survives; Theodosius banned sacrifice, Cedrenus's 393 date is late inference, and inscriptions and site evidence suggest contests continued into the fifth century.

Carry forward

  1. Greek athletics was cult first: every crown festival was a sacrifice to a god, grounded in a funeral or heroic myth, and the truce, nudity and oath belong to ritual as much as to sport.
  2. The wreath was symbolic but the system was not: cities, tyrants and families converted victory into cash, meals, statues and odes, so games are a window on how Greek elites competed for honour.
  3. Festivals organised time and membership: the Athenian calendar sorted citizens by sex, age and phratry, Sparta's shaped its wars, and the Olympiad became the Greeks' shared clock.
