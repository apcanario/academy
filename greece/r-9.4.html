How to sit this exam

Closed book. Write every answer in full before opening a model answer; a question you can only answer after reading the answer is a question you have not answered. Allow about four and a quarter hours: two minutes a question in Part A, five in Parts B and C, twenty-five for each essay. The parts may be sat on different days, but sit each part in one sitting, in order.

Scoring. Part A: two marks a question, one for the fact and one for the source or reason, 30 marks. Part B: five marks, awarded only when the answer names the mechanism linking the two items rather than describing them side by side, 50 marks. Part C: five marks, one for identifying the source, two for the interest it serves, two for the independent check that would confirm or undermine it, 50 marks. Part D: fourteen marks an essay, 70 marks. Total 200. Above 150 means the course has settled; between 100 and 150 means the connections are in place but the sources are not; below 100 means revisit the source-criticism modules first, because every other weakness follows from that one.

Where sources disagree. Several model answers take a position on open points: the date of the Spartan reforms, the size of Xerxes' army, the authenticity of Alexander's Royal Journals. Give yourself full marks for an answer that names the dispute and the evidence on each side even where it lands differently from the model. Give yourself nothing for an answer that states one side as settled fact.

Part A: short answer (15)

  1. The Greek landscape is often said to have "produced" the polis. State the geographical argument in one sentence, then give one piece of evidence that it is incomplete.
    Model answer

    Mountains and sea cut Greece into small plains, each with its own water, arable land and harbour, so a community of a few thousand could be self-sufficient and defensible without a territorial state. The argument is incomplete because the same landscape had supported Mycenaean palace-states controlling several plains (Pylos administers two provinces in the Linear B tablets), and because Thessaly and Macedonia, with open plains, produced the ethnos and the kingdom instead. Geography set limits; it did not choose the institution.

  2. Name three Olympian gods attested by name in Linear B and one who is conspicuously uncertain or absent. What does the pattern tell you about the gap between Mycenaean and Homeric religion?
    Model answer

    Zeus, Hera, Poseidon, Hermes, Artemis and Dionysus appear in the Pylos and Knossos tablets; Poseidon is the chief recipient at Pylos (PY Tn 316). Apollo and Aphrodite are not securely attested, and the most frequent recipient is a goddess called only Potnia, "Mistress". The names were old but the shape was not: Poseidon's prominence and Potnia's dominance belong to a palace religion Homer no longer describes. Homer's Olympus is an eighth-century arrangement of twelfth-century material.

  3. Why is Zeus's swallowing of Metis (Hesiod, Theogony 886–900) structurally necessary for the poem to end?
    Model answer

    The Theogony is a succession myth in which each king of heaven is overthrown by a son, and the pattern is driven by the goddess who can bear that son. Warned that Metis will bear a child stronger than his father, Zeus swallows her and bears Athena from his own head, taking over the female power of generation and closing the cycle. Without the episode Zeus would be a Cronus waiting for his Zeus; the same logic makes him refuse Thetis, who is married to Peleus instead (Pindar, Isthmian 8.26–47).

  4. The Judgement of Paris is the first cause of the Trojan War in the Cypria, yet the Iliad mentions it only once. Where, and what does the near-silence tell you about Homer's handling of the Cycle?
    Model answer

    Iliad 24.25–30, where Hera and Athena hate Troy because of the madness of Alexandros, who insulted the goddesses at his farmstead. The Iliad assumes its audience knows the Cycle and refuses to retell it, preferring human causation and the wrath of Achilles to a war that began as a beauty contest. Aristarchus wanted the lines deleted as un-Homeric, which shows how uncomfortable later critics were with the poem's one admission that the folk-tale stood behind it.

  5. What is the Great Rhetra, where is it preserved, and what is the single most important thing it does not mention?
    Model answer

    A short oracular text of constitutional rules quoted by Plutarch (Lycurgus 6) as sent from Delphi to Lycurgus: it founds the cult of Zeus Syllanios and Athena Syllania, divides the people into tribes and obai, sets up a gerousia of thirty including the two kings, and requires regular assemblies with power to decide. Tyrtaeus fr. 4 (West) paraphrases it. It never mentions the ephors, which is why most scholars treat the ephorate as later and the Rhetra as a seventh-century rather than a ninth-century document.

  6. Contrast the dead of Odyssey 11 with the dead promised by the Homeric Hymn to Demeter 480–482. What changed, and which later text shows the second view fully developed?
    Model answer

    In the Nekuia the dead are strengthless shades, and Achilles would rather be a landless labourer among the living than king of the dead (11.489–491); apart from a few mythological sinners there is neither reward nor punishment. The Hymn promises that the Eleusinian initiate is blessed and the uninitiated has no share of good things below: the afterlife is now differentiated by ritual act. Pindar's Olympian 2.56–80 (476 BC) shows the second view complete, with judgement, the Isle of the Blessed and reincarnation. What changed was the arrival of mystery cult and the Orphic and Pythagorean current.

  7. Why did the decision to fight at Salamis rather than at the Isthmus matter beyond the outcome of the battle?
    Model answer

    Herodotus 8.56–63 presents it as Themistocles' threat to sail the fleet to Italy if the Peloponnesians fell back to the Isthmus. Fighting at sea with 200 triremes built from Laurion silver (7.144) made the thetes who rowed them the decisive class in the war, which is exactly the reason the Old Oligarch (Constitution of the Athenians 1.2) later gives for the poor rightly holding power at Athens. Salamis also made the Aegean the theatre of the war's continuation, which is why the Delian League and not Sparta inherited it in 478.

  8. Give three institutional features of the City Dionysia that tied tragedy to the democratic state rather than to a private patron.
    Model answer

    The eponymous archon chose the poets and assigned a choregos to each, so the state selected and a rich citizen paid as a liturgy; the judges were drawn by lot from tribal lists; and before the plays the allied tribute was displayed, war-orphans raised at public expense were paraded in armour and assembly honours were proclaimed (Isocrates 8.82; Aeschines 3.154). The festival was a session of the city about itself.

  9. Heracles is Panhellenic and strongly Dorian; Theseus becomes the Athenian hero only in the late sixth and fifth centuries. Give two pieces of evidence for the promotion of Theseus and one political reason for it.
    Model answer

    Attic vases show Theseus's cycle of deeds on the road from Troezen suddenly from about 510, and Bacchylides 17 and 18 make him a democratic youth-hero; Cimon's recovery of his bones from Skyros in 476/5 (Plutarch, Theseus 36; Cimon 8) made him a civic cult. The new democracy needed a hero who was not the Peisistratids' Heracles, and Theseus could be made founder of the synoikismos (Thucydides 2.15) and so of the unified Attica that Cleisthenes' tribes had in fact created. He is a hero manufactured to a specification.

  10. What did Xenophanes say about Homer's gods, and how does his argument connect to Herodotus's remark (2.53) that Homer and Hesiod made the theogony for the Greeks?
    Model answer

    Xenophanes (B11, B12) says Homer and Hesiod attributed to the gods everything shameful among men; that Ethiopians make their gods black and Thracians blue-eyed (B16); and that there is one god unlike mortals (B23). He is the first Greek to treat the poets' gods as human constructions. Herodotus takes the same step as a historian: the poets, four hundred years before him, gave the gods their genealogy and honours. Both recognise that the poems are the scripture, and can therefore be criticised.

  11. What happened in the proskynesis affair at Bactra in 327, and why did it matter more than a quarrel over etiquette?
    Model answer

    Alexander tried to introduce the Persian court gesture of bowing before the king at a banquet; Callisthenes refused and was later executed on a charge of complicity in the pages' conspiracy (Arrian 4.10–12; Plutarch, Alexander 54). For Persians proskynesis was due to a king, for Greeks only to gods, so the gesture asked Macedonians to behave as Persian subjects and Greeks to treat Alexander as divine. It was the clearest sign that his kingship was becoming neither Macedonian nor Greek, and the point at which Aristotle's circle broke with him.

  12. What was Sarapis, and what does his cult show about how Hellenistic kings used religion?
    Model answer

    An Egyptian-Greek god promoted at Alexandria under Ptolemy I, combining Osiris-Apis with the iconography of Zeus, Hades and Asclepius and paired with Isis; Tacitus (Histories 4.83–84) and Plutarch (Isis and Osiris 28) record that his statue came from Sinope after a dream. The cult gave Greeks and Egyptians a god they could share under royal patronage. It shows a king using religion as civic adhesive in a new city, and foreshadows the Isis and Sarapis cults of the Roman world.

  13. Why does Thucydides present the Melian episode (5.84–116) as a dialogue, when almost everything else in the History is narrative or set speech?
    Model answer

    The dialogue strips away rhetoric and makes the Athenians state the logic of empire baldly, that the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must (5.89), while the Melians answer with justice and hope; it is power talking to weakness with no audience to persuade. Thucydides places it immediately before the Sicilian expedition, so the reader meets Athens at the height of its confidence just before the catastrophe that confidence produced. The form is itself a judgement.

  14. Distinguish the deipnon from the symposium and explain what the symposium did for the men who attended it.
    Model answer

    The deipnon was the meal; the symposium began when the tables were cleared, the libation poured and the krater mixed under a symposiarch. Xenophon's and Plato's Symposia and Theognis show it as the place where elite men bonded, exchanged songs, competed in wit and courted younger men, with respectable women excluded. It transmitted aristocratic culture and alliance outside the institutions of the polis, which is why democratic Athens regarded hetaireiai of drinking companions with suspicion after 415 and 411.

  15. Winckelmann's "noble simplicity and quiet grandeur" (1755) shaped the modern image of Greek sculpture. Name two things the phrase caused viewers to miss.
    Model answer

    Winckelmann worked from Roman marble copies and never saw Greece, so he missed, first, that Greek statues were painted and gilded with inlaid eyes, and second, that most major cult statues were bronze or chryselephantine rather than marble. The phrase also led him to rank the Laocoon and the Apollo Belvedere as the summit when they are Hellenistic and Roman; the Hellenistic baroque and the polychrome korai had no place in his ideal. Our white museum galleries are his legacy.

Part B: connections (10)

  1. Explain the connection between Hesiod's Works and Days and Solon's Eunomia (fr. 4 West), composed a century or more apart.
    Model answer

    Both are poems of social crisis addressed to men who hold power. Hesiod warns the gift-devouring kings (Works and Days 38–39, 263–264) that Dike sits beside Zeus and reports crooked verdicts, and that a just city flourishes while an unjust one suffers famine and plague (225–247). Solon moves the scheme into the city: Athens will not perish by the gods' will, but the citizens destroy it through greed, and Dike, silent, comes in time to exact payment, so that a public evil enters every house. The connection is a theology of justice as natural law punishing the whole community for its leaders' wrongs, transferred from a Boeotian farm to the Athenian assembly and turned from complaint into programme. Solon's laws are the practical answer to a problem Hesiod could only pray about; the step from poet to lawgiver is the step from the eighth century to the polis.

  2. Explain the connection between the Odyssey's description of the island off the Cyclopes' land (9.116–141) and the Greek colonising movement of the eighth century.
    Model answer

    Odysseus describes the goat island in the language of a settler's survey: uncultivated, without hunters or herdsmen, with well-watered meadows where vines would never fail, level ploughland and a harbour needing no anchor. No Homeric hero needs that information; a colonist does. The poem took shape when Euboeans were founding Pithekoussai and Cumae (c. 770–750) and Corinthians Syracuse (733), and the Phaeacian foundation legend (6.7–10: Nausithous walling the city, building temples, dividing the fields) is an oikist's charter in miniature. The Odyssey's wandering is a Mediterranean imagined by sailors who had begun to settle it, and its Cyclopes and Laestrygonians are native populations seen through a settler's mixture of contempt and fear. Colonisation is not a footnote to the poem; it is the poem's world.

  3. Explain the connection between Aeschylus's Eumenides (458 BC) and the reforms of Ephialtes (462/1).
    Model answer

    Ephialtes stripped the Areopagus of the powers by which it supervised magistrates and guarded the laws, leaving it homicide jurisdiction; he was murdered soon after and Cimon was ostracised (Aristotle, Constitution of the Athenians 25). Three years later Aeschylus has Athena found the Areopagus as a homicide court, praise it as the land's bulwark, and warn the citizens neither to pour new laws into old nor to admit anarchy or despotism (Eumenides 681–710). The Furies' conversion is a plea that old guardians accept a new, limited role, and Orestes' promise of eternal Argive alliance (762–774) refers to the alliance Athens made with Argos in 462/1 on breaking with Sparta. Whether Aeschylus endorses the reform by locating the court's original function in homicide, or warns against going further, is disputed; what is not disputed is that a tragedy about the house of Atreus is a document of a constitutional revolution.

  4. Explain the connection between the Spartan agoge as later sources describe it and the way Herodotus tells Thermopylae.
    Model answer

    Herodotus's Thermopylae is a demonstration of Spartan nomos: Demaratus tells Xerxes the Spartans are free but have a master, the law, which forbids flight (7.104); they comb their hair and exercise while the Persian scout watches (7.208–209); Leonidas stays because Spartans do not leave their post (7.220); the epigram says they lie there obedient to Sparta's words (7.228). Each element enacts the training Xenophon (Constitution of the Lacedaemonians 2–4) and Plutarch (Lycurgus 16–18) describe. But the connection runs the other way too. Thermopylae made the Spartan mirage; from 480 the Spartans had an episode to live up to, and the agoge as Plutarch describes it was hardened in the fourth and third centuries into the thing that could explain Thermopylae. Battle and institution each made the other legible.

  5. Explain the connection between the figure of Heracles and the kingship of Alexander.
    Model answer

    The Argead kings claimed descent from Temenus of Argos and so from Heracles (Herodotus 8.137–139), which made Heracles the guarantee that a Macedonian king was Greek; Alexander's tetradrachms carry a young Heracles in the lion-skin, and Hellenistic eyes came to see the head as Alexander's own. On campaign Heracles is a measure: the insistence on sacrificing to him inside Tyre justified a seven-month siege (Arrian 2.15–16), and at Aornos Alexander was gripped by pothos because Heracles was said to have failed there (Arrian 4.28). Heracles was also the mortal who earned divinity by labours, the fiction under which Alexander's deification became thinkable. The connection is a political theology: a Dorian and Panhellenic hero made a Macedonian king a Greek, and a hero who became a god made a Greek king a god.

  6. Explain the connection between Orphic and Pythagorean teaching about the soul and the arguments of Plato's Phaedo and Republic.
    Model answer

    Pythagoras taught transmigration (Xenophanes B7 mocks him for recognising a friend's soul in a beaten dog); Empedocles B115 describes the soul's exile through many bodies; the Orphic gold leaves and the Derveni papyrus show a cult promising release through purity and the right words. Plato names the inheritance: in the Phaedo (69c–70a) the founders of the mysteries were not fools when they said the unpurified lie in the mire, and the argument from recollection (Meno 81a–b) rests on a pre-existence he credits to priests, priestesses and Pindar. The Gorgias (493a) attributes to a Sicilian or Italian the pun that the body is a tomb, and the Republic ends with the Myth of Er (614b–621d). Plato took a marginal tradition's eschatology, stripped it of ritual and attached it to argument, making the immortality of the soul a philosophical thesis. Western dualism descends from a cult Athens barely tolerated.

  7. Explain the connection between the tribute of the Delian League and the Parthenon.
    Model answer

    Plutarch (Pericles 12–14) records the charge that Athens was adorning herself like a vain woman with the allies' money, and Pericles' reply that so long as Athens defended the allies it owed them no account. The quota lists from 454/3, after the treasury moved from Delos, record one-sixtieth of each city's tribute dedicated to Athena; the building accounts (447–432) and Thucydides' 6,000 talents in reserve in 431 (2.13) show the scale. Whether the programme drew directly on tribute or on Athena's own treasury is disputed, but the sculpture answers: the frieze shows the Panathenaic procession, to which allies had to send a cow and a panoply, and the metopes repeat the victories over Amazons, Trojans, centaurs and giants that Athens had made the pattern of its leadership against Persia. The Parthenon is the League's monument, and its programme the empire's self-justification in marble.

  8. Explain the connection between Thucydides' account of the plague (2.47–54) and the Hippocratic writings of the late fifth century.
    Model answer

    Thucydides says he will describe the symptoms so that anyone may recognise the disease if it returns, having had it himself, and declines to speculate on its cause (2.48); he then gives an ordered account by stage, head to extremities, with course and prognosis. This is the method of the Epidemics and the Prognostic: observation, sequence, the body as a system with a foreseeable course, and the exclusion of divine explanation that Airs, Waters, Places and On the Sacred Disease make explicit. Thucydides extends it to society: the plague breaks the nomoi of burial and restraint, and the moral collapse is a symptom with its own course, exactly as the Corcyraean stasis is later (3.82–83). The shared epistemology tells you what Thucydides thinks history is: a case history, written for the physician of the next outbreak.

  9. Explain the connection between the Trojan War and the way Herodotus and his contemporaries explained the Persian Wars.
    Model answer

    Herodotus opens with the Persian learned men's account of the quarrel as a chain of abductions, Io, Europa, Medea, Helen, with the Greek destruction of Troy as the first great wrong of Europe against Asia (1.1–5), then sets it aside as unverifiable and begins with Croesus. The frame returns when Xerxes at Troy sacrifices a thousand cattle to Athena of Ilium (7.43) as if avenging Priam. On the Greek side Aeschylus's Persians treats Salamis as tragic reversal, Attic vases of the 460s dress Persians as Trojans, and the Parthenon metopes set the sack of Troy beside the Amazonomachy as ancestor of the Persian defeat. The Trojan War supplied the only model for a war between Greece and Asia, so both sides read 480 through it; Herodotus's achievement was to accept the frame while refusing its explanatory power.

  10. Explain the connection between the resistance myths of Dionysus (Pentheus, Lycurgus, the daughters of Minyas, the Tyrrhenian pirates) and the social order of the polis.
    Model answer

    In each myth a king, a city or a company of men refuses the god's arrival and is destroyed by madness, dismemberment or transformation, while the women who accept him leave the house for the mountain. Euripides' Bacchae has Pentheus insist on male control of the women of Thebes and be torn apart by his mother; Homeric Hymn 7 turns the pirates into dolphins. The pattern dramatises what the polis feared and needed: the suspension of the boundaries that constituted it, male and female, citizen and foreigner, human and animal, sober and mad, and the recognition that a community which cannot suspend them in licensed form will have them broken. Dionysus is not in fact a late arrival, since his name stands on Linear B tablets from Pylos and Chania, so the arriving god is a myth about the experience of ecstasy, not a record of religious history. The Dionysia and the maenadic thiasoi are the safe forms of what the myths show arriving unsafely.

Part C: sources and their bias (10)

  1. "Lycurgus gave the Spartans their whole constitution at once, with the sanction of Delphi." Who says this and why should you doubt it?
    Model answer

    Herodotus (1.65) says the Spartans attribute all their institutions to Lycurgus; Xenophon's Constitution of the Lacedaemonians and Plutarch's Lycurgus expand him into the author of everything from the agoge to the iron currency, though Plutarch opens (1.1) by admitting that nothing about the man is agreed. Doubt it because the attribution is a Spartan device for making every institution ancient and divine; because Thucydides (1.18) dates Spartan good order to about 820 while the Great Rhetra knows nothing of the ephors and Tyrtaeus in the seventh century is the first to quote it; and because Laconian archaeology shows imported luxuries and ivory dedications at Artemis Orthia until about 550, after which austerity sets in. The check is the material record, which places Lycurgan Sparta in the sixth century and makes the lawgiver a name retrojected over a long process.

  2. "Xerxes crossed into Greece with 1,700,000 infantry, and his whole host, with camp followers, exceeded five million." Who says this and why should you doubt it?
    Model answer

    Herodotus 7.60 gives the infantry figure, counted by penning 10,000 men at a time, and 7.184–187 totals the host at 5,283,220. Doubt it on logistics: an army that size would have stretched from the Hellespont back to Sardis, and its daily grain and water would have exceeded any depot system. Doubt it because the numbers serve the story of the few against the many, and the Thermopylae epigram quoted at 7.228 already speaks of three million, so Herodotus inherited an inflated tradition. Modern estimates run from about 70,000 to 300,000 combatants, based on the Hellespont bridges, known Persian organisation and the watering points on the route. The check is the fleet figure of 1,207 triremes, which Aeschylus's Persians (341–343) also gives and which is plausible; the naval numbers come from a better tradition than the land numbers.

  3. "Theseus united the scattered communities of Attica into one city with one council, and founded the Synoikia to commemorate it." Who says this and why should you doubt it?
    Model answer

    Thucydides (2.15) says it as fact, prefacing the evacuation of Attica in 431, and Plutarch (Theseus 24) adds the festival. Doubt it because a unified Attica is not visible in the Bronze Age, when Athens, Eleusis, Marathon and Thorikos were separate centres; because Eleusis has a tradition of war with Athens and the Hymn to Demeter never mentions Athens, so the two seem joined only in the seventh century; and because the Theseus who could be a political founder was created in the late sixth century, exactly when Cleisthenes' tribes gave Attica a single structure for the first time. The story projects a fifth-century reality backwards, and Thucydides, who elsewhere corrects popular memory, accepts it because it serves his point about Athenian attachment to the land. The check is the Theseus cycle appearing on vases from about 510.

  4. "Socrates was condemned because he did not acknowledge the gods the city acknowledges, introduced new divinities and corrupted the young." Who says this and why should you doubt it as the real reason?
    Model answer

    This is the indictment, preserved by Diogenes Laertius (2.40) and paraphrased by Xenophon (Memorabilia 1.1.1) and Plato (Apology 24b–c). Doubt it as the whole story because Plato and Xenophon are defenders; because Aristophanes' Clouds (423) had already fixed Socrates as a sophist who taught the young to argue down their fathers, and Plato has Socrates call this old accusation the real one (Apology 18b–19c); and because the amnesty of 403 forbade prosecution for acts under the Thirty, so a religious charge was the only legal route to the man who had taught Critias and Alcibiades. Aeschines (1.173), in 345, says bluntly that the Athenians executed Socrates because he had taught Critias. The check is the date: 399, four years after the Thirty fell, not the 420s when Clouds was staged.

  5. "The subjects of Athens hated the empire and longed for liberation." Who says this and why should you doubt it?
    Model answer

    Thucydides is the main source: the Corinthians at Sparta (1.68–71), Sparta's claim to be liberating Greece (2.8), the Mytilenean envoys (3.9–14); the Old Oligarch (1.14) adds that Athens backs the demos in allied cities and prosecutes the good men. Doubt it because Thucydides and his informants belonged to the class that led the revolts, and the revolts tell another story: Mytilene's was the work of its oligarchy and collapsed when the demos was armed (3.27–28); the Samian demos fought for Athens in 412–411; and Thucydides has Phrynichus say (8.48.5–6) that the allies wanted neither oligarchy nor Spartan freedom but to be left alone. De Ste. Croix argued in 1954 that the lower classes of the allied cities were broadly pro-Athenian because Athenian courts protected them from their own oligarchs, and the debate is open. The check is to count, revolt by revolt, which class rose and who was restored afterwards.

  6. "Sappho was a schoolmistress who taught girls music, and she died throwing herself from the Leucadian rock for love of the ferryman Phaon." Who says this and why should you doubt it?
    Model answer

    The Suda gives the school; Menander (fr. 258 Kassel–Austin) and Ovid (Heroides 15) give the leap; fourth-century Attic comedies named Sappho made her a figure of ridicule, and the leap for Phaon is a plot from those plays attached to her name. Doubt it because the school is a nineteenth-century defence, Welcker's of 1816, built to protect a canonical poet from the homoerotic plainness of fragments 1, 16, 31 and 94; because Phaon is a Lesbian folk-tale figure attracted to Sappho's name because she wrote about Aphrodite; and because nothing in the fragments supports a school, a husband named Kerkylas (a comic obscenity) or a suicide. The check is the papyri, which show a poet writing for a circle of women, for weddings and for civic occasions, and the Brothers Poem published in 2014, which shows her writing about family politics in Mytilene. Every life of Sappho is an argument about what a woman poet could be.

  7. "Alexander was poisoned by Iollas, Antipater's son, with a drug Aristotle supplied." Who says this and why should you doubt it?
    Model answer

    Arrian (7.27) and Plutarch (Alexander 77) record the story and both distance themselves: Arrian reports it only so as not to seem ignorant of what is said, Plutarch says most consider it fabricated. Diodorus (17.117–118) and Justin (12.13–14) give it more weight, and the Liber de Morte names the whole guest list. Doubt it because the story is traceable to the propaganda war of 317, when Olympias, fighting Antipater's son Cassander, was circulating the charge, and because the Royal Journals quoted by Arrian and Plutarch describe a twelve-day fever that fits malaria or typhoid; whether the Journals are authentic is contested, but no ancient poison acts over twelve days. The check is motive and opportunity: Antipater had reason to fear recall, but nothing places Iollas with a poison. The story tells you about the Successors, not about Alexander's death.

  8. "Pericles started the Peloponnesian War to escape the Pheidias scandal and his own accounts, and because the Megarians stole two of Aspasia's girls." Who says this and why should you doubt it?
    Model answer

    Aristophanes (Acharnians 524–539; Peace 605–618) says both, as comic aetiology; Plutarch (Pericles 31–32) reports the Pheidias prosecution and the charge; Diodorus (12.38–40), following Ephorus, turns the comic version into history. Doubt it because comedy's job was to make the great man small and the great cause petty; because Ephorus took the comic poets as witnesses; because Thucydides (1.23, 1.88) gives the truest cause as Spartan fear of Athenian growth and treats the Megarian decree as pretext; and because Pheidias's chronology, with the Zeus at Olympia after 438, is hard to reconcile with the trial as Plutarch has it. The check is to ask what evidence Aristophanes could have had of cabinet motive. None, and he needed none; he needed a laugh in 425 from an audience that wanted peace.

  9. "Harmodius and Aristogeiton freed Athens from tyranny." Who says this and why should you doubt it?
    Model answer

    The Athenians said it: the drinking-song preserved by Athenaeus (15.695a–b) calls them the men who made Athens isonomos; Antenor's bronze group of about 510, replaced by Critius and Nesiotes in 477/6, stood in the Agora as the only statues of mortals there; Aristotle (Constitution of the Athenians 18) repeats the honour. Thucydides corrects it twice (1.20; 6.53–59): the pair killed Hipparchus, who was not the tyrant, in a private quarrel over a love affair, and the result was four years of harsher tyranny until Cleomenes of Sparta and the Alcmaeonids expelled Hippias in 510. Doubt the Athenian version because a democracy needed a founding act by its own citizens rather than a liberation by Spartans at the prompting of an exiled aristocratic family, and because the statue group fixed the story against correction. The check is Herodotus (5.62–65), who gives Delphi, the Alcmaeonids and Cleomenes the credit. A public monument manufactured history, and a historian struggled against it.

  10. "Homer was a blind bard from Chios, and he competed with Hesiod at Chalcis, where the judges gave the prize to Hesiod." Who says this and why should you doubt it?
    Model answer

    The blindness and Chios come from the Homeric Hymn to Apollo (165–173), where the poet asks the Delian girls to name a blind man from rocky Chios as the sweetest singer, a passage Thucydides (3.104) already takes as self-portrait; the Lives of Homer and the Certamen Homeri et Hesiodi, a second-century AD text built on a fourth-century work of Alcidamas, give the contest. Doubt it because the Hymn to Apollo is composite, probably stitched together in the sixth century, and the blind singer is a type like Demodocus in Odyssey 8; because Hesiod (Works and Days 654–659) describes winning a tripod at Chalcis and never mentions a rival; and because the biographical tradition is manufactured from the poems, which is why seven cities claimed him. The check is the oral-formulaic analysis of Parry and Lord, which makes "Homer" a tradition's name for its product. The biography is reception, not record.

Part D: essays (5)

Where sources disagree. None of the essays has a single right answer. On the second, Isocrates (Panegyricus) and Thucydides (1.23) would give opposite verdicts on whether 480 ruined Greece; on the fourth, Pausanias and Lucian curate very different pasts from the same material. An essay that argues one side and shows it knows the other earns the marks; an essay that balances without deciding earns half.
  1. Greek religion had no scripture, yet it had Homer and Hesiod. Discuss how the poets shaped cult and were shaped by it, with reference to at least three periods.
    Model answer

    Greek religion was a religion of practice, sacrifice, festival, procession, oracle and vow, administered by the polis and tested only by whether it was the way things had always been done. Into that world the epic poets inserted a shared account of who the gods were, and Herodotus (2.53) states the result: Homer and Hesiod gave the Greeks their gods' genealogy, names, honours and forms. This was not a scripture governing ritual; the Panathenaic recitation of Homer was a festival event, not a liturgy. But it was a canon, and a canon has consequences.

    In the Archaic period the traffic ran both ways. The poets drew on cult, so that the Hymn to Demeter is an aetiology of Eleusis; cult drew on the poets, so that sixth-century cult statues and the pediments of Olympia give the gods Homeric faces and quarrels. Xenophanes' attack on the poets' immoral gods (B11, B12) only makes sense because they had become authoritative. In the Classical period the authority became a problem: Plato's censorship of Homer in Republic 2–3 is a quarrel with a canon, not with a cult, and the impiety prosecutions of Anaxagoras, Diagoras and Socrates concern denial of the gods the city honours rather than of anything in a book.

    In the Hellenistic and Roman periods the poets were defended by allegory, Stoic and then Neoplatonic, precisely because they could not be given up: Heraclitus the Allegorist and later Porphyry read the battles of the gods as physics and the cave of the nymphs as the soul's descent, which is what one does with a scripture one cannot discard. Pausanias measures every local cult against Homer and Hesiod and records the discrepancies with pleasure. Across the three periods the poets never commanded ritual but increasingly governed what the gods could be thought to be, so the history of Greek religion is in part the history of a people arguing with two poems they had made sacred.

  2. Trace the consequences of the Persian Wars down to Chaeronea (338). Did the victory of 480–479 ruin Greece by creating an empire?
    Model answer

    The first consequence was institutional. Salamis made Athens a naval power dependent on its poorest citizens, and continuing the war in the Aegean required an alliance Sparta would not lead; the Delian League of 478/7, with assessed tribute and a treasury at Delos, was the Persian War made permanent. By 454 the treasury was at Athens, by 449 the war with Persia was effectively over, and the League did not dissolve; Thucydides (1.96–99) describes the slide from alliance to empire as a sequence of defections and reductions each of which seemed justified. His truest cause of the next war (1.23), Athenian growth and Spartan fear, is already present, and both are products of 480.

    The Peloponnesian War destroyed the empire and left Sparta unable to run what it had won. The King's Peace of 387/6 handed the Asian Greeks back to Persia, reversing the war's original object, and the fourth century became a cycle of hegemonies, Spartan, Theban, briefly Athenian, each broken by the others with Persian gold. Isocrates' Panegyricus (380) is the clearest diagnosis: the Greeks have turned victory over the barbarian into wars among themselves, and only a renewed crusade under one leader can heal them. Philip took the diagnosis and the role, and Chaeronea ended the independent polis as arbiter of Greek affairs.

    Did the victory ruin Greece? The case for yes is that it created the only Greek state with the resources and self-belief to attempt hegemony, that hegemony provoked the war that exhausted the Greek world, and that exhaustion made the Macedonian solution possible. The case for no is that the alternative in 480 was a Persian satrapy, under which the fifth century's drama, history and philosophy are unimaginable, and that the polis system's inability to form a stable federal order predates Athens and outlasts it. The victory did not ruin Greece, but it decided what kind of Greek failure would follow: an imperial one rather than a provincial one. Whether that was worse is a judgement about what the fifth century was worth, and Thucydides and Isocrates would answer differently.

  3. "The hero is a figure the polis both needs and fears." Discuss with reference to at least two mythical heroes and one historical figure.
    Model answer

    The Greek hero is a being of excess, stronger, angrier and more dangerous than the men around him, and the myths are as much about containing him as celebrating him. Heracles kills the Nemean lion and also his music teacher, his wife and his children; Achilles is the best of the Achaeans and the man whose wrath kills them by the hundred; Ajax is the army's bulwark and the man who tries to murder its commanders. The polis needed these figures as founders and protectors, and buried them at its boundaries as cult heroes whose power was both protective and perilous; Oedipus at Colonus, a polluted outcast whose grave will defend Athens, is the clearest statement.

    Tragedy is where the fifth-century polis thought the problem through. Sophocles' Ajax shows a hero whose code cannot survive in a community governed by persuasion and law, then has Odysseus, the man of the new order, insist he be buried with honour; the Philoctetes shows the army needing the wounded, hating hero it had abandoned. Euripides' Heracles brings the saviour home to kill his family in a madness sent by Hera, then has Theseus take him into Athens, which is Athens claiming to be the only polis that can hold such a man. The hero is indispensable, the hero is intolerable, and the city's excellence lies in managing the contradiction.

    Alcibiades is the historical case, and Thucydides frames him in exactly these terms (6.15): his private excesses frightened the people into thinking him a would-be tyrant, so they entrusted the war to others and ruined the city, though his public conduct of it was the best available. Ostracism, the institution for removing a man too big for the city, is the polis's answer to the hero in its midst, and Athens applied it to Themistocles and Cimon, the architects of Salamis and the League. The thesis holds because the polis was a community of equals that could neither produce greatness without excess nor survive excess without law, and the hero is where the two requirements collide.

  4. How did the Greeks of the Roman period curate their own past, and how does their curation still shape what we take "Greece" to have been?
    Model answer

    After 146 BC the Greeks were a subject people with a prestigious past, and the Roman period is when that past was turned into heritage. Plutarch wrote the Parallel Lives to set Greek statesmen beside Roman ones as equals in virtue; his Greece is Archaic and Classical, moral and exemplary, and almost entirely pre-Macedonian. Pausanias walked the mainland in the second century AD recording temples, statues and local traditions, but he chose: he wants what is old, Greek and religious, dismisses Roman buildings in a line and barely notices the Hellenistic cities, so his Greece is a landscape of memory in which nothing has happened since Chaeronea. Atticism trained men to write the Greek of Demosthenes rather than the Greek they spoke, Lucian mocked both the pretension and the gods it kept alive, and Hadrian's Panhellenion gave the curation an imperial institution.

    The curation set the frame we still use. The periodisation that makes the fifth and fourth centuries Classical and everything after a decline is a Roman-period judgement that Plutarch and Pausanias embody; the canon of ten orators, three tragedians and nine lyric poets is Alexandrian and Roman library work; the idea that Greek religion is Homer's Olympians rather than the local cults Pausanias records comes from reading the curators' summaries rather than their footnotes. Winckelmann's Greece was built from Roman copies and from Pausanias's descriptions of originals he could not see, and the museum and the school syllabus followed.

    What the curation hid matters as much as what it preserved: the Hellenistic world of Alexandria, Pergamon and Antioch, the Greek East's engagement with Egypt, Persia and Judaea, the vitality of Greek cities under Rome, and the Byzantine millennium after. The reader of this course has learned a Greece shaped by men already nostalgic for it. The remedy is not to discard Plutarch and Pausanias but to read them as the first and most influential reception of Greece, written by Greeks who had decided that the best of their history was behind them.

  5. Set the women of myth and tragedy, Clytemnestra, Medea, Antigone, Penelope, against the evidence for women's lives in the historical polis. What does the gap between the two tell us?
    Model answer

    The evidence for citizen women in Athens is of legal incapacity and domestic confinement: a woman was under a kyrios for life, could not own land or contract above a trivial sum, inherited only as an epikleros married to pass property to a male heir, and was expected to be invisible, so that Pericles in Thucydides (2.45) tells the widows that their greatest glory is to be least spoken of. Xenophon's Oeconomicus (7–10) describes the ideal wife as a girl of fifteen trained by her husband to run the storeroom. Sparta was the exception, and Aristotle's complaint (Politics 1269b–1270a) that Spartan women owned two-fifths of the land tells us how abnormal the exception seemed. Sappho's circle shows that aristocratic women in the Archaic Aegean had a cultural life invisible in Classical Athens.

    The women of myth are nothing like this. Clytemnestra rules Argos and kills her husband; Medea negotiates with kings and destroys her husband's future; Antigone defies the state's decree and is proved right by the gods; Penelope governs Ithaca and out-manoeuvres Odysseus himself. All four were written by men for male audiences, and the simplest reading is that they are projections of fear, the imagination of what a woman would do with the power she was denied. That reading is right but incomplete, because the plays also give the women arguments the audience is made to hear, and Medea's speech on the condition of wives (Euripides, Medea 230–251) is not a parody.

    The gap tells us three things. Myth was a space in which the polis could think about what it had excluded, as the Dionysus and Amazon myths also do. The heroic age, imagined as a world of households rather than cities, gave women a scope the polis had deliberately removed, so the myths preserve a distorted memory of a different distribution of power. And the texts we have are evidence not for women's experience but for men's anxieties about it; the real evidence, stelai, inscriptions, wills and the Gortyn code, is thin, legal and almost silent on what women thought. The gap is not a contradiction to be resolved; it is the shape of the evidence.

Where the gaps will still be

A forty-nine module course is a strong map with blank areas. These are the ten most likely blanks, each with a prompt that would produce the module to fill it.

Where sources disagree. Several of these are gaps because the field itself is unsettled: the function of Linear A, the degree of continuity across the Early Iron Age and the character of the Greek economy are live controversies, and the prompts ask for the dispute, not a verdict.
  • Minoan Crete and Thera. The course reached the Bronze Age through Mycenae; Knossos, Linear A and Akrotiri were background. 
    Write a module on Minoan Crete from the Prepalatial period to the Mycenaean takeover: palace function, Linear A and what can and cannot be said about it, religion from frescoes and seals, the Thera eruption and its disputed date, and what later Greeks remembered as Minos.
  • The Early Iron Age. The centuries between the palaces and Homer were crossed quickly. 
    Write a module on Greece from 1200 to 800 BC: the end of the palaces, depopulation and continuity, Lefkandi and the Toumba building, Protogeometric and Geometric pottery, the Ionian migration tradition, the alphabet, and the debate between collapse and transformation.
  • The Greek economy. Coinage, slavery and trade were touched in passing; the Finley model and its critics were not confronted. 
    Write a module on the ancient Greek economy: the agrarian base, the invention and spread of coinage, slavery and its scale, long-distance trade and the emporion, the Finley primitivist model and the Morris and Ober case for growth, with the evidence for each.
  • The Greek West. Sicily and Italy appeared as colonies and as Athens's graveyard; Syracuse and the Western thinkers need their own module. 
    Write a module on the Greeks of Sicily and southern Italy from the foundations to Agathocles: Syracuse under the Deinomenids and Dionysius I, Himera and Carthage, Pythagoreans at Croton, Empedocles and Gorgias, Plato's Sicilian visits, and why the West produced tyranny where the mainland produced the polis.
  • Macedon before Philip and the northern Greeks. Macedon entered fully formed in 359; the Argead monarchy, Thessaly and Epirus were not examined. 
    Write a module on Macedon and northern Greece before 359 BC: Argead kingship from Alexander I to Perdiccas III, Macedonian institutions, the Thessalian tagos and Jason of Pherae, Epirus and the Molossians, Archelaus's court, and the ancient and modern debate over whether the Macedonians were Greeks.
  • Hellenistic science and medicine. Alexandria was treated as a library and a court; Euclid, Archimedes, Aristarchus and Herophilus were names. 
    Write a module on Hellenistic science and medicine: Euclid and the axiomatic method, Archimedes, Aristarchus's heliocentrism and why it failed, Eratosthenes' measurement of the earth, Hipparchus, the Alexandrian anatomists and human dissection, and what the Museum and the kings had to do with it.
  • Greek law and the lawcourts. Draco, Solon and Gortyn were mentioned; the working of Athenian courts and the orators as evidence were not. 
    Write a module on Greek law: the Gortyn code, Draco's homicide law, Athenian procedure from summons to verdict, dicasts and the lot, the speeches of Lysias, Demosthenes and Isaeus as evidence for social life, sycophancy and the graphe paranomon, and how law without lawyers worked.
  • Mystery cults, magic and the religion of the margins. Eleusis and Orphism appeared in the underworld module; Samothrace, curse tablets and the magical papyri did not. 
    Write a module on Greek mystery cults and magic: Eleusis in full, Samothrace, the Bacchic and Orphic associations with the gold leaves and the Derveni papyrus, curse tablets and binding spells, the Greek Magical Papyri, and how the polis distinguished acceptable from unacceptable religion.
  • The transmission of the texts. The course read Greek literature as if it had always been there. 
    Write a module on how Greek texts survived: Alexandrian editing and the canon, papyri and the codex, the Byzantine renaissance under Photius and Arethas, Planudes and the Palaeologan scholars, the Arabic translation movement and what it chose, the flight of manuscripts to Italy, and Aldus Manutius.
  • Modern Greece and the politics of antiquity. Reception ended with the Western museum; the use of the ancient past by and about modern Greece was left aside. 
    Write a module on antiquity in modern Greek politics: Philhellenism and the War of Independence, Fallmerayer's continuity thesis and the Greek response, the Great Idea, the language question and katharevousa, the Parthenon marbles dispute, the naming dispute over Macedonia, and how the ancient past is taught and displayed in Greece today.

Carry forward

  1. Every Greek source is an argument addressed to someone. Before asking whether a statement is true, ask who is speaking, to whom, with what interest, and what independent check exists; the ten cases in Part C are the template for every future one.
  2. A myth has no single form, only versions with dates and tellers, and each version describes the society that told it in the register of the past. Theseus in 510, the Furies in 458 and Heracles on Alexander's coins are evidence for their own moments, not for the heroic age.
  3. Greek history did not end at Chaeronea, and the sense that it did is itself a Greek creation of the Roman period. The polis, the agon and the gods outlived the independence of the city-state, and the curators who told us otherwise are the first chapter of reception, not the last word on Greece.
