Why history and myth together

the method of the track
μῦθοςmythos

In Homer, simply what someone says with authority: a speech, a command, a tale. By the fifth century it had come to mean the story you cannot check.

λόγοςlogos

A word, a count, an account. By the same century it meant the argued explanation, the one that gives its reasons and invites you to test them.

The Greeks drew that line themselves. Herodotus calls his work a historiē, an inquiry, and still reports what the Persians and Egyptians told him. Thucydides warns that his history leaves out the mythōdes, the storylike, and will seem less pleasant for it (1.22). Plato expels the poets from his ideal city and then ends the Republic with a myth of his own.

The tidy story of a clean progress "from mythos to logos" (the title of Wilhelm Nestle's book of 1940) is now itself treated as a modern myth: the Greeks never stopped telling myths; their philosophers used them, their historians argued with them, and their cities were governed by them. So each course reads a culture's myths and its history together, asks who told each story and what they gained by it, and keeps the evidence in view. The aim is not to debunk the myths but to understand them as well as the history. Later courses point back to earlier ones where they touch: Rome to Greece's units 7 and 10, Persia to units 6 and 11, Egypt to units 1 and 7.

How every course works

the constants
  1. One reader. Every course is written for the same person and read in order, on a phone, with the citations checked.
  2. Sources first. A unit names its sources and says where they disagree; a claim that cannot be sourced is marked unsourced or left out.
  3. One unit per chat, built in full. Each unit is written from a published brief or prompt, in its own chat, with its closing artefact: a self-test and carry-forward ideas for the history courses, a shipped deliverable for Brand + AI.
  4. Audited every four units. After every four units written in a hub, a stratified sample of claims is checked against its sources with a second model; a unit that fails gets a full audit. The model that wrote each unit is recorded.
  5. An open syllabus. Every prompt and brief is published with its course, so any unit can be regenerated, extended or checked.

Procedure

  1. Make a Project ("Courses — Brand & AI"). Add to its knowledge: this page, the research report, the five notes files. Project instructions, three lines: who the reader is; this page holds every brief; each unit is written in full, not described.
  2. One chat per unit. Copy the unit's brief from its Briefs page, paste it into a new chat in that Project. Never reuse a chat across units; leaked context is how a course starts repeating itself.
  3. Save the output to the vault as courses/brand-ai/<code>-<slug>.md (e.g. B1-mental-model.md), same shape as the history course. Hand it to the assistant: it commits, adds the unit to Learn, moves the counters, adds a changelog line.
  4. Units that need live facts (A8, B2, B8, DD-A1, DD-B3) are written with web search on; the briefs say so. Everything else without.
  5. After every fourth unit, run the audit prompt from the Audits page. The first fires after B1.

Models

  • Writing: Opus 5.5 for all remaining units. The quality gap shows most on long-form synthesis, which is this.
  • The shipped deliverable is written in the same chat as its unit, never separately, so it inherits the unit's reasoning.
  • Reviewing: Fable 5.1, in the personal-assistant Project where this hub lives. One claim per section per unit checked against its source; more than one in ten fails → full audit.
  • Not Sonnet or Haiku for units or audits. They are the right tools for turning a finished unit into posts, a Shorts list or an email; that is B7's pipeline.

Review rule

After every four written units, across both courses, Fable 5.1 audits by stratified sample: one claim per section per unit, checked against its source; more than one in ten fails → full audit of that unit. Units written with live search (A2, A6, A9, A8, B2, B8, DD-B3) are re-checked for staleness six months after writing.

Stratified-sample audit (every four units)
You are auditing four units of a self-study course written by Opus 5.5 for one reader. Units: {list the four unit codes and their vault paths}.

Method, in this order:
1. For each unit, list its sections. From every section draw one claim that carries a number, a named source, a mechanism or a recommendation. That is the sample; record it before checking anything.
2. Check each sampled claim against the source the unit cites: open the source; confirm the figure, the date, the sample size and that the claim is what the source says, not a stretch of it. Mark each claim PASS, FAIL (wrong, unsupported, miscited, or invented) or STALE (true when written, superseded now, with the newer figure).
3. For each unit compute the failure rate. If more than one claim in ten is FAIL, audit that unit in full: every cited claim, same procedure.
4. Check the deliverable: it exists in full inside the unit, it follows from the unit's evidence, and it is usable without reading the unit.
5. Check against the course goal and sourcing standard (in the unit's preamble): does the unit move the reader toward the stated outcomes, and is anything in it advice without evidence?

Output: a table per unit (section, claim, source, verdict, note); the failure rate; a verdict per unit (PASS / FULL AUDIT RUN, result / REWRITE with the sections named); the three most consequential errors across the four units in plain language; and one line each for the bibliography (new sources to add, sources to mark failed) and the changelog. Do not fix the units; report.
Staleness check (six months after a live-search unit)
You are re-checking one unit written with live search six months ago: {unit code, vault path, date written}. Search again, today, for every fact in it that can change: platform rules and thresholds, prices and plan limits, model lineups, benchmark figures, regulatory status, the numbers attached to named creators. For each: the value in the unit, the value now, the source and date for the new value, and whether the unit's conclusion still holds. End with a verdict: CURRENT / PATCH (list the lines to change) / REWRITE (say which sections). Do not rewrite; report.

You are auditing four units of a self-study course written by Opus 5.5 for one reader. Units: {list the four unit codes and their vault paths}.

Stratified-sample audit (every four units)
You are auditing four units of a self-study course written by Opus 5.5 for one reader. Units: {list the four unit codes and their vault paths}.

Method, in this order:
1. For each unit, list its sections. From every section draw one claim that carries a number, a named source, a mechanism or a recommendation. That is the sample; record it before checking anything.
2. Check each sampled claim against the source the unit cites: open the source; confirm the figure, the date, the sample size and that the claim is what the source says, not a stretch of it. Mark each claim PASS, FAIL (wrong, unsupported, miscited, or invented) or STALE (true when written, superseded now, with the newer figure).
3. For each unit compute the failure rate. If more than one claim in ten is FAIL, audit that unit in full: every cited claim, same procedure.
4. Check the deliverable: it exists in full inside the unit, it follows from the unit's evidence, and it is usable without reading the unit.
5. Check against the course goal and sourcing standard (in the unit's preamble): does the unit move the reader toward the stated outcomes, and is anything in it advice without evidence?

Output: a table per unit (section, claim, source, verdict, note); the failure rate; a verdict per unit (PASS / FULL AUDIT RUN, result / REWRITE with the sections named); the three most consequential errors across the four units in plain language; and one line each for the bibliography (new sources to add, sources to mark failed) and the changelog. Do not fix the units; report.
Staleness check (six months after a live-search unit)
You are re-checking one unit written with live search six months ago: {unit code, vault path, date written}. Search again, today, for every fact in it that can change: platform rules and thresholds, prices and plan limits, model lineups, benchmark figures, regulatory status, the numbers attached to named creators. For each: the value in the unit, the value now, the source and date for the new value, and whether the unit's conclusion still holds. End with a verdict: CURRENT / PATCH (list the lines to change) / REWRITE (say which sections). Do not rewrite; report.

You are auditing four units of a self-study course written by Opus 5.5 for one reader. Units: {list the four unit codes and their vault paths}.

Method, in this order:
1. For each unit, list its sections. From every section draw one claim that carries a number, a named source, a mechanism or a recommendation. That is the sample; record it before checking anything.
2. Check each sampled claim against the source the unit cites: open the source; confirm the figure, the date, the sample size and that the claim is what the source says, not a stretch of it. Mark each claim PASS, FAIL (wrong, unsupported, miscited, or invented) or STALE (true when written, superseded now, with the newer figure).
3. For each unit compute the failure rate. If more than one claim in ten is FAIL, audit that unit in full: every cited claim, same procedure.
4. Check the deliverable: it exists in full inside the unit, it follows from the unit's evidence, and it is usable without reading the unit.
5. Check against the course goal and sourcing standard (in the unit's preamble): does the unit move the reader toward the stated outcomes, and is anything in it advice without evidence?

Output: a table per unit (section, claim, source, verdict, note); the failure rate; a verdict per unit (PASS / FULL AUDIT RUN, result / REWRITE with the sections named); the three most consequential errors across the four units in plain language; and one line each for the bibliography (new sources to add, sources to mark failed) and the changelog. Do not fix the units; report.
Staleness check (six months after a live-search unit)
You are re-checking one unit written with live search six months ago: {unit code, vault path, date written}. Search again, today, for every fact in it that can change: platform rules and thresholds, prices and plan limits, model lineups, benchmark figures, regulatory status, the numbers attached to named creators. For each: the value in the unit, the value now, the source and date for the new value, and whether the unit's conclusion still holds.
