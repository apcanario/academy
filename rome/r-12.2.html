01The Odyssean half

Donatus gives the plan: a poem to match both of Homer's, with Rome's origin and Augustus' as its theme (Life 21; module 12.1). Books 1–6 answer the Odyssey: a hero at sea, a storm, a landfall at a foreign court, a banquet at which he tells his own wanderings, and a visit to the dead. Books 7–12 answer the Iliad and belong to deep dive module 12.3. Module 6.3 set out the poem in outline and left it to these two modules to take it book by book; module 12.1 has the composition, the half-lines, the reading of books 2, 4 and 6 to Augustus and Octavia, the Helen episode, the models and the metre, and is not repeated here.

The Odyssean frame is turned inside out from the start. Odysseus sails towards a home that exists and a wife who waits; Aeneas sails away from a home that has burned, and his wife is lost on the night of the sack. Odysseus loses every ship and every companion; Aeneas must bring a people with him. Virgil keeps Homer's shape, beginning in medias res in the seventh year, with the earlier story told by the hero at a banquet (books 2–3, as Odyssey 9–12), but the shape now carries a founding, not a return. The voyage also runs in Odysseus' wake: Aeneas meets a man Odysseus left behind, passes the Cyclopes and steers round Scylla, months after the Greek.

The Homeric halves are not the only pattern readers find. The odd books (1, 3, 5) move by sea and are quieter, the even books (2, 4, 6) stay on land and are darker; others divide the poem into thirds, 1–4 around Carthage, 5–8 around the arrival in Italy, 9–12 around the war. George Duckworth (Structural Patterns and Proportions in Vergil's Aeneid, 1962) pressed the design further into mathematical ratios of line counts, a claim most readers have found too exact. The sea–land alternation, at least, is visible on any reading of books 1–6.

02Book 1: storm and Carthage

The proem and Juno's anger

The poem opens Arma virumque cano (1.1): arms and the man, the Iliad and the Odyssey in two nouns, war first, the wanderer second. The man is a refugee, fato profugus (1.2), driven by the gods and by Juno's unforgetting anger until he could found a city and bring his gods into Latium. The invocation asks the Muse for the causes, Musa, mihi causas memora (1.8), and closes on a question Homer never asks: tantaene animis caelestibus irae?, can heavenly minds hold such anger? (1.11). The answer is given at once. Juno loves Carthage and has heard that a race from Trojan blood will one day overturn it (1.12–22); the judgement of Paris, the slight to her beauty, and Ganymede's rape add older grudges (1.23–28). The section ends on the poem's motto: tantae molis erat Romanam condere gentem, so great a labour it was to found the Roman race (1.33).

The action follows at once. Juno, in soliloquy, asks whether she must give up, beaten, when Pallas could sink a fleet for one man's crime (1.37–49), and goes to Aeolus, king of the winds, offering him the nymph Deiopea in marriage (1.65–75). He strikes the mountain open and the winds pour out (1.81–123). Why Juno hates, and what her anger means for the poem's theology, is deep dive module 12.6's; here it is the engine of the plot.

The storm and Neptune's statesman

The storm is Poseidon's storm on Odysseus' raft (Odyssey 5.291–332), and Aeneas' first appearance is a translation. His limbs go slack with cold (1.92), as Odysseus' knees are loosed (5.297), and he cries that those who died beneath Troy's walls were three and four times blessed (1.94–101), Odysseus' own words (5.306–312). Virgil's hero enters the poem wishing he had died at Troy. Then Virgil reverses Homer: in the Odyssey Poseidon raises the storm; here Neptune calms it, rebuking the winds with a broken threat, quos ego, "whom I…" (1.135), and sweeping the sea smooth (1.124–156).

The first simile of the poem marks the reversal. As when a riot breaks out in a great people and the mob rages, torches and stones flying, if they see a man heavy with piety and service, pietate gravem ac meritis, they fall silent and stand with ears pricked while he rules their minds with words (1.148–153). Homer's similes compare men to nature: the assembly in Iliad 2.144–149 surges like the sea. Virgil compares nature to a Roman political scene, and the man is defined by pietas, the word that marks Aeneas himself at 1.10.

Where sources disagree. Who is the statesman? R. G. Austin's commentary on book 1 (1971) recalled Cato the Younger, whom Plutarch shows quietening a hostile crowd. Stephen Harrison ("Vergil on Kingship: the first simile of the Aeneid", 1988) read the figure as a type, a Roman model for Aeneas, whose calming speech to his men follows a few lines later. Viktor Pöschl (Die Dichtkunst Virgils, 1950) had already made the storm and its simile the key to the poem: elemental violence subdued by order.

Seven ships reach a harbour on the Libyan coast (1.157–169), Homer's harbour of Phorcys on Ithaca with its cave of nymphs (Odyssey 13.96–112). Aeneas shoots seven stags, one for each ship, as Odysseus kills a stag on Circe's island (10.156–184), and speaks to his men: they have known worse, and forsan et haec olim meminisse iuvabit, perhaps one day it will be a pleasure to remember even this (1.198–207). Odysseus said the same before Scylla (Odyssey 12.208–212). Virgil adds what Homer never shows, the cost to the speaker: he feigns hope in his face and presses his grief deep in his heart (1.209).

On Olympus Venus complains to Jupiter, who unrolls Rome's future (1.254–296); module 6.3 reads the prophecy and sets out the dispute over which Caesar is meant. Mercury is sent to make Carthage welcome the Trojans (1.297–304).

Carthage, Dido's past and the pictures of Troy

Venus meets her son disguised as a Spartan or Thracian huntress, as Athena meets Odysseus on Scheria disguised as a girl (Odyssey 7.19–36). Aeneas guesses a goddess, o dea certe (1.328), but not his mother. She tells him whose land this is: Dido, her husband Sychaeus murdered by her brother Pygmalion for gold, the ghost who revealed the crime and the treasure, the flight from Tyre and the purchase of as much land as a bull's hide would surround (1.340–368). Module 4.1 traces this story to Timaeus and to Trogus in Justin's epitome and shows what Virgil changed in it. Venus reveals herself only as she leaves, and her son's complaint at the disguise comes too late (1.402–409).

Wrapped in a cloud by his mother, as Athena wraps Odysseus in mist on his way to Alcinous' palace (Odyssey 7.14–17), Aeneas watches Carthage rising, the citizens busy as bees (1.430–436), and says what every exile in the poem feels: o fortunati, quorum iam moenia surgunt, lucky ones, whose walls already rise (1.437). In the grove at the city's centre Dido is building a temple to Juno, and its doors carry the Trojan war in pictures (1.446–493): the Atreidae, Priam, Achilles, the Greeks in flight and the Trojans, Rhesus' white horses taken, Troilus dragged by his chariot, the Trojan women bringing a robe to a hostile Pallas, Achilles selling Hector's body for gold, and Penthesilea last. Aeneas weeps: sunt lacrimae rerum et mentem mortalia tangunt (1.462), whose ambiguity module 12.1 sets out. He takes the pictures as a sign that suffering is known and pitied here, and that this fame will bring the Trojans some safety (1.461–463).

The model is Demodocus' song of the wooden horse at the Phaeacian court, at which Odysseus weeps like a woman over her husband's body (Odyssey 8.499–531). Virgil changes the medium and the reader. A song is sung for a guest; these pictures were made for Juno, the goddess who hates Troy, on her own temple, and Aeneas, reading them as sympathy, misses whose victory they record. The reader knows from the proem what he does not.

Dido, Diana and the banquet

Dido enters among her young men like Diana leading her dancers on the Eurotas or on Cynthus (1.498–504). The simile is Homer's for Nausicaa among her maids (Odyssey 6.102–109). Gellius reports that Valerius Probus judged it Virgil's least successful borrowing: a girl at play by a river suits the hunting goddess among nymphs, a queen walking among her chiefs in the middle of a city does not (Gellius 9.9). Probus counted it a failure of fit; read forward, the misfit is the point. Virgil has given Nausicaa's simile to a woman with Arete's power, the queen of the Phaeacians whom Odysseus must win, and the huntress image returns in book 4, when Dido, already compared to a deer wounded by a shepherd's arrow (4.68–73), goes hunting with Aeneas.

The lost companions appear with Ilioneus, whose speech asks only for shelter (1.520–558); the cloud parts and Aeneas stands revealed (1.586–593). Dido welcomes them as one who has suffered: non ignara mali miseris succurrere disco, not unacquainted with misfortune, I learn to help the wretched (1.630). Venus, fearing Juno's Carthage, replaces Ascanius with Cupid, who sits in Dido's lap at the feast and wears away her memory of Sychaeus (1.657–722). At the banquet the court singer Iopas sings of the moon and sun (1.740–746; module 12.1), as Demodocus sings at Alcinous' table, and Dido asks for the whole story from the beginning: it is now, she says, the seventh summer of his wandering (1.753–756). Beroe in book 5 also calls it the seventh summer (5.626), though most of a year has passed between: a seam of the kind module 12.1 describes in the unrevised poem.

03Book 2: the fall of Troy

Aeneas begins: Infandum, regina, iubes renovare dolorem, a grief beyond speaking, queen, you bid me renew (2.3), a story he saw and of which he was a great part, quorum pars magna fui (2.6). Odysseus at the Phaeacian court has the first-person narration (Odyssey 9–12) but not this subject; Demodocus sings the sack in some twenty lines (8.499–520), and Helen and Menelaus recall the horse in Sparta (4.271–289). For the rest Virgil turned to the poems of the Epic Cycle that told the end of the war, the Little Iliad and the Sack of Ilion, lost except for Proclus' summaries and fragments (Greek module 4.3). Macrobius later claimed that Virgil translated book 2 almost word for word from an epic by one Pisander (Saturnalia 5.2.4–5); the poet he names is usually taken for a writer of the third century AD who followed Virgil, not the reverse.

The Greeks feign departure and leave the horse (2.13–39). Laocoön runs down from the citadel, warns timeo Danaos et dona ferentes, I fear the Greeks even bearing gifts (2.49), and drives a spear into its flank (2.40–56). Shepherds bring in Sinon, a Greek with bound hands, whose speech (2.57–198) is a long, circumstantial lie: he was marked for sacrifice by Odysseus' plotting, escaped, and the horse is an offering to Athena, built huge so that it cannot pass the gates, for if it enters Troy, Asia will carry war to Greece. Priam has him untied. Then two serpents cross from Tenedos and crush Laocoön and both his sons (2.199–227), and the Trojans, taking it for punishment of his spear, breach their own wall (2.234–249). Cassandra speaks and is not believed (2.246–247). Greek module 4.3 sets out how Virgil reverses Arctinus here, where the serpents strike after the horse is in and kill one son, and the earlier versions of Laocoön's offence.

In sleep Hector appears, filthy and wounded as Achilles dragged him, quantum mutatus ab illo, how changed from that Hector (2.274), and gives Aeneas Troy's sacred things and household gods: sacra suosque tibi commendat Troia penatis (2.293). The mission of the whole poem is handed over in a dream on the night of the fall. Aeneas wakes and runs to arms anyway. The priest Panthus, carrying the gods and his grandson, gives the epitaph, fuimus Troes, fuit Ilium, we were Trojans, Ilium was (2.325), and Aeneas rallies a band with a paradox, the one safety of the beaten is to hope for none (2.354). They put on Greek armour and fight in disguise; Coroebus dies trying to rescue Cassandra, and the righteous Rhipeus falls too, dis aliter visum, the gods thought otherwise (2.428).

At the palace Pyrrhus, Achilles' son, glitters at the threshold like a snake sloughing its skin after winter (2.469–475), Hector's snake waiting for Achilles in Iliad 22.93–95 turned against his family. He kills Polites before his father's eyes; the old king throws a feeble spear, and Pyrrhus drags him slipping in his son's blood to the altar and kills him (2.506–558). The episode ends not at the altar but on a shore: iacet ingens litore truncus, / avulsumque umeris caput et sine nomine corpus, he lies a huge trunk on the shore, the head torn from the shoulders, a body without a name (2.557–558). Greek module 4.3 gives the two Cyclic deaths, at the altar of Zeus Herkeios in the Sack of Ilion and at the door of the palace in the Little Iliad.

Where sources disagree. Why a shore, when Priam dies in his courtyard? Servius, on 2.557, says that Virgil touches on Pompey's story, killed on an Egyptian beach and beheaded in 48, by writing ingens, "huge", for Magnus; he adds that "on the shore" also looks to a tragedy of Pacuvius. A. M. Bowie ("The Death of Priam: Allegory and History in the Aeneid", Classical Quarterly, 1990) accepted the Pompeian resonance but denied that the scene is allegory: history is taken up into myth, and the headless king evokes Pompey without standing for him.

Aeneas sees Helen hiding at Vesta's shrine and thinks of killing her (2.567–588), lines whose authenticity module 12.1 weighs. Venus stops him and lifts the mist from his eyes: Neptune is levering up the walls, Juno holds the Scaean gate, Pallas sits on the citadel and Jupiter himself rouses the gods against Troy (2.589–620). The city falls like an ancient ash hacked by farmers on a mountain (2.626–631), an Iliadic simile for a warrior's death (Iliad 16.482–484) given to a city.

Anchises refuses to leave until a harmless flame plays on Iulus' head and a star falls towards Ida (2.679–698). The image that Rome put on coins and in the Forum of Augustus follows: Aeneas takes his father on his shoulders, Anchises holds the Penates, Iulus holds his hand, and Creusa walks behind (2.707–720). In the dark she is lost. Aeneas runs back through the burning city calling her name, and her ghost meets him, larger than life: it is the gods' will; a long exile, a land of Hesperia where the Lydian Tiber flows, and a royal bride await him; the great mother of the gods keeps her on these shores (2.771–789). Three times he tries to put his arms around her neck, three times the image escapes his hands (2.792–794), the lines in which Odysseus tries to embrace his mother's shade (Odyssey 11.204–208). Pausanias says that Lesches and the Cypria named Aeneas' wife Eurydice, and that in the tradition behind Polygnotus' painting at Delphi the Mother of the gods and Aphrodite saved Creusa from slavery (Pausanias 10.26.1). Virgil kept the rescue and made it a parting. The book ends at dawn, with Aeneas lifting his father and taking the road to the mountains (2.796–804). In Arctinus Aeneas leaves for Ida before the sack begins; Virgil's Aeneas leaves only when it is over, which answers the hostile story that he betrayed the city (Greek module 4.3; module 1.3).

04Book 3: the wanderings

Book 3 is Aeneas' Apologoi, the voyage told at the banquet, and the least dramatic book of the poem: a sequence of landings in which the Trojans keep founding the wrong city. Its subject is misreading. Every oracle is half understood, and the destination becomes clear only by stages.

The fleet is built under Ida at Antandros (3.1–12). In Thrace, where Odysseus raided the Cicones (Odyssey 9.39–61), Aeneas begins a city and tears up myrtle shoots that bleed: Polydorus, Priam's son, sent with gold to the Thracian king and murdered for it, speaks from the mound (3.19–68). The comment is the poet's best-known moral: quid non mortalia pectora cogis, / auri sacra fames!, to what do you not drive mortal hearts, accursed hunger for gold (3.56–57). The source is Euripides' Hecuba, not Homer. At Delos Apollo orders them to seek their ancient mother, antiquam exquirite matrem (3.96); Anchises reads it as Crete, home of Teucer, and they found Pergamea there until plague and drought drive them out and the Penates explain in a dream that the mother is Hesperia, Italy, whence Dardanus came (3.121–191). On the Strophades they kill cattle that are not theirs and the Harpies foul their feast (3.209–269): Odysseus' men and the cattle of Helios (Odyssey 12.260–402) crossed with Apollonius' Harpies, driven to those same Turning Islands from the blind Phineus (Argonautica 2). The Harpy Celaeno's prophecy that hunger will make them eat their tables (3.255–257) is credited to Anchises when it comes true in book 7, a seam that module 12.1 describes.

The fleet then sails into Augustus' own geography. At Actium the Trojans hold games on the shore, Actiaque Iliacis celebramus litora ludis (3.280), and Aeneas fixes to the temple doors a shield taken from a Greek, inscribed Aeneas haec de Danais victoribus arma, Aeneas dedicates these arms from the conquering Greeks (3.286–288). Octavian founded Nicopolis near Actium after his victory and instituted games there (Suetonius, Augustus 18.2); Virgil gives the games a Trojan founder more than a thousand years earlier.

At Buthrotum the Trojans find Helenus, Priam's son, ruling Greek cities and married to Andromache, who has passed from Pyrrhus to him after Orestes killed Pyrrhus at his ancestral altars (3.325–332). Andromache is pouring offerings at an empty tomb of Hector; the town is a little Troy, with a false Simois, a dry brook named Xanthus and a Scaean gate (3.302, 3.349–351). This is the poem's picture of what Aeneas must not do: rebuild the old city in miniature and live in memory. Helenus, a seer, gives the route (3.374–462): look for a white sow with thirty piglets where the city will stand (3.389–393), placate Juno above all (3.435–440), avoid Scylla and Charybdis by sailing round Sicily, and consult the Sibyl at Cumae. He is Homer's Tiresias (Odyssey 11.100–137) and Circe with her sailing directions (12.37–141) in one. Andromache gives Ascanius a cloak and sees her dead son in him, o mihi sola mei super Astyanactis imago, you, the only image left me of my Astyanax (3.489).

Italy is sighted, Italiam, shouts Achates (3.523), but the Greek coast of the south must be passed. Off Etna they take on Achaemenides, a Greek of Odysseus' crew left behind in the Cyclops' cave three months before (3.588–654), and see Polyphemus himself, blind, wading to bathe his eye (3.655–681). Virgil enters the Odyssey's own story at the moment after Homer leaves it, and makes the Trojans rescue a Greek. Ovid took up Achaemenides again in his little Aeneid (Metamorphoses 14; module 10.1). At Drepanum Anchises dies (3.707–715), foretold by neither Helenus nor Celaeno: hic me, pater optime, fessum / deseris, here, best of fathers, you leave me in my weariness (3.710–711). The storm of book 1 followed, and Aeneas falls silent.

Where sources disagree. Virgil's route is one of several. Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Virgil's contemporary at Rome, has Aeneas found Aeneia in Pallene (1.49.4), hold games at Zacynthus with a foot-race called the course of Aeneas and Aphrodite (1.50.3), build temples at Leucas and Actium (1.50.4) and march to Dodona while Anchises takes the fleet to Buthrotum (1.51.1); and some, he reports, held that Anchises lived to reach Latium and died the year before the war there (1.64.5). Virgil moves the games to Actium, sends no Aeneas to Dodona and kills Anchises in Sicily, so that the father's tomb can hold the games of book 5.

05Book 4 in outline

Dido confesses her love to her sister Anna, who urges her to set aside her vow to Sychaeus (4.1–55). Juno and Venus, for opposite reasons, agree a marriage (4.90–128). In the hunt a storm drives Dido and Aeneas into the same cave, speluncam Dido dux et Troianus eandem (4.165); she calls it marriage, coniugium vocat, hoc praetexit nomine culpam, and with that name covers her fault (4.172). Rumour spreads it (4.173–197); Iarbas, the rejected suitor, complains to Jupiter, who sends Mercury to find Aeneas laying out Carthage in a Tyrian cloak (4.196–278). Aeneas prepares to leave, Dido confronts him, and he answers Italiam non sponte sequor, I seek Italy not of my own will (4.361). She curses him and calls up an avenger from her bones, exoriare aliquis nostris ex ossibus ultor (4.625), and kills herself on the pyre, released by Iris cutting a lock of her hair (4.642–705).

The Homeric model is Calypso. In Odyssey 5.28–147 Hermes is sent to the goddess who detains Odysseus, and she protests; Virgil sends Mercury to the man instead, because Aeneas, unlike Odysseus, has stopped wanting to leave. Dido is also Nausicaa, the princess a stranger might marry (Alcinous offers her, 7.311–315), and Apollonius' Medea, from whom Servius says the whole book was taken (module 12.1). What Virgil adds is a heroine who has her own city and her own story, and whose curse founds the Punic wars; Silius Italicus made Hannibal's war her revenge (module 10.2) and Ovid gave her a letter in the Heroides (module 10.1). The book scene by scene, the questions of marriage and fault, and the reception are deep dive module 12.4's.

06Book 5: the games and the burning of the ships

From the open sea Aeneas sees the glow of Dido's pyre without knowing what it is (5.1–7). A storm turns the fleet back to Sicily and to Acestes, a Trojan woman's son (5.8–41). It is a year since Anchises died, and at his tomb a great snake glides from the shrine, tastes the offerings and vanishes, a sign Aeneas cannot read (5.42–103). He proclaims games for the ninth day.

The model is the funeral games for Patroclus in Iliad 23 (Greek module 4.2), an Iliadic book set inside the Odyssean half. Virgil replaces Homer's chariot race (Iliad 23.262–652) with a boat race, four ships rounding a rock (5.114–285), and roots it in Rome: Mnestheus is ancestor of the Memmii, Sergestus of the Sergii, Cloanthus of the Cluentii (5.116–123). Gyas, leading, throws his cautious helmsman Menoetes overboard, and the old man surfaces, climbs a rock and spits out salt water while the Trojans laugh (5.159–182); Sergestus runs aground; Cloanthus wins by praying to the sea gods (5.232–243). In the foot race Nisus slips in the blood of the sacrifice and, falling, trips Salius so that his friend Euryalus wins (5.327–338), as Ajax son of Oileus slips in dung in Homer's race (Iliad 23.773–777); the pair die together in book 9 (deep dive module 12.3). The boxing, in Homer Epeius' knockout of Euryalus (Iliad 23.653–699), pits the young Trojan Dares against old Entellus, who is knocked down, rises in fury and beats Dares until Aeneas stops it; Entellus then kills the prize bull with one blow, a better life for Eryx in place of Dares' death (5.362–484). In the archery a dove is tied to a mast: one arrow hits the mast, the next cuts the cord, the third brings down the bird, and Acestes, shooting last into the empty sky, sees his arrow catch fire and vanish like a shooting star (5.485–534), an omen the poem never explains. Homer's archery at a tethered dove (Iliad 23.850–883) has the cord cut and the bird killed; the burning arrow is Virgil's. The boys' cavalry display that ends the games, the Troy game, is set out in Greek module 10.12.

While the men watch, the women, weary of seven years at sea, mourn Anchises on the shore. Juno sends Iris disguised as the old woman Beroe, who urges them to burn the ships and stay (5.604–663); they set the fleet alight, and Jupiter's rain saves all but four ships (5.680–699). On Nautes' advice and at the bidding of Anchises' ghost, who summons Aeneas to meet him in the underworld (5.719–740), the old, the weary and the women who wish it stay behind in a new city, Acesta (5.746–761), the Segesta that claimed Trojan kinship in the First Punic War (module 1.3). The model is again Odyssean in shape, a homecoming that sheds companions, but where Odysseus loses every ship, Aeneas loses four and leaves a city.

Where sources disagree. The burning of ships by Trojan women is older than Virgil and belonged to Italy. Dionysius reports that the author of the Priestesses of Hera at Argos, whom module 1.3 identifies as Hellanicus, made a Trojan woman, Rhome, lead the others in firing the ships, and gave her name to Rome (1.72.2; module 1.3); he reports Aristotle as saying that captive Trojan women burned the ships of Achaeans storm-driven to Latinium (1.72.3–4). Virgil moves the act to Sicily, makes it Juno's work through Iris, and turns a story of Rome's naming into the founding of Acesta.

Venus asks Neptune for a safe crossing and he grants it at one price: unum pro multis dabitur caput, one life shall be given for many (5.815). Sleep, disguised as Phorbas, tips the helmsman Palinurus overboard on a calm night, and Aeneas, taking the helm, grieves that he trusted too much to a quiet sky and sea and will lie naked on an unknown shore (5.827–871). Palinurus is Elpenor, the companion who falls from Circe's roof and asks Odysseus for burial among the dead (Odyssey 10.552–560, 11.51–83); his own account in book 6, where a storm sweeps him away, does not match this one, and module 12.1 treats the clash.

07Book 6 in outline

The fleet lands at Cumae. Aeneas visits Apollo's temple, whose doors Daedalus carved, and the Sibyl, possessed by the god, prophesies wars in Latium and a second Achilles (6.1–101). The descent is easy, facilis descensus Averno; the return is the labour, hoc opus, hic labor est (6.126–129). He must find the golden bough and bury Misenus, the trumpeter drowned by Triton (6.136–235). Through the monsters at the entrance and across the river by Charon's boat (6.268–416) he meets Palinurus unburied, Dido in the fields of mourning, Deiphobus mutilated, hears of the sinners of Tartarus and reaches Elysium, where Anchises explains the world-soul and rebirth and shows the souls of the Romans to come (6.679–892). He leaves by the gate of ivory, through which false dreams pass (6.893–898).

The model is Odyssey 11, changed at the root: Odysseus calls the dead up to a trench of blood, Aeneas goes down bodily, and where Tiresias tells one man how to get home, Anchises shows a people its history. Book 6 closely, its Platonic and Pythagorean sources and the gates of horn and ivory are deep dive module 12.5's; Dido's meeting is 12.4's; module 6.3 reads Augustus' place in the parade and Marcellus. Lucan inverted the book in Erictho's necromancy (module 10.2).

09Self-test

  1. In what ways does the Odyssean half invert the Odyssey it imitates?
    Answer

    Odysseus sails home to a waiting wife and loses all his companions; Aeneas sails away from a burned home, loses his wife on the night of the sack and must bring a people with him. The Homeric shape (storm, foreign court, narrated wanderings, the dead) now carries a founding rather than a return.

  2. How does Aeneas' first appearance in the poem translate Homer, and what does it reveal about him?
    Answer

    His limbs go slack in the storm (1.92) and he calls those who died at Troy three and four times blessed (1.94–101), as Odysseus does on his raft (Odyssey 5.297, 5.306–312). Virgil's hero enters the poem wishing he had died with his city.

  3. What is reversed in Neptune's calming of the storm, and why does the simile at 1.148–153 matter?
    Answer

    In the Odyssey Poseidon raises the storm; Virgil's Neptune ends it. The simile compares the sea to a riot quelled by a man of pietas, nature likened to politics instead of the reverse, and Pöschl and Harrison read it as programmatic for the poem; Austin recalled Cato as the statesman.

  4. How do the pictures in Juno's temple differ in effect from Demodocus' song in Odyssey 8?
    Answer

    Both make the hero weep at his own war, but Demodocus sings for a guest, while the pictures adorn the temple of Troy's enemy. Aeneas reads them as sympathy and a promise of safety (1.461–463); the reader, told of Juno's hatred in the proem, sees whose victory they record.

  5. What was Probus' objection to the simile comparing Dido to Diana, and how can it be answered from the poem?
    Answer

    Gellius reports that Probus thought the Nausicaa simile (Odyssey 6.102–109) unsuited to a queen walking among her chiefs in a city (Gellius 9.9). The misfit can be read as deliberate: the hunting image returns in book 4, where Dido is a wounded deer (4.68–73) before she rides to the hunt.

  6. What does Hector's ghost give Aeneas, and why does it matter that he receives it in a dream on the night of the fall?
    Answer

    Troy's sacred things and Penates (2.293), with the charge to found a city for them overseas. The poem's mission is handed over before Aeneas has fought at all, and he runs to arms regardless; the fight is futile, the mission is what survives.

  7. What did Servius see in the headless body of Priam on the shore (2.557–558), and how did Bowie qualify it?
    Answer

    Servius says Virgil touches on the death of Pompey (Magnus, beheaded on an Egyptian beach), writing ingens for Magnus. Bowie (1990) accepted the resonance but denied that the scene is allegory: history is absorbed into myth.

  8. Why is Buthrotum a warning to Aeneas, and what does Helenus contribute to the voyage?
    Answer

    Andromache and Helenus live in a little Troy with a false Simois, a dry Xanthus and a Scaean gate, honouring an empty tomb (3.300–351): the old city rebuilt in memory, which Aeneas must not do. Helenus, Homer's Tiresias and Circe combined, gives the route and the signs (3.374–462).

  9. How does Virgil rework Iliad 23 in the games of book 5?
    Answer

    The chariot race becomes a boat race whose captains found Roman families (5.116–123); Nisus slips in blood as Ajax slips in dung (Iliad 23.773–777); the archery at a dove keeps Homer's cut cord and killed bird but adds Acestes' burning arrow (5.485–534).

  10. Where else is the burning of ships by Trojan women told, and what does Virgil change?
    Answer

    Dionysius reports the Rhome of the Priestesses of Hera at Argos, who led the burning and gave Rome her name (1.72.2), and Aristotle's captive Trojan women who burned Achaean ships at Latinium (1.72.3–4). Virgil moves it to Sicily, makes Juno the cause through Iris disguised as Beroe, and turns it into the founding of Acesta.

10Carry forward

  1. Virgil keeps Homer's shapes and changes what they carry: the storm is calmed instead of raised, the hero's narration tells a defeat, and the wanderer seeks a new home instead of an old one. Read every Homeric echo in the poem by asking what has been reversed.
  2. The Odyssean half is a sequence of half-understood signs (the pictures, the oracle of the ancient mother, Celaeno, the snake at Anchises' tomb, Acestes' arrow), and its hero, like the reader of history, learns his destination only by stages.
  3. Rome is written into the myth by aetiology and allusion: Actium's games, the Roman families of the boat race, Segesta, the headless Priam. The same technique works on the largest scale in the prophecies of books 1, 6 and 8, and the debate over what it means is deep dive module 12.7's.
