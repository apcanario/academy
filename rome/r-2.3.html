Priests without a priestly caste

Rome had no clergy. Its senior priests were senators holding a priesthood for life beside their magistracies: Caesar was pontifex maximus while consul, Cicero an augur from 53 BC. Priests advised; the Senate decided and magistrates acted (module 2.1). Tradition, written down five to seven centuries later, gave nearly every priesthood to Numa (Livy 1.20; module 1.5), but the colleges grew with the governing class.

These were the "four most ample colleges" of the Res Gestae (9.1). They co-opted their members until 104 BC, when the tribune Gnaeus Domitius Ahenobarbus gave the choice to seventeen of the thirty-five tribes drawn by lot, a minority so that the people did not formally confer a sacred office (Cicero, De lege agraria 2.18); the college then co-opted the winner. Sulla abolished the law; Labienus restored it in 63 BC (Dio 37.37.1), and Caesar was at once elected pontifex maximus. Tenure was for life: even a convicted augur kept his office (Plutarch, Roman Questions 99).

The Sibylline Books, bought by a Tarquin in the story of module 1.6, lay under the Capitoline temple and were opened only on the Senate's decree; the cults they prescribed (the Magna Mater of module 2.5) were performed by Greek rite. Burned with the temple in 83 BC, they were replaced from Erythrae and elsewhere (Tacitus, Annals 6.12), and Augustus shut the purged collection under the base of Palatine Apollo (Suetonius, Augustus 31.1).

The king's heirs: rex sacrorum, pontifex maximus, flamines

The pontifical college also held the rex sacrorum, fifteen flamines and the Vestals, all under the pontifex maximus, who lived in the domus publica beside the Regia. Festus' order of precedence (s.v. ordo sacerdotum) seats the rex first, the flamines of Jupiter, Mars and Quirinus next, the pontifex maximus fifth. Power ran the other way. Module 1.5 introduced the rex as the king's substitute (Livy 2.2.1–2); his duties show what kingship had been. On the Kalends a junior pontiff watched for the new moon and called out (calare, hence Kalendae) the days to the Nones; on the Nones the rex proclaimed the month's festivals (Varro, On the Latin Language 6.27–28). The king had kept the city's time. Barred from office and from addressing the people, the rex sacrificed in the Comitium and then fled the Forum (Plutarch, Roman Questions 63). Athens kept a sacral king too, the archon basileus of Greek module 8.1, but as an annual magistrate. Nobody ambitious wanted the Roman post: in 180 BC Lucius Cornelius Dolabella preferred a fine to resigning a naval command for it (Livy 40.42).

Each flamen served one god: three major and patrician (Dialis, Martialis, Quirinalis: Dumézil's triad, module 2.1), twelve minor. Livy has Numa create the Flamen Dialis so that the king's rites would not lapse while kings were at war (1.20.1–2). Gellius 10.15, citing Fabius Pictor among others, lists his rules. He might not ride a horse, see an army in battle array or swear an oath; he wore no knot and no ring unless pierced; a man in chains entering his house was freed and the fetters drawn up through the roof opening and let down into the street. He might not touch or name a she-goat, raw meat, ivy or beans, never went outdoors without his cap, and every day was a holy day for him. Plutarch calls him an animate and sacred image (Roman Questions 111). The cost was political: after Lucius Cornelius Merula killed himself in 87 BC the post stood empty; the young Caesar was designated and then removed by Sulla (Suetonius, Julius 1), and Augustus filled it only in 11 BC (Dio 54.36.1).

The Vestal Virgins

Six Vestals kept the fire in Vesta's round temple beside the Regia, where there was no image: Vesta is "nothing but living flame" (Ovid, Fasti 6.291). Gellius (1.12), citing the jurists Labeo and Capito, gives the rules of selection: a girl of six to ten, both parents living, without defect of body, speech or hearing. The pontifex maximus took her from her father with the words te, Amata, capio (1.12.14); she left her father's power without emancipation and could make a will. She served thirty years, ten learning, ten performing, ten teaching (Dionysius 2.67.2).

The Vestals renewed the fire each 1 March, made the salted meal sprinkled on public victims, and guarded a store that included the Palladium (Livy 26.27.14). Their privileges were a man's: a lictor, testimony without oath, custody of wills (Suetonius, Julius 83.1), the power to save a condemned man met by chance (Plutarch, Numa 10; Gellius 10.15.31 for the oath). If the fire went out, the pontifex maximus flogged the Vestal responsible (Livy 28.11.6). An unchaste Vestal was carried in a closed litter through a silent Forum to the Colline Gate and sealed in an underground chamber with a lamp and a little food (Plutarch, Numa 10; Dionysius 2.67); her lover was flogged to death. Ovid explains that she is given to the Earth she violated, Earth and Vesta being one (Fasti 6.457–460). Livy records Opimia and Floronia after Cannae (22.57.2–3); under Domitian the chief Vestal Cornelia went down protesting her innocence (Pliny, Letters 4.11).

Where sources disagree. Mary Beard (Journal of Roman Studies 1980) read the Vestal as ambiguous by design, at once virgin, matron and almost male; she later retreated from that scheme (1995). Holt Parker (American Journal of Philology 2004) argued that her intact body stood for the city's, so that trials cluster in crises such as 216 and 114 BC. Pliny would not say whether Cornelia was guilty, "I do not know whether she was innocent, but she was certainly led out as if innocent" (4.11.9), and made her burial Domitian's theatre; Suetonius (Domitian 8.3–4) presents a strict emperor restoring discipline.

The brotherhoods: Salii, Arvals, Luperci, fetials

The Salii were two companies of twelve patricians: the Palatine Salii of Mars, ascribed to Numa (Livy 1.20.4), and the Colline Salii of Quirinus, vowed by Tullus Hostilius (1.27.7). In March and again in October they danced through the city in bronze breastplates, beating figure-of-eight shields (ancilia); one had fallen from heaven, and Mamurius made eleven copies so that no thief could find it (Ovid, Fasti 3.259–392). Their hymn was barely understood by its own priests (Quintilian 1.6.40), yet in 29 BC the Senate had Octavian's name inserted into it (Res Gestae 10.1; Dio 51.20.1). The two outings frame the season of war.

The Arval Brothers, twelve senators, worshipped Dea Dia in a grove on the Via Campana. Augustus refounded them, and their minutes were cut on marble there from 21 BC to AD 304: co-optations, the May festival, vows for the emperor's safety each 3 January, sacrifices for accessions and foiled conspiracies, expiations whenever iron entered the grove. The entry for AD 218 preserves the hymn they danced, read from booklets, beginning enos Lases iuvate, "help us, Lares" (CIL VI 2104). John Scheid (Romulus et ses frères, 1990) used the records to show an archaic rite turned into a register of loyalty to the dynasty. The Luperci, two companies joined by the Iuliani in Caesar's honour in 44 BC (Suetonius, Julius 76.1), were to Cicero "a wild brotherhood, plainly pastoral and rustic" (Pro Caelio 26).

The fetials handled dealings with other peoples. In Livy's procedure (1.32.5–14), set down some six centuries after Ancus' supposed reign, the envoy calls Jupiter and the boundaries to witness at the frontier and demands restitution. If nothing is returned within thirty-three days and the Senate votes for war, a fetial throws a spear tipped with iron, or of fire-hardened cornel stained with blood, into enemy land before at least three adult witnesses. Treaties were struck by killing a pig with a flint (1.24.7–8). For distant enemies a column before Bellona's temple stood in for the frontier (Ovid, Fasti 6.205–208); Octavian threw the spear there against Cleopatra in 32 BC (Dio 50.4.5). The rite's place in decisions for war is module 4.4.

Where sources disagree. Livy gives the fetial law to Ancus Marcius (1.32.5), Dionysius to Numa (2.72), Cicero to Tullus Hostilius (On the Republic 2.31). Thomas Wiedemann (Classical Quarterly 1986) argued that the spear at Bellona's column was Octavian's antiquarian revival, and John Rich (1976) that ordinary envoys made the demands in the middle Republic; Alan Watson (International Law in Archaic Rome, 1993) took Livy's formulae for archaic law.

Augury and the auspices

The auspices, literally "bird-watching", belonged to the magistrates: a consul took them before every assembly, levy or battle. The augurs held the law of signs, inaugurated priests and places, and ruled whether a fault (vitium) had occurred; an augur's alio die, "on another day", could dissolve an assembly (Cicero, On the Laws 2.31). Its political use is module 2.1. Signs were sought or sent unasked; Festus lists five kinds, from the sky, birds, feeding chickens, four-footed animals and ill-omened accidents. Left was lucky for a Roman augur, right for a Greek (Cicero, On Divination 2.82). A templum was not a building but a space marked out by spoken formula (Varro, On the Latin Language 7.6–9); the Senate could decree validly only in one (Gellius 14.7.7), and Vesta's shrine was not one (Servius on Aeneid 7.153).

In the field generals consulted caged chickens: if they ate so greedily that grain fell from their beaks, the sign was perfect, and Cicero objected that hungry birds always eat (On Divination 2.71–74). At Aquilonia in 293 BC the keeper reported a good sign the birds had not given; Papirius Cursor said a false report fell on the reporter and put him in the front line, where a spear killed him (Livy 10.40). The sign was what was announced. In 249 BC Publius Claudius Pulcher, told before Drepana (module 4.2) that the chickens would not eat, threw them overboard: "let them drink, since they will not eat" (Cicero, On the Nature of the Gods 2.7). He lost most of his fleet and was tried.

Where sources disagree. Polybius, writing about a century after Drepana, blames Claudius' rashness and says nothing of chickens (1.49–52). The story appears in Cicero, two centuries on, then in Livy (Periochae 19), Valerius Maximus 1.4.3 and Suetonius (Tiberius 2.2), where it marks the arrogance of the Claudii. Whether it was invented to explain the defeat, or omitted by a Greek who thought Roman religion a device for managing the crowd (Polybius 6.56), is uncertain.

Haruspices in Roman service

The haruspices were Etruscan experts, not a Roman priesthood; their discipline is module 1.2. The Senate summoned them to read prodigies, and Romans argued back. In 163 BC, when the man reporting the first century's vote dropped dead at the consular elections, they declared the presiding consul, Tiberius Sempronius Gracchus, improper; he called them Etruscan barbarians, then found in his augural books that he had indeed erred, and the consuls of 162 resigned (Cicero, On the Nature of the Gods 2.10–11). Roman augural law decided; the outsiders were merely right. In 56 BC a reply blaming polluted sacred places became Clodius' weapon against Cicero's house, answered in De haruspicum responso.

The calendar from Romulus to Caesar

How Roman years convert into ours is module 0.3; here the calendar is a religious instrument. Writers of six to ten centuries later gave Romulus ten months, March to December, 304 days (Censorinus, De die natali 20.2–3; Ovid, Fasti 1.27–44), which is why September to December are the seventh to tenth. Numa added January and February and set the months by the moon, with intercalation (Livy 1.19.6). The republican year had 355 days, in months of 29 or 31, odd numbers being lucky, and a February of 28. Days were counted back from the Kalends, Nones and Ides; an eight-day market cycle ran alongside, and each day was marked F if legal business was allowed, N if not, C if assemblies could meet.

Every second year the pontiffs inserted a month of 22 or 23 days after 23 February, and they granted or refused it for political favour (Censorinus 20.7; Suetonius, Julius 40.1): Curio, tribune in 50 BC, went over to Caesar when refused one (Caelius in Cicero, Letters to Friends 8.6.5). By 46 BC the calendar ran about three months ahead of the sun. Caesar, pontifex maximus, advised by the astronomer Sosigenes (Pliny, Natural History 18.211), gave 46 BC 445 days, then from 1 January 45 BC a year of 365 with a leap day every fourth year. The pontiffs miscounted, intercalating every third year, until Augustus suspended leap years (Macrobius, Saturnalia 1.14.13–15). The system ran until 1582.

One pre-Julian calendar survives: the Fasti Antiates Maiores, painted on plaster between 84 and 55 BC, found at Antium in 1915 and now in the Palazzo Massimo. Thirteen columns give the twelve months and the intercalary month, with market letters, day marks, the old festivals in large capitals and later temple anniversaries in small letters. The forty or so other surviving calendars, Verrius Flaccus' at Praeneste among them (module 0.1), are Julian.

Where sources disagree. Censorinus reports that Licinius Macer and Fenestella gave Rome twelve months from the start, against Junius Gracchanus, Fulvius Nobilior and Varro, whom he follows (20.2); Tuditanus and Cassius Hemina credited intercalation to the decemvirs, not Numa (Macrobius 1.13.21). Agnes Kirsopp Michels (The Calendar of the Roman Republic, 1967) dated the republican calendar to the decemvirs; Jörg Rüpke (The Roman Calendar from Numa to Constantine, 2011) ties the written calendar to Gnaeus Flavius' posting of the fasti in 304 BC (Livy 9.46.5). A 304-day year may be an antiquarian inference from the month names.

The priests tied the year together. The Vestals made the Parilia's smoke from the ash of unborn calves, the blood of the October Horse and bean-straw (Fasti 4.731–734): Mars' autumn victim purified the shepherds' spring. Festivals also changed. The Saturnalia was reorganised in 217 BC with the shout io Saturnalia (Livy 22.1.19–20), and the Lupercalia lasted until Pope Gelasius protested around AD 495. Athens' great festivals centred on contests in which citizens competed (Greek modules 8.1 and 8.3); Rome's games, most added during the Republic, were shows that citizens watched.

Ovid's Fasti, written about AD 2–8 and revised in exile (module 6.3), is the one sustained ancient guide, and it sets traps. It covers only January to June, so the Consualia, October Horse and Saturnalia come from Varro, Festus, Plutarch and Macrobius. It is late and Augustan, the new dates crowding the old. It multiplies causes: for the Lupercalia Ovid offers Arcadian Pan, Faunus tricked in the tale of Hercules and Omphale, Romulus and Remus chasing rustlers, and a Juno oracle on fertility, learned display on the model of Callimachus' Aitia. It historicises: the Regifugium becomes Tarquin's expulsion (2.685–852), though the rite was the rex sacrorum's flight. And its witness may be a persona when Ovid says he carried the purifying ash himself (4.725–728). Trust the actions, dates and materials over the reasons.

Where sources disagree. Timaeus explained the October Horse as revenge for the Trojan horse, and Polybius mocked him (12.4b). James Frazer, who edited the Fasti (1929), mined it for primitive agrarian magic, the horse as corn-spirit; Georges Dumézil compared it to the Vedic royal horse sacrifice. Mary Beard ("A complex of times", Proceedings of the Cambridge Philological Society 1987) replied that festivals had no single original meaning: the Parilia, a shepherds' rite turned Rome's birthday, was reinterpreted in every generation, and Ovid records one stage, not the origin.

Self-test

  1. Why is it misleading to speak of a Roman clergy, and how were members of the great colleges chosen by the late Republic?
    Answer

    Priests were senators holding a priesthood for life beside magistracies; they advised while the Senate decided and magistrates acted. Co-optation gave way in 104 BC to election by seventeen of thirty-five tribes chosen by lot, followed by formal co-optation; Sulla abolished this and Labienus restored it in 63 BC, when Caesar won the office of pontifex maximus.

  2. In what sense is the rex sacrorum the king's religious ghost?
    Answer

    He ranked first in Festus' order of precedence and kept the king's time-keeping rites, proclaiming each month's festivals on the Nones and fleeing the Comitium after his sacrifice (Plutarch, Roman Questions 63). But he was barred from office and from speaking to the people, and real authority lay with the pontifex maximus; Dolabella in 180 BC preferred a fine to the post (Livy 40.42).

  3. What do the Flamen Dialis' taboos have in common, and what did they cost the state?
    Answer

    They keep him free of every bond (knots, closed rings, chains), of war (horses, armies) and of oaths, and bind him to his god every day of the year (Gellius 10.15). He could not command or swear the magistrate's oath, so the post was hard to combine with a career and stood empty from 87 to 11 BC.

  4. How did a Vestal's legal position differ from that of other Roman women, and how has it been read?
    Answer

    She left her father's power without emancipation, could make a will, had a lictor and testified without oath (Gellius 1.12; Plutarch, Numa 10). Beard read this mix of virgin, matron and male privilege as a deliberately ambiguous status, a reading she later qualified.

  5. Why, on Parker's argument, do Vestal trials cluster in years of crisis?
    Answer

    Because the Vestal's intact body stood for the city's integrity, so a disaster such as Cannae in 216 BC prompted a search for her violation, with live burial as the repair. Pliny's doubts about Cornelia under Domitian show such trials could also be political theatre.

  6. Describe the fetial declaration of war and the modern doubts about it.
    Answer

    Demands at the frontier with Jupiter as witness, thirty-three days' grace, then a bloodied spear thrown into enemy land before three witnesses (Livy 1.32.5–14), later at Bellona's column, as Octavian did in 32 BC. Wiedemann thought that version an Augustan revival and Rich that envoys did the work in the middle Republic; Watson defended the formulae as archaic.

  7. Distinguish the auspices from augury, and explain what the Aquilonia episode shows.
    Answer

    Magistrates took the auspices before public acts; augurs held the law of signs, inaugurated, and ruled on faults. At Aquilonia Papirius held that a falsely reported sign stood for the general and fell on the reporter (Livy 10.40): a sign was a formal announcement, with responsibility attached.

  8. What does the episode of 163 BC show about the standing of the haruspices?
    Answer

    The Senate used them but did not defer to them: Gracchus abused them as Etruscan barbarians, and only his own reading of the augural books persuaded him of a fault, after which the consuls of 162 resigned (Cicero, On the Nature of the Gods 2.10–11). Roman augural law, not Etruscan expertise, was decisive.

  9. Why had the calendar drifted by 46 BC, and how did Caesar fix it?
    Answer

    The 355-day year depended on intercalation decided by the pontiffs, who granted or withheld it for political ends (Censorinus 20.7; Curio in 50 BC), so it ran about three months ahead. Caesar made 46 BC 445 days long and then fixed 365 days with a leap day every fourth year; Augustus corrected the pontiffs' miscount.

  10. What traps does Ovid's Fasti set for anyone reconstructing a festival?
    Answer

    It covers only January to June; it is late and overlaid with Augustan dates; it piles up rival explanations that are learned display rather than cult testimony; it historicises rites, turning the Regifugium into Tarquin's flight; and its first-person witness may be a persona. Trust the actions and materials more than the reasons.

Carry forward

  1. Roman priests were the governing class in another capacity: senators holding priesthoods for life, increasingly by election, advising a Senate that decided. Religious authority and political competition were one system, which is why the emperors absorbed the priesthoods (module 6.2).
  2. Rome preserved ritual fossils whose meaning had gone: the rex sacrorum, the Flamen Dialis' taboos, the Salian and Arval hymns. Read the rite as old and the explanation as late, whether the explainer is Livy, Plutarch or Ovid.
  3. Time was administered by priests: the pontiffs controlled the calendar until Caesar replaced their discretion with a rule, and festivals kept their dates while their meanings changed, so a shepherds' Parilia could become Rome's birthday on the same day.
