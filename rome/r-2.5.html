01Evocatio: calling a god out of an enemy city

Conquest posed a religious problem: an enemy city had gods with a claim on it, and Rome would have to live beside them. Evocatio, "calling out", settled it. Before the final assault the commander invited the city's protecting deity to desert it for better cult at Rome: a transfer, not a defeat, on the do ut des logic of module 2.1.

The classic case is Juno Regina of Veii in 396 BC. Module 3.3 narrates the siege; the rite is told by Livy some 370 years later. Camillus prays to Juno Regina, "who now dwells at Veii", to follow the victors to a city soon to be hers, where a temple worthy of her will receive her (5.21.3). After the sack, young men bathed and dressed in white entered her temple in awe, since by Etruscan custom only a priest of one family touched the image. One asked, from inspiration or in jest,

Do you want to go to Rome, Juno?

and the others cried that she had nodded. She proved light to carry, as if willing, and went to the Aventine, where Camillus dedicated her temple in 392 (5.22.3–7, 5.31.3).

The formula itself survives for Carthage in 146 (Macrobius, Saturnalia 3.9.7–8): it addresses the power "whether god or goddess" protecting the city, asks it to desert the people and come to Rome, and promises temples and games. An inscription from Isaura Vetus in Asia Minor shows P. Servilius Vatia paying a vow to that town's god or goddess in 75 BC. Hence, Pliny says, Rome kept its own guardian's name secret (Natural History 28.18).

Where sources disagree. Beard, North and Price (Religions of Rome, 1998) treat evocatio as a regular instrument of expansion, practised more often than recorded, and modern lists add Vortumnus from Volsinii in 264. Blomart (1997) finds very few secure cases among the many uses of the verb evocare, and some read the Isaura text as a plain vow. Gustafsson (Evocatio Deorum, 2000) reads each account as the story of a unique crisis, not an instance of a fixed rite.

02Invited gods: the Sibylline route and Aesculapius

Most new gods were invited, through the Sibylline Books, opened only on the senate's order (module 2.3): crisis, consultation, prescription. Apollo's temple was vowed for plague in 433 (Livy 4.25.3, writing four centuries later); Venus of Eryx received one on the Capitol after Trasimene (22.9–10, 23.31.9). Greek books prescribed Greek gods.

A plague of 293 sent the senate to the Books, which named Aesculapius of Epidaurus (Livy 10.47.6–7). The rest fell in Livy's lost book 11 and survives in its epitome (Periocha 11), in Valerius Maximus (1.8.2) and in Ovid (Metamorphoses 15.622–744), all nearly three centuries later. The envoys brought back a sacred snake, which swam from the ship to the Tiber Island, and the temple was dedicated on 1 January, conventionally in 291. The cult kept its Greek incubation (Greek module 8.2), and the island became a refuge: Claudius ruled that slaves abandoned there who recovered were free (Suetonius, Claudius 25.2).

Where sources disagree. Wissowa (Religion und Kultus der Römer, 1912) held that imported gods were housed outside the pomerium, the sacred boundary, and Juno on the Aventine and Aesculapius on the island fit. Orlin (Foreign Cults in Rome, 2010) points to Castor in the Forum, Venus Erycina on the Capitol and the Magna Mater on the Palatine, and argues that the senate sited each god case by case.

03The Magna Mater, 204 BC

In 205, with Hannibal still in Italy, a shower of stones prompted a consultation: a foreign invader could be driven out if the Idaean Mother were brought from Pessinus to Rome (Livy 29.10.4–5). Attalus I of Pergamon, Rome's ally, handed the envoys the sacred stone the locals called the Mother of the Gods (29.11.7). Publius Scipio Nasica, judged the best man in the state, received her at Ostia; the leading matrons passed her hand to hand into the city, and on 12 April 204 she was lodged in the temple of Victory on the Palatine, the day made a holiday (29.14.5–14); the Megalesia of the calendars opened on 4 April. Her temple followed in 191 (36.36.3–4).

Livy gives Claudia Quinta one clause: a doubtful reputation made illustrious by her chastity (29.14.12). The miracle is Ovid's (Fasti book 4): the ship grounds in the Tiber, and Claudia, praying to be followed if she is chaste, draws it free alone. Herodian (1.11) makes her a Vestal.

Rome divided the cult. Magistrates gave the goddess Roman games and sacrifice, and aristocratic dining clubs feasted at her festival (Cicero, On Old Age 45). Her priests were the Galli, self-castrated devotees of Attis who processed with drums and cymbals collecting alms; by law and senatorial decree, Dionysius says, no native Roman joined their procession or rites (2.19.4–5). In 77 BC a consul denied the Gallus Genucius an inheritance as neither man nor woman (Valerius Maximus 7.7.6).

Where sources disagree. Livy's goddess comes from Pessinus, Varro's from Pergamon (On the Latin Language 6.15). Gruen (Studies in Greek Culture and Roman Policy, 1990) read the import as diplomacy, a claim on the Greek East through Attalus and Aeneas' Trojan descent; Beard (1994) argued that the cult was built to stay foreign, so that Rome could define itself against it. Attis figurines from Republican levels of the Palatine sanctuary show that her consort came with her from the start.

04The Bacchanalia, 186 BC

Livy's account (39.8–19), written about 170 years later, is a comedy turned melodrama. A Greek sacrificer and seer brought nocturnal rites to Etruria and then Rome, where wine and darkness bred debauchery, forged wills and poisonings (39.8). The consul Spurius Postumius learns of it through young Publius Aebutius, whose mother and stepfather want him initiated to hide their embezzlement, and his lover, the freedwoman courtesan Hispala Faecenia. She testifies that the Campanian priestess Paculla Annia had turned a women's rite held three days a year into nocturnal initiations five times a month, admitting men and no one over twenty, until the initiates were almost a second people (39.9–14). Postumius told the assembly that nothing is more deceptive than a depraved religion (39.16.6). Over seven thousand were implicated, and more were executed than imprisoned (39.17.6, 39.18.5).

The consuls' letter relaying the senate's decree survives on a bronze found at Tiriolo in Bruttium in 1640 (CIL I² 581).

Where sources disagree. North ("Religious toleration in Republican Rome", 1979) argued that the senate struck at an autonomous organisation with its own priests, funds and oaths, cutting across family and state, not at Bacchus. Gruen (1990) saw the senate asserting authority over Italy and over its own members; Walsh (1996) showed how much of Livy's narrative is stage comedy.

05Expulsions: astrologers, Jews and Egyptians

Rome seldom banned a god; it removed practitioners, and every expulsion lapsed.

Suetonius' Chrestus is usually read as Christ under a common slave name, the riots as synagogue quarrels over Christian preaching; Acts 18.2 has Aquila and Priscilla leave Rome after the expulsion. Orosius (7.6.15) dates it to 49; Dio says that in 41 Claudius did not expel the Jews but forbade their meetings. Lüdemann (1980) dates the measure to 41; Riesner (1994) defends 49. Uncertain.

06Isis and Serapis

Isis came in the Ptolemaic form of Greek module 8.2, with Serapis, through traders: a Serapeum stood at Puteoli by 105 BC. After the demolitions of the 50s politics reversed policy. The triumvirs voted her a temple in 43 (Dio 47.15.4); Actium made Egypt suspect; under the Flavians the cult was imperial. Vespasian and Titus spent the night before their triumph of 71 at the Iseum (Josephus, Jewish War 7.123), and Domitian rebuilt the Iseum in the Campus Martius. At Pompeii a freedman's six-year-old son, Numerius Popidius Celsinus, rebuilt the temple after the earthquake of 62 and was made a councillor for it (CIL X 846).

Apuleius' Metamorphoses, the Golden Ass, of the later second century AD, ends with the only first-person account of a pagan initiation. Isis rises from the sea at Cenchreae and names herself the one goddess whom other peoples call Mother of the Gods, Minerva, Venus or Ceres (11.5); at her festival of the ship Lucius eats the priest's roses and becomes human again (11.13). After abstinence and fees he is initiated by night, approaching the boundary of death and passing through the elements (11.23):

At midnight I saw the sun gleaming with brilliant light.

Two more initiations follow at Rome (11.26–30), and a priest's dream expects a man of Madauros, Apuleius' own town (11.27).

Where sources disagree. Griffiths (The Isis-Book, 1975) read book 11 as sincere and largely autobiographical, the Madauros slip as the author's signature. Winkler (Auctor and Actor, 1985) argued that the novel leaves the reader unable to decide, and that a Lucius paying for three initiations may be the last dupe in a book of dupes. The ritual details match other evidence either way.

07Mithras: the bull, the cave and the grades

Greek module 8.2 covers the cult's Greek mediation; here the Roman evidence. Statius gives the first notice of the bull-slaying, about AD 90 (Thebaid 1.719–720). The tauroctony is standard from Britain to Syria: Mithras in a Phrygian cap kneels on the bull and stabs it, looking away; dog and snake go for the blood, a scorpion for the genitals; Sol, Luna and a raven watch; two torch-bearers flank the scene. The mithraeum imitated a cave (Porphyry, On the Cave of the Nymphs 6): a vaulted room with benches for a shared meal, holding twenty to forty men, who rose through seven grades from Raven to Father (Jerome, Letters 107.2). They were soldiers, imperial slaves and freedmen, customs officials; women were excluded.

Under Santa Prisca on the Aventine, excavated by Vermaseren and van Essen, paintings of about AD 200 show the grades in procession with verses of disputed reading. Under San Clemente, beneath two superimposed churches, a mithraeum with its tauroctony altar was fitted into a first-century building around the same date.

Where sources disagree. Justin (First Apology 66) and Tertullian (Prescription against Heretics 40) said demons had imitated Christian sacraments in Mithras' bread and cup, and Renan (Marc-Aurèle, 1882) suggested that had Christianity been halted the world would have become Mithraist. Clauss (Mithras: Kult und Mysterien, 1990) and most specialists answer that small, all-male groups without public temples could never have become a mass religion.

08The "oriental religions" since Cumont

Franz Cumont's Les religions orientales dans le paganisme romain (1906) made Cybele, Isis, the Syrian gods and Mithras one wave of eastern salvation religions that filled the void of a cold civic cult and prepared the way for Christianity. Each element has fallen. The cults reached Rome already Hellenised and differed in structure (Burkert, Ancient Mystery Cults, 1987; Greek module 8.2). None was exclusive: Vettius Agorius Praetextatus, who died in 384, was pontiff, augur and Mithraic Father of Fathers (CIL VI 1779). The cold civic religion was a Protestant caricature (J. Z. Smith, Drudgery Divine, 1990). Beard, North and Price prefer "elective" cults, joined by choice rather than birth.

Where sources disagree. The centenary conference of 2006 (published 2009 by Bonnet, Pirenne-Delforge and Praet) largely retired the category. Alvar (Romanising Oriental Gods, 2008) defends a revised one: the three cults share a soteriology and values distinct from civic cult, and Romans marked them as foreign. Versluys replies that their easternness was itself a Roman construction.

09Jews at Rome

Judaism was the one foreign worship Romans met as a people's inheritance rather than a cult to be joined. A community existed by the second century BC; Pompey's captives enlarged it, and in 59 Cicero complained of the Jewish crowd at Flaccus' trial (Pro Flacco 66–69). Caesar exempted their associations from his ban on collegia (Josephus, Jewish Antiquities 14.213–216); Augustus, Philo says, held their grain over when the distribution fell on the Sabbath (Embassy to Gaius 155–158). Horace jokes about the Sabbath (Satires 1.9.69–70); Tacitus is hostile (Histories 5.4–5). After 70 every Jew paid two drachmas a year to Jupiter Capitolinus (Josephus, Jewish War 7.218). Catacomb epitaphs name about a dozen synagogues.

Where sources disagree. Older handbooks call Judaism a religio licita with a charter from Caesar. The phrase is a Christian polemicist's (Tertullian, Apology 21.1), and Rajak ("Was there a Roman charter for the Jews?", 1984) showed that the privileges were grants to particular communities, renewed and contested city by city.

10First encounters with the Christians

Christians were in Rome by the 50s, when Paul wrote to them. To Romans they were a superstitio (module 2.1) with no ancestral people behind it.

Tacitus, writing about fifty years after the fire of 64 (module 7.1), says Nero fastened the blame on people hated for their outrages, whom the crowd called Christians, followers of a Christus executed by Pontius Pilatus. They were convicted less of arson than of hatred of the human race, and were torn by dogs, crucified, or burned as night-lights in Nero's gardens (Annals 15.44.2–5). Suetonius lists their punishment among Nero's police measures, without the fire (Nero 16.2).

Where sources disagree. Shaw ("The myth of the Neronian persecution", 2015) argued that the name Christian was not current in Rome in 64 and that Tacitus projected the conflicts of his own day onto the fire. Jones (2017) defended the traditional reading; Suetonius' independent notice is its strongest support.

Pliny, governing Bithynia-Pontus about 110–112, asked Trajan whether the name itself was punished or only the crimes attached to it (Letters 10.96). He had asked the accused three times whether they were Christians and executed those who persisted, for their obstinacy; he released those who invoked the gods, offered incense and wine to Trajan's image and cursed Christ. The lapsed said they had met before dawn to sing a hymn to Christ as to a god and swore an oath against theft and adultery, then shared ordinary, harmless food. Two slave women called ministrae, tortured, revealed only a depraved and excessive superstition.

Trajan replied (10.97) that Christians were not to be sought out; if accused and convicted they were to be punished, but one who sacrificed was pardoned, and anonymous accusations were "not of our age". Tertullian asked why the guilty were not hunted and the innocent were punished (Apology 2.6–9). The rescript held until Decius' order of 250 (module 8.1).

Where sources disagree. De Ste. Croix ("Why were the early Christians persecuted?", 1963) argued that the charge was the name itself, standing for a refusal of the gods that endangered the pax deorum. Sherwin-White (1964; commentary on the letters, 1966) stressed the governor's discretionary coercion and the Christians' contumacia, defiance of his order, which is what Pliny says he punished.

11Self-test

  1. What did evocatio assume about the bond between a god and a city, and what evidence outside Livy shows the rite was real?
    Answer

    That a god's protection was a relationship that could be renegotiated and transferred by the promise of better cult at Rome. Macrobius preserves the formula used at Carthage in 146 (Saturnalia 3.9.7–8), the Isaura Vetus inscription of 75 BC repeats its "god or goddess" hedge, and Pliny (Natural History 28.18) explains Rome's secret tutelary name by the same logic.

  2. How did Aesculapius come to Rome, and why does his island not prove a rule that foreign gods stayed outside the pomerium?
    Answer

    After the plague of 293 the Sibylline Books prescribed him; envoys brought a sacred snake from Epidaurus, which chose the Tiber Island (Periocha 11; Valerius Maximus 1.8.2). Castor in the Forum, Venus Erycina on the Capitol and the Magna Mater on the Palatine break Wissowa's rule, so Orlin argues for case-by-case siting.

  3. What did the oracle of 205 promise, and why was the import useful beyond the war?
    Answer

    That a foreign invader of Italy could be driven out if the Idaean Mother were brought from Pessinus (Livy 29.10.4–5). It also tied Rome to Attalus of Pergamon and to the Trojan ancestry of Aeneas, a claim on standing in the Greek East (Gruen).

  4. How did Rome accept the Magna Mater while keeping her priests at arm's length?
    Answer

    Magistrates gave her Roman games and sacrifice and aristocrats feasted at the Megalesia, but citizens were barred by law and decree from joining the procession or rites of her eunuch Galli (Dionysius 2.19.4–5). The Genucius case of 77 BC (Valerius Maximus 7.7.6) shows a Gallus losing civic capacity.

  5. What does the Tiriolo bronze show about the senate's aims that Livy's narrative obscures?
    Answer

    It names no crime and regulates structure: no male priests, no masters or officers, no common fund, no oaths, no secret rites, no more than five worshippers, all subject to licence (CIL I² 581). The cult was licensed and shrunk, not abolished, which supports North's view that independent organisation was the target.

  6. What did astrologers, Isiacs and Jews have in common as targets of expulsion, and how lasting were the measures?
    Answer

    Each formed a group outside civic control, perceived as foreign and low, and astrologers could also predict a ruler's death. The measures were temporary and repeated, as Tacitus admits of the astrologers, a breed always banned and always retained (Histories 1.22).

  7. Why is Metamorphoses 11 both the best and the most hazardous source for the mysteries of Isis?
    Answer

    It is the only first-person account of a pagan initiation, with details of abstinence, fees, the night rite and the festival that match other evidence. But it closes a comic novel, and Winkler showed that its sincerity cannot be settled against Griffiths' autobiographical reading.

  8. State Cumont's thesis about the oriental religions and two objections to it.
    Answer

    That Cybele, Isis, the Syrian gods and Mithras formed one wave of eastern salvation religions filling the void of cold civic cult and preparing for Christianity. Against it: each cult reached Rome already Hellenised and differed in structure (Burkert), none was exclusive, as Praetextatus' priesthoods show, and the cold civic religion is a Protestant caricature (J. Z. Smith).

  9. Why is religio licita a misleading description of the Jews' position at Rome?
    Answer

    The phrase is Tertullian's, not a Roman legal term, and Rajak showed that Jewish privileges were specific grants to communities, renewed and contested. Rome tolerated Judaism because it was the ancestral worship of a people, as Caesar's exemption of their associations shows.

  10. What did Pliny ask Trajan, and why did Tertullian find the answer incoherent?
    Answer

    Whether the name of Christian was punishable in itself or only the crimes attached to it (Letters 10.96). Trajan ruled that Christians were not to be sought out but were to be punished if accused and persistent, and pardoned if they sacrificed (10.97); Tertullian asked why the guilty were not hunted and the innocent were punished (Apology 2.6–9).

12Carry forward

  1. Roman polytheism was absorptive by design: gods could be captured by evocatio, invited through the Sibylline Books or tolerated as a people's ancestral worship, and in each case the senate decided. Newness itself was never the offence.
  2. Repression targeted organisation, secrecy and allegiance, not belief: priests, funds and oaths outside the state's control in 186 BC, prophecy about the ruler, and finally refusal of the common cult, which Pliny turned into a sacrifice test. Watch the same logic harden into the persecutions of unit 8.
  3. "Oriental religions" is a modern category. Read each cult as a specific Roman construction (Roman games beside self-castrated Galli, an Egyptian style made in Italy, a Persian name on a Latin mystery) and keep each source's problem in view: Livy's melodrama, Apuleius' novel, Tacitus' hindsight.

Give me an in-depth report on how Rome took in, regulated and resisted foreign gods.
