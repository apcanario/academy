01Tyre and the Phoenician west

Carthage was a colony of Tyre and never forgot it. The Phoenician cities of the Levant coast, Tyre, Sidon, Byblos and Arwad, were small, rich and short of land, living by cedar, purple dye, metalwork and shipping. Tyre, an island city, rose to primacy under Hiram I in the tenth century, Solomon's partner in the Hebrew Bible (1 Kings 5). Assyrian demands for tribute in silver drew Tyrian ships west: copper from Cyprus, where Kition became a Tyrian foundation, then silver from the ores behind Huelva in southern Spain, the Greeks' Tartessos. Gadir (Cádiz), beyond the Strait, housed the great temple of Melqart; Strabo (3.5.5) tells how Tyre founded it at the third attempt, on an oracle's instruction. Utica, Lixus in Morocco, Motya in Sicily, and Nora and Sulcis in Sardinia made a chain of harbours.

Where sources disagree. Velleius Paterculus (1.2.3) puts Gades about eighty years after Troy's fall, around 1100, with Utica shortly after, and Pliny (Natural History 16.216) claims Utica's temple kept cedar beams 1,178 years old. Nothing Phoenician in the west is so early. Fernando González de Canales and colleagues (2004) dated Phoenician pottery at Huelva to about 900; María Eugenia Aubet placed settlement proper in the eighth century, after a phase of "precolonial" trade. The twelfth-century dates tie the west to the age of Troy.

02Qart-hadasht: the date of the foundation

The Phoenician name was Qart-hadasht, "New City"; Greeks made it Karchedon, Romans Carthago. It sat on a peninsula in the Gulf of Tunis with a defensible hill, the Byrsa. Timaeus of Tauromenium, writing in the early third century, five and a half centuries after the date he gave, put the foundation 38 years before the first Olympiad, 814/813 BC, the same year as Rome (Dionysius of Halicarnassus 1.74.1; module 1.3). Menander of Ephesus, quoted by Josephus from what he presents as Tyrian annals, places Elissa's flight in King Pygmalion's seventh year, 143 years and eight months after Hiram began Solomon's temple (Against Apion 1.125–126): again the late ninth century. Justin (18.6.9) makes Carthage 72 years older than Rome; Appian (Punica 1) puts it before the fall of Troy.

The ground does not quite reach 814. The earliest securely dated finds, Greek cups of Euboean type and Phoenician wares from below the Byrsa and from Bir Massouda, found by excavations led by Hans Georg Niemeyer and Roald Docter, belong to the mid-eighth century; the oldest tophet deposit, the "Chapelle Cintas", is usually put in the second half of the eighth century.

Where sources disagree. Docter and colleagues (2008) obtained radiocarbon dates on animal bone from the lowest levels that reach into the later ninth century. Those who trust the Tyrian king list see near-confirmation of 814; those who weight the pottery prefer about 760, with Timaeus' year a learned synchronism. The gap is two or three generations, and the question is open.

03Elissa before Virgil

The earliest surviving version is Timaeus', preserved in an anonymous Greek treatise On Women (FGrHist 566 F 82). Her Phoenician name was Elissa, and she was the sister of Pygmalion, king of Tyre. When Pygmalion killed her husband she fled by ship with some citizens and, after many hardships, reached Libya, where the Libyans called her Dido "because of her wandering". A Libyan king sought to marry her. She refused; pressed by the citizens, she feigned a rite to release her from an oath, built a pyre beside her house, lit it and threw herself in.

Pompeius Trogus, a Gaul writing under Augustus, eight centuries after the event, told the full story; it survives in Justin's epitome (18.4–6). Elissa marries her uncle Acherbas, priest of Hercules (Melqart) and second man in Tyre, and her brother Pygmalion murders him for his hidden gold. She loads the treasure on ships, then has sacks of sand thrown overboard as if they were the gold, so that the king's servants, fearing punishment, must flee with her. At Cyprus a priest of Jupiter joins on condition that his line keeps the priesthood, and some eighty girls, taken from the shore where they earned dowries by prostitution, become wives for her young men.

In Africa she buys as much land as an ox's hide can cover, cuts the hide into strips and encloses a hill: hence Byrsa, Greek for "hide". The settlers dig up an ox's head, sign of a fertile but servile city, and move; a horse's head promises a warlike and powerful one. Then Iarbas, king of the Maxitani, demands her in marriage under threat of war. His envoys trap her: he wants a Carthaginian, they say, to teach his people a civilised life, but who would live among savages? She rebukes their cowardice, and they reveal that the duty is hers. She takes three months, builds a pyre at the city's edge, sacrifices as if to her husband's shade, climbs it with a sword, tells the people she goes to her husband as they wished, and kills herself. While Carthage stood unconquered she was worshipped as a goddess (18.6). This Elissa is a chaste widow who dies to keep faith with the dead and to save her city. She never meets a Trojan.

Where sources disagree. Timaeus' Elissa leaps into the fire; Trogus' uses a sword on the pyre, the combination Virgil keeps. Timaeus glosses Dido as "wanderer"; Servius says it was Punic for a woman of manly courage. "Byrsa" is probably a Greek pun on a Semitic word for citadel. Whether a real princess lies behind her is uncertain: Menander makes Pygmalion a plausible historical king, but Elissa's cult may have generated her biography rather than the reverse.

04Virgil's Dido and an impossible meeting

Virgil took Trogus' story almost whole into Venus' account at Aeneid 1.340–368, renaming the husband Sychaeus, then turned it inside out. The chaste widow breaks her vow for Aeneas; Iarbas becomes a rejected suitor (4.196–218); pyre and sword remain, but the motive is desertion; and her curse calls up an avenger from her bones (4.622–629), making Hannibal a Trojan inheritance. Naevius may already have brought Aeneas to Carthage, and Varro said it was Anna who died for love of him (Servius on Aeneid 4.682).

The ancients saw the difficulty: Eratosthenes put Troy's fall in 1184/3, some 370 years before Timaeus' Carthage. Macrobius (Saturnalia 5.17.5–6) calls wanton Dido a story the whole world knows is false, yet artists treat as true. A Greek epigram on her statue (Planudean Anthology 16.151), put into Latin by Ausonius, has Dido protest that she never saw Aeneas, and Jerome (Against Jovinian 1.43) praises Elissa's widowed chastity. Book 4 belongs to deep dive module 12.4.

05The Carthaginian state

Aristotle, writing in the 330s, is the earliest analyst. In Politics 2.11 (1272b24–1273b26) he ranks Carthage's constitution with Sparta's and Crete's: the people stay loyal, and there has been no serious civil strife and no tyrant. He names "kings", a council of elders, a Hundred and Four comparable to the Spartan ephors but chosen on merit, and self-recruiting boards of five that elect them. When kings and elders agree, they decide whether to consult the people; when they disagree, the people decide, and any citizen may speak against a proposal, as at Sparta none could. His criticisms concern oligarchy: wealth counts in elections, the highest offices can be bought, and one man may hold several. Discontent is drained by sending citizens out to grow rich in the subject cities.

The "kings" are the suffetes (šptm, "judges"), two annual chief magistrates attested in Punic inscriptions and in Livy, who shows Hannibal as chief magistrate in 196 attacking the life tenure of the order of judges (33.46–47). Generals were separate, elected for a war and answerable to a court of a hundred judges created, Justin says, to restrain them (19.2); failed generals were sometimes crucified. Polybius (6.51) calls the constitution well designed but past its prime by the Hannibalic war: at Carthage the people had the greatest say, at Rome the Senate, so Rome deliberated better.

Two families dominate. The Magonids descend from Mago, who in the mid-sixth century first gave Carthage an ordered army (Justin 18.7, 19.1); a Magonid Hamilcar fell at Himera in 480 (Herodotus 7.165–167). The Barcids, Hamilcar Barca ("lightning") and his sons, built a near-monarchy in Spain after 237 (modules 4.2 and 4.3).

Where sources disagree. Herodotus (7.166) says Hamilcar was made king for his valour; Aristotle's kings are elected and not tied to one family. Whether a Magonid monarchy gave way to paired suffetes, or Greeks always styled elected magistrates kings, is uncertain, as is whether the Hundred and Four is Justin's court of a hundred.

06An empire of coasts

When Nebuchadnezzar besieged Tyre for thirteen years in the early sixth century, Carthage took over the mother city's role in the west. Its empire was naval and coastal. In Sicily the Phoenicians held Motya, Soloeis and Panormus, near the Elymians and the short crossing to Africa (Thucydides 6.2.6); Carthage led them against the Greeks, losing at Himera in 480 and replacing Motya, destroyed by Dionysius I in 397 (Diodorus 14.47–53), with Lilybaeum. Off Corsica about 540 Carthaginian and Etruscan fleets fought the Phocaeans at Alalia (Herodotus 1.166); the gold tablets of about 500 from Pyrgi, Caere's port, with a dedication in Etruscan and Phoenician, show Carthage's Etruscan ties. Sardinia was conquered in the later sixth century after hard fighting (Justin 19.1). In Spain Carthage inherited Gadir and colonised Ebusus (Ibiza), 160 years after its own foundation by Diodorus' reckoning (5.16); the land empire there was Barcid. In Africa Carthage paid the Libyans rent for its site until the fifth century (Justin 19.1–2), then took the hinterland and taxed the Libyans hard; Agathocles of Syracuse, landing in 310, showed how thinly it was held.

07Gods and the tophet

The chief god was Baal Hammon, whom Greeks called Kronos and Romans Saturn; the African Saturn of Roman times continues him. Beside him from the fifth century stands Tanit, "Tanit face of Baal" on thousands of stelae, with her sign of triangle, bar and disc; Romans identified her with Juno Caelestis, which is why Virgil's Carthage is Juno's city. Melqart, "king of the city", whom Greeks called Heracles and Romans Hercules, tied colony to mother city: Carthaginian envoys were at his festival in Tyre when Alexander besieged it (Arrian, Anabasis 2.24.5). Names carry the theology: Hannibal, "grace of Baal"; Hasdrubal, "Baal helps".

Greek and Roman writers accused Carthage of sacrificing children. Diodorus (20.14.4–6), writing nearly three centuries after the event, reports that in 310, with Agathocles in Africa, the Carthaginians blamed their disasters on Kronos, whom they had cheated with bought children; they sacrificed two hundred children of the noblest families, and at least three hundred others offered themselves. Each child rolled from the sloping hands of a bronze statue into fire. Plutarch (On Superstition 13) adds a mother standing by without tears while flutes and drums drowned the cries; Tertullian (Apology 9.2) says the rite lasted in Africa until a proconsul crucified its priests.

In 1921 stelae on the antiquities market led François Icard and Paul Gielly to a precinct in the Salammbô quarter. Dug since by Francis Kelsey, Pierre Cintas and, in 1976–79, Lawrence Stager's team, it held thousands of urns of burnt bones of infants and of lambs and kids, under stelae to Baal Hammon and Tanit recording vows the god "heard"; Stager estimated 20,000 urns for 400–200 alone. Archaeologists borrowed the biblical word tophet (Jeremiah 7.31); similar precincts exist at Motya, Sulcis and Tharros, none in Phoenicia. The inscriptions call the offering mlk, which Otto Eissfeldt (1935) showed is a sacrificial term, not a god "Moloch".

Where sources disagree. Sabatino Moscati (1987), M'hamed Fantar and Hélène Bénichou-Safar read the tophet as a cemetery for infants who died naturally and the classical reports as propaganda; Jeffrey Schwartz and colleagues (2010) found many of the infants prenatal or newborn. Patricia Smith, Stager and colleagues (2011) replied that the ages cluster at one to two months, unlike natural mortality, and Paolo Xella, Josephine Quinn and others (2013) set the vow formulae beside the texts. Opinion now leans to sacrifice; its frequency and scale remain disputed.

08The treaties with Rome

Polybius copied three treaties from bronze tablets in the treasury of the aediles beside the temple of Jupiter Capitolinus (3.26.1); the first was in Latin so archaic that the best-informed Romans understood it only with effort (3.22.3).

TreatyPolybius' dateMain terms
First (3.22–23)consuls Brutus and Horatius, 28 years before Xerxes crossed to Greece: 508/7 (509 in Varro's reckoning)Romans not to sail beyond the Fair Promontory unless forced, and then to leave within five days; trade in Libya and Sardinia only before a herald or clerk; equal terms in Carthaginian Sicily; Carthage not to harm Ardea, Antium, Circeii, Tarracina or other Latins subject to Rome, nor build a fort in Latium
Second (3.24)undated; usually 348 (Livy 7.27.2; Diodorus 16.69.1)Tyre and Utica join Carthage; Mastia of Tarseion added as a western limit; Romans barred from Sardinia and Libya; trade open at Carthage and in Carthaginian Sicily
Third (3.25)Pyrrhus' invasion, 279/8earlier terms confirmed; any alliance with Pyrrhus to leave room to help the other; Carthage to supply ships

Carthaginians swore by their ancestral gods, Romans by Jupiter Lapis, the oath-taker casting a stone and praying to be cast out likewise if he broke faith (3.25.6–9). The pattern is clear: Carthage guards a trading monopoly in Africa and Sardinia and treats Rome as a Latin power whose coast it will leave alone. Polybius cites the treaties against Philinus of Agrigentum, who claimed that one barred Romans from Sicily and Carthaginians from Italy, so that Rome broke it by crossing in 264; Polybius says no such document exists (3.26). The crossing itself belongs to module 4.2.

Where sources disagree. Diodorus (16.69.1) calls the treaty of 348 the first; module 1.6 sets out why most, Beaumont and Cornell among them, keep Polybius' date near 509. Livy (9.43.26) records a renewal "for the third time" in 306 that Polybius omits, and some identify it with the treaty Philinus cited (module 4.2).

09Carthaginian voices

Carthage's literature is lost. In 146 the Senate gave its libraries to African kings but had Mago's twenty-eight books on agriculture translated into Latin (Pliny, Natural History 18.22); Cassius Dionysius of Utica made a Greek version (Varro, On Agriculture 1.1.10), and Columella calls Mago the father of farming (1.1.13). The fragments, on vines, olives, bees and oxen, speak for a landowning elite: whoever buys land should sell his town house (Pliny, Natural History 18.35).

Hanno's Periplus survives in Greek, essentially in one ninth-century manuscript at Heidelberg, claiming to translate the account that Hanno, "king of the Carthaginians", set up in the temple of Kronos. He sails past the Pillars with sixty penteconters and thirty thousand settlers to found towns, then south past a burning mountain, the Chariot of the Gods, to hairy people his interpreters called Gorillai; Pliny says their skins hung in Juno's temple at Carthage until its fall (6.200).

The longest Punic text in Latin letters is in Plautus' Poenulus, adapted from a Greek Karchedonios: the Carthaginian Hanno enters praying in Punic (930–949) before switching to Latin. He is a pious father seeking his kidnapped daughters, though the prologue says he knows every language and pretends not to: "Poenus plane est" (113). Polybius' Greek text of Hannibal's oath with Philip V (7.9) and thousands of votive inscriptions complete the record.

Where sources disagree. Gilbert Germain (1957) judged the Periplus a Greek literary fiction; most scholars accept a Punic core, perhaps reworked. Its date is uncertain, and its furthest point is placed anywhere from Mount Cameroon to southern Morocco, Raymond Mauny doubting that ancient ships could return past Cape Bojador. Pliny (2.169) even has Hanno sail round Africa to Arabia.

10Punica fides: the Roman stereotype

Romans made Carthage a proverb. Sallust (Jugurtha 108.3) suspects Bocchus of "Punic faith"; Livy gives Hannibal "perfidia plus quam Punica" (21.4.9). Cicero (De lege agraria 2.95) blames place, not race: harbours full of traders made Carthaginians liars. The type is older than Rome: Homer's Phoenician is a guileful trader (Odyssey 14.288–289), and Polybius says that at Carthage nothing profitable was thought shameful (6.56). Yet Polybius calls Rome's seizure of Sardinia in 238 contrary to all justice (3.28.1–2), and Rome's conduct in 149 (module 4.4) was no model of good faith. The stereotype is the victor's: Carthage left no history of its wars, and its treaties show a state that renewed terms with Rome for more than two centuries. Jonathan Prag (2006) and Josephine Quinn (In Search of the Phoenicians, 2018) add that "Punic" and "Phoenician" were outsiders' labels; Carthaginians named themselves by their city.

11Self-test

  1. What dates did ancient writers give for the foundation of Carthage, and how does the archaeology compare?
    Answer

    Timaeus gave 814/813, synchronised with Rome (Dionysius 1.74.1), and Menander's Tyrian list via Josephus points to the late ninth century; Appian put it before the fall of Troy. The earliest pottery belongs to the mid-eighth century and the Chapelle Cintas to its second half, though radiocarbon dates reach into the late ninth, so the gap is two or three generations and still debated.

  2. How does Elissa die in Timaeus, and how does Justin's version differ?
    Answer

    In Timaeus she refuses a Libyan king, pretends a rite to release an oath, builds a pyre beside her house and leaps into it (FGrHist 566 F 82). In Justin 18.6 she asks three months, builds a pyre at the city's edge, sacrifices to her husband's shade and kills herself with a sword on the pyre.

  3. How did Elissa trick Pygmalion's servants into joining her flight?
    Answer

    At sea she had sacks of sand thrown overboard as if they were Acherbas' gold; the servants, fearing the king's punishment for its loss, had to flee with her (Justin 18.4).

  4. What does the oxhide story explain, and why is it probably not an old Carthaginian tradition?
    Answer

    It explains the name of the citadel, Byrsa, which in Greek means "hide". The name is probably a Semitic word for citadel reinterpreted by a Greek pun, so the story was built on the etymology.

  5. What did Virgil change in the Elissa story, and what objection did ancient readers raise?
    Answer

    He made her Aeneas' contemporary and lover, broke her vow to Sychaeus, motivated her suicide by desertion and gave her a curse that founds the Punic wars. Troy fell about 370 years before Carthage was founded; Macrobius (Saturnalia 5.17.5–6) says the whole world knows the story is false, and a Greek epigram has Dido deny she ever saw Aeneas.

  6. Name the parts of the Carthaginian constitution in Aristotle and the feature of its assembly he contrasts with Sparta and Crete.
    Answer

    "Kings" (the suffetes, a pair in Punic and Latin evidence), a council of elders, the Hundred and Four, the boards of five, and the people. When a matter came before the people any citizen could speak against it (Politics 2.11).

  7. Why, according to Polybius 6.51, was Rome better placed than Carthage at the time of the Hannibalic war?
    Answer

    Both had mixed constitutions, but Carthage's had passed its prime: the people had the greatest voice there, while at Rome the Senate still guided deliberation.

  8. What does Diodorus 20.14 report, and what should a reader note about its date?
    Answer

    That in 310, during Agathocles' invasion, the Carthaginians sacrificed two hundred noble children to Kronos (Baal Hammon), with three hundred more volunteering, rolling them from a bronze statue's hands into fire. Diodorus wrote nearly three centuries later, from a Greek tradition hostile to Carthage.

  9. What evidence do the two sides of the tophet debate rely on?
    Answer

    The urns of infant and animal bones and the stelae with vow formulae and the term mlk. Schwartz and colleagues stress prenatal and newborn ages consistent with natural death; Smith, Stager and colleagues find ages clustered at one to two months, and Xella, Quinn and others combine inscriptions and texts to argue for sacrifice.

  10. What did the first two treaties in Polybius protect for each side, and what did the third add?
    Answer

    Carthage kept a trading monopoly in Libya and Sardinia behind the Fair Promontory and Mastia; Rome secured its Latin coast and equal access to Carthaginian Sicily. The third, at Pyrrhus' invasion, confirmed the earlier terms, let each side help the other against him and had Carthage supply the ships (3.25).

12Carry forward

  1. Carthage is known almost entirely through its enemies, Greek historians, Roman annalists and a comic poet; read every verdict on it (Punic faith, child sacrifice, its constitution) by asking who wrote it, when and for whom, and treat the treaties, Mago, Hanno and the inscriptions as the nearest thing to Carthaginian testimony.
  2. The founding queen has two layers: the Tyrian Elissa of Timaeus and Trogus, a chaste widow who dies rather than marry Iarbas, and Virgil's Dido, made Aeneas' contemporary against a chronology the ancients knew, so that the Punic wars begin in her curse.
  3. Rome and Carthage were treaty partners with complementary spheres for more than two centuries before 264; the wars that follow broke a long arrangement, and who broke faith was argued from the start, by Philinus against Polybius.

The prompt this answers

Give me an in-depth report on Carthage and on the legend of its founding queen. Cover: the Phoenician expansion west and Tyre; the foundation of Carthage (Timaeus' date of 814/813 BC against the archaeology of the eighth century); Elissa-Dido before Virgil (Timaeus, Justin's epitome of Pompeius Trogus 18.4–6, the oxhide and the Byrsa, her suicide to avoid marrying Iarbas) and Virgil's rewriting of her into Aeneas' lover, with the chronological impossibility the ancients already noticed; the Carthaginian state as Aristotle (Politics 2.11) and Polybius 6.51 describe it: suffetes, council and assembly, the Magonids and the Barcids; the empire in Africa, Sardinia, Spain and western Sicily; religion (Baal Hammon, Tanit, Melqart) and the tophet controversy over child sacrifice, from Diodorus 20.14 to the excavations at Salammbô and the modern argument; the treaties with Rome in Polybius 3.22–26; the Carthaginian voices that survive (Mago's agronomy, Hanno's Periplus, the Punic speeches in Plautus' Poenulus); and the Roman stereotype of Punic faithlessness. Format: about 2500 words, headed sections, name sources, flag disagreements, end with a 10-question self-test with answers and the three ideas I must carry forward.
