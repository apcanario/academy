01Polybius' war and its partisans

The war survives as a connected story only in Polybius book 1, written after 167, a century after the events, as background to his fifty-three years from 220 (module 4.4). He starts in 264 because Timaeus stopped there and because the Romans then first crossed the sea (1.5, 1.12.5–9). His main sources were two contemporaries he distrusts: Philinus of Agrigentum, whose city Rome sacked in 261, and Fabius Pictor, the senator who wrote in Greek about 200 (module 4.5). Each wrote like a lover, Philinus finding wisdom and courage only in Carthaginians, Fabius only in Romans (1.14).

The rest is later and broken. Diodorus, writing about 30 BC, survives here in Byzantine excerpts (books 23–24) and cites Philinus by name (23.8). Cassius Dio, early in the third century AD, survives in fragments and in the twelfth-century epitome of Zonaras (book 8). Livy's books 16–19 are lost but for the late antique Periochae. Naevius, a veteran of the war (Gellius 17.21.45), made it a Latin epic (module 4.5).

02Messana, 264: a hesitant Senate and a people that voted

Leaving Sicily in 276, Pyrrhus called it a wrestling ground for Carthaginians and Romans (module 3.5). The bout began at Messana. The Mamertines, "men of Mars", were Campanian mercenaries of Agathocles who seized the city after his death in 289, killing or expelling its men and taking their wives and property (Polybius 1.7.2–4). Hiero, general and then king of Syracuse, beat them at the Longanus river (1.9.7–8). Divided, one faction let a Carthaginian garrison into the citadel; the other offered the city to Rome and asked help as kinsmen (1.10.1–2).

Polybius presents a dilemma. Rome had just executed its own garrison at Rhegium for the same crime (module 3.5), and helping its imitators was inconsistent (1.10.3–4). Against that stood fear: Carthage held Africa, much of Spain and the western islands, and with Messana would hold the bridge to Italy (1.10.5–9). The Senate never gave its sanction. The people, worn out by recent wars, listened to the commanders, who promised plunder, and voted to send help under the consul Appius Claudius Caudex (1.11.1–3). The Mamertines got the Carthaginian commander out of the citadel; Carthage crucified him (1.11.4–5), allied with Hiero and besieged Messana. Appius crossed at night in ships lent by the Greek allies (1.20.13–14) and beat Hiero, then the Carthaginians (1.11.9–12.4).

Where sources disagree. Polybius' version, probably Fabian, puts reluctance in the Senate and greed in the people. W. V. Harris (War and Imperialism in Republican Rome, 1979) reads it as an alibi for an aristocracy that lived on military glory. B. D. Hoyos (Unplanned Wars, 1998) argues that Rome voted to help the Mamertines against Hiero, not for war with Carthage. Philinus had Appius beaten twice, a version Polybius shows to be self-contradictory (1.15).

The treaties Polybius copied from bronze tablets on the Capitol (3.22–26) are module 4.1; one disputed clause bears on war guilt.

Where sources disagree. Philinus said a treaty barred Romans from Sicily and Carthaginians from Italy, so that the crossing broke an oath; Polybius says no such document ever existed (3.26.2–5). Yet Livy's tradition made a Carthaginian fleet off Tarentum in 272 a breach of treaty (Periochae 14), and Servius (on Aeneid 4.628) knew a clause keeping each power off the other's shores. Hoyos ("Treaties True and False", CQ 1985) found most historians following Philinus and argued that he erred.

03Hiero changes sides; Agrigentum

In 263 both consuls crossed with four legions and Sicilian cities came over (1.16.1–3), Segesta with its claim of Trojan kinship (module 1.3). Hiero asked for peace, returning prisoners and paying 100 talents (1.16.9); Diodorus gives a quarter of that sum and a fifteen-year term (23.4.1). Rome accepted because, with Carthage holding the sea, its army needed Syracusan grain (1.16.6–8). Hiero stayed loyal until his death in 215. Manius Valerius took the name Messalla from Messana and hung a painting of his victory on the Curia Hostilia (Pliny, Natural History 35.22).

Agrigentum fell in 261 after a long siege, and its people were sold, more than 25,000 by Diodorus' count (Polybius 1.19.15; Diodorus 23.9.1). The Senate now aimed to drive Carthage out of the island (1.20.1–2): the war for Messana had become a war for Sicily.

04A fleet from a wreck: Mylae and the corvus

Land victories could not win Sicily while Carthage held the sea and raided Italy (1.20.5–8). In 261 Rome ordered 100 quinqueremes and 20 triremes, though it had never built a quinquereme. The model, Polybius says, was a Carthaginian decked ship captured when it ran aground in 264, and crews learned to row on benches set up on land (1.20.9–21.2). The first seventeen ships, under the consul Gnaeus Cornelius Scipio, were trapped at Lipara and taken with him (1.21.4–8).

Because the new ships were slow, someone proposed the raven (korax, Latin corvus): a gangway about 1.2 metres wide and 11 long, pivoting on a pole in the bow, dropped so that an iron spike pinned the enemy deck and marines ran across (1.22). Off Mylae in 260 Hannibal's 130 ships came on in contempt. The first thirty were grappled and taken, among them his flagship, a "seven" that had belonged to Pyrrhus; ships trying the flanks met ravens swung round to face them, and Carthage lost fifty ships (1.23). Gaius Duilius held Rome's first naval triumph (Periochae 17) and was escorted home from dinner for life by a torch and a piper (Cicero, On Old Age 44). The inscription of his column of ships' beaks survives in an archaising imperial copy (CIL I² 25).

Where sources disagree. The captured-model story (Polybius 1.20.15–16) makes Rome a nation of amateurs, yet its Greek allies had built warships for centuries, the ground on which Thiel (1954) and Goldsworthy (2000) doubt it. The Punic warship excavated off Marsala, its timbers marked with Punic letters for assembly, shows building methods that could be copied. The corvus appears only in Polybius and leaves his narrative after 256; its weight in the bow has been blamed for losses in heavy seas, though a calculation in H. T. Wallinga's The Boarding-Bridge of the Romans (1956) found it unlikely to upset a quinquereme seriously. Eutropius gives Mylae as 31 ships taken and 14 sunk (2.20).

05Ecnomus and Regulus in Africa

In 256 the consuls Marcus Atilius Regulus and Lucius Manlius Vulso sailed for Africa with 330 ships, each carrying 300 rowers and 120 marines, about 140,000 men; Hamilcar and Hanno met them off southern Sicily with 350 ships and over 150,000 (1.25.7–26.9). The Romans came in a wedge, with the horse transports in tow (1.26.10–16). In the separate fights the ravens told: Rome lost 24 ships sunk, Carthage more than 30 sunk and 64 taken (1.28.14).

The army landed at Aspis, and the Senate recalled Manlius with the fleet, leaving Regulus 15,000 infantry and 500 cavalry (1.29.9). He beat a Carthaginian army at Adys on hills where its elephants were useless and took Tunis (1.30). Hoping to finish before a successor came, he offered terms so harsh that Carthage chose to fight (1.31.4–8); Dio's list in Zonaras (8.13) amounts to surrender. Carthage hired Xanthippus, a Spartan who blamed its generals rather than Roman quality (1.32). With 12,000 foot, 4,000 horse and nearly 100 elephants on open ground in 255, he broke the Roman army; about 2,000 escaped and Regulus was taken with 500 (1.34). Polybius draws Euripides' lesson that one wise counsel defeats many hands (1.35). Appian (Punica 4) and Valerius Maximus (9.6 ext. 1) say Carthage had Xanthippus drowned on his voyage home.

Where sources disagree. If Polybius' totals stand, Ecnomus may have involved more men than any other sea battle. W. W. Tarn ("The Fleets of the First Punic War", JHS 1907) and J. H. Thiel (A History of Roman Sea-Power before the Second Punic War, 1954) reduced his largest Roman fleets as implausible; Tipps ("The Battle of Ecnomus", Historia 1985) defended them, and Goldsworthy (The Punic Wars, 2000) advises broad acceptance. H. H. Scullard (Cambridge Ancient History VII.2, 1989) judged Dio's terms for Regulus scarcely reliable.

06Regulus returns to Carthage: the making of an exemplum

Polybius says nothing of Regulus after his capture; the Roman tradition kept him alive. Livy's book 18, in the Periochae, had him kill a monstrous serpent by the Bagradas, whose 120-foot skin Tubero says went to Rome (Gellius 7.3). About 250 Carthage sent him to Rome to negotiate peace or an exchange of prisoners, on oath to return if he failed. He urged the Senate to refuse both, went back, and was put to death. Tuditanus, consul in 129, had him kept awake until he died; Tubero, in the first century BC, had his eyelids sewn open against the sun (Gellius 7.4). Cicero, two centuries on, made him the proof that an oath to an enemy binds (On Duties 3.99–111); Horace's Regulus pushes away wife and sons and leaves as if for a country holiday (Odes 3.5.13–56).

Where sources disagree. Polybius, who admired Roman good faith, is silent. Diodorus (24.12) tells how the mother of the Atilii, angry at her husband's death, had her sons torture two Carthaginian prisoners until one died and the tribunes intervened. In Tuditanus it is Regulus' sons who shut Carthaginian captives in a spiked chest (Gellius 7.4); in Augustine Regulus himself dies in one (City of God 1.15). The cruelty migrated, and most modern historians treat the torture as family legend.

07The sea as enemy: storms and Drepana

In 255 a rescue fleet took 114 Carthaginian ships off the Hermaean promontory (1.36.11). Returning along southern Sicily against its pilots' advice, between the risings of Orion and Sirius, it met a storm off Camarina: of 364 ships 80 survived, the greatest disaster at sea Polybius knew (1.37.1–4). He blamed the consuls and a Roman habit of meeting every obstacle with force, which works against men but not against wind and water (1.37.7–10). Rome built 220 ships in three months and took Panormus (1.38); in 253 another storm sank more than 150, and the Senate gave up the sea for a time (1.39.6–8). Lucius Scipio, consul in 259, whose epitaph records Corsica taken, also gave a temple to the Storms, the Tempestates (CIL I² 9). The war turned to land: at Panormus in 250 Lucius Caecilius Metellus broke Hasdrubal's elephants against a ditch, and the siege of Lilybaeum began (1.40–48).

In 249 Publius Claudius Pulcher tried to surprise Adherbal at Drepana. The Carthaginian slipped out as the Roman column entered, pinned it against the shore and took 93 ships (1.49–51). Later writers remembered the sacred chickens Claudius drowned (module 2.3); Polybius blamed rashness. Claudius named a man of the lowest standing as dictator, who was forced to resign (Periochae 19), and was fined heavily (1.52.2–3). His colleague Lucius Junius Pullus anchored his convoy on a rocky coast to avoid Carthalo, and a storm destroyed it (1.54).

Where sources disagree. Diodorus has 340 warships lost off Camarina (23.18.1) against Polybius' 284; Claudius losing 117 ships and 20,000 men (24.1.5) against 93 ships; and two warships surviving of 105 in Junius' storm (24.1.9). If Diodorus follows Philinus and Polybius here follows Fabius, each source had reason to bend the numbers.

08Hamilcar Barca and the Aegates Islands

In 247, the eighteenth year of the war, Carthage gave the Sicilian command to Hamilcar Barca (1.56.1–2). He held Heircte, a mountain stronghold near Panormus, for three years and raided Italy as far as Cumae (1.56). In 244 he took the town of Eryx, between a Roman garrison by the summit sanctuary of Venus Erycina (module 1.3) and a Roman army at the foot, and held it two years more (1.58). Polybius compares the struggle to champion boxers and to game-cocks fighting on when their wings fail (1.57–58), and names Hamilcar the best general of the war (1.64.6). He was unbeaten but could not win while Carthage kept few ships at sea.

Rome's last fleet was privately funded: leading citizens, singly or in twos and threes, each provided a quinquereme, to be repaid after victory; 200 were built on the model of the ship of Hannibal the Rhodian, captured running the blockade of Lilybaeum (1.59.6–8). In March 241 Hanno brought ships loaded with supplies, meaning to unload at Eryx and embark Hamilcar's best men. The consul Gaius Lutatius Catulus caught him off the Aegates islands, attacking though the wind favoured the enemy; the heavy Carthaginian ships with raw crews lost 50 sunk and 70 taken, with nearly 10,000 prisoners (1.60–61). Diodorus has 117 Carthaginian ships lost and 80 Roman ships destroyed or damaged, and Philinus 6,000 prisoners (24.11).

Where sources disagree. Surveys off Levanzo led by Sebastiano Tusa with the RPM Nautical Foundation have recovered more than twenty bronze rams, with helmets and amphorae; Latin inscriptions on several name the quaestors who passed them (Jonathan Prag, JRA 2014). The rams are uniform and small: William Murray judges from their size that they come from ships smaller than "fours", not Polybius' quinqueremes. Either Polybius used "quinquereme" loosely, or the rams found so far come from the smaller ships in each fleet; the question is open.

Diodorus (24.13) and Nepos (Hamilcar 1) say the Romans first demanded Hamilcar's arms and deserters, and he refused. Lutatius agreed that Carthage would leave Sicily, make no war on Hiero, return all prisoners without ransom, and pay 2,200 Euboean talents over twenty years (1.62.8–9). The Roman people refused to ratify; ten commissioners added 1,000 talents payable at once, cut the term to ten years and took the islands between Sicily and Italy (1.63.1–3). Each side was barred from the other's allies (3.27.2–6).

09The Mercenary War, Sardinia and Corsica, and the first province

Carthage could not pay the roughly 20,000 mercenaries brought home from Sicily (1.66–67). They mutinied, Libyan subjects squeezed by wartime levies joined them (1.72), and under Spendius, a Campanian runaway slave, and Matho, a Libyan, they besieged Utica and threatened Carthage, striking coins inscribed "of the Libyans". Polybius calls it the truceless war, unmatched in cruelty and lawlessness (1.65, 1.88.7): the rebels mutilated and killed the envoy Gisgo and some 700 prisoners (1.79–80); Hamilcar trapped one army at the Saw, where the starving men ate their prisoners and slaves (1.84–85); Matho was tortured to death in Carthage (1.88.6). It lasted three years and four months.

Rome at first behaved correctly, freeing Carthaginian prisoners and refusing Utica's surrender (1.83.5–11). Hiero supplied Carthage on a principle Polybius states as a rule: never help a power grow so great that no one can dispute even its acknowledged rights (1.83.3–4). Then, in 238 or 237, mercenaries expelled from Sardinia invited Rome in. When Carthage prepared to recover the island, Rome voted war; Carthage gave up Sardinia and paid 1,200 talents more (1.88.8–12). Polybius calls this contrary to all justice (3.28.1–4) and counts it the second cause of the Hannibalic war, after Hamilcar's anger (3.10); Hamilcar's departure for Spain in 237 is module 4.3. Corsica, not named in the cession, was subdued in the 230s (Periochae 20).

Sicily outside Hiero's kingdom passed to Rome; the former Carthaginian cities probably paid Rome the grain tithe they had paid Carthage. Cicero calls Sicily the first of all to be called a province (Verrines 2.2.2). In 227 the praetors were raised to four (Periochae 20), giving Sicily and Sardinia governors.

Where sources disagree. Modern textbooks date the first province to 241, but no regular governor is attested before 227, and provincia meant a magistrate's task before it meant a territory. J. S. Richardson (The Language of Empire, 2008) traces that shift; on this view 241 began Roman taxation in Sicily, and 227 the governed province.

10The cost in Polybius' figures

The war lasted twenty-four years without a break, counting inclusively, the longest and greatest Polybius knew; the Romans lost about 700 quinqueremes, the Carthaginians about 500 (1.63.4–6).

At Polybius' complement of 300 rowers and 120 marines (1.26.7), the 284 ships wrecked off Camarina would have carried nearly 120,000 men; some were lightly crewed prizes from the Hermaeum, so the toll is unknown, but it ran to tens of thousands, mostly allied rowers and the poorest citizens, whom Polybius says served in the fleet (6.19). The registered citizens fell from 382,234 before the war (Periochae 16) to 241,212 in 247 (19); a wartime count missed men abroad, so the fall measures strain, not deaths. Polybius' verdict is that Rome's later rise was not Fortune's gift but the natural result of schooling itself in such contests (1.63.9).

11Self-test

  1. In Polybius' account of 264, what argued against helping the Mamertines, what argued for it, and who finally decided?
    Answer

    Against: Rome had just executed its own Rhegium garrison for the same crime (1.10.3–4). For: fear that Carthage, already holding Africa, Spain and the islands, would take the bridge to Italy (1.10.5–9). The Senate withheld its sanction; the people, promised plunder by the commanders, voted (1.11.1–3).

  2. What was the "Philinus treaty", and why does it matter?
    Answer

    A treaty Philinus claimed barred Romans from Sicily and Carthaginians from Italy. If real, Rome broke it by crossing to Messana; Polybius denies it ever existed (3.26), while Livy's tradition and Servius imply a clause of that kind.

  3. Why did Hiero change sides in 263, and why did Rome welcome him?
    Answer

    Both consuls had crossed with four legions and Sicilian cities were defecting, so Hiero bought peace for 100 talents and the prisoners (1.16.9). Rome needed his grain while Carthage held the sea (1.16.6–8), and he stayed loyal until 215.

  4. How did the corvus work, and why might it have been dropped?
    Answer

    A pivoting gangway with an iron spike was dropped on an enemy deck so that marines could board, turning a sea fight into a land fight (1.22). It disappears from Polybius after 256; some blame its weight for losses in heavy seas, though Wallinga's book doubts it.

  5. According to Polybius, why did Regulus' African campaign fail?
    Answer

    Regulus, eager to finish before a successor came, demanded terms so harsh that Carthage chose to fight (1.31). Xanthippus then fought on open ground with cavalry and elephants and destroyed the Roman army (1.32–34); Polybius makes it a lesson in Fortune and in intelligence (1.35).

  6. How did the story of Regulus' death grow, and why do historians doubt it?
    Answer

    Polybius is silent; Tuditanus has sleeplessness, Tubero sewn eyelids (Gellius 7.4), Augustine the spiked chest (City of God 1.15). Diodorus (24.12) records the family's torture of Carthaginian prisoners, and Tuditanus puts the spiked chest in the sons' hands, which suggests a legend built to cover that scandal.

  7. What does Polybius blame for the Camarina disaster of 255?
    Answer

    The consuls, who ignored their pilots' warnings about the coast and the season (1.37.4–6), and a Roman habit of using force against every obstacle, effective against men but not against the sea (1.37.7–10).

  8. What did Hamilcar Barca achieve in Sicily, and why could he not win the war?
    Answer

    He held Heircte for three years and Eryx for two more, raided Italy and was never beaten; Polybius calls him the best general of the war (1.64.6). But Carthage did not control the sea, and his army depended on supply fleets like the one destroyed off the Aegates.

  9. How was Rome's last fleet paid for, and what have the Aegates rams added to Polybius?
    Answer

    Wealthy citizens built ships singly or in small groups, to be repaid after victory (1.59.6–8). The rams, some inscribed with the names of approving quaestors, are small and uniform, suggesting ships smaller than "fours" rather than the quinqueremes Polybius describes.

  10. How did the Roman commissioners harden the peace of 241, and why did Polybius condemn the seizure of Sardinia?
    Answer

    They added 1,000 talents at once, halved the term to ten years and took the islands between Sicily and Italy (1.63.1–3). Rome took Sardinia while Carthage was crippled by the Mercenary War and extorted 1,200 talents more; Polybius calls it contrary to all justice (3.28) and a cause of the next war (3.10).

12Carry forward

  1. Rome's war aims grew with success: help for Messana in 264, all Sicily after Agrigentum (1.20.1–2), Africa in 256. A limited intervention that turns into a war for mastery is the pattern of Rome's later wars.
  2. Rome won by refusing to accept losses as final: it rebuilt fleets after storms that drowned tens of thousands of allied and poor rowers and paid for the last one by private loans. Polybius reads this as Roman force, ruinous at sea, and as proof that Rome's rise was not luck (1.63.9).
  3. The peace made the first province and the next war: Sicily taxed and later governed by praetors, Sardinia taken by force, and Hamilcar's resentment are the causes Polybius sets behind Hannibal (3.10), told by a Greek who judged between the partisans Philinus and Fabius.

The prompt this answers

Give me an in-depth report on the First Punic War, 264 to 241 BC, following Polybius book 1. Cover: the Mamertines at Messana and the decision to cross to Sicily, with Polybius' account of a hesitant Senate and a people that voted for war; Hiero of Syracuse's switch to Rome; the building of a fleet on the model of a captured warship, Mylae in 260 BC and the boarding-bridge (corvus); Ecnomus and the invasion of Africa; Regulus' defeat by Xanthippus and the later legend of his return to Carthage to die; the storms that sank whole Roman fleets; Claudius Pulcher at Drepana; Hamilcar Barca in western Sicily; the Aegates Islands in 241 BC and the peace terms; and the aftermath: the Mercenary War, the Roman seizure of Sardinia and Corsica, Sicily as the first province, and the human cost in Polybius' figures. Format: about 2500 words, headed sections, name sources, flag disagreements, end with a 10-question self-test with answers and the three ideas I must carry forward.
