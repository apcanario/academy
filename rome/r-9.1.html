01Ius, fas and mos

Romans sorted binding rules under three heads. Ius was what one person could claim against another and a magistrate would enforce; Celsus, quoted by Ulpian, called it "the art of the good and the fair" (Digest 1.1.1 pr.). Fas was what the gods allowed: fas lex divina, ius lex humana, says Isidore (Etymologies 5.2.2). Mos was ancestral custom, enforced by shame, the censor's mark and the family council; Cicero quotes Ennius' line that the Roman state stands on ancient customs and on men (On the Republic 5.1, preserved by Augustine, City of God 2.21).

The three overlapped. The calendar marked days fasti, on which the praetor might speak the three words of jurisdiction, do, dico, addico, and days nefasti, on which he might not (Varro, On the Latin Language 6.29–30). The oldest sanction declared the offender sacer, given to the gods and so killable. Custom made law: Julian holds that inveterate custom is rightly kept as statute and that statutes may lapse by disuse (Digest 1.3.32.1). A father who killed his son acted within ius, yet mos expected a council of kin first (module 9.3). Roman legal history is the growth of ius at the expense of the other two.

02The Twelve Tables and the pontiffs

Module 3.1 tells how the decemvirs wrote the Tables in 451–450, how they survive and what they said; module 2.4 gives the funeral rules and the evil song. The Tables gave rules, not the means to use them. Pomponius, a jurist of the second century AD, says that interpretation and the forms of action stayed with the pontiffs, one of whom presided over private law each year (Digest 1.2.2.6).

The forms were the legis actiones, five in Gaius' list, from the wager (sacramentum) to the seizure of a pledge (Institutes 4.12), each with set words: a man who sued for his vines cut down lost, because the Tables spoke of trees (4.11). Interpretation also stretched old words to new uses. The clause freeing a son sold three times became emancipation (module 9.3), and the sale by bronze and scales (mancipatio, 1.119) was adapted to make wills (2.102–104).

The monopoly broke in stages. Gnaeus Flavius published the forms and the calendar in 304 (module 3.1); Pomponius says he stole the book (1.2.2.7). Tiberius Coruncanius, the first plebeian pontifex maximus, was the first to profess law in public (1.2.2.35). Sextus Aelius Paetus, consul in 198, wrote the Tripertita, the Tables, their interpretation and the actions, which Pomponius calls the cradle of the law (1.2.2.38).

03The praetor's edict and the formula

From 367 a praetor administered justice in the city (Livy 6.42.11), and from 242 a second praetor heard cases involving foreigners. Each posted an edict at the start of his year, stating the claims he would grant a remedy for; some called it an annual law (Cicero, Verrines 2.1.109). He could not legislate, but he could refuse an action or invent one: praetorian law, Papinian says, was introduced "to aid, supplement or correct the civil law" (Digest 1.1.7.1). Praetors gave estates to blood kin whom the Tables' rules of male descent passed over, and Cicero's colleague Gaius Aquillius Gallus devised formulas against deceit (On Duties 3.60). A tribune's law of 67 bound praetors to their own edicts (Asconius on Pro Cornelio).

Trial under the formula had two stages. Before the praetor the parties settled a written instruction to a private judge, a citizen and not a lawyer; before the judge they proved their case. In Gaius' model the judge is told that if it appears that Numerius Negidius ought to pay Aulus Agerius ten thousand sesterces, he is to condemn him, and if not, to absolve him (4.39–43); every condemnation was in money (4.48). The old actions, Gaius says, fell from favour because the smallest slip lost the case, and the Aebutian law and two Julian laws abolished them (4.30).

Under Hadrian, Salvius Julianus fixed the edict's final text (Eutropius 8.17), and governors' edicts copied it (module 7.5). Meanwhile cognitio, in which an imperial official heard the whole case subject to appeal, displaced the formula, which a law of 342 abolished outright (Code of Justinian 2.57.1).

04The jurists

The Republican jurist was usually a senator who advised for nothing, skilled, in Cicero's definition, in answering, pleading and drafting (On the Orator 1.212). Quintus Mucius Scaevola, pontifex and consul in 95, was the first to arrange the civil law by categories, in eighteen books (Pomponius, Digest 1.2.2.41). Under Augustus, Pomponius sets Antistius Labeo, an innovator, against Ateius Capito, who held to tradition (1.2.2.47), and Tacitus sets Labeo's freedom against Capito's compliance (Annals 3.75). Their followers formed the Proculian and Sabinian schools; Gaius calls the Sabinians "our teachers". Augustus first let chosen jurists give opinions by his authority (1.2.2.49), and under Hadrian their agreed opinions had the force of law (Gaius 1.7).

Where sources disagree. Gaius records the schools' quarrels. If you make wine from my grapes or a cup from my silver, Sabinus and Cassius held that the product belongs to the owner of the material, Nerva and Proculus that it belongs to the maker (2.79; Gaius, Digest 41.1.7.7). Justinian's Institutes chose a middle view: the owner's if the thing can be reduced to its material, the maker's if not (2.1.25).

Gaius is a first name and a book. He wrote about 161, no other classical jurist cites him, yet the Law of Citations of 426 ranked him with the great Severans. His Institutes, a four-book course for students, is the one classical textbook to survive. In 1816 Barthold Georg Niebuhr, travelling to Rome as Prussian envoy, found in the Chapter Library at Verona a manuscript of Jerome's letters written over an erased legal text; Savigny identified it as Gaius, and Johann Göschen published it in 1820. Before 1816 classical law was known almost only through Justinian's excerpts; Gaius showed it whole and unrevised, procedure included.

The Severan jurists, whose careers module 7.4 tells, closed the classical age: Papinian on cases, Paul and Ulpian in vast commentaries on the edict and the civil law. Ulpian defines justice as the constant and perpetual will to give each his right, and the law's precepts as to live honourably, harm no one and give each his own (Digest 1.1.10 pr.–1).

05Persons, things and actions

Gaius' plan has outlived his book: all the law we use concerns persons, things or actions (1.8). Justinian's Institutes kept it, and through them the modern civil codes.

Persons. The free are divided from slaves, the freeborn from the freed (1.9–11), the independent (sui iuris) from those in another's power (1.48). Status rested on freedom, citizenship and family, and losing any of them was a "diminution of head" (1.159–162).

Things. Temples and tombs lay outside ownership (2.2–9). The archaic res mancipi, Italian land, slaves, oxen, horses, mules and asses, passed only by mancipation or a fictitious lawsuit (2.14a–22). Obligations arose from contract or delict (3.88). Contracts bound by delivery, words, writing or consent (3.89): the stipulation, spondesne? spondeo (3.92), bound whoever said the words; sale, hire, partnership and mandate bound by agreement alone. The delicts were theft, robbery, damage under the Aquilian law and insult (3.182). The Tables' fixed twenty-five asses for assault (Gaius 3.223) grew absurd as money lost value: Labeo, as Gellius reports, told of Lucius Veratius, who slapped passers-by while a slave paid out the coins (20.1.13), and the praetor substituted damages assessed case by case.

Actions. An action was in rem when it claimed a thing, in personam when it claimed that someone ought to give or do (4.2–3). Romans thought from remedies: a right was what the edict granted an action for, and the jurists' commentaries follow the edict's order.

06Paternal power, slavery and manumission

Module 9.3 gives paternal power in life; here are its mechanics. Power came by birth in lawful marriage, by adoption through three sales and a cession in court (Gaius 1.134), or by adrogation of an independent man, voted by the curiate assembly after the pontiffs' inquiry (1.99; Gellius 5.19). A son in power could contract, but his acquisitions went to his father (2.87), and for a son's or slave's delict the father could pay or surrender the offender (4.75). After a son named Macedo killed his father to clear his debts, a decree of the Senate under Vespasian made loans to sons in power irrecoverable (Ulpian, Digest 14.6.1; Suetonius, Vespasian 11).

The slave was a thing and a person at once. Florentinus calls slavery an institution of the law of nations subjecting one man to another's ownership against nature (Digest 1.5.4.1); Ulpian, that by civil law slaves count for nothing, while by natural law all men are equal (50.17.32). Slaves ran shops and ships with a peculium, and creditors sued the master on their dealings (Gaius 4.69–74). Antoninus Pius punished masters who killed their own slaves without cause and forced cruel ones to sell (1.53); the Silanian decree of AD 10 exposed every slave under the roof to torture and death when a master was murdered (Digest 29.5), the rule behind the execution of Pedanius' household (module 9.3).

Formal manumission made a citizen: by the rod, a collusive claim of freedom before a magistrate; by enrolment in the census; or by will (Gaius 1.17). Slaves freed informally became, under a lex Junia, Junian Latins (1.22), who lived free and died as slaves, their goods returning to the patron (3.56). Augustus narrowed the gate (Suetonius, Augustus 40.4). The lex Fufia Caninia of 2 BC capped freedom by will on a sliding scale, never above a hundred slaves (1.42–46). The lex Aelia Sentia of AD 4 required an owner of twenty and a slave of thirty unless a council approved the cause (1.18, 1.38), voided manumissions that cheated creditors (1.37), and made slaves once chained, branded or sent to the arena dediticii, free but never citizens (1.13–15). A tax of a twentieth on manumissions dated from 357 (Livy 7.16.7). Constantine validated manumission in church (module 8.3); Justinian made every freedman a citizen (Institutes 1.5.3).

Where sources disagree. Justinian's Institutes call the law the lex Junia Norbana (1.5.3), which points to the consuls of AD 19. Gaius calls it simply the lex Junia, and the Aelian-Sentian law of AD 4 already makes slaves freed under thirty into Latins (1.29), which seems to presuppose it; hence the common date of 17 BC.

07Citizenship and its grades

Citizenship was a bundle: the vote, office, marriage with Roman effect (conubium), trade and ownership under Roman law (commercium), and protection from summary flogging. Module 3.4 gives the Republican ladder. Under the emperors there were Romans, Latins and peregrines. Under the greater Latin right a town's councillors became citizens, under the lesser only its magistrates (Gaius 1.96; module 7.5); a child of a Roman and a peregrine without conubium took the lower status (1.78). Paul, about to be flogged at Jerusalem, asked whether a Roman might be scourged uncondemned, and later appealed to Caesar (Acts 22.25–29, 25.10–12). After the grant of 212 (module 7.4) the line that mattered was rank.

08Criminal law and its penalties

Rome had no public prosecutor. In the Republic capital charges went to the assembly, subject to appeal (module 3.1), and the accused could leave before the last tribe voted (Polybius 6.14.7). From 149 standing jury courts took over offence by offence (module 4.4), and Sulla's courts for murder, forgery and treason punished with interdiction from water and fire, exile in effect. Under the emperors trial passed to the emperor, the Senate, the prefects and the governors, and penalties followed rank, honestiores against humiliores (module 7.4). Callistratus ranks the capital penalties: the fork, Justinian's word for the cross, then burning alive, then beheading; below them the mines and deportation (Digest 48.19.28 pr.).

The worst penalties were meant to be seen. The Tables burned the arsonist (Gaius, Digest 47.9.9) and threw the false witness from the Tarpeian Rock (Gellius 20.1.53). The parricide was flogged, sewn into a sack and thrown into water; the Livian epitome makes the matricide Publicius Malleolus the first, in 101 (Periochae 68). Cicero praised the ancestors for denying him sky, sea and earth (Pro Roscio Amerino 70–72). Modestinus adds a dog, a cock, a viper and an ape, and Hadrian's rule sending him to the beasts where there is no sea (Digest 48.9.9 pr.); Constantine revived the sack (Theodosian Code 9.15.1).

Where sources disagree. Cicero and the Rhetorica ad Herennium (1.23) know no animals; Juvenal has the ape and the snake (8.213–214), and Mommsen (Römisches Strafrecht, 1899) doubted that the ape was ancient. J. D. Cloud (1971) argued that Pompey's law on parricide (Marcian, Digest 48.9.1) replaced the sack with interdiction, so that the imperial sack was a revival; Richard Bauman (Crime and Punishment in Ancient Rome, 1996) warned against assuming that Pompey changed the penalty at all.

Crucifixion was the slave's penalty, servile supplicium in Tacitus' phrase (Histories 4.11), and the bandit's. Cicero calls it the cruellest and foulest (Verrines 2.5.165); module 5.3 has Verres' crucified citizen and Spartacus' men. A heel bone pierced by a nail, found at Giv'at ha-Mivtar in Jerusalem in 1968, is the clearest physical trace. Aurelius Victor says Constantine abolished the old penalty of the gibbet and leg-breaking (Caesares 41.4). Condemnation to the beasts or to a gladiatorial school delivered the criminal to the games (module 9.4): at Carthage in 203 the procurator Hilarianus condemned Perpetua and her companions to the beasts (Passion of Perpetua 6).

09From the jurists to the codes

After the Severans the emperor, whose pleasure had the force of law (modules 7.2, 7.4), made new law by rescript, and private compilers gathered the rescripts in the Gregorian and Hermogenian codes of the 290s (module 8.2). The Law of Citations of 426 settled juristic authority by count: Papinian, Paul, Gaius, Ulpian and Modestinus were authoritative, the majority prevailed, and in a tie Papinian (Theodosian Code 1.4.3).

Theodosius II ordered a code of general laws from Constantine onwards in 429 (1.1.5). The Theodosian Code was promulgated on 15 February 438, valid from 1 January 439, and adopted in the West; its sixteen books arrange dated excerpts by subject, which is why the laws of modules 8.3 and 8.4 can be dated. The West knew it chiefly through the Visigothic Breviary of Alaric (506), issued with an abridged Gaius.

Module 8.6 outlines Justinian's compilation; its method matters here. Deo auctore (530) empowered Tribonian to cut and alter. Tanta (533) reports nearly two thousand books and over three million lines reduced to some 150,000 (Tanta 1). By the modern count the Digest holds about 9,000 extracts from thirty-nine jurists, Ulpian supplying over a third; each names author, work and book, which let Lenel reassemble the lost works (Palingenesia iuris civilis, 1889). Friedrich Bluhme showed in 1820 that the extracts in each title fall into three blocks, Sabinian, edictal and Papinianist, as if three subcommittees had read in parallel. The Code's twelve books ran from Hadrian to Justinian himself, and the Novels kept changing the law: Novel 118 (543) based intestate succession on blood kinship and ended the agnates' priority.

Where sources disagree. Tribonian was licensed to alter, but how much did he change? Otto Gradenwitz (Interpolationen in den Pandekten, 1887) and Gerhard Beseler made the hunt for interpolations the main business of Romanists, catalogued in the Index interpolationum (1929–1935). Franz Wieacker (Textstufen klassischer Juristen, 1960) moved many changes to editions made before Justinian, and since Max Kaser most scholars judge the compilers' interventions fewer and lighter than once supposed.

10Bologna and the civil-law world

The Digest nearly vanished in the West: it is not cited between Gregory the Great and a lawsuit at Marturi in Tuscany in 1076. At Bologna, early in the twelfth century, Irnerius taught it word by word with glosses; Accursius' Glossa ordinaria, of the first half of the thirteenth century, fixed the commentary, and Bartolus of Sassoferrato in the fourteenth fitted it to the statutes of Italian cities. With canon law it formed the ius commune taught in the universities of Latin Europe. The German imperial chamber court applied Roman law from 1495, and the Pandectists built it into the German Civil Code of 1900. The French Civil Code of 1804 follows the Institutes' order of persons, property and modes of acquiring. Through conquest and borrowing, from Chile in 1855 to Japan in the 1890s, these codes made the civil-law world; Scotland and South Africa mix Roman with common law.

Where sources disagree. Odofredus, teaching at Bologna in the thirteenth century, says a certain Pepo first lectured on the laws but won no name, and calls Irnerius the lamp of the law. Burchard of Ursperg's chronicle says Irnerius renewed the neglected law books at the request of Countess Matilda of Tuscany. Charles Radding (The Origins of Medieval Jurisprudence, 1988) traced the new method further back, to the Lombard lawyers of Pavia.

11Self-test

  1. Distinguish ius, fas and mos, and give one example of their overlap.
    Answer

    Ius is enforceable human law, "the art of the good and the fair" (Celsus in Digest 1.1.1 pr.); fas is what the gods allow (Isidore, Etymologies 5.2.2); mos is ancestral custom. The dies fasti and nefasti decided when the praetor might speak the words of jurisdiction (Varro, On the Latin Language 6.29–30), and a father's lawful power to kill a son was hedged by the custom of a family council.

  2. Why did publishing the Twelve Tables not end the pontiffs' control of law?
    Answer

    The Tables gave rules, but interpretation and the set forms of action stayed with the pontiffs (Pomponius, Digest 1.2.2.6), and a wrong word lost the case, as with the vines and trees (Gaius 4.11). The monopoly broke with Flavius' publication in 304, Coruncanius' public teaching and Sextus Aelius' Tripertita.

  3. How could a praetor change the law without legislating?
    Answer

    Through his annual edict he granted or refused actions, creating praetorian law "to aid, supplement or correct" the civil law (Papinian, Digest 1.1.7.1): estates for blood kin, Aquillius Gallus' formulas against deceit, assessed damages for insult in place of the Tables' fixed fine.

  4. Describe the formulary procedure and say what replaced it.
    Answer

    Before the praetor the parties agreed a written formula instructing a private judge, who heard the proof and condemned in money or absolved (Gaius 4.39–43, 4.48). It replaced the legis actiones under the Aebutian and Julian laws (4.30) and gave way to cognitio by imperial officials; a law of 342 abolished it.

  5. Why is the Verona palimpsest so important?
    Answer

    It preserves Gaius' Institutes of about 161, the only classical textbook to survive, unrevised by Justinian's compilers and including procedure. Niebuhr found it under Jerome's letters in 1816, Savigny identified it, and Göschen published it in 1820.

  6. What is Gaius' scheme of the law, and why has it mattered beyond Rome?
    Answer

    All law concerns persons, things or actions (1.8). Justinian's Institutes adopted it, and through them it shaped modern codes: the French Civil Code of 1804 is arranged by persons, property and modes of acquiring.

  7. Name the formal modes of manumission and the Augustan limits on them.
    Answer

    By the rod, by the census and by will (Gaius 1.17). The lex Fufia Caninia of 2 BC scaled freedom by will, never above a hundred slaves (1.42–46); the lex Aelia Sentia of AD 4 required an owner of twenty and a slave of thirty without a council's approval, voided frauds on creditors and created the dediticii. The informally freed were Junian Latins, who died as slaves (3.56).

  8. How did status shape criminal punishment, and what were the aggravated death penalties?
    Answer

    Republican citizens could escape into exile (Polybius 6.14.7); under the emperors penalties followed rank, honestiores against humiliores. Callistratus ranks the cross, burning alive and beheading (Digest 48.19.28 pr.); the parricide was sewn into the sack (Modestinus, 48.9.9 pr.), slaves and bandits were crucified, and the condemned were given to the beasts.

  9. How did the Theodosian Code differ from Justinian's Digest, and how did each reach the medieval West?
    Answer

    The Theodosian Code (438) collected imperial laws from Constantine onwards in sixteen books and reached the West mainly through the Breviary of Alaric (506). The Digest (533) excerpted the classical jurists; it nearly vanished in the West, reappearing at Marturi in 1076 and in Irnerius' teaching at Bologna.

  10. Why must the Digest be read with care as evidence for classical law, and how has the view changed?
    Answer

    Deo auctore licensed Tribonian to cut and alter, so a text may be his rather than its named author's. Gradenwitz and Beseler found interpolations everywhere; Wieacker moved many changes to earlier editions, and since Kaser most scholars think the compilers changed less than once supposed.

12Carry forward

  1. Roman law grew less by statute than by interpretation and remedy: pontiffs, praetors and jurists made new law out of old forms and annual edicts, so the first question about any rule is who had the power to interpret it.
  2. Law was the grammar of status: freedom, citizenship and family defined the person, and rank fixed the penalty. Devices such as adoption, the triple sale, the Junian Latins and the grades of Latin right were instruments of social policy.
  3. Roman law survived as text, not as institution: what reached later Europe was what Theodosius' and Justinian's commissioners chose and altered, read at Bologna and recast in the modern civil codes, so every "Roman" rule has passed through a compiler's hands.
