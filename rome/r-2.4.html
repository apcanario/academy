The household as a congregation

Roman religion had two arenas: sacra publica, performed for the people at public expense, and sacra privata, for individuals, families and gentes (Festus s.v. publica sacra). Module 2.1 deals with the first. The second had its own priest, the paterfamilias, and its own congregation, the familia of wife, children, slaves and freedmen, whose law belongs to module 9.3.

Cato's farm manual, of the mid-second century BC, shows the system at work. The master arriving at his villa first greets the household Lar (On Agriculture 2.1); the slave overseer may sacrifice only at the Compitalia, at the crossroads or the hearth, and must consult no haruspex or Chaldaean (5.3–4); his wife crowns the hearth on the Kalends, Nones and Ides and prays to the Lar (143.2). Private rites were property: Cicero's law code makes them perpetual and binds them to the estate (On the Laws 2.22, 2.47–53). A gens had rites of its own: Livy (5.46.2–3) has Gaius Fabius Dorsuo walk through the Gallic lines in 390 BC to perform his clan's sacrifice on the Quirinal, a story written down some three and a half centuries later.

The Lares: house and crossroads

The prologue of Plautus' Aulularia, staged about 200 BC from a Greek original, is spoken by the Lar familiaris, who has guarded a pot of gold under the hearth for generations and will reveal it because the daughter of the house honours him daily with incense, wine and garlands. In Plautus and Cato the household Lar is singular. The pair of dancing youths raising a drinking horn is the image of the crossroads Lares; in the surviving house shrines, almost all from Pompeii's last decades, it has replaced the single Lar of the texts.

Pompeii and Herculaneum preserve several hundred shrines (lararia): niches, painted walls and small aediculae in atria, gardens and above all kitchens, where slaves tended them. The standard painting shows the Genius of the master, togate and veiled, pouring a libation between the two Lares; below, one or two great snakes glide towards an altar laid with eggs or a pine cone. The snakes are the genius of the place, usually shown, Servius says, as a serpent; when a snake glides from Anchises' tomb to taste the offerings, Aeneas cannot tell whether it is that or his father's attendant (Virgil, Aeneid 5.84–96).

The Lares compitales lived at the compita, shrines where neighbourhood roads met. At their winter festival, the Compitalia, households hung woollen dolls for each free member and woollen balls for each slave, so that the Lares would take the effigies and spare the living (Festus s.v. pilae). The compital associations were political: dissolved by the senate in 64 BC, they were revived by Clodius for his street following (deep dive module 11.3). In 7 BC Augustus divided the city into fourteen regions and their neighbourhoods (vici), 265 of them by Vespasian's census (Pliny, Natural History 3.66), each with a crossroads shrine where elected magistri, mostly freedmen, served the Lares Augusti with the Genius of Augustus between them (Ovid, Fasti 5.145–146). Module 6.2 follows the politics.

Where sources disagree. Were the Lares the dead? Apuleius (On the God of Socrates 15) calls the kindly ancestral soul a Lar familiaris and the restless one a larva, and Ernst Samter (1901) built an ancestor theory on such texts. Georg Wissowa made the Lares gods of farmland, and Harriet Flower (The Dancing Lares and the Serpent in the Garden, 2017) argues that they were gods of place, and the Lares Augusti the old compital Lares renamed, not a cult of Augustus' Genius.

Penates, Vesta, the Genius and the Juno

The Penates were the gods of the penus, the household store; Cicero gives that etymology or one from penitus, "deep within" (On the Nature of the Gods 2.68). Nameless and plural, they were statuettes in the lararium and a presence in hearth and table; a portion of each meal went into the fire. The Penates of the Roman people at Lavinium are the household cult enlarged to the state (module 1.3). Vesta was the hearth fire itself, the focus; her priestesses belong to module 2.3.

The Genius was a man's divine double, the god under whose protection, Censorinus says, each person lives from birth (De die natali 3.1). The name belongs with gignere, "to beget"; the marriage bed was the lectus genialis. On the master's birthday the household honoured his Genius, and slaves swore by it; places, guilds and legions had Genii too. A woman had her Juno: Seneca says the ancients gave each person a Genius or a Juno (Letters 110.1), and Pliny mocks a crowd of gods larger than the human race (Natural History 2.16).

Where sources disagree. Censorinus (2.2–3), writing in AD 238, says the Genius received no blood on the birthday, so that no life was taken on the day one received it; Horace promises his a two-month-old pig (Odes 3.17.14–16), though in Epistles 2.1.143–144 farmers give it flowers and wine. The woman's Juno is attested almost only in imperial sources; whether it is old or a late analogy to the Genius is uncertain.

Rites of passage

On the dies lustricus, the eighth day for a girl and the ninth for a boy, the child was purified and named (Macrobius, Saturnalia 1.16.36). A freeborn boy wore the bulla, a protective locket, gold for the rich and a knot of leather for the poor (Juvenal 5.164–165). At fifteen or sixteen he put off the purple-bordered toga praetexta, hung up the bulla for the Lares (Persius 5.30–31) and was escorted to the Forum in the plain toga virilis, often at the Liberalia on 17 March (Ovid, Fasti 3.771–788). A girl's coming of age was her wedding.

Gaius, in the second century AD, lists three ways a wife entered her husband's manus (Institutes 1.110–113): usus, a year's cohabitation, interrupted if she stayed away three nights; coemptio, a ritual sale with scales and bronze; and confarreatio, the patrician sacrifice to Jupiter Farreus with a cake of spelt (far) before ten witnesses, to which later sources add the pontifex maximus and the Flamen Dialis. The major flamens and the rex sacrorum had to be born of such a marriage and to make one; by AD 23 so few patricians used it that the rules for the Flamen Dialis were eased (Tacitus, Annals 4.16). Most marriages were by then without manus (module 9.3).

Whatever the form, the bride's hair was parted with a spear-point (Plutarch, Roman Questions 87); she wore the flame-coloured flammeum. After the joining of right hands and a sacrifice, a torchlit procession with obscene Fescennine verses took her to her new home (Catullus 61). She wound the doorposts with wool, was lifted over the threshold and was received with fire and water. Varro, quoted by Nonius, says she brought three coins: for her husband, for the Lar of the house, and for the Lar of the nearest crossroads. Marriage was entry into a new set of gods.

The aristocratic funeral and the ancestor masks

Death began with the conclamatio, calling the dead by name. The body was washed and laid out in the atrium, feet to the door. The Twelve Tables, of about 450 BC and known through Cicero four centuries later, forbade burial or burning inside the city, limited flute-players to ten and forbade women to tear their cheeks (On the Laws 2.58–59); Cicero says the decemvirs took these rules almost word for word from Solon (2.59, 2.64), whose laws Greek module 8.4 covers.

Polybius, a Greek hostage at Rome from 167 BC, explains the noble funeral for Greek readers (6.53–54). The body is carried to the Rostra, usually upright, and a son or kinsman recounts the dead man's virtues and deeds. Afterwards his mask, closely reproducing features and complexion, is set in a wooden shrine in the most public part of the house. At later funerals the masks are worn by men of similar build, dressed in the toga of each ancestor's highest office: purple-bordered for a consul or praetor, purple for a censor, gold-embroidered for a triumphator. They ride behind the fasces and sit in ivory chairs on the Rostra while the speaker recounts each ancestor's deeds, oldest first. No sight, Polybius concludes, could be finer for a young man who loves glory.

Pliny describes wax faces in cupboards round the atrium, linked by painted lines into a family tree (Natural History 35.6); Sallust's Fabius Maximus and Scipio said the sight of them set their hearts on fire (Jugurtha 4.5–6). Absence spoke too: at Junia's funeral in AD 22 Cassius and Brutus outshone the rest because their images were not carried (Tacitus, Annals 3.76). Augustus' funeral carried images of Romulus and of Pompey (Dio 56.34).

Where sources disagree. Were the masks taken from the corpse? Older scholarship treated them as death masks and as the origin of the "veristic" Republican portrait (module 10.3). Harriet Flower (Ancestor Masks and Aristocratic Power in Roman Culture, 1996) argues that they were made in life, once a man reached the aedileship or higher, so that display followed office, not birth. Polybius says nothing of casting.

Burning, burial and the street of tombs

Rome practised both rites from the start (module 1.1). By the late Republic cremation was normal. The patrician Cornelii kept to inhumation until Sulla, who had his body burned so that it could not be dug up as he had dug up Marius' (Cicero, On the Laws 2.56–57; Pliny, Natural History 7.187). Even after burning, a bone was buried in earth to end the household's pollution (Varro, On the Latin Language 5.23). From the second century AD inhumation in carved sarcophagi returned.

The dead lined the roads out of the city: the tomb of the Scipios on the Via Appia, where the sarcophagus of Scipio Barbatus, consul in 298 BC, bears an epitaph in Saturnian verse; the baker Eurysaces' tomb, carved with his ovens. Epitaphs address the passer-by and wish that the earth lie light. One sums up the female ideal: Claudia loved her husband, bore two sons, kept the house, worked wool (CIL VI 15346). Slaves and freedmen of great houses went into columbaria, chambers lined with urn niches like dovecotes. Burial clubs pooled costs: the club at Lanuvium inscribed its entry fee, dues and funeral grant in AD 136 (CIL XIV 2112). The poorest went into pits on the Esquiline (Horace, Satires 1.8.8–16).

Where sources disagree. Why did the empire turn from burning to burial? Christianity cannot explain a change that began in the second century. Arthur Darby Nock ("Cremation and Burial in the Roman Empire", 1932) argued for a change of fashion led from the top, with no change of doctrine; Ian Morris (Death-Ritual and Social Structure in Classical Antiquity, 1992) traces a social shift spreading outward from Rome. Ancient writers give no reason.

The Manes and the days of the dead

The Romans called the dead collectively di Manes, "the good gods", a euphemism; from the first century AD epitaphs open D M, Dis Manibus. Cicero's code says: hold your dead kin as gods (On the Laws 2.22). The dead were a plural divinity, met at fixed times and kept at a distance.

Ovid's Parentalia asks little: a garlanded tile, grain, salt, bread soaked in wine, violets (Fasti 2.535–542). Once, during a war, the rites lapsed and the dead howled through the streets until they resumed (2.547–556). On the Feralia an old woman binds hostile tongues for Tacita, the silent goddess, with thread, lead and a fish's head sewn shut (2.571–582): a binding spell inside the state calendar.

In May the dead came uninvited. At midnight on the Lemuria the father rises barefoot, washes his hands and throws black beans behind him without looking, saying nine times that with these he redeems himself and his own; the shade gathers them. He clashes bronze and nine times bids the ghosts go: Manes exite paterni (Fasti 5.429–444). Ovid derives the name from Remus' ghost (module 1.4), almost certainly wrongly. Beans belonged to the dead; the Flamen Dialis might not even name them (Gellius 10.15.12). Three times a year the mundus was opened, and then, says Varro, a door of the grim gods below stood open (in Macrobius, Saturnalia 1.16.18).

Where sources disagree. Plutarch (Romulus 11), eight centuries on, puts Romulus' foundation pit, filled with first-fruits and soil, in the Comitium; Festus, citing Ateius Capito and Cato, gives the three opening days and says the mundus was shaped like the vault of the sky. Whether these are one structure is unknown, and no excavated pit is securely identified. Warde Fowler ("Mundus patet", 1912) noted that the dates fit the farming year and conjectured a store-pit for seed corn that later acquired its underworld meaning.

Against Greek module 8.4, the Twelve Tables echo Solon, the Lemuria recalls the close of the Anthesteria, and the Parentalia does the work of the Genesia. But Rome had no hero cult tied to bones: a noble's ancestors acted through faces and offices paraded in the Forum. Nor had Rome its own map of the underworld; Virgil's is Greek in plan (deep dive module 12.5).

Ghosts

Roman ghosts are the family dead of the festivals and the murdered or unburied who haunt one place. Plautus' Mostellaria, adapted about 200 BC from a Greek comedy, makes farce of the second: the slave Tranio keeps his returning master out of the house by claiming that a guest murdered and buried there without rites has complained in a dream that Acheron will not take him, since he died before his time.

Pliny the Younger asks Licinius Sura whether ghosts exist (Letters 7.27). A house at Athens was haunted by an old man in chains until the philosopher Athenodorus rented it, followed the ghost to a spot in the courtyard and had it dug: chained bones were found and buried, and the haunting ceased; Lucian tells a Greek twin (Lover of Lies 30–31). Suetonius says Caligula's half-burned body haunted the Lamian gardens until his sisters cremated and buried it properly (Caligula 59).

Private magic

The Twelve Tables punished anyone who charmed away another's crops or chanted a malum carmen, as Pliny cites them five centuries later (Natural History 28.17–18). His book 30 opens with a history of magic, "the most fraudulent of arts" (30.1), a Persian invention; he credits the senate's decree of 97 BC against human sacrifice (30.12) and tells how Nero found magic empty (30.14–17). Yet his medical books record charms without censure, and amulets were respectable: the bulla, and the phallic fascinum hung on infants and under the triumphing general's chariot (28.39).

Lead curse tablets (defixiones) carry the Greek practice (Greek module 8.1) into Latin. They bind charioteers, opponents at law, lovers and above all thieves: some 130 from the sacred spring at Bath hand stolen goods over to Sulis Minerva. In Germanicus' house at Antioch in AD 19 were found human remains, spells and lead tablets bearing his name (Tacitus, Annals 2.69). Horace's witch Canidia pours a black lamb's blood into a pit on the Esquiline to call up the shades (Satires 1.8.23–33).

Apuleius' Apology is the only surviving speech of a man tried for magic. About AD 158–159, at Sabratha, his wife Pudentilla's relatives accused him of winning the rich widow by spells: he had bought rare fish, a boy collapsed in his presence, he kept something wrapped in linen among a household's Lares, and he worshipped a skeletal figure they said he called basileus, king. He replied as a philosopher: in Persian a magus is a priest (25–26), the fish were for dissection, the figure a Mercury. Rome had no crime called magic; the charge ran under Sulla's law of 81 BC on assassins and poisoners. The case shows how thin the line was between philosopher, priest and sorcerer.

Where sources disagree. Pliny read the Twelve Tables' malum carmen as a spell; Cicero (On the Republic 4.12, preserved by Augustine, City of God 2.9) took it as defamatory verse. James Rives ("Magic in Roman Law: The Reconstruction of a Crime", 2003) argues that magic became a distinct crime only under the empire, as jurists stretched the law on poisoning. For Fritz Graf, "magic" labels disapproved ritual, not a separate technique.

Self-test

  1. What do Cato's instructions for the farm show about who performed household religion?
    Answer

    The paterfamilias was its priest, greeting the Lar on arrival (On Agriculture 2.1), but cult was delegated to slaves under his control: the overseer might sacrifice only at the Compitalia (5.3) and his wife crowned the hearth and prayed to the Lar on fixed days (143.2).

  2. Why does the pair of dancing Lares in Pompeian kitchens not straightforwardly represent the household Lar of Plautus and Cato?
    Answer

    The early texts know a single Lar familiaris; the dancing pair is the iconography of the crossroads Lares, and the surviving lararia date almost entirely from Pompeii's last decades, when the domestic shrine had taken over the street image.

  3. What did Augustus change at the crossroads in 7 BC, and how does Flower read it?
    Answer

    He organised the city into fourteen regions and their vici, each crossroads shrine tended by elected magistri, mostly freedmen, for the Lares Augusti and the Genius of Augustus. Flower argues these were the old compital Lares renamed, not a cult of the emperor's Genius.

  4. What were the Penates, and how did the household honour them?
    Answer

    The nameless, plural gods of the household store (penus; Cicero, On the Nature of the Gods 2.68), kept as statuettes and present in hearth, table and salt-cellar; a portion of each meal was burned for them.

  5. Why is the Genius's birthday offering a point of disagreement?
    Answer

    Censorinus (2.2–3) says no blood was shed for the Genius on the birthday, but Horace promises his a young pig (Odes 3.17.14–16), though in the Epistles (2.1.143–144) farmers give it flowers and wine.

  6. Name the three forms of marriage with manus, and say why the decline of confarreatio was a religious problem.
    Answer

    Usus, coemptio and confarreatio (Gaius, Institutes 1.110–113). The major flamens and the rex sacrorum had to be born of and married by confarreatio, so when patricians abandoned it the rules for the Flamen Dialis had to be eased in AD 23 (Tacitus, Annals 4.16).

  7. According to Polybius 6.53–54, how were the ancestor masks used at a funeral, and why did he think it mattered?
    Answer

    Men of similar build wore them in the insignia of each ancestor's highest office, rode behind the fasces and sat on ivory chairs at the Rostra while the eulogist recited each ancestor's deeds. Polybius saw it as the finest school of ambition for the young.

  8. What did Tacitus mean by saying Cassius and Brutus outshone everyone at Junia's funeral?
    Answer

    Their masks could not be carried because they were Caesar's murderers, and the gap was conspicuous to everyone (Annals 3.76). The parade of masks was a political statement, and so was an absence from it.

  9. What does the Lemuria rite imply about the family dead?
    Answer

    At midnight the father throws black beans behind him nine times as ransom for himself and his own, clashes bronze and nine times orders the ghosts of his fathers out (Ovid, Fasti 5.429–444). The family dead are dangerous visitors to be paid off and expelled, not welcomed.

  10. On what legal basis was Apuleius prosecuted, and what was his defence?
    Answer

    Rome had no crime of magic as such; the charge ran under Sulla's law on assassins and poisoners. He argued that a magus is properly a Persian priest (Apology 25–26) and gave a philosopher's explanations for the fish, the boy's collapse and the statuette.

Carry forward

  1. The household was a miniature state: the paterfamilias was its priest, the familia including slaves its congregation, and the Lares, Penates, Vesta and Genius its gods. Augustus' crossroads reform shows how easily the domestic model could be enlarged into a cult centred on the ruler.
  2. The Roman dead were a collective divinity, the Manes, to be fed in February and expelled in May; the aristocratic dead, through masks, eulogies and offices, were also a political resource, and Roman nobility was a claim about ancestors displayed in public.
  3. Magic in Rome was defined by harm and social disapproval, not by technique: the curse tablet, the Feralia spell and the amulet use the same means as religion, and the label fell on whatever was foreign, secret or aimed at others.
