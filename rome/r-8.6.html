01Why the East survived

Module 8.5 ends in 476 with the western regalia on their way to Zeno. Nobody at Constantinople thought the Roman empire had ended: its emperor still reigned in Constantine's New Rome (module 8.3) and legislated for the Roman people. The question is why the half that Theodosius left to Arcadius in 395 outlasted the other by nearly a thousand years.

Four reasons do most of the work. Money: Egypt, Syria and Asia Minor were the most urban and productive provinces, and their taxes never stopped; Anastasius (491–518) left 3,200 centenaria of gold in the treasury, 320,000 pounds (Procopius, Secret History 19.7). Geography: the Danube peoples could ravage the Balkans but could not cross the Bosporus or the Hellespont against the fleet, so Anatolia and the East beyond were safe. The capital sat on a peninsula with water on three sides, and the Theodosian land walls, built in 413 and rebuilt after the earthquake of 447, were not broken until 1453; the crusaders of 1204 came over the sea walls. The frontier: Persia was a state that made treaties and kept them for long stretches, not a stream of migrating armies. Politics: the eastern court kept its generals under control. The Gothic general Gainas was driven out in 400 and his men massacred in the city; Leo I had the Alan Aspar, who had made him emperor, killed in 471, and recruited Isaurian highlanders as a counterweight; and in 488 Zeno sent Theoderic's Ostrogoths, the most dangerous army in the Balkans, to take Italy from Odoacer. The East exported its problem to the West.

A. H. M. Jones drew the conclusion in The Later Roman Empire (1964): the East had the same institutions, taxes, Church and army, and survived, so internal weakness cannot by itself explain the western collapse. The West was poorer and more exposed.

02Justinian: reconquest, plague, law and the Great Church

Justinian (527–565), nephew of the guardsman-emperor Justin I, had a programme: to restore the empire to the limits of the old Roman world, purge it of heresy and give it one law. His historian, Belisarius' secretary Procopius of Caesarea, wrote the Wars, the panegyric Buildings and the Secret History, an invective against Justinian and Theodora unpublished in his lifetime: one author praises and damns the same reign.

The reign nearly ended in January 532, when the circus factions joined in the Nika riot, burned the city centre and proclaimed a rival emperor. Procopius has Theodora shame the council out of flight (Wars 1.24.33–37); Belisarius and Mundus then trapped the crowd in the Hippodrome, where more than 30,000 died by Procopius' figure (1.24.54).

Belisarius took Vandal Africa in one campaign, 533–534. Italy took twenty years: Rome fell to Belisarius in 536 and Ravenna in 540, then Totila's Goths retook most of the peninsula until the eunuch Narses destroyed them at Busta Gallorum in 552. A foothold in south-eastern Spain followed. In 568, three years after Justinian's death, the Lombards entered Italy and most of it was lost again.

In 541 bubonic plague reached Pelusium in Egypt, and in 542 Constantinople, where Procopius says the dead reached 5,000 a day and then 10,000 and more (Wars 2.22–23). Ancient DNA from sixth-century Bavarian graves confirms the plague bacillus, Yersinia pestis. The disease returned for two centuries.

Where sources disagree. Kyle Harper (The Fate of Rome, 2017) treats the plague, with the cold decades after 536, as a demographic catastrophe that ended late antique prosperity. Lee Mordechai, Merle Eisenberg and colleagues ("The Justinianic Plague: an inconsequential pandemic?", PNAS, 2019) found no clear break in papyri, laws, coinage or pollen and judge the maximal toll unproven.

The law is outlined here and analysed in module 9.1. Commissions on which the jurist Tribonian sat, leading them from 530, issued a Code of imperial constitutions in 529, revised in 534; the Digest, fifty books of extracts from the classical jurists, in 533; and the Institutes, a students' textbook, the same year. Justinian's later laws, mostly in Greek, were collected as the Novels. The name Corpus iuris civilis was given to the whole only in the sixteenth century.

Hagia Sophia, the church of Holy Wisdom, replaced the basilica burned in the riot. Anthemius of Tralles and Isidore of Miletus, mathematicians and engineers, built it in under six years, and it was dedicated on 27 December 537. Procopius says the dome seemed to hang from heaven on a golden chain (Buildings 1.1). The dome fell in 558 and was rebuilt higher for a second dedication in 562, celebrated in verse by Paul the Silentiary. Justinian's boast that he had outdone Solomon comes from a legendary account of the building written centuries later. Late antique style, the frame for reading it as art, is module 10.3; the closing of the Athenian schools in 529 is in Greek module 7.4.

03Persians and Arabs: the seventh-century amputation

In 602 the Danube army mutinied and the centurion Phocas killed the emperor Maurice and his sons. Khusro II, whom Maurice had restored to the Persian throne, went to war as his avenger. Heraclius, son of the exarch of Africa, overthrew Phocas in 610 and lost almost everything: Antioch in 613, Jerusalem in 614, where the relic of the True Cross was carried off, and Egypt by 619. In 626 Avars and Slavs besieged Constantinople while a Persian army waited across the Bosporus. The walls held. Heraclius, campaigning in the Persian heartland, won near Nineveh in December 627; Khusro was overthrown and killed in 628, and the Cross returned to Jerusalem about 630. George of Pisidia's court poems, the Chronicon Paschale and the ninth-century chronicle of Theophanes tell the story.

Victory lasted less than a decade. Arab armies united by Islam beat the Romans at the Yarmuk in 636; Jerusalem surrendered about 638, and Alexandria was given up in 641–642. Syria, Palestine and Egypt, the richest provinces, were gone for good; Carthage fell in 698. Constantinople survived two Arab attacks, the first in the 660s or 670s (its date is disputed), the second by land and sea in 717–718, broken by Leo III, the Bulgars and the winter. Theophanes credits a Syrian refugee, Kallinikos, with inventing Greek fire.

What survived was a smaller and different state: Anatolia, the capital, footholds in the Balkans, Sicily and parts of Italy. Cities shrank to fortified kernels, coin grew scarce outside the capital, and the field armies were settled in Anatolian districts, the themes, which became the units of government. Greek replaced Latin in administration; a law of 629 is the first to style Heraclius basileus.

Where sources disagree. George Ostrogorsky made the themes a deliberate reform of Heraclius. John Haldon (Byzantium in the Seventh Century, 1990) traced a gradual settlement of the retreating armies from the 640s, and most now follow him.

04Rhomaioi: Romans who spoke Greek

The people of this state called themselves Rhomaioi, Romans, their country Romania and their ruler the emperor of the Romans. Their capital was New Rome: the council of Constantinople in 381 gave its bishop rank next after Rome's, "because it is New Rome" (canon 3). They spoke Greek, but in Christian usage "Hellene" meant a pagan. Latin held on in the army and the law into the sixth century; John Lydus, Justinian's official, lamented its retreat from his department (On the Magistracies). From the seventh century the state was Greek in language and Roman in name, law and self-understanding.

Westerners denied them the name. From the ninth century popes and Frankish writers called them Greeks and their ruler emperor of the Greeks; when Liudprand of Cremona came to Nikephoros II for Otto I in 968, a papal letter so addressing the emperor caused fury at court (Liudprand, Embassy). "Byzantine", from the city's old name, is an early modern coinage, popularised by Hieronymus Wolf in 1557 (module 0.3). Montesquieu and Gibbon added the verdict: for them the eastern empire was Rome's degenerate sequel.

Where sources disagree. George Ostrogorsky (History of the Byzantine State, English 1956) defined Byzantium as a fusion of the Roman state, Greek culture and the Christian faith: a new civilisation built on Rome. Anthony Kaldellis (Romanland, 2019) argues that its people were a nation in the ordinary sense, Romans by language, custom and Orthodoxy, and that "Byzantine" hides a Roman state that never stopped being one.

Basil I, a peasant from the theme of Macedonia who rose as groom and favourite of Michael III, murdered his patron in 867 and founded a dynasty that ruled for almost two centuries. It made legitimacy hereditary: Constantine VII was Porphyrogennetos, born in the palace's porphyry chamber, and the house outlasted regents and usurping generals until 1056. Leo VI issued the Basilika, a Greek recompilation of Justinian's law in sixty books. Constantine VII sponsored the Book of Ceremonies and On the Administration of the Empire, a handbook of foreign peoples for his son. The copying of classical texts in the new minuscule belongs to this age and is told in Greek modules 7.4 and 9.3.

The soldier emperors then attacked. Nikephoros Phokas took Crete in 961 and, as emperor, Antioch in 969, and John I Tzimiskes drove the Rus' out of Bulgaria. Basil II (976–1025) spent decades destroying Tsar Samuel's Bulgarian state and annexed it in 1018. About 988 he sent his sister Anna to marry Vladimir of Kiev, who was baptised; the Varangian Guard and the Christianity of Rus' both date from this alliance. At Basil's death the empire ran from the Danube to the Euphrates and into southern Italy.

Where sources disagree. John Skylitzes, writing late in the eleventh century, says that after Kleidion in 1014 Basil blinded 15,000 Bulgarian prisoners, leaving every hundredth man one eye to lead the rest home; Kekaumenos gives 14,000. Paul Stephenson (The Legend of Basil the Bulgar-Slayer, 2003) argued that the epithet "Bulgar-slayer" and the picture of a war of annihilation took shape only in the later twelfth century.

06Charlemagne and a second Roman empire

In 797 the empress Irene blinded her son Constantine VI and ruled alone. In the West the throne could be called vacant: the Annals of Lorsch say the imperial name had lapsed among the Greeks because a woman held power. On Christmas Day 800, in St Peter's, Pope Leo III crowned the Frankish king Charles and the Romans acclaimed him emperor; Theophanes, at Constantinople, has the pope anoint him with oil from head to foot.

There were now two Roman emperors. Constantinople withheld recognition until 812, when Michael I's envoys at Aachen hailed Charles as basileus; thereafter the eastern rulers styled themselves more insistently basileus ton Rhomaion, emperor of the Romans, and allowed the western ruler an emperor's title without the Romans. The western title lapsed with the Carolingians and was revived for Otto I in 962. Frederick Barbarossa's chancery added "holy" in 1157, the full "Holy Roman Empire" appears from 1254, and the institution ended when Francis II laid down the crown on 6 August 1806, under pressure from Napoleon. The idea of Rome it carried is module 10.4.

Where sources disagree. Einhard says Charles would not have entered the church that day had he known the pope's plan (Life of Charles 28). The Royal Frankish Annals and the papal Liber Pontificalis describe a ceremony with set acclamations by the Romans, and Leo had met Charles at Paderborn in 799. Einhard's story is now usually read as a modesty topos, or as dislike of owing the crown to a pope.

07Manzikert, the Crusades and 1204

On 26 August 1071, near Manzikert north of Lake Van, the Seljuk sultan Alp Arslan defeated and captured Romanos IV Diogenes. Released on terms within days, Romanos found himself deposed and was blinded the next year. Michael Attaleiates, who was present, blames the withdrawal of the rearguard under Andronikos Doukas; Michael Psellos, a Doukas partisan, blames Romanos' rashness. In the civil wars that followed, rival generals hired Turkish bands, and by 1081 Turkmen held most of the Anatolian plateau, with a sultan at Nicaea.

Where sources disagree. Speros Vryonis (The Decline of Medieval Hellenism in Asia Minor, 1971) made the battle the opening of a Turkish conquest that broke Greek society in Anatolia. Jean-Claude Cheynet ("Mantzikert: un désastre militaire?", Byzantion, 1980) argued that the losses were modest and much of the army withdrew intact; the disaster was the decade of civil war after it.

Alexios I Komnenos (1081–1118) rebuilt the state as a family regime and asked the West for soldiers; what came, after Urban II's appeal at Clermont in 1095, was the First Crusade. Its leaders swore oaths to Alexios and returned Nicaea to him, but Bohemond kept Antioch; Anna Komnene's Alexiad records her father's distrust of the Latins (book 10). John II and Manuel I recovered the Anatolian coasts; Manuel's defeat at Myriokephalon in 1176 ended hopes of the plateau, and the massacre of the Latins in Constantinople in 1182 poisoned relations with the Italians.

The Fourth Crusade owed Venice for ships it could not pay for. It took Zara for the Venetians in 1202, then sailed to Constantinople to install the exiled prince Alexios IV, who promised money and Church union he could not deliver. After his murder the crusaders stormed the city on 12 April 1204 and sacked it for three days; Niketas Choniates, who escaped, wrote the lament. A Latin emperor was crowned in Hagia Sophia, Venice took three-eighths of the empire, and the bronze horses of the Hippodrome went to St Mark's, as did the porphyry Tetrarchs of module 8.2. Three Greek states claimed the succession: Nicaea, Epirus, and Trebizond, seized that April by two grandsons of Andronikos I with Georgian help.

08The Palaiologan revival and the last fragments

Nicaea won the contest of the exiles. On 25 July 1261 its general Alexios Strategopoulos found Constantinople weakly held and walked in; Michael VIII Palaiologos entered in August and later blinded the boy Laskarid emperor. The restored empire was a regional power with an imperial capital. Michael kept Charles of Anjou at bay by accepting Church union at Lyons in 1274 and, by his own later claim, by financing the Sicilian Vespers of 1282. Civil wars in 1321–1328 and 1341–1347, the Black Death of 1347 and Stefan Dušan's Serbia reduced it to little more than the capital, Thessalonica and the Morea. The Ottomans crossed to Gallipoli in 1354; Bayezid I blockaded Constantinople until Timur destroyed his army at Ankara in 1402, while Manuel II toured the West for help as far as London. Union with Rome, proclaimed at Florence in 1439, was rejected at home.

The siege and fall of 1453, when Mehmed II took the city on 29 May, are told in module 0.3. The fragments outlasted the capital. Constantine XI's brothers Thomas and Demetrios, despots of the Morea, quarrelled until Mehmed II came in person: Demetrios surrendered Mistra in 1460 and Thomas fled to Italy. Trebizond surrendered in August 1461, and its last emperor, David, was executed in 1463; the Orthodox principality of Theodoro in the Crimea fell in 1475. Thomas' son Andreas sold his claim to the imperial title to Charles VIII of France in 1494 and left it in his will of 1502 to Ferdinand and Isabella.

09The afterlives of the Roman title

Three powers claimed the inheritance. Mehmed II called himself Kayser-i Rum, Caesar of Rome; Kritoboulos of Imbros wrote the history of his reign in the manner of Thucydides, and George of Trebizond told him in a letter that whoever lawfully held Constantinople, the capital of the Roman empire, was emperor of the Romans. The Ottomans called their Balkan provinces Rumelia, the land of the Romans. Moscow claimed the faith and the blood. Ivan III married Thomas' daughter Zoe, renamed Sophia, in 1472 and took the double-headed eagle; Ivan IV was crowned tsar in 1547; Moscow received its own patriarch in 1589. Early in the sixteenth century the monk Philotheus of Pskov wrote to Vasily III that two Romes had fallen, that Moscow was the third, and that there would be no fourth. The Holy Roman Empire carried the western title to 1806. Kaiser and tsar both descend from Caesar: the last tsar fell in 1917, the last Kaisers in 1918, the last sultan in 1922.

Where sources disagree. The charter founding the Moscow patriarchate in 1589 repeats Philotheus' formula, and Hildegard Schaeder (Moskau das dritte Rom, 1929) made it the core of Muscovite political ideology. Marshall Poe ("Moscow, the Third Rome: the origins and transformations of a 'pivotal moment'", 2001) argued that it was a monk's eschatological warning, rare in Muscovite state documents, and became a national creed only in the nineteenth century.

10Self-test

  1. Give four reasons the eastern empire survived the fifth century when the western did not.
    Answer

    A richer, more urban tax base (Anastasius left 320,000 pounds of gold, Procopius, Secret History 19.7); the straits, the fleet and the Theodosian Walls, which kept Danube invaders out of Asia Minor; a Persian neighbour that made treaties; and a court that controlled its generals (Gainas in 400, Aspar in 471) and sent Theoderic to Italy in 488. Jones (1964) concluded that the West's poverty and exposure, not shared institutions, made the difference.

  2. What did the people we call Byzantines call themselves, and where does "Byzantine" come from?
    Answer

    Rhomaioi, Romans, living in Romania under the emperor of the Romans, with New Rome as capital. "Byzantine", from the city's old name, was popularised by Hieronymus Wolf in 1557; Montesquieu and Gibbon then treated the empire as Rome's degenerate sequel.

  3. Why is Procopius both indispensable and treacherous as a witness to Justinian?
    Answer

    He was Belisarius' secretary and is the main narrative source for the wars, the Nika riot, the plague and the buildings. But the same man wrote the panegyric Buildings and the invective Secret History, so each portrait has to be tested against the others and against documents.

  4. What did the reconquest win, and how much of it lasted?
    Answer

    Vandal Africa in 533–534, held until Carthage fell in 698; Italy after twenty years of war, ending with Narses' victory in 552, mostly lost to the Lombards from 568; and a foothold in south-eastern Spain.

  5. What is the dispute over the Justinianic plague?
    Answer

    Procopius reports 5,000 and then 10,000 dead a day at Constantinople (Wars 2.22–23), and DNA confirms Yersinia pestis. Harper (2017) makes it a demographic catastrophe; Mordechai, Eisenberg and colleagues (2019) found no clear break in papyri, laws, coinage or pollen and call the maximal toll unproven.

  6. What are the parts of Justinian's law, and which module treats them?
    Answer

    The Code (529, revised 534), the Digest of the classical jurists (533), the Institutes (533) and the later Novels, compiled under Tribonian; the name Corpus iuris civilis is sixteenth-century. Module 9.1 analyses the law.

  7. How did the seventh century change the empire?
    Answer

    After the Persian war and Heraclius' victory of 627, the Arabs took Syria, Palestine and Egypt (Yarmuk 636, Alexandria 641–642) and later Africa. The state shrank to Anatolia, the capital and western footholds, its cities contracted, Greek became the language of government, and the armies settled in the themes, whether by Heraclius' reform (Ostrogorsky) or gradually from the 640s (Haldon).

  8. Why did Constantinople object to Charlemagne's coronation, and how was the quarrel settled?
    Answer

    There could be only one Roman emperor, and the East had one, even if Irene's rule let westerners call the throne vacant. In 812 eastern envoys hailed Charles as basileus at Aachen, while the eastern rulers kept "emperor of the Romans" for themselves.

  9. Was Manzikert the disaster that lost Anatolia?
    Answer

    It ended in Romanos IV's capture, which Attaleiates blames on Andronikos Doukas' withdrawal and Psellos on Romanos' rashness. Cheynet (1980) argued the losses were modest and that the decade of civil war, in which rival generals hired Turks, lost the plateau; Vryonis (1971) stressed the conquest the battle opened.

  10. Name three claimants to the Roman title after 1453 and the right each claimed.
    Answer

    The Ottoman sultans, by possession of Constantinople (Kayser-i Rum; George of Trebizond's letter to Mehmed II); Moscow, by Orthodoxy and Ivan III's marriage to Sophia Palaiologina, with Philotheus' Third Rome; and the Holy Roman Empire, by papal coronation since 800 and 962, until 1806.

11Carry forward

  1. The Roman state did not end in 476: it continued at Constantinople with Roman law, the Roman name and an unbroken imperial succession until 1453. The East's survival is the control case for every explanation of the western fall.
  2. Medieval Rome survived by money, walls and diplomacy more than by conquest: a tax base, an impregnable capital and the habit of buying, dividing and redirecting enemies. Each time the tax base was lost (the seventh century, after Manzikert, after 1204), the empire shrank to what its capital could defend.
  3. From 800 the Roman title had rival claimants, each by a different right: papal coronation in the West, conquest of the capital for the Ottomans, Orthodox faith and dynastic marriage for Moscow. Ask of any later "Rome" which of these rights it claims.
