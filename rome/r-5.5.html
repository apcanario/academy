After the Ides: the amnesty, the will and the funeral

Module 5.4 ends at the murder. For 44–30 the narrative rests on Appian, Civil Wars 3–5 (to 35; his Egyptian books are lost), Cassius Dio 44–51, Plutarch's Antony, Brutus and Cicero, Suetonius' Augustus and Velleius 2.58–87, all written under the victor's successors or long after. Cicero's letters and speeches, which stop in mid-43, are the only contemporary voice not writing for the winner.

The conspirators had planned the killing, not the day after. Antony, as consul, held Caesar's papers and money, and Lepidus had a legion on the Tiber Island (Appian 2.118). On 17 March the Senate, meeting in the temple of Tellus, compromised: the assassins were amnestied, and Caesar's acts, on which many senators' offices and provinces depended, were confirmed. Cicero claimed the amnesty as his, an Athenian precedent (Philippics 1.1; Greek module 6.3 covers the amnesty of 403). The price was the will and a public funeral. The will made Gaius Octavius, Caesar's great-nephew, heir to three quarters of the estate and adopted him; it left the people gardens across the Tiber and 300 sesterces a man (Suetonius, Julius 83). At the funeral the crowd burned the body in the Forum and went for the conspirators' houses with torches. What Antony said there differs between Appian 2.143–147 and Suetonius, Julius 84; deep dive module 11.7 compares the versions. Within a month Brutus and Cassius had left Rome for good.

Caesar's heir and Cicero's war: Octavian and Mutina

Antony had the dictatorship abolished for ever (Cicero, Philippics 1.3), but enacted measures he claimed to find in Caesar's papers and drew on Caesar's reserve in the temple of Ops, which Cicero puts at 700 million sesterces (Philippics 2.93). A plebiscite in June gave him the two Gauls in place of Macedonia; Brutus and Cassius were fobbed off with grain commissions and left Italy in August.

Octavius, eighteen and studying at Apollonia, came to Italy, accepted the inheritance and called himself Gaius Julius Caesar; "Octavian" renders the Octavianus of Cicero's letters (Letters to Atticus 15.12.2), a name he never used. Antony would not release Caesar's money, so the heir paid the people's legacy himself and in July held Caesar's victory games, when a comet appeared that the people, Augustus later wrote, took for Caesar's soul received among the gods (Pliny, Natural History 2.93–94). In the autumn he did what no private citizen could lawfully do: he recruited Caesar's veterans in Campania at 500 denarii a man (Cicero, Letters to Atticus 16.8), and two of Antony's legions, the Martian and the Fourth, came over to him. The Res Gestae opens with it: at nineteen, "by private decision and at private expense", he raised the army that freed the Republic (1.1).

Cicero made the young man his instrument. His fourteen Philippics, from September 44 to April 43 (deep dive module 11.5 treats them as oratory), had one political aim: Antony declared a public enemy and Octavian's army made the Senate's. In January 43 Octavian received propraetorian command and joined the consuls Hirtius and Pansa to relieve Decimus Brutus, besieged by Antony in Mutina. Antony was beaten in April at Forum Gallorum and at Mutina, but both consuls died; Tacitus, Annals 1.10, records the rumour that Octavian arranged it. The Senate then slighted him, and Cicero's quip reached him: the young man was to be praised, honoured and tollendum, "raised up" or "removed" (Decimus Brutus in Cicero, Letters to Friends 11.20.1). Antony, joined in Gaul by Lepidus, Plancus and Pollio, recovered; Decimus was killed in flight. Octavian marched on Rome and was made consul on 19 August 43, aged nineteen (deep dive module 11.2 takes the march and its army).

The triumvirate and the proscriptions

That autumn Antony, Lepidus and Octavian met on a river islet between Mutina and Bononia and divided the western provinces (Appian 4.2). The lex Titia of 27 November made them triumviri rei publicae constituendae, three men for setting the state in order, for five years, with power to legislate and appoint magistrates. Unlike the private pact of 60 (module 5.3), this was an office.

The proscription edict, which Appian gives in Greek (4.8–11), argued that killing was safer than Caesar's mercy, which had cost him his life; in practice the lists settled hatreds and raised money for the legions. Heads carried a price, slaves who killed their masters were promised freedom, and estates went to auction. When money still fell short, the triumvirs taxed the 1,400 richest women until Hortensia, the orator's daughter, protested in the Forum and the levy was cut (Appian 4.32–34; deep dive module 11.4).

Cicero headed Antony's list; Octavian, Plutarch says, held out for him for two days (Cicero 46). On 7 December 43 he was overtaken near Formiae and put his head out of his litter to the sword (Plutarch, Cicero 48). Antony had the head and the writing hands fixed to the Rostra. The man who had armed Octavian against Antony died with Octavian's consent.

Where sources disagree. Appian 4.5 gives about 300 senators and 2,000 equestrians proscribed; Livy, Periochae 120, 130 senators; Plutarch a little over 200 (Cicero 46) or 300 (Antony 20). The lists grew in stages, and some totals may include those who escaped. Blame is disputed too: Velleius 2.66 lays Cicero's death on Antony alone, while Suetonius, Augustus 27, says Octavian resisted at first but, once begun, was harsher than either colleague.

Philippi, 42

Brutus and Cassius had taken over the eastern provinces and their armies. In October 42 they met Antony and Octavian on the Via Egnatia at Philippi: nineteen legions on each side by Appian's count, with 20,000 cavalry against the triumvirs' 13,000 (4.108). In the first battle Brutus stormed Octavian's camp while Antony took Cassius', and Cassius, believing all lost, had his freedman kill him (Plutarch, Brutus 43). Some three weeks later Brutus offered battle, lost and fell on his sword. Antony covered the body with his own cloak (Plutarch, Antony 22); Octavian sent the head to Rome to be laid at Caesar's statue (Suetonius, Augustus 13). Philippi made Antony the first soldier of the Roman world.

Where sources disagree. Where was Octavian when his camp fell? Pliny, Natural History 7.148, lists among his humiliations three days hiding sick in a marsh, as Agrippa and Maecenas admitted. His own memoirs, cited by Plutarch, Brutus 41, and followed by Suetonius, Augustus 91, said his doctor Artorius dreamed a warning and had him carried out just before the camp was taken. The dream is the official version; an admission from Octavian's own friends is harder to dismiss.

Perusia, Brundisium and Sextus Pompey

Antony took the East and its revenues; Octavian took the task of settling veterans on land confiscated from eighteen Italian towns (Appian 4.3), whose dispossessed owners haunt Virgil's first and ninth Eclogues. Antony's brother Lucius, consul in 41, and his wife Fulvia championed the dispossessed and Antony's interest, and Octavian starved them out of Perusia in the winter of 41–40. Sling bullets from the siege carry obscenities about Fulvia and Octavian (CIL XI 6721). Fulvia died in Greece in 40 (deep dive module 11.4 takes her further).

Where sources disagree. Suetonius, Augustus 15, reports that "some write" that 300 men of both orders were sacrificed at an altar of the deified Julius on the Ides of March 40; Dio 48.14 tells the same story. Appian 5.48–49 has Octavian pardon most of the citizens and execute the councillors, and Velleius 2.74 blames the soldiers' anger, not their commander. Many modern historians treat the "Perusine altars" as hostile tradition, though executions there certainly were.

The soldiers would not fight each other, and the Treaty of Brundisium in autumn 40 gave Antony the East, Octavian the West and Lepidus Africa; Antony married Octavian's sister Octavia. Virgil's fourth Eclogue, with its child of a returning golden age, belongs to that hopeful season.

Sextus Pompey, Pompey's younger son, held Sicily with a fleet, sheltered the proscribed and runaway slaves, and starved Rome of grain. Bought off at Misenum in 39, he was at war again in 38 and beat Octavian at sea, until Agrippa built and trained a new navy and destroyed him at Naulochus on 3 September 36; Antony had lent ships at Tarentum in 37, when the triumvirate was renewed for five years. Lepidus then claimed Sicily, his legions deserted, and he was stripped of power but kept the chief priesthood (Appian 5.123–126). The Res Gestae calls the war a clearing of the seas of pirates and boasts of returning some 30,000 slaves to their owners for punishment (25.1).

Antony in the East: Cleopatra, Parthia and the Donations

Cleopatra VII, queen since 51, was the last of a Macedonian dynasty that ruled Egypt as Greek kings and as pharaohs; Greek module 7.1 describes that double kingship and Greek module 7.3 her alliance with Caesar. Summoned to Tarsus in 41, she came up the Cydnus as Aphrodite visiting Dionysus (Plutarch, Antony 26), and Antony wintered with her in Alexandria. In 37/36 Antony acknowledged their twins and gave Cleopatra Phoenicia, Coele-Syria, Cyprus and much of Cilicia (Plutarch, Antony 36), much of what Ptolemy II had held. Her aim was any Ptolemy's, the old kingdom restored through a Roman protector; Romans saw provinces handed to a foreign mistress.

His legate Ventidius Bassus had thrown back a Parthian invasion of Syria (Plutarch, Antony 33–34); in 36 Antony invaded Media through Armenia with, by Plutarch's count, 60,000 Roman infantry besides cavalry and allies (Antony 37). The siege train was destroyed, the siege of Phraaspa failed, and the retreat took twenty-seven days; Plutarch counts 20,000 infantry and 4,000 cavalry lost, more than half to disease (Antony 50). Octavia came east in 35 with troops and supplies; Antony told her to stay in Athens, and she went home (Antony 53). In 34, in the Gymnasium of Alexandria, came the Donations: Cleopatra was proclaimed queen of Egypt, Cyprus, Libya and Coele-Syria with Caesarion, called Caesar's son, as co-ruler; Armenia, Media and Parthia, not yet conquered, went to Alexander Helios, Phoenicia, Syria and Cilicia to the infant Ptolemy (Plutarch, Antony 54). Proclaiming Caesarion struck at the one asset Octavian had that Antony lacked: Caesar's name.

Cleopatra is the clearest case of the victor writing the record. The Roman Cleopatra is Egyptian, though she was a Macedonian Greek; drunk, lustful, a sorceress who drugged Antony, set on ruling from the Capitol (Dio 50.5). Plutarch, less hostile, says her beauty was not beyond compare but her conversation irresistible, and that she was the first of her line to learn Egyptian (27). Her own hand may survive once: a papyrus of 33 granting tax privileges to Antony's general Publius Canidius ends with the Greek for "let it be done", which its editor, Peter van Minnen (2000), took to be her autograph. Maria Wyke (The Roman Mistress, 2002) reads the Augustan Cleopatra as a construction of Roman ideas about women and the East; Duane Roller (Cleopatra: A Biography, 2010) reconstructs a capable Hellenistic ruler.

The war of words, the will and the declaration

From 33 the two men fought with pamphlets and letters. Octavian's charges survive best: Antony was the drugged slave of an Egyptian woman, giving away Roman lands (Plutarch, Antony 60). Antony's survive in Suetonius: Octavian's ancestry was base and his adoption bought with his body (Augustus 2, 68). Antony's answer to the moralising is quoted there too: what had changed Octavian, that Antony slept with the queen? "She is my wife", or, as some editors punctuate it, "Is she my wife?" (Augustus 69).

In 32 both consuls were Antonians; after a clash in the Senate they and many senators left for Antony (Dio 50.2), though Octavian later claimed more than 700 senators under his own standards (Res Gestae 25.3). The defectors Plancus and Titius revealed that Antony's will was lodged with the Vestals; Octavian seized it and read to the Senate the clause that Antony, even if he died in Rome, was to be buried beside Cleopatra in Alexandria (Plutarch, Antony 58). All Italy swore an oath to Octavian "of its own accord" (Res Gestae 25.2). The Senate stripped Antony of the consulship he was to hold in 31 and declared war on Cleopatra alone, Octavian hurling the fetial spear at the temple of Bellona (Dio 50.4). A war on a foreign queen was not a civil war; if Antony stood by her, he made himself the enemy.

Where sources disagree. Plutarch, Antony 58–59, says senators thought it monstrous to call a living man to account for his will, and that most of the further charges brought by Calvisius were thought false. Dio 50.3 reports the will's contents, adding the recognition of Caesarion and legacies to Cleopatra's children, without doubt. Ronald Syme (The Roman Revolution, 1939) found the discovery suspiciously opportune; J. R. Johnson (1978) argued that the will was genuine and that its legal flaws tell for its authenticity.

Actium and Alexandria, 31–30

Plutarch, Antony 61, gives Antony 500 warships, 100,000 infantry and 12,000 cavalry against Octavian's 250 warships and 80,000 infantry; Orosius 6.19 gives Antony 170 ships against Octavian's 230. In spring 31 Agrippa took Methone and cut the supply route from Egypt, Octavian camped north of the strait at Actium, and Antony's fleet was penned in the Ambracian Gulf. Through the summer, blockade, malaria and desertion did the work (Plutarch, Antony 63; Dio 50.13).

On 2 September Antony brought the fleet out, having burned all the Egyptian ships but sixty and ordered the sails taken aboard, on the pretext, Plutarch says, that no fleeing enemy must escape (Antony 64). In the afternoon Cleopatra's sixty ships hoisted sail and broke through, and Antony followed with a handful of companions (Antony 66). The rest fought on or surrendered; Augustus' memoirs, cited by Plutarch, gave not more than 5,000 dead and 300 ships captured (Antony 68). The army surrendered within a week. Octavian founded Nicopolis, Victory City, by his camp, with a memorial set with captured rams whose sockets, studied by William Murray and Photios Petsas (1989), show ships of very large classes.

Where sources disagree. Was Actium a battle lost or an escape achieved? Plutarch, Antony 66, and Velleius 2.85 tell of flight: Cleopatra lost her nerve, and Antony deserted men still dying for him. Dio 50.15 says the pair had already resolved to break out for Egypt, and the sails point the same way; most modern accounts accept a planned breakout that half succeeded, while Carsten Lange (2011) argues that Antony fought to win. Tarn's view that the fleet hardly fought is in Greek module 7.3.

In summer 30 Octavian advanced on Egypt. On 1 August, as his fleet and cavalry deserted, Antony stabbed himself and died in Cleopatra's arms (Plutarch, Antony 76–77). She was taken alive and died about ten days later, by asp or poison: Plutarch, Antony 86, admits that no one knew (Greek module 7.3). Caesarion was killed on the advice that too many Caesars was not good (Antony 81). Egypt was annexed under an equestrian prefect and closed to senators without leave (Tacitus, Annals 2.59): "I added Egypt to the empire of the Roman people" (Res Gestae 27.1). What Octavian built on the victory is module 6.1.

The poets' Actium

The poets made a blockade and a breakout into the battle of the age. Horace's ninth Epode, probably of 31, is close to the event: Roman soldiers serving a woman and her wrinkled eunuchs, a mosquito net among the standards. Odes 1.37, written after her death, opens nunc est bibendum, "now we must drink". The queen is unnamed and Antony absent; "scarcely one ship" escapes the flames (13), against Plutarch's sixty, and Caesar pursues her like a hawk after doves (17–20), though he reached Egypt only the next summer. Then the poem turns: the "deadly monster" (21), "no humble woman" (32), chooses death over a triumph.

Virgil, Aeneid 8.675–713, sets Actium at the centre of Aeneas' shield: Augustus leads the Italians with the Senate, the people and the great gods (678–679), Antony brings the wealth of the East, "and, shame, his Egyptian wife follows" (688), and Actian Apollo's bow routs Egypt's monstrous gods (698–706). Deep dive module 12.3 reads the shield in its book. Propertius makes Cleopatra the harlot queen of Canopus (3.11) and in 4.6 turns the battle into a hymn for Palatine Apollo. Module 6.3 sets these poets in their Augustan context.

The pattern is plain. A civil war becomes a foreign war, as the declaration of 32 had made it; Antony shrinks or vanishes; a campaign decided by Agrippa's seamanship, disease and desertion becomes a clash of gods, West against East. The poets gave Octavian's official version the form in which it would be remembered.

Self-test

  1. What did the Senate decide on 17 March 44, and why did the compromise fail to protect the conspirators?
    Answer

    An amnesty for the assassins and confirmation of Caesar's acts (Cicero, Philippics 1.1), at the price of reading the will and holding a public funeral. The will's gifts to the people (Suetonius, Julius 83) and the funeral turned the city against the conspirators, who left Rome within a month.

  2. How did a nineteen-year-old with no office come to command an army, and how was it made legal?
    Answer

    Caesar's will adopted him; he recruited Caesar's veterans in Campania at 500 denarii a man (Cicero, Letters to Atticus 16.8) and won over two of Antony's legions. In January 43, at Cicero's urging, the Senate gave him propraetorian command.

  3. What was the political aim of Cicero's Philippics, and how did the policy end?
    Answer

    To have Antony declared a public enemy and use Octavian's army as the Senate's weapon. After Mutina both consuls were dead, the Senate slighted Octavian, he took the consulship by force and joined Antony and Lepidus, and Cicero was proscribed and killed on 7 December 43.

  4. How did the triumvirate of 43 differ from the alliance of 60?
    Answer

    It was a magistracy created by law, the lex Titia of 27 November 43, for five years, with power to legislate and appoint magistrates; the alliance of 60 was a private agreement with no legal standing.

  5. Why do the ancient totals for the proscribed differ so widely?
    Answer

    Appian 4.5 gives about 300 senators and 2,000 equestrians, Livy, Periochae 120, 130 senators, Plutarch 200 or 300. The lists were issued in stages, many escaped, and the writers may be counting the listed, the killed or both.

  6. Why was Philippi Antony's victory, and how did Augustus' memoirs handle his own part?
    Answer

    Antony took Cassius' camp in the first battle and won the second, while Octavian's camp fell to Brutus. The memoirs said his doctor Artorius' dream had him carried out of the camp in time (Plutarch, Brutus 41); Pliny 7.148 has him hiding sick in a marsh for three days.

  7. What was at stake in the war with Sextus Pompey, and how does the Res Gestae describe it?
    Answer

    Rome's grain supply and control of the western seas; Agrippa's fleet won it at Naulochus in 36, and Lepidus was removed in the aftermath. The Res Gestae 25.1 calls it a war on pirates and boasts of returning some 30,000 slaves to their owners for punishment.

  8. What happened at the Donations of Alexandria, and why was the treatment of Caesarion so provocative?
    Answer

    In 34 Antony proclaimed Cleopatra queen of Egypt, Cyprus, Libya and Coele-Syria with Caesarion as co-ruler, and gave eastern kingdoms, some unconquered, to her sons by him (Plutarch, Antony 54). Presenting Caesarion as Caesar's son challenged Octavian's standing as Caesar's heir.

  9. Why did Rome declare war on Cleopatra alone in 32, and what prepared opinion for it?
    Answer

    A war on a foreign queen was not a civil war, and it left Antony to make himself the enemy by standing with her. The will's burial clause, read to the Senate (Plutarch, Antony 58), and the oath of Italy (Res Gestae 25.2) prepared it; Octavian declared it by the fetial rite (Dio 50.4).

  10. Give two ways Horace, Odes 1.37, departs from the historical Actium, and say what it concedes to Cleopatra.
    Answer

    It has "scarcely one ship" escape where Plutarch, Antony 66, has sixty; it has Caesar pursue her at once, though he reached Egypt only in 30; and it leaves Antony out. It concedes her a defiant death: "no humble woman", she refuses to be led in triumph.

Carry forward

  1. Between 44 and 30 Caesar's name was the decisive political asset: Octavian's adoption, the comet, the veterans' loyalty and the rivalry with Caesarion show legitimacy passing by inheritance and army rather than by office.
  2. The triumvirate was a dictatorship by commission, and its instruments (private armies, proscriptions, confiscations, the oath of Italy) are what the Principate was built from; the Res Gestae rewrites each of them as service to the Republic.
  3. Actium as Octavian framed it, a foreign war fought by Italy and its gods against an Eastern queen, became the founding myth of the new order; read every Augustan Cleopatra and Antony against the declaration of 32 that created it.
