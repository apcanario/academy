01Power without office: what a woman could do

Module 9.3 sets out the law: no vote, no office, no jury, no pleading for others after Gaia Afrania; a guardian's authority for a will or a sale of land; the Voconian law against making a rich man's daughter his heir. It also gives the practice that ran ahead of the law, above all marriage without manus, under which a wife stayed in her father's family and, once he was dead, owned property in her own right. This module starts from there. The women of the late Republic acted through four channels the law did not close.

  • Marriage. Alliances between great houses were made and unmade by marriage and divorce, and a woman who married three times, as Fulvia did, carried her connections from one husband to the next. Pompey's marriage to Caesar's daughter Julia sealed the compact of 59 (module 5.4).
  • Money. Women of the nobility held fortunes and lent them. Clodia's gold is the pivot of the case against Caelius; Terentia's estates kept Cicero's household afloat during his exile.
  • The house. Politics was done in houses: the morning reception, the dinner, the family council. A widow with a house of her own, such as Servilia, could summon senators to it.
  • Grief and memory. Funerals and mourning were public acts. Catulus gave the first funeral speech for a woman, his mother Popilia (Cicero, On the Orator 2.44), and Caesar used his aunt Julia's in 69 to show Marius' images (module 5.3).

Valerius Maximus collects three women who spoke in court on their own account or for others: Maesia of Sentinum, Afrania and Hortensia (8.3.1–3). He calls them exceptions, and his opening sentence treats them as women whom neither their nature nor the matron's modesty could keep silent. Suzanne Dixon ("A family business: women's role in patronage and politics at Rome, 80–44 BC", Classica et Mediaevalia 1983) read Cicero's letters as showing the ordinary case: wives and mothers working the family's network of favours, marriages and money, as partners in a family enterprise rather than as politicians in their own name.

02Cornelia: the mother of the Gracchi, made and remade

Module 5.1 gives Cornelia's family, her refusal of a king, Cicero's verdict on her sons' upbringing (Brutus 211) and the letter to Gaius preserved among the fragments of Nepos, with the dispute over its authenticity. What it leaves to this module is how Rome used her.

Three stories made her an exemplar. Gaius Gracchus withdrew a bill aimed at Octavius, the tribune his brother had deposed, saying he spared him at his mother's request; the people approved and honoured her (Plutarch, Gaius Gracchus 4.2–3). After Gaius' death she lived at Misenum, received Greeks and men of letters, exchanged gifts with kings, and spoke of her father Africanus and of her sons' deaths without grief or tears, as if of men of early times (Gaius Gracchus 19). And when a Campanian lady showed off her jewellery, Cornelia kept her talking until her sons came home from school, then said that these were her ornaments (Valerius Maximus 4.4 preface, from Pomponius Rufus). Quintilian adds that her very learned speech came down to later generations in her letters and contributed much to her sons' eloquence (1.1.6).

The statue is the political part. Plutarch says the people later set up a bronze statue of her inscribed "Cornelia, mother of the Gracchi" (Gaius Gracchus 4.3). Pliny saw it: seated, with strapless sandals, placed first in the public portico of Metellus and in his day among the works of Octavia (Natural History 34.31). A marble base found in 1878 in the area of the portico of Octavia and now in the Capitoline Museums reads Cornelia Africani f. Gracchorum (CIL VI 10043 = 31610; ILS 68). It is often called the first public statue at Rome of a Roman woman of the historical period.

Where sources disagree. Plutarch's inscription names Cornelia only as the Gracchi's mother; the surviving base puts her father first. Filippo Coarelli ("La statue de Cornélie, mère des Gracques, et la crise politique à Rome au temps de Saturninus", 1978) dated the statue to the popular politics of about 100 BC and argued that the surviving base is the original, its first text erased and the present one cut under Augustus, when the statue stood among Octavia's works. Mika Kajava ("Cornelia Africani f. Gracchorum", Arctos 1989) accepted the Augustan lettering but thought a date near 121 BC possible for the statue, and doubted that its first text can be recovered from Plutarch. Who first erected the statue, and when, is uncertain; that its text was changed is not.

Either way the monument was rewritten. Emily Hemelrijk (Matrona Docta, 1999) concluded that the Cornelia we know is to a high degree a creation of later times: the learned widow at Misenum and the mother whose jewels were her sons are the Cornelia that imperial writers needed, and the woman who may have argued politics with Gaius is visible only through a letter whose authenticity module 5.1 leaves open.

03Sempronia in Sallust

Sallust's Catiline, written about 42–41, sets a woman beside the conspirators. Catiline, he says, recruited women who had paid for their extravagance with their bodies and, as age cut their income, ran into debt; through them he hoped to stir the city slaves, fire Rome, and win their husbands over or kill them (24.3–4). Among them was Sempronia, who had often committed crimes of a man's daring (25.1). The portrait that follows is a paradox. She was well born, beautiful, fortunate in husband and children; learned in Greek and Latin literature; she played the lyre and danced more elegantly than an honest woman needs to. She valued nothing less than modesty and chastity; it was hard to tell whether she spared her money or her reputation less; she sought men more often than she was sought. She had broken her word, denied a debt on oath and been party to murder. Yet her gifts were real: she could write verse, raise a laugh, and talk modestly, tenderly or wantonly (25.2–5).

She does almost nothing in the story. Sallust's only later mention is that late in 63 the envoys of the Allobroges were brought to the house of Decimus Brutus, near the Forum, which was not foreign to the plot because of Sempronia, Brutus being away from Rome (40.5). Her husband is usually identified as Decimus Junius Brutus, consul in 77, which would make her the mother or stepmother of Decimus Brutus who stabbed Caesar in 44 (deep dive module 11.7). The woman who in fact affected the plot was another: Fulvia, a noblewoman and the mistress of the conspirator Quintus Curius, who passed on what Curius told her and brought the warning that saved Cicero's life (23.3–4, 26.3, 28.2; module 5.3).

Where sources disagree. Why is Sempronia there? Ronald Syme (Sallust, 1964) thought she was welcome to fill space, lend variety and show a female counterpart to Catiline, and that naming her house was meant to put the reader in mind of Decimus Brutus, not in a friendly way. John Ramsey's commentary (2007) treats the passage as a digression that does not advance the narrative. Barbara Weiden Boyd ("Virtus Effeminata and Sallust's Sempronia", 1987) argued instead that it is thematically and structurally central, and Matthew Loar (Histos, 2019) pairs her with Curius as emblems of decayed noble houses. No other source mentions her part in the plot.

The portrait's vocabulary is the point. Audacia, lubido, luxuria and inopia are the words Sallust uses of the conspirators and of the Rome that bred them; Sempronia shows the disease reaching the women of the nobility, and her talents make it worse, because cultivation without restraint is exactly what Sallust says Rome had become.

04Clodia Metelli, the Pro Caelio and Lesbia

Clodia, born about 95, was one of the three sisters of Publius Clodius (deep dive module 11.3) and the wife of her cousin Quintus Metellus Celer, consul in 60, who died in 59. Cicero, the only contemporary witness to her life, first appears in his letters as an interested observer, calling her by Homer's epithet for Hera, the ox-eyed, and so making her brother Jupiter (Letters to Atticus 2.9.1). Plutarch adds that Terentia was jealous of her, believing that Clodia wanted to marry Cicero, and that this was one reason Cicero testified against Clodius after the Bona Dea affair (Cicero 29).

In April 56, during the Megalesian games, Marcus Caelius Rufus, Cicero's former pupil, was tried for violence. Among the charges were the murder of the Alexandrian envoy Dio and an attempt to poison Clodia, from whom, the prosecution said, he had taken gold to pay for the murder. Cicero's Pro Caelio turned the trial into a trial of Clodia. He calls up her ancestor Appius Claudius the Blind from the dead to rebuke her (33–34), then lets her brother speak in the modern idiom of the smart set (36). He calls her the Medea of the Palatine (18), hints that she poisoned her husband (59–60), and plays on the nickname quadrantaria, the woman who costs a quarter-as (62); Plutarch explains it as the copper coins a lover passed off on her as silver (Cicero 29). Caelius was acquitted. Clodia vanishes from the record until 45, when Cicero, looking for a site for Tullia's shrine, thought of buying her gardens by the Tiber (Letters to Atticus, book 12).

The speech is advocacy. It needed the jury to believe that a widow's revenge, not Caelius' guilt, had brought the case, and it is the source of nearly everything later said about her.

Catullus' Lesbia is the second problem. Module 5.6 gives the poems and Apuleius' statement that Lesbia's real name was Clodia (Apology 10). The case for Clodia Metelli rests on more: poem 79, "Lesbius is pretty", reads as a pun on Pulcher, her brother's family name; poem 58 tells a Caelius what Lesbia has become, and poem 77 rebukes a Rufus who stole the poet's love; and the woman of Cicero's speech seems made for the woman of the poems.

Where sources disagree. Ludwig Schwabe in 1862 made Lesbia Clodia Metelli, and most twentieth-century classicists followed him. T. P. Wiseman (Catullan Questions, 1969) objected that the datable poems cluster in the mid-50s, after Celer's death, and that Apuleius' Clodia could be any of the three sisters, Metellus' widow the least likely; poem 83, where Lesbia's husband is present, sits ill with a widow. In Catullan Questions Revisited (2023) he abandoned the sisters too: Catullus calls her a puella, and she may have been a young Clodia, a daughter of Appius Claudius, consul in 54. Caelius and Rufus were common names; the identification remains possible and unproved.

05Servilia and her family councils

Servilia was Cato's half-sister, the widow of Marcus Brutus, whom Pompey had killed in 77, and of Decimus Silanus, consul in 62. Her son was Marcus Brutus; her daughters married Lepidus, Cassius and Publius Servilius Isauricus. She was the great love of Caesar's life. Suetonius says Caesar loved her above all others, bought her a pearl for six million sesterces, and in the civil war let her buy confiscated estates at auction for next to nothing, which drew Cicero's pun that the price was a third off, Tertia being her daughter (Julius 50.2). Plutarch tells how, during the debate on the Catilinarians in 63, a note was brought to Caesar; Cato, suspecting a plot, demanded that it be read, and Caesar handed it to him: a love letter from Cato's own sister (Cato Minor 24; Brutus 5).

Where sources disagree. Plutarch (Brutus 5) reports that Caesar believed Brutus to be his son, the affair having been at its height when Brutus was born. Brutus was born in 85, when Caesar was fifteen, and the story is generally rejected as a back-projection from the Ides. Syme ("No Son for Caesar?", Historia 1980) suggested that if any Brutus passed as Caesar's son it was Decimus. What Caesar said as he died is deep dive module 11.7's question.

After the Ides the letters show her in charge. On 8 June 44, at Antium, Brutus asked Cicero's advice before a gathering that included Servilia, his wife Porcia, Cassius' wife Tertulla and Favonius, with Cassius coming in later. When Cicero said they should have summoned the Senate in March, Servilia broke in that she had never heard such a thing; and she promised to have the grain commission, the insulting post the Senate meant to give Brutus and Cassius, removed from the decree (Letters to Atticus 15.11). In July 43 Cicero was summoned to her house with Casca, Labeo and Scaptius to decide whether Brutus should bring his army back to Italy; she put the question, Cicero says, and asked his view (Letters to Brutus 1.18). Susan Treggiari's life (Servilia and Her Family, 2019) builds on these councils. Deep dive module 11.5 reads the letters as a collection; here they show a woman who held no office presiding over the inner council of the Liberators, and promising to change a decree of the Senate.

06Fulvia: three husbands, Perusia and the sling bullets

Fulvia was married three times to men who made popular politics: Clodius, killed in January 52; Curio, killed in Africa in 49; and Antony. In 52 she and her mother laid out Clodius' body and lamented over it, and testified at Milo's trial (Asconius, on Cicero's Pro Milone; the trial and the burning of the Senate house are deep dive module 11.3). Her daughter by Clodius, Claudia, was briefly married to Octavian in 43, and sent back untouched when he quarrelled with her mother (Suetonius, Augustus 62.1).

Every source on her is hostile. In the Philippics Cicero has centurions butchered at Brundisium before the eyes of Antony's wife, whom he had taken with him to the army (5.22; again 3.4, 13.18). Plutarch makes her a woman who cared nothing for spinning or housekeeping, who wished not to rule a private man but to rule a ruler and command a commander, so that Cleopatra owed her a fee for teaching Antony to obey women (Antony 10). Dio has her take Cicero's head on her knees, pull out the tongue and pierce it with her hairpins (47.8.4). In the Perusine war she was, for Velleius, a woman with nothing womanish about her but her body (2.74.3); for Dio she girded on a sword at Praeneste, gave out the watchword and harangued the soldiers (48.10).

The war itself is module 5.5's. Its lead sling bullets, from the siege lines round Perusia in 41–40, carry the soldiers' own voices (CIL XI 6721). Octavian's men aimed at Fulvia's private parts and bade bald Lucius Antonius and Fulvia bend over; Lucius' men called Octavian a passive homosexual. Martial preserves six lines he says Octavian wrote: because Antony sleeps with Glaphyra, Fulvia demands that Octavian sleep with her, or fight (11.20). Judith Hallett ("Perusinae glandes and the changing image of Augustus", American Journal of Ancient History 1977) set the bullets and the epigram side by side as the language of the war before the victor tidied it. Fulvia left Italy after the surrender and died at Sicyon in 40 on her way to Antony, who was spared a quarrel with Octavian by her death (Plutarch, Antony 30).

Where sources disagree. Whose war was Perusia? Velleius (2.74) and Dio (48.4–10) make Fulvia its driver and Lucius her tool. Appian makes Lucius a champion of the dispossessed and of the old constitution, and has Fulvia drawn in by Antony's agent Manius, who told her that war in Italy would bring Antony home from Cleopatra (Civil Wars 5.19). Diana Delia ("Fulvia Reconsidered", 1991) took the jealous wife for the victor's caricature of a real political actor; Charles Babcock ("The Early Career of Fulvia", 1965) had already traced her influence through the Clodian following to Curio and Antony.

A Phrygian city, Eumeneia, took the name Fulvia and about 41–40 struck bronze coins inscribed "of the Fulvians" with a winged female head that may be hers (RPC I 3140). If so, she is among the first living Roman women to be portrayed on coinage, and she did it in the East, where rulers' wives had long been honoured that way.

07Hortensia and the tax of 42

In 42 the triumvirs, short of money for the war against Brutus and Cassius, ordered the 1,400 richest women to value their property and contribute (Appian 4.32). The women went first to the women of the triumvirs' houses, Octavian's sister Octavia and Antony's mother Julia; Fulvia turned them from her door. They then forced their way to the triumvirs' tribunal in the Forum, where Hortensia, daughter of the orator Hortensius, Cicero's rival, spoke for them. Her argument in Appian is constitutional. The women had proscribed no one, raised no army, sought no office; why should they share the penalty when they had no share in the guilt? Why pay tax when they had no part in honours, commands or statecraft? Their mothers had given jewellery, freely, against Carthage; they would give again against Gauls or Parthians, but never to civil war (4.32–33). The triumvirs ordered the lictors to drive them off, the crowd protested, and the matter was put off to the next day (4.34).

She claimed no rights, only the logic of exclusion turned round: if women have no part in the state, the state has no claim on them.

Where sources disagree. Appian (4.34) has the triumvirs cut the women assessed from 1,400 to 400 and levy instead on every man worth more than 100,000 drachmas (denarii), citizen, foreigner, freedman or priest, a loan of a fiftieth of his property and a year's income. Valerius Maximus (8.3.3) says only that she won remission of the greater part of the sum and that her father's eloquence lived again in her. Appian wrote in Greek two centuries later; Quintilian says her speech was still read in his day, and not merely as a compliment to her sex (1.1.6), so a text existed that Appian may have reworked. How much of his speech is hers is uncertain.

08Terentia and Tullia

Terentia brought Cicero a large dowry and property of her own, which she managed herself. Plutarch calls her no gentle or timid woman but ambitious, and, as Cicero himself says, readier to share her husband's political cares than to share her household cares with him (Cicero 20.3); it was she, he says, who brought Cicero the Vestals' sign in December 63 urging him to act against the conspirators (20). During his exile in 58–57 she worked for his recall and kept the household going on her own means. They divorced in 46 (Cicero 41), and Pliny says she lived to 103 (Natural History 7.158).

Tullia was married three times, to Gaius Piso Frugi, to Furius Crassipes and to Publius Dolabella, the last match arranged by her mother and herself in 50 while Cicero was governing Cilicia; she died in February 45, soon after giving birth, and her death drove the burst of philosophy that module 5.6 describes. The letters to Terentia and the letters of grief for Tullia are deep dive module 11.5's material. What matters here is that both women appear only in Cicero's words, the wife's voice in his replies to her, and that a marriage arranged in the father's absence by mother and daughter shows where such decisions could lie.

09Octavia, Cleopatra and Livia's beginnings

Octavia, Octavian's elder sister, was the widow of Gaius Marcellus, consul in 50, when the Treaty of Brundisium married her to Antony in 40; the Senate waived the widow's waiting period (Plutarch, Antony 31). In 37 she mediated between her brother and her husband at Tarentum (35). In 35 she came east with troops and supplies and was told to stay in Athens (53; module 5.5). She refused to leave Antony's house in Rome and went on raising his children, Fulvia's as well as her own, which, Plutarch says, made Antony hated for wronging such a woman (54). In 32 he divorced her and sent men to put her out of his house (57). After Actium she brought up his children by Fulvia and by Cleopatra with her own (87).

Cleopatra is module 5.5's and Greek module 7.3's: the Donations, the war of words, Actium and the poets. Here she is the other half of a pair. The propaganda war of 33–32 needed a Roman wife to set against the Egyptian queen, and Octavia's patience, which Plutarch tells as character, was also policy. Both were honoured to the same end: in 35 Octavian gave Octavia and his wife Livia the sacrosanctity of tribunes, freedom from guardianship and statues (Dio 49.38.1), at the moment Antony's wife was being slighted in Athens.

Livia, born in 58, was the daughter of Marcus Livius Drusus Claudianus, who killed himself after Philippi (Velleius 2.71). Her first husband, Tiberius Claudius Nero, fought for Lucius Antonius in the Perusine war, and she fled with him and the infant Tiberius to Sicily and Greece (Suetonius, Tiberius 6; Velleius 2.75). In 38 Octavian took her from her husband while she was pregnant (Suetonius, Augustus 62.2; Tacitus, Annals 5.1). Dio puts her in her sixth month, says the pontiffs were consulted on whether the marriage was lawful, that Nero gave her away like a father, and that when Drusus was born three months later Rome joked that the lucky have children in three months (48.44). Her power under the Principate begins here (module 6.1).

10The problem of evidence

Nearly every word about these women was written by a man with a case to make. Cicero was an advocate defending Caelius against Clodia, an orator destroying Antony through Fulvia, a husband and father writing to his own household. Sallust was a moralist for whom Sempronia was a symptom. Velleius wrote under Tiberius, Plutarch, Appian and Dio under emperors whose monarchy the victors had founded, and behind them stand Augustus' lost memoirs and the propaganda of 33–32. Their women fall into types: the matron, the learned mother, the adulteress, the poisoner, the virago who rules her husband. The last is aimed at the husband: Antony governed first by Fulvia and then by Cleopatra is Antony unfit to govern Romans.

Against this the women's own words are few and doubtful: Cornelia's letter in Nepos' fragments; Hortensia's speech in Appian's Greek; Servilia's one exclamation, reported by Cicero; Cleopatra's subscription on a papyrus (module 5.5). The material evidence is not theirs either: Cornelia's base was set up and re-cut by others, and the sling bullets are men's words about women. The Laudatio Turiae (module 9.3) is a husband's speech. The way through extends module 0.1's rule for stories first found in Livy, to look for later politics and family interest: ask who is speaking, to whom and for what, and treat the agreement of hostile witnesses as evidence of a type before evidence of a person.

Where sources disagree. Richard Bauman (Women and Politics in Ancient Rome, 1992) treats the episodes as evidence of real and growing political influence exercised by women of the nobility. Tom Hillard ("On the Stage, Behind the Curtain: Images of Politically Active Women in the Late Roman Republic", 1992) argued that the image of the politically active woman is largely a rhetorical weapon against the men around her, so that its frequency in the sources measures invective more than power. Between them lies the question of whether Servilia's councils, attested in private letters with no motive to invent them, are the rule or the exception.

11Self-test

  1. Through which channels could a woman of the late Republic exercise influence that the law did not forbid?
    Answer

    Marriage and divorce, which made and broke alliances; her own money, held under marriage without manus; the house, where receptions and family councils took place; and mourning and memory, as at funerals. Dixon (1983) read the letters as showing women working the family's network as partners in a family enterprise.

  2. How do Plutarch's inscription on Cornelia's statue and the surviving base differ, and what has been inferred from the difference?
    Answer

    Plutarch gives "Cornelia, mother of the Gracchi" (Gaius Gracchus 4.3); the base reads Cornelia Africani f. Gracchorum, her father first. Coarelli (1978) dated the statue to about 100 BC and argued that the present text replaced an erased one under Augustus; Kajava (1989) accepted the Augustan lettering but allowed an earlier statue, around 121.

  3. Why does Hemelrijk call the familiar Cornelia a creation of later times?
    Answer

    Because the stories that make her an exemplar, the jewels (Valerius Maximus 4.4), the serene widow at Misenum (Plutarch, Gaius Gracchus 19), the learned letters (Quintilian 1.1.6), come from imperial writers who needed a model mother, while her political voice survives only in a letter whose authenticity is disputed.

  4. What does Sempronia do in Sallust's narrative, and why is her portrait there?
    Answer

    Almost nothing: her husband's house is where the Allobroges were brought (40.5). Syme saw a female counterpart to Catiline and a hit at Decimus Brutus; Ramsey a digression; Boyd and Loar a portrait central to Sallust's theme of noble decline, since her talents without restraint mirror the Rome he describes.

  5. How did Cicero's Pro Caelio win, and why does that limit its value as evidence for Clodia?
    Answer

    It turned the trial into a trial of Clodia, mocking her through Appius Claudius the Blind (33–34), calling her the Medea of the Palatine and hinting that she poisoned Celer; Caelius was acquitted. Since the speech needed the jury to see a widow's revenge, its portrait is advocacy, and it is the source of nearly everything later said about her.

  6. What evidence links Lesbia with Clodia Metelli, and what are Wiseman's objections?
    Answer

    Apuleius (Apology 10) says Lesbia's name was Clodia; poem 79 puns on Pulcher; poems 58 and 77 name a Caelius and a Rufus. Wiseman (1969) noted that the poems date to the mid-50s, after Celer's death, and that there were three Clodias; poem 83 gives Lesbia a living husband. In 2023 he proposed a young daughter of Appius Claudius, consul in 54.

  7. What do Cicero's letters show Servilia doing after the Ides?
    Answer

    At Antium in June 44 she sat in Brutus' council, interrupted Cicero, and promised to have the grain commission struck from the Senate's decree (Letters to Atticus 15.11); in July 43 she summoned Cicero, Casca, Labeo and Scaptius to her house to decide whether Brutus should return to Italy (Letters to Brutus 1.18).

  8. Who made Fulvia responsible for the Perusine war, and how does Appian's account differ?
    Answer

    Velleius (2.74), who says she had nothing womanish but her body, and Dio (48.4–10), who has her wear a sword at Praeneste. Appian makes Lucius Antonius the war's champion and has Fulvia drawn in by Manius, who told her that war in Italy would bring Antony home (5.19). Delia (1991) reads the hostile picture as the victor's caricature.

  9. On what ground did Hortensia refuse the tax, and what did she achieve?
    Answer

    That women, having no share in honours, commands or statecraft and no guilt for the civil war, should not pay for it; they would give freely only against a foreign enemy, as their mothers had against Carthage (Appian 4.32–33). Appian says the women assessed were cut from 1,400 to 400 and the burden shifted to men worth over 100,000 drachmas (4.34); Valerius Maximus says she won remission of the greater part (8.3.3).

  10. Why were Octavia and Livia given sacrosanctity in 35, and what did Livia's marriage of 38 show?
    Answer

    To set Octavian's household, and above all the slighted Octavia, against Antony and Cleopatra in the propaganda war (Dio 49.38.1). Livia's marriage, made while she was six months pregnant by her husband, who gave her away, with the pontiffs consulted (Dio 48.44; Suetonius, Augustus 62.2), showed that the triumvir could bend custom for a match with the Claudii.

12Carry forward

  1. Roman women of the late Republic held no office but worked through marriage, money, the house and mourning; their power was real where those channels mattered, as in Servilia's councils, and invisible to any account that looks only at magistracies and votes.
  2. The woman who rules a man is an attack on the man: Fulvia and Cleopatra are Antony's unfitness, Sempronia is Rome's decay, Clodia is Caelius' defence. Read every portrait of a political woman first for what it says about the men around her.
  3. From 35 BC the honours of women became instruments of the regime: sacrosanctity, statues and freedom from guardianship for Octavia and Livia begin the imperial pattern in which nearness to the ruler replaces the family council as a woman's route to power.

The prompt this answers

Give me an in-depth report on women in the politics of the late Republic, extending modules 5.1 to 5.5 and 9.3. Cover: what a woman could and could not do in law and in practice; Cornelia, mother of the Gracchi; Sempronia in Sallust's Catiline; Clodia Metelli in Cicero's Pro Caelio and as Catullus' Lesbia, and the identification problem; Servilia, Caesar's lover and Brutus' mother, and her family councils in Cicero's letters; Fulvia, wife of Clodius, Curio and Antony, at Perusia and on the sling bullets; Hortensia's speech against the tax of 42 BC; Terentia and Tullia in Cicero's letters; Octavia and Cleopatra as the two poles of the propaganda war; Livia's beginnings; and the problem of evidence: nearly every word about these women comes from men with a case to make. Format: about 2500 words, headed sections, name sources, flag disagreements, end with a 10-question self-test with answers and the three ideas I must carry forward.
