01The citizen militia: census, levy and oath

For most of the Republic Rome had no standing army. Each year the consuls raised legions from citizens who could arm themselves and dismissed them after the campaign. The census was in origin a military register; how Livy's five classes (1.43) grew from a single hoplite classis is module 1.5's question. Pay began, Livy says nearly four centuries later, at the siege of Veii in 406, funded by the citizens' war tax, tributum (4.59.11–4.60).

Polybius describes the levy he knew in the mid second century (6.19–21). Citizens rated above 400 drachmas owed ten campaigns in the cavalry or sixteen in the infantry before the age of forty-six; poorer men rowed (6.19). On the Capitol the tribunes chose men four at a time, tribe by tribe, matched in age and build (6.20). One man swore to obey his officers and each of the rest swore the same (6.21.1–3); Livy dates the first oath imposed by the tribunes to 216, before which there had been only an oath to muster and a voluntary pledge not to flee (22.38.1–5). The youngest and poorest became light-armed velites, then came the hastati, the principes and the oldest, the triarii (6.21.7–8). A foot-soldier drew two obols a day, a centurion twice as much, a cavalryman a drachma, less food, clothing and arms (6.39.12–15), until Gaius Gracchus made clothing free in 123 (Plutarch, Gaius Gracchus 5.1). Allies raised on the formula of module 3.4 marched beside each legion. The army was the citizen body under arms, graded by age and property and paid little more than its keep.

02The manipular legion and its discipline

Module 3.4 sets the manipular legion against the hoplite phalanx and asks where it came from; module 4.5 reads Polybius' camp, punishments and rewards as his case for Roman success. Polybius' legion had 4,200 infantry, 5,000 in a crisis, and 300 cavalry (6.20.8–9): 1,200 each of velites, hastati and principes, and 600 triarii (6.21.9). Each heavy line formed ten maniples with two centurions apiece (6.24). The velites carried a sword, javelins and a round shield (6.22); the hastati and principes the curved oblong scutum, the Spanish sword, two pila, a plumed bronze helmet and a breastplate a span square, or mail if rated above 10,000 drachmas (6.23); the triarii thrusting spears. Centurions were chosen for steadiness rather than daring, men who would hold their ground and die at their post (6.24.9).

Each night the watchword went round the camp on a wooden tablet (tessera) and returned to the tribune (6.34); the sentry who failed and the unit that fled met the cudgel and the decimation that module 4.5 records. Decimation was rarer than Polybius' manual implies. Livy, writing more than four centuries after the event, gives a first case to Appius Claudius in 471 (2.59.11); Crassus revived it (module 5.3); Octavian decimated a cohort that gave way in Dalmatia and fed the survivors barley (Appian, Illyrian Wars 26; Dio 49.38), as Suetonius says he did with any cohort that yielded (Augustus 24.2); and Tacitus calls Lucius Apronius' decimation of a fleeing cohort in AD 20 rare in that age and a thing of old memory (Annals 3.21.1).

Rewards became a graded system. Gellius lists the crowns (5.6): the civic crown of oak leaves for saving a citizen's life; the mural and camp crowns for the first over a wall or rampart; and the grass crown, given by a rescued army to its rescuer, which Pliny says very few ever won (Natural History 22.6–13). Under the emperors decorations followed rank, as Valerie Maxfield showed (The Military Decorations of the Roman Army, 1981): torques, armlets and discs (phalerae) for the lower ranks, crowns and spears for officers. The centurion Marcus Caelius wears crown, torques, armlets and phalerae on his cenotaph (module 6.4).

03Marius, the cohort and the end of the militia

Module 5.2 unpacks the "Marian reforms" and the modern doubt that Marius changed recruitment much. Wars in Spain kept men under arms for years, the property threshold sank, and an annual militia turned by degrees into a long-service army. The cohort, three maniples (one from each heavy line) with their light troops, is already named by Polybius at Ilipa in 206 (11.23.1); by Caesar's day it was the tactical unit, ten to a legion, and under the empire it held six centuries of eighty (Pseudo-Hyginus, On the Fortification of Camps). The velites vanished, their work passing to non-citizen specialists: Numidian horse, Balearic slingers, Cretan archers. Hastatus, princeps and pilus survived as the titles of each cohort's six centurions, ranked up to the primus pilus, senior centurion of the first cohort. That cohort grew larger than the rest. Vegetius' figure of 1,105 infantry (2.6) stands alone, but discharge lists and the fortress at Inchtuthil in Scotland, where the first cohort had ten barracks against six for each of the others, show it at about double strength by the late first century AD (David Breeze, "The Organisation of the Legion: the First Cohort and the Equites Legionis", Journal of Roman Studies 1969).

Where sources disagree. Mommsen's picture, long the standard one, was of legions filled by volunteers from Marius onwards. P. A. Brunt ("Conscription and Volunteering in the Roman Imperial Army", 1974) answered from levies attested in Italy and the provinces that conscription stayed far more common in the early Principate, and that volunteering prevailed only once legions recruited locally in the second century. Tiberius himself complained that volunteers were few and mostly needy vagrants (Tacitus, Annals 4.4).

04The standing army of the emperors

Module 6.4 sets out the Augustan terms: twenty-five legions in AD 23, sixteen and then twenty years' service, 225 denarii a year, 12,000 sesterces on discharge. Domitian added a fourth instalment to the three, taking pay to 300 denarii, and after Saturninus' revolt in Upper Germany, which the savings banked with two legions had encouraged, capped any soldier's deposit at the standards at 1,000 sesterces (Suetonius, Domitian 7.3); Severus and Caracalla raised pay again (module 7.4). A papyrus of AD 81 (P.Gen.Lat. 1) shows two legionaries in Egypt, one of them Quintus Julius Proculus of Damascus, charged against each instalment for hay, food, boots and straps, the camp Saturnalia, clothing and the standards, with the balance banked.

A senatorial legate commanded the legion, with a senatorial tribune, five equestrian tribunes and a camp prefect risen from the centurionate. Its sixty centurions were the professionals, and a primus pilus could reach equestrian rank. Giovanni Forni (Il reclutamento delle legioni da Augusto a Diocleziano, 1953) showed from tombstones and discharge lists that Italians dominated the legions under Augustus and were rare by the second century, when legions recruited in their own provinces and many recruits gave their birthplace as "the camp" (castris): soldiers' sons.

The auxiliaries were non-citizen provincials in cavalry alae, infantry cohorts and mixed cohorts, nominally 500 or 1,000 strong, under equestrian prefects, many raised as ethnic units, such as Batavians and Tungrians, that kept their names after recruiting locally. After twenty-five years' service, the term the diplomas name, the reward was regular from Claudius onwards: citizenship for the veteran and his children and lawful marriage (conubium) with one wife, granted by an imperial constitution posted at Rome. The veteran could obtain, probably at his own cost, a certified copy on two bronze tablets sealed by seven witnesses: the military diploma. The earliest surviving diploma dates from 52; around 140, on the usual reading, children born during service ceased to be covered; the last auxiliary diploma dates from 203, nine years before universal citizenship (module 7.4). After 212 the legal line between legion and auxiliary unit lost its meaning, though the units kept their old names.

05The soldier's life: tablets and tombstones

Module 7.5 uses the Vindolanda tablets to show the army running a province. The same archive shows life inside the fort. Flavius Cerialis, prefect of the Ninth Cohort of Batavians about 100, kept his letters at the fort. Claudia Severa, wife of a neighbouring commander, invited his wife Sulpicia Lepidina to her birthday and added a greeting in her own hand, among the earliest Latin known to be written by a woman (Tab. Vindol. II 291). The decurion Masclus asked Cerialis for the next day's orders and added that his fellow-soldiers had no beer (III 628); socks, sandals and underpants were sent to a soldier (II 346); men applied to the prefect for leave. Officers' families lived in the fort, but soldiers could not marry lawfully while serving: Dio says Claudius gave them the privileges of married men because the law denied them wives (60.24.3).

Tombstones follow a formula: name, voting tribe, home, unit, rank, age, years of service and heir. At Colchester the centurion Marcus Favonius Facilis stands in armour holding his vine stick (RIB 200), and Longinus Sdapeze, a double-pay trooper from Sardica, rides down a naked barbarian (RIB 201); at Hexham Flavinus, standard-bearer of the ala Petriana, died at twenty-five after seven years' service (RIB 1172). The vine stick was used: in the mutiny of AD 14 the soldiers killed a centurion nicknamed "Bring another" for the sticks he broke on their backs (Tacitus, Annals 1.23). Josephus, who had fought the legions in Galilee, called their drills bloodless battles and their battles bloody drills (Jewish War 3.75), and Hadrian's address to the army of Numidia at Lambaesis in 128, cut in stone, praises unit after unit (ILS 2487).

06Equipment and engineering

Equipment is known mainly from finds, sorted by modern typologies named after find-spots: Montefortino and Coolus helmets in the Republic, Imperial Gallic and Italic types later, Mainz and Pompeii types of short sword. The segmented iron cuirass, a modern name (lorica segmentata), appears at Kalkriese, probably the Varian battlefield of AD 9 (module 6.4), and is best known from a chest found at Corbridge in 1964; mail and scale were worn throughout and outlasted it. Hobnailed boots (caligae) gave Gaius, dressed as a little soldier in the Rhine camps, his nickname Caligula (Suetonius, Gaius 9). By the later third century the long spatha and an oval shield had replaced the short sword and scutum.

Where sources disagree. Conrad Cichorius, who published the reliefs of Trajan's Column in 1896–1900, and many readers after him took the frieze literally, so its division of legionaries in segmented cuirasses from auxiliaries in mail shaped reconstructions for a century. J. C. Coulston ("The Value of Trajan's Column as a Source for Military Equipment", 1989) argued that the division is a sculptors' device for marking citizens off from auxiliaries, and that the Column is no independent source for equipment.

The army was also Rome's largest body of builders. A marching army ditched and ramparted its camp every night, a city of streets, gates and headquarters in Josephus' description (Jewish War 3.76–84). Caesar bridged the Rhine in ten days (Gallic War 4.17–18) and at Alesia ran lines of eleven and fourteen miles, by his own figures (7.69, 7.74); the ramp against Masada still stands (Josephus, Jewish War 7.304–307). In peace soldiers built roads, forts and walls (module 7.5), and Corbulo set his men to dig a canal from the Rhine to the Meuse against idleness (Tacitus, Annals 11.20).

07The triumph

Module 1.2 holds the dispute over the triumph's origins, module 4.4 counts the cash it displayed, and module 6.4 shows Augustus confining it to his own house after 19 BC. A general with imperium asked the Senate for a triumph and waited outside the city boundary, since crossing it ended his command. He entered by the Triumphal Gate and went by the Circus Flaminius and the Circus Maximus, round the Palatine and along the Sacred Way to the Capitol, behind spoils, captives and sacrificial oxen. He rode a four-horse chariot in the gold-embroidered purple toga, painted red with cinnabar as the face of Jupiter's statue was on festivals (Pliny, Natural History 33.111–112, citing Verrius). A slave held a crown over his head (Zonaras 7.21, from Dio) and, in Tertullian's version, told him to look behind him and remember he was a man (Apology 33.4); an amulet under the chariot warded off envy (Pliny 28.39), and the soldiers sang abuse of their general (Suetonius, Julius 49.4, 51). On the Capitol he sacrificed to Jupiter Best and Greatest, while enemy leaders were put to death, as Simon bar Giora was in the triumph of Vespasian and Titus in 71, which Josephus watched (Jewish War 7.123–157) and the Arch of Titus records (modules 7.2 and 9.5). A lesser victory earned an ovation, with a myrtle crown and no chariot (Gellius 5.6.20–23; Plutarch, Marcellus 22).

Where sources disagree. Valerius Maximus says a law required at least 5,000 enemy killed in one battle before a triumph could be granted, and sets out further conditions in the same chapter (2.8.1). Mary Beard (The Roman Triumph, 2007) treats such rules as fragile and changing, arguments deployed in particular Senate debates rather than a code applied, and finds only the general's wait outside the city consistently observed.

08The late army: field and frontier

Module 8.1 saw Gallienus build up mobile cavalry, and module 8.2 sets Lactantius' fourfold army against John the Lydian's 389,704 and notes that the formal division of troops is usually credited to Constantine. The division made two armies of different status. Field armies (comitatenses, from the emperor's comitatus), with an elite of palatini, marched with an emperor or a regional commander; frontier troops (limitanei or ripenses) served under the duces of frontier provinces, with lower pay and fewer privileges. Constantine disbanded the praetorians after the Milvian Bridge (Aurelius Victor 40.25; Zosimus 2.17), let guard regiments, the scholae palatinae, take their place, and gave military command to masters of infantry and cavalry (Zosimus 2.33). Zosimus accused him of stripping the frontiers to quarter soldiers in cities where theatres softened them (2.34).

Units shrank: legions far smaller than the old stood beside new infantry regiments (auxilia palatina) and armoured cataphracts, whom Ammianus took for polished statues (16.10.8). State factories (fabricae), listed in the Notitia Dignitatum of about 400, made the arms. Soldiers' sons were bound to serve and landowners supplied recruits or gold; some men cut off their thumbs to escape, which no Gaul, Ammianus says, ever did (15.12.3). Barbarians served as recruits, as settlers (laeti) and, after 382, as whole peoples under their own leaders (module 8.4). Vegetius, about 400, set a lost ancient legion against an infantry that had given up helmet and cuirass since Gratian (1.20). The picture of the limitanei as a hereditary peasant militia rests chiefly on the Historia Augusta's claim that Severus Alexander gave frontier soldiers land on condition that their heirs served (Severus Alexander 58.4); Benjamin Isaac ("The Meaning of the Terms Limes and Limitanei", Journal of Roman Studies 1988) argued that the word meant simply the troops under a frontier dux.

Where sources disagree. Agathias, writing in the 570s, says the army had once numbered 645,000 and in his own day had shrunk to 150,000 (5.13.7–8). A. H. M. Jones (The Later Roman Empire, 1964) reached a similar total of about 600,000 by giving each unit in the Notitia an estimated strength. Richard Duncan-Jones (Structure and Scale in the Roman Economy, 1990), working from pay orders sent to units in the Thebaid in 298–300, judged Jones's unit strengths too high, which would cut the total sharply.

09The army as political actor

The army entered politics through its oath and its pay. The Republican oath bound soldiers to a consul for a campaign; once commands lasted years and land on discharge depended on the general, the tie became personal. Sulla's march of 88 set the precedent (module 5.2); deep dive module 11.2 follows generals and armies from Marius to Octavian. Augustus made himself the only general. Soldiers swore to the emperor, renewed the oath each 1 January (Tacitus, Histories 1.55) and on the anniversary of his accession (Pliny, Letters 10.52), and carried his image among the standards. In AD 41 the guard made Claudius emperor for a donative (module 7.1), and in 69 the provincial armies showed that an emperor could be made elsewhere than at Rome (Tacitus, Histories 1.4; module 7.2). The great provincial armies were the electors; the praetorians could kill and sell (module 7.4) but not hold the throne against them.

The third century put soldiers in command as well. Severus gave his new Parthian legions equestrian prefects (module 7.4), and Aurelius Victor says Gallienus barred senators from military commands by edict (33.34). The inscriptions show a slower change: senatorial legates and tribunes of legions thin out through the century and are last attested in the 260s, replaced by equestrian prefects, many risen from the ranks, the Danubian officers who gave the empire its rulers from Claudius II to Diocletian (module 8.1). Constantine's masters of the soldiers separated military from civil careers, and in the fifth century those masters made and unmade western emperors (module 8.5).

10Self-test

  1. How did Polybius' levy assign recruits to the four kinds of infantry, and what does the arrangement tell you about the Republican army?
    Answer

    By age and wealth: the youngest and poorest became velites, then hastati and principes, and the oldest triarii (6.21.7–8). It was a citizen militia graded like the census, armed largely at its own cost and paid little more than its keep (6.39.12–15).

  2. Why does Tacitus' comment on Apronius qualify Polybius' account of decimation?
    Answer

    Polybius describes decimation as a standing penalty for units that fled, but Tacitus calls Apronius' decimation of AD 20 rare in that age and a thing of old memory (Annals 3.21.1). The recorded cases (Appius Claudius in 471, Crassus, Octavian in Dalmatia) are few and scattered.

  3. What was a cohort, and why can it not be credited to Marius?
    Answer

    Three maniples, one from each heavy line, with their light troops; under the empire, six centuries of eighty. Polybius already names it at Ilipa in 206 (11.23.1), and it replaced the manipular order gradually as the militia became a long-service army; no ancient source credits Marius with it (module 5.2).

  4. On what grounds did Brunt challenge the picture of a volunteer army from Marius onwards?
    Answer

    He gathered levies in Italy and the provinces and Tiberius' complaint that volunteers were few and mostly needy vagrants (Tacitus, Annals 4.4), and argued that conscription stayed common in the early Principate, with volunteering prevailing only once legions recruited locally in the second century.

  5. What did a military diploma grant, to whom, and how did the grant change?
    Answer

    It was a veteran's certified bronze copy of an imperial constitution giving an auxiliary, after twenty-five years, citizenship for himself and his children and lawful marriage with one wife. The earliest dates from 52; around 140 children born in service ceased to be covered; the last auxiliary diploma dates from 203, nine years before the edict of 212.

  6. What do the Vindolanda tablets of Flavius Cerialis add to the picture module 7.5 draws from the same site?
    Answer

    Life inside the fort rather than the army as government: Claudia Severa's birthday invitation with its greeting in her own hand (Tab. Vindol. II 291), Masclus asking for orders and beer (III 628), a parcel of socks, sandals and underpants (II 346), and leave requests. Officers' families lived in the fort though soldiers could not marry lawfully (Dio 60.24.3).

  7. Why does Coulston refuse to use Trajan's Column as evidence for legionary and auxiliary equipment?
    Answer

    He argued that the split between legionaries in segmented armour and auxiliaries in mail is a sculptural device marking citizens off from auxiliaries, not a record of what was issued (1989). He thus rejected the literal reading that goes back to Cichorius.

  8. Describe the triumphator's appearance and the warnings built into the ritual, and say what Beard doubts about its rules.
    Answer

    He rode a four-horse chariot in the gold-embroidered purple toga, painted red like Jupiter's statue (Pliny, Natural History 33.111–112), with a slave holding a crown over him and reminding him he was mortal (Tertullian, Apology 33.4), an amulet against envy and soldiers singing abuse. Beard (2007) treats conditions such as Valerius Maximus' 5,000 dead (2.8.1) as changing arguments rather than a fixed code.

  9. How did comitatenses and limitanei differ, and why does Isaac reject the idea that limitanei were a peasant militia?
    Answer

    Comitatenses were field troops with an emperor or regional commander, with the palatini as their elite; limitanei served under frontier duces with lower pay and privileges. The peasant-militia idea rests chiefly on Historia Augusta, Severus Alexander 58.4; Isaac (1988) argued the word meant simply troops under a frontier dux.

  10. How did the soldiers' oath bind the army to the emperor, and how did the third century change who commanded the legions?
    Answer

    Soldiers swore to the emperor, renewed the oath each 1 January (Tacitus, Histories 1.55) and on his accession day (Pliny, Letters 10.52), and carried his image among the standards. In the third century equestrian prefects, many from the ranks, replaced senatorial legates, more gradually than Aurelius Victor's edict of Gallienus (33.34) implies, and Danubian officers became the emperors (module 8.1).

11Carry forward

  1. Rome's army changed by degrees from an annual citizen militia graded by the census into a long-service professional force paid by the state; whoever paid, settled and commanded the soldiers held their loyalty, which is why the army became a political actor from Sulla to the soldier emperors.
  2. The army and citizenship were bound together: service was a citizen's duty under the Republic and auxiliary service the road to citizenship under the emperors; after 212 the late army drew on soldiers' sons, conscripted peasants and barbarians, so changes in who served are changes in who counted as Roman.
  3. Normative texts (Polybius' manual, Vegetius' lost ancient legion, Valerius Maximus' rules of the triumph, Trajan's Column) describe an ideal; documents and finds (Vindolanda, papyri, diplomas, tombstones, Inchtuthil, Corbridge) show practice, and each should be read against the other.
