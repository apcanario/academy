01Spain, the oath and the question of war guilt

Two narratives carry the war. Polybius wrote in the mid-second century BC, two generations after it, from documents, survivors and his own journey over the Alps; his book 3 survives whole as far as Cannae, books 7–15 only in excerpts. Livy's books 21–30 survive complete but were written under Augustus, nearly two centuries after Zama, from Polybius and the Roman annalists.

After the Mercenary War and the loss of Sardinia (module 4.2), Hamilcar Barca took an army to Spain in 237. When he died in 229 or 228, his son-in-law Hasdrubal founded New Carthage and agreed with Rome, in 226 or 225, that the Carthaginians would not cross the Ebro in arms (Polybius 2.13). Hasdrubal was murdered in 221, and the army acclaimed Hamilcar's son Hannibal, then about twenty-six.

The oath comes from Hannibal himself. At the court of Antiochus III he recalled that at nine, when Hamilcar was sacrificing to Zeus (probably Baal Hammon, module 4.1) before sailing for Spain, his father made him swear, hand on the victim, never to be well disposed to the Romans (Polybius 3.11; Livy 21.1.4). It was told to reassure a suspicious king, which does not make it false.

Saguntum, south of the Ebro, had placed itself in Roman good faith some years earlier (Polybius 3.30.1). Hannibal took it in 219 after an eight-month siege. In 218 a Roman envoy at Carthage, Quintus Fabius in Livy, held out a fold of his toga and offered peace or war; told to give whichever he liked, he let fall war (Polybius 3.33.1–4; Livy 21.18.13–14).

Polybius separates beginning, pretext and cause (3.6–7). Saguntum and the Ebro crossing were beginnings; the causes were Hamilcar's anger, the seizure of Sardinia with its added indemnity, and the Spanish success that gave Carthage the means (3.9.6–3.10.6). He rejects Fabius Pictor's claim that Barcid ambition made the war against the will of Carthage's leading men (3.8), and concludes that if Saguntum is the cause, Carthage was in the wrong; if Sardinia, it had right on its side (3.30.3–4).

Where sources disagree. Livy writes Saguntum's freedom into the Ebro treaty (21.2.7); Polybius' treaty names only the river, and he reports the Carthaginian argument that it was never ratified at Carthage (3.21.1–2). Carcopino argued that the treaty's Iber was the Júcar, south of Saguntum, which would make the siege a breach; few follow him. Harris (War and Imperialism in Republican Rome, 1979) sees Roman aggression in the Saguntine alliance; Hoyos (Unplanned Wars, 1998) denies any long Barcid plan of revenge.

02Over the Alps

Hannibal left New Carthage in late spring 218 and crossed the Pyrenees with 50,000 foot and 9,000 horse (Polybius 3.35.7). The elephants, thirty-seven in Appian (Hannibalic War 4), crossed the Rhône on rafts heaped with earth (Polybius 3.46). The consul Publius Scipio, landing at Massilia, arrived three days late (3.49.1). He sent his army on to Spain under his brother Gnaeus and returned to meet Hannibal in Italy, a choice that kept the Barcid base under attack for the rest of the war.

The Alpine crossing took fifteen days, the whole march five months (3.56.3). Mountain peoples ambushed the column; at the summit, with snow falling, Hannibal showed his men the plains of the Po (3.54.1–2), and in Livy tells them they are crossing the walls of Rome itself (21.35.9). A landslip on the descent cost days of road-cutting (Polybius 3.54–55); Livy adds rock split with fire and vinegar (21.37.2–3). Polybius, who had walked the route, mocks historians who needed gods and heroes to guide the army (3.47.6–48.12). Hannibal's own bronze tablet at the Lacinian promontory recorded what reached Italy: 12,000 African and 8,000 Iberian infantry and at most 6,000 cavalry (3.33.18, 3.56.4). After the Trebia all the elephants but one died of cold (3.74.11).

Where sources disagree. Polybius names the Allobroges and a confluence he calls the Island, usually taken as the Isère, which points north; Livy brings Hannibal through the Tricastini and Vocontii to the Durance (21.31), which points south. Mommsen chose the Little St Bernard, Napoleon the Mont Cenis, Lancel the Col du Clapier, and de Beer (Alps and Elephants, 1955) the Col de la Traversette, which Walbank rejected (1956). A churned layer of animal dung below the Traversette, reported by Mahaney's team in 2016, cannot on its radiocarbon margin prove which army made it. Uncertain.

03Ticinus, Trebia, Trasimene and the Delayer

In November 218 Scipio's cavalry met Hannibal's on the Ticinus; the Numidians enveloped them, the consul was wounded, and the Boii and Insubres went over to Hannibal. Polybius heard from Gaius Laelius that Scipio's seventeen-year-old son, the future Africanus, saved him (10.3); Livy notes that Coelius Antipater gave the rescue to a Ligurian slave (21.46). At the Trebia, near the winter solstice, the consul Tiberius Sempronius Longus took the bait of Numidian horsemen and led his army unfed through the icy river; 2,000 men under Hannibal's brother Mago, hidden in a watercourse, struck the Roman rear, and only about 10,000 legionaries cut their way through to Placentia (Polybius 3.70–74).

In 217 Hannibal crossed the Arno marshes, losing an eye to infection (3.79.12), and drew the consul Gaius Flaminius, whom Livy makes contemptuous of the auspices (21.63; 22.3), along the north shore of Lake Trasimene. Out of the morning mist the Carthaginians fell on an army strung out in column; Flaminius and some 15,000 Romans died (Polybius 3.82–84; Livy 22.4–7, citing Fabius Pictor, who lived through it). In the Forum a praetor announced, "We have been defeated in a great battle" (Livy 22.7.8). Hannibal released his Italian prisoners without ransom, claiming to fight Rome for the freedom of Italy (Polybius 3.77.3–7, 3.85.4): his strategy was to break the alliance on which Roman manpower rested (module 3.4).

Fabius Maximus and the strategy of delay

The people now made Quintus Fabius Maximus dictator (Polybius 3.87.6; Livy 22.8). He refused battle, shadowed Hannibal along the high ground and picked off foragers, letting Italy be ravaged at the cost of his own reputation. Trapped in the Falernian plain, Hannibal escaped by night by driving 2,000 oxen with burning faggots on their horns up a ridge (Polybius 3.93–94). The master of horse, Marcus Minucius Rufus, was given equal power by popular vote, a step without precedent, and had to be rescued by Fabius at Gerunium (3.103–105). Cunctator, the Delayer, began as abuse. Ennius made it praise, one man by delaying restored the state (quoted by Cicero, On Old Age 10), and Posidonius called Fabius the shield of Rome and Marcellus its sword (Plutarch, Fabius 19).

04Cannae, 216

For 216 Rome fielded eight legions. The consuls Lucius Aemilius Paullus and Gaius Terentius Varro, commanding on alternate days, led some 80,000 infantry and 6,000 cavalry against Hannibal's 40,000 foot and 10,000 horse at Cannae on the Aufidus (Polybius 3.107, 3.113–114), on 2 August by the Roman calendar (Gellius 5.17.5).

Varro, in command that day, put the Roman cavalry by the river, the allied cavalry on the left, and the maniples closer than usual, many times deeper than their front (3.113.2–4): a plan to break the centre by weight. Hannibal set his Spanish and Gallic foot in a crescent bulging towards the enemy, his Africans, in captured Roman armour, in deep columns on either flank, his heavy horse under Hasdrubal by the river and the Numidians on the right (3.113.6–114.5).

Hasdrubal's horse destroyed the Roman cavalry, rode behind the army to scatter the allied horse, and turned on the legions' rear. In the centre the crescent gave ground until it became a hollow; the Romans pressed in; the Africans faced inwards and closed (3.115–116). Packed too tightly to fight, the legions were cut down where they stood. Paullus died among the infantry; Varro escaped to Venusia with seventy horsemen.

Polybius counts some 70,000 dead and 10,000 captured, against Hannibal's loss of about 5,700 (3.117). Livy gives 45,500 infantry and 2,700 cavalry killed, among them twenty-nine military tribunes and eighty senators or former magistrates (22.49.15–17). Maharbal urged an immediate march on Rome and, refused, told Hannibal:

You know how to win, Hannibal; you do not know how to use victory.

Livy believed that day's delay saved the city (22.51.1–4).

Where sources disagree. Polybius' 70,000 dead requires the full eight-legion army, but Livy found his sources divided over whether four new legions or only 10,000 reinforcements were raised (22.36.1–4), and Plutarch gives some 50,000 dead (Fabius 16). Most modern estimates fall between Livy and Polybius. Which bank of the Aufidus saw the battle is still argued.

05Rome after Cannae

Much of the south went over: the Hirpini, most of the Samnites, the Bruttii, the Lucanians, part of Apulia and much of the Greek coast (Livy 22.61.11–12), with Tarentum following in 212. The great prize was Capua, second city of Italy, which joined Hannibal keeping its own laws and magistrates (23.2–10), and whose luxury Livy blames for softening his army (23.18.10–16). But the Latin colonies held; in 209 twelve declared themselves unable to give more men or money, yet none defected (27.9–10).

Rome's answer was religious, social and moral at once. Two Vestals were convicted of unchastity (module 2.3); Fabius Pictor was sent to Delphi; and on the instruction of the Sibylline Books a Gallic man and woman and a Greek man and woman were buried alive in the Forum Boarium, "by no means a Roman rite", as had been done before (Livy 22.57.2–6; Plutarch, Marcellus 3). The state bought and armed 8,000 slave volunteers, the volones, freed after their victory at Beneventum in 214 (Livy 22.57.11, 24.14–16).

The 8,000 prisoners taken in the camp asked to be ransomed. The Senate refused, so that soldiers would learn to conquer or die; Polybius makes this the proof of Roman spirit and says it dismayed Hannibal more than his victory had elated him (6.58; Livy 22.58–61). The survivors of the battle were shipped to Sicily to serve without discharge (Livy 25.5–7), while Varro was thanked for not having despaired of the state (22.61.14). There would be no negotiation with an invader on Italian soil (module 3.5).

Hannibal marched on Rome only in 211, to draw the besiegers from Capua. The ground he camped on was sold in Rome at full price (Livy 26.11.6), and Capua fell and lost its self-government and its land (26.13–16).

Where sources disagree. Maharbal's reproach assumes Rome could have been taken by a march. Polybius records no such advice, and Livy's own Hannibal tells the prisoners that he is contending for honour and empire, not waging a war of extermination (22.58.3), the language of a man expecting a negotiated peace. Lazenby (Hannibal's War, 1978) and Goldsworthy (The Punic Wars, 2000) argue that an army without a siege train could not have stormed a walled city full of citizen soldiers.

06Marcellus at Syracuse

When Hiero II, Rome's ally since the first war, died in 215, his grandson Hieronymus went over to Carthage and was murdered, and Hannibal's agents Hippocrates and Epicydes took Syracuse (Polybius 7.2–7; Livy 24.4–32). Marcus Claudius Marcellus attacked by land and sea in 213, and Archimedes defeated him: engines graded by range struck the ships at every distance, and iron claws on cranes lifted vessels by the prow and let them fall (Polybius 8.3–7; Plutarch, Marcellus 14–17). Marcellus settled into a blockade. In 212, during a festival of Artemis, his men climbed the wall at Epipolae, and in the sack a soldier killed Archimedes at his diagrams (Livy 25.23–31). The city's statues and paintings went to Rome, which Livy marks as the beginning of Roman admiration for Greek art (25.40.1–3); Polybius condemned the practice (9.10). Marcellus died in an ambush in 208 (Livy 27.26–27).

Where sources disagree. Polybius, Livy and Plutarch know nothing of burning mirrors, which first appear in the second century AD in Lucian and Galen and are elaborated by Byzantine writers such as Zonaras and Tzetzes. Experiments since Buffon's in 1747 show that mirrors can ignite wood at short range; few historians think Archimedes used them. Plutarch gives three versions of his death (Marcellus 19).

07Spain, New Carthage and the Metaurus

Gnaeus and Publius Scipio held the Barcid base in check until 211, when both were killed after their Celtiberian mercenaries deserted (Livy 25.32–36). The people then gave proconsular command in Spain to Publius' son, who had held no office above the aedileship (26.18–19).

The young Scipio marched on New Carthage, the Barcid capital and arsenal. He had learned from fishermen that the lagoon behind it could be waded at the ebb; he told his men that Neptune had promised help in a dream, and when the water fell they crossed to an undefended wall (Polybius 10.9–15; Livy 26.42–46). Polybius makes the episode proof that Scipio's luck was calculation dressed as divine favour (10.2–5, 10.11.7–8). The city yielded treasure, a fleet and the Spanish hostages, whom Scipio sent home, and the story of the captive girl he returned untouched, the continence of Scipio (Polybius 10.19.3–7; Livy 26.50).

Beaten at Baecula in 208, Hasdrubal Barca slipped away and crossed the Alps in 207 (Polybius 10.38–40; Livy 27.18–20, 27.39). His dispatch to Hannibal was intercepted, and the consul Gaius Claudius Nero took 7,000 picked men north by forced march to join Marcus Livius Salinator. Hasdrubal, hearing the trumpet sound twice in the Roman camp, guessed that both consuls were present and was caught on the Metaurus, dying, Polybius says, worthily of his father (11.1–3; Livy 27.43–51). Nero had his head thrown before Hannibal's outposts, and Hannibal recognised in it the fortune of Carthage (Livy 27.51.11–12).

At Ilipa in 206 Scipio, after days of identical deployments, reversed his order before dawn, Spaniards in the centre and legions on the wings, and the wings wheeled in and broke the Carthaginian line. Punic Spain was finished (Polybius 11.20–24; Livy 28.12–16).

Where sources disagree. Livy puts New Carthage in 210, Polybius' chronology in 209, which most moderns accept. Livy reports the rumour of a divine snake-father and Scipio's habit of sitting alone in Jupiter's temple before any business, leaving belief open (26.19.5–9); Polybius insists on calculation. Walbank ("The Scipionic legend", 1967) read Polybius as correcting a legend already growing in Scipio's lifetime. Whether Scipio believed in his own inspiration is uncertain.

08Africa, Zama and the peace of 201

Consul in 205, Scipio won Sicily as his province against Fabius' opposition (Livy 28.40–45) and crossed to Africa in 204 with the Cannae legions at the core of his army. That year, on a Sibylline oracle promising that a foreign invader could be driven out, the Idaean Mother came to the Palatine (module 2.5). In 203 Scipio burned the winter camps of Hasdrubal son of Gisgo and King Syphax, won at the Great Plains and made Masinissa king of Numidia (Polybius 14.1–10). Carthage recalled Hannibal, who left Italy in the autumn of 203, fifteen years after he had entered it (Livy 30.20).

The generals met before the battle, and Hannibal spoke on the instability of fortune (Polybius 15.6–8). The name Zama is Nepos' (Hannibal 6.3); Polybius sets the battle near Margaron, Livy near Naraggara. Hannibal put eighty elephants in front, then mercenaries, then new Carthaginian levies, and, held back, his veterans from Italy (Polybius 15.11). Scipio left lanes between his maniples, and the elephants ran down them or back on to their own cavalry (15.12). Laelius and Masinissa routed the Carthaginian horse; the first two lines broke; against the veterans the fight was even until the cavalry returned and struck their rear. Carthage lost over 20,000 dead, Rome about 1,500 (15.14), and Polybius judged that Hannibal had done all a good general could (15.15–16).

The peace of 201 left Carthage its laws and African territory but took its fleet except ten triremes, its elephants, a hundred hostages and 10,000 talents over fifty years, and forbade war outside Africa, or inside it without Rome's consent (Polybius 15.18; Livy 30.37). Scipio triumphed as Africanus, the first general named after a people he had conquered (Livy 30.45.6–7). The African clause, set beside Masinissa's ambitions, led to the third war (module 4.4).

09Hannibal after Zama

As suffete in 196 Hannibal made the judges, usually identified with Aristotle's Hundred and Four (module 4.1), serve annual terms, and showed that the indemnity could be paid without new taxes once embezzlement stopped (Livy 33.46–47). Denounced to Rome as in touch with Antiochus III, he fled to the king in 195 (33.47–49), lost a sea battle to the Rhodians off Side in 190 (37.23–24), and after Magnesia (Greek module 7.3) was named in Rome's demand for surrenders (Polybius 21.17.7). Nepos takes him to Crete and then to Prusias I of Bithynia, for whom he beat Eumenes of Pergamon at sea by hurling jars of snakes aboard (Hannibal 9–11). When Titus Quinctius Flamininus came to Prusias and soldiers surrounded his house at Libyssa, Hannibal took poison (Livy 39.51; Plutarch, Flamininus 20). Juvenal made the ring that held it the avenger of Cannae (10.163–166).

Where sources disagree. Nepos records that Atticus dated the death to 183, Polybius to 182 and Sulpicius Blitho to 181 (Hannibal 13.1). Livy places it in 183, the year of Philopoemen's death, and adds Scipio's, though he admits his sources disagreed on that (39.50–52). The snake jars rest on Nepos, Justin (32.4) and Frontinus, who has Hannibal suggest them to Antiochus as well as Prusias (Stratagems 4.7.10–11).

10Hannibal in Greek and Roman hands

Polybius, who questioned veterans and Masinissa, made Hannibal the cause of everything that befell both peoples, "one man and one mind" (9.22.1). His verdict is admiring and guarded. Hannibal held an army of Africans, Spaniards, Gauls and Italians together for sixteen years without a mutiny (11.19.3–5). The charges of cruelty and greed Polybius finds hard to judge: he blames the worst advice on Hannibal Monomachus, who proposed teaching the army to eat human flesh, while admitting on Masinissa's word that Hannibal loved money (9.24–26).

Livy's Hannibal is the antagonist of a tragedy written after the civil wars. Book 21 opens by calling this the most memorable war ever waged, in which the victors came nearer to ruin (21.1.1–3). The sketch at 21.4 matches every military virtue with vices: inhuman cruelty, "perfidy worse than Punic", no fear of gods (21.4.9; module 4.1 for the stereotype). The arc runs from the vision of Italy on the Alps, through the victory unused and the softening at Capua, to his departure from Italy cursing gods, men and himself (30.20.7–9). The vices are asserted more than shown; the narrative keeps his greatness.

The Carthaginian side is lost. Nepos names two Greeks in Hannibal's camp who wrote his war, Silenus of Caleacte and Sosylus of Sparta, his teacher of Greek (Hannibal 13.3). Polybius dismissed Sosylus as the gossip of a barber's shop (3.20.5); a Würzburg papyrus preserves a sober page of his on a sea fight off the Ebro. Silenus survives through Coelius Antipater: Cicero retells the dream in which Jupiter gave Hannibal a guide who forbade him to look back, until he turned and saw a monstrous beast coiled with serpents destroying everything, the devastation of Italy (On Divination 1.49; Livy 21.22.6–9).

The war became the core of Roman identity because Rome won it in Italy, over a generation, by refusing to lose. Polybius ends book 6 with the Cannae prisoners as proof of the constitution at its best (6.58). The war supplied the Republic's exempla: Fabius and Marcellus, shield and sword; Paullus dying and Varro thanked; Scipio carrying the war to Africa. Ennius made it epic within a generation; Virgil gave it a mythic cause in Dido's curse, "Arise, some avenger, from my bones" (Aeneid 4.625); Silius Italicus retold it in the seventeen books of the Punica. By Juvenal's day Hannibal had crossed the Alps "to please schoolboys and become a declamation" (10.167).

Where sources disagree. Polybius and Livy both make Varro the rash plebeian and Paullus the reluctant aristocrat (Polybius 3.110, 3.116; Livy 22.38–49). Yet Polybius himself says the Senate had resolved on battle (3.107.7), his patron Scipio Aemilianus was Paullus' grandson, and the Senate went on employing Varro for years. Many moderns suspect family apologetic in the received account of Cannae.

11Self-test

  1. How does Polybius distinguish the beginning of the war from its causes, and what causes does he give?
    Answer

    The siege of Saguntum and the crossing of the Ebro were beginnings, not causes (3.6–7). The causes were Hamilcar's anger, Carthaginian resentment at the seizure of Sardinia and the added indemnity, and the Barcid success in Spain that supplied the means (3.9.6–3.10.6).

  2. Why does the wording of the Ebro treaty matter to the question of war guilt?
    Answer

    Saguntum lay south of the Ebro, so if the treaty named only the river, as in Polybius (2.13.7), Hannibal's siege broke no clause and Rome's case rested on its separate Saguntine friendship. Livy writes Saguntum's freedom into the treaty (21.2.7), which makes Carthage the aggressor.

  3. What do Polybius' figures from the Lacinian tablet reveal about the march to Italy?
    Answer

    That Hannibal crossed the Pyrenees with about 59,000 men and reached Italy with about 26,000 (Polybius 3.35.7, 3.56.4): losses, garrisons and desertions more than halved the army before the first battle.

  4. Why did Hannibal release his Italian prisoners after the Trebia and Trasimene?
    Answer

    His strategy was to dissolve Rome's alliance, the source of its manpower: he claimed to be fighting Rome for Italian freedom (Polybius 3.77.3–7, 3.85.4), hoping to detach allies rather than besiege the city.

  5. What was Fabius' strategy in 217, and why was it unpopular?
    Answer

    To shadow Hannibal along high ground, refuse pitched battle and harass his foragers. It left allied land to be ravaged and looked like cowardice, so the people gave Minucius equal power, until his near-disaster at Gerunium vindicated Fabius (Polybius 3.103–105).

  6. Explain the tactical mechanism of Cannae.
    Answer

    Hannibal's convex centre of Spaniards and Gauls fell back into a hollow as the deep Roman maniples pressed in; the African infantry on the flanks turned inwards; Hasdrubal's cavalry, having beaten both Roman cavalry wings, closed the rear (Polybius 3.113–116). The Romans were enclosed and too crowded to fight.

  7. Name three Roman responses to Cannae and what each shows.
    Answer

    Any three of: the burial alive of a Gallic and a Greek couple in the Forum Boarium (Livy 22.57.6), religious repair by an un-Roman rite; the arming of 8,000 slave volunteers (22.57.11), manpower at any cost; the refusal to ransom prisoners (Polybius 6.58), discipline over sentiment; the thanks to Varro (22.61.14), refusal to admit defeat.

  8. Why did the Metaurus decide the war in Italy?
    Answer

    It destroyed the only army that could have reinforced Hannibal. Nero's march north let both consuls combine against Hasdrubal (Livy 27.43–51), and afterwards Hannibal withdrew to Bruttium and could only hold on.

  9. Which terms of the peace of 201 shaped what followed, and how?
    Answer

    The surrender of the fleet and elephants ended Carthage as a military power, the fifty-year indemnity bound it to Rome, and the ban on war in Africa without Rome's consent left it defenceless against Masinissa (Polybius 15.18), the road to the Third Punic War (module 4.4).

  10. Contrast Polybius' and Livy's Hannibal, and say what survives of the Carthaginian side.
    Answer

    Polybius gives an analytical verdict: the single mind behind the war, a commander who kept a polyglot army without mutiny, with charges of cruelty and greed weighed and partly excused (9.22–26, 11.19). Livy builds a tragic antagonist with Punic vices (21.4.9). Silenus and Sosylus are lost except for fragments, such as a Würzburg papyrus and Silenus' dream in Cicero, On Divination 1.49.

12Carry forward

  1. Hannibal won the battles and lost the war of allegiance: Rome's power lay in its Italian alliance and its refusal to negotiate in defeat, and the colonies that held after Cannae mattered more than the legions that died there.
  2. The war reaches us through two frames, Polybius' analysis of causes and constitution and Livy's tragedy of virtue against a Punic antagonist; the Carthaginian account survives only as quotation, so every judgement on Hannibal is a Roman or Greek one.
  3. The war made the Republic's models and its means of empire: Fabian delay and Scipionic daring, commands given to private men, permanent armies overseas, and a treaty with Carthage whose terms set up the conquests of 201–133.

The prompt this answers

Give me an in-depth report on the Second Punic War, 218 to 201 BC, following Polybius and Livy books 21 to 30. Cover: the Barcids in Spain, Hannibal's oath, Saguntum and the Ebro treaty and the question of war guilt; the march over the Alps (the route debate) and the elephants; Ticinus, Trebia and Trasimene; Fabius Maximus Cunctator and the strategy of delay; Cannae in 216 BC in tactical detail and its casualty figures; the Roman response (the defections in the south and Capua, the human sacrifice in the Forum Boarium, the slave volunteers, the refusal to ransom prisoners); Marcellus and the siege of Syracuse with Archimedes; Hasdrubal and the Metaurus in 207 BC; Scipio in Spain (New Carthage, Ilipa) and the invasion of Africa; Zama in 202 BC and the peace of 201; and Hannibal's later career and death. Then the historiography: Polybius' judgement of Hannibal, Livy's Hannibal as tragic antagonist, the lost Carthaginian side (Silenus and Sosylus), and why this war became the core of Roman identity. Format: about 3000 words, headed sections, name sources, flag disagreements, end with a 10-question self-test with answers and the three ideas I must carry forward.
