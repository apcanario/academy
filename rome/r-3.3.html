01The Latin alliance and the hill peoples

Livy, books 2 to 8, and Dionysius of Halicarnassus, whose account after 443 survives only in excerpts, wrote between three and four and a half centuries after these events, from annalists who began about 200 BC. Diodorus' book 14, thought to draw on an older and sparer source, and Plutarch's Camillus, of about AD 100, are the other narratives. Dates before 300 are Varronian (module 0.3).

After Lake Regillus (module 1.6) the consul Spurius Cassius made a treaty with the Latins in 493 (Livy 2.33). Dionysius gives its terms (6.95): peace while heaven and earth keep their places; no passage for each other's enemies; full aid to a partner attacked; equal shares of booty; contract disputes settled within ten days. Cicero remembered its bronze column behind the Rostra (Pro Balbo 53). The league's older history is module 1.1. The Hernici joined in 486 (Livy 2.41), and colonies were planted on the front, such as Signia, Norba and Ardea; Livy calls them Roman, but they became Latin states and were probably joint foundations.

The enemies were Sabellic-speaking hill peoples. The Aequi came down from the upper Anio each summer through the Algidus pass; the Volsci spread from the Liri valley to the coast, taking Antium, Velitrae, Satricum and Tarracina. At Satricum, Marijke Gnade's Dutch team found burials of a new type from the early fifth century, which fits Livy's Volscian conquest. The Sabines fought sporadically; in 460 the Sabine Appius Herdonius seized the Capitol with exiles and slaves and was dislodged with Tusculan help, as the treaty required (Livy 3.15–18). Coriolanus and Cincinnatus belong to module 3.2. The turn came in 431, when the dictator Aulus Postumius Tubertus beat Aequi and Volsci on the Algidus (4.26–29); Tarracina fell in 406 (4.59).

Where sources disagree. Livy expected readers to wonder where the Volsci and Aequi kept finding soldiers after so many defeats (6.12.2–5). R. M. Ogilvie (A Commentary on Livy Books 1–5, 1965) traced the stock scenes, and Gary Forsythe (A Critical History of Early Rome, 2005) treats most of the fighting as invention. Cornell (The Beginnings of Rome, 1995) keeps the pattern of mountain pressure and a defensive alliance, supported by Satricum and by the Tabula Veliterna, a third-century bronze from Velitrae in Volscian.

02Veii: the ten-year siege

Veii, 16 km north of Rome, is described as an Etruscan city in module 1.2. In Livy's first war with it the Fabii fell at the Cremera (module 3.2); in the second, about 437, Aulus Cornelius Cossus killed its king Lars Tolumnius (4.17–20). The third, 406–396, Livy tells as Rome's Trojan War (5.1–23), and his own speaker draws the comparison (5.4.11). Two institutional details are probably sound: pay for citizen soldiers from 406 (4.59–60), funded by a war tax, and winter campaigning (5.2).

In 398 the Alban lake rose to an unheard-of height without rain. A Veientine haruspex let slip that Veii would not fall until the water was let out; Delphi agreed, and the Romans drew it off on to the fields (5.15–16, 5.19.1). The outlet, the emissarium, a tunnel over a kilometre long through the crater rim, still drains the lake.

In 396 Marcus Furius Camillus, named dictator (5.19), drove a tunnel (cuniculus) towards the citadel and prayed twice: to Pythian Apollo, promising a tenth of the spoils, and to Juno Regina, Veii's protectress, that she follow the victors to a worthier temple (5.21.1–3). The tunnel came up in her temple. Livy adds that the Romans snatched the entrails from the king's sacrifice, a tale he calls fit for the stage and not worth affirming or refuting (5.21.8–9). The free population was sold (5.22.1; Diodorus 14.93.2). Her consent, a nod in Livy and speech in later tellers (5.22.3–7), and her Aventine temple of 392 belong with the rite in module 2.5.

Where sources disagree. Diodorus says only that Veii fell through an underground passage (14.93.2), so the tunnel stood in the earliest version; the haruspex, the lake and the entrails are fuller annalistic growth, which Plutarch (Camillus) tells with more wonder than Livy. Cornell (1995) accepts the fall in 396 and doubts the ten years. The lake story may be an aetiology of the emissarium, and the tunnel an inference from the drainage cuniculi that riddle Veii's countryside.

03What Veii did for Rome

Camillus triumphed behind white horses, likening himself to Jupiter (5.23.5–6); a tenth of the booty went to Delphi as a golden bowl (Diodorus 14.93.3–5), and in 391 a tribune's prosecution over the spoils drove him into exile at Ardea (Livy 5.32.7–9). The real prize was land, the ager Veientanus. Stephen Oakley calls it the largest single increase in Roman territory; Lily Ross Taylor reckoned it added about half. In 393 every plebeian, children included, was voted seven iugera, about 1.75 hectares (5.30.8); Diodorus gives four plethra or, in other accounts, twenty-eight (14.102.4). Veientine deserters received citizenship and land (6.4.4); in 387 four new rural tribes raised the total to twenty-five (6.5.8); Latin colonies at Sutrium and Nepet, the gates of Etruria (6.9.4), held the frontier. Rome now outweighed any combination of Latin states, and a treaty of equals could not survive that.

04Veii in the ground

Juno Regina's temple has never been securely identified. The best-known sanctuary lies below the western edge at Portonaccio, outside the walls. Votives name Menerva, and a sixth-century bucchero vessel was given there by Avile Vipiienas, the Aulus Vibenna of Roman tradition (module 1.5). Its temple, of about 510 BC, carried life-size painted terracotta figures along its roof-ridge. In 1916 Giulio Quirino Giglioli found the Apollo, striding in contest with Hercules over the hind, with a goddess holding a child and a head of Hermes; they are in the Villa Giulia. Pliny names a Veientine sculptor, Vulca, as maker of the Capitoline Jupiter (Natural History 35.157); giving him the Apollo is a modern guess. Propertius hears a shepherd's horn within Veii's walls (4.10.27–30), but votive deposits run on into the third century and Augustan Veii was a municipium: the ghost town is rhetoric.

05The Gauls in Italy and the Allia

Livy gives two accounts of the Gauls' arrival (5.33–35). In one, Arruns of Clusium, wronged by his ward Lucumo, lured them over the Alps with wine (5.33.2–4; Plutarch, Camillus 15). In the other they crossed two centuries earlier, under Tarquinius Priscus, and settled in waves: Insubres round Mediolanum, Cenomani, Boii and Lingones, and last the Senones on the Adriatic (5.34–35). Polybius describes unwalled villages and wealth in cattle and gold (2.17). From about 400 La Tène graves appear in the Po plain and the Marche, as in the Senonian cemetery at Montefortino, and Etruscan Felsina became Bononia of the Boii.

Where sources disagree. Polybius and the Arruns story make the occupation recent; Livy's preferred chronology puts it about 600 (5.34). Archaeology allows both: Celtic speakers of the Golasecca culture were writing Lepontic by the sixth century (module 1.1), while La Tène material marks a new movement shortly before 400. Livy, nearly four centuries later, may have fused the two.

In 391 the Senones besieged Clusium, which appealed to Rome. Three Fabian envoys joined the fighting and one killed a chieftain, breaking the law of nations; asked to surrender them, the people elected them consular tribunes (Livy 5.35–36; Diodorus 14.113). On 18 July, ever after the unlucky dies Alliensis (Livy 6.1.11), the Gauls met the Roman army at the Allia, eleven miles north of Rome; it broke at the first shout and most survivors fled to Veii (5.37–38). Diodorus puts the battle on the Tiber's right bank (14.114). The year is 390 for Varro and 387/6 by Polybius' synchronisms (1.6.1–2); module 0.3 explains the gap, and this module uses 390.

06The sack, the Capitol and the gold

Livy's sack (5.39–55), written some 360 years later, is his most finished set-piece. The Vestals carried the sacred objects to Caere in the cart of a plebeian, Lucius Albinius, who had put his family down to make room (5.40.7–10). The old men who had held curule office sat in their courtyards in their robes, on ivory chairs; the Gauls took them for statues until one stroked the beard of Marcus Papirius, who struck him with his ivory staff, and the killing began (5.41). The city was plundered and burned.

The Capitol held. Gaius Fabius Dorsuo walked through the Gallic lines to perform his clan's sacrifice on the Quirinal and back (5.46.1–3). When the Gauls climbed by night, the dogs were silent, but Juno's sacred geese, spared despite the famine, gave the alarm, and Marcus Manlius threw down the first Gaul (5.47; Diodorus 14.116). A yearly rite crucified dogs (Pliny, Natural History 29.57) and carried a goose in state (Plutarch, On the Fortune of the Romans). Manlius' later fall is module 3.2.

Famine forced terms: a thousand pounds of gold. When the Romans protested at false weights, the chief Livy calls Brennus threw his sword on the scale with vae victis, woe to the conquered (5.48.8–9). At that moment Camillus, recalled as dictator, arrived with the army from Veii, declared that Rome would be recovered with iron, not gold, and routed the Gauls in the city and again at the eighth milestone on the road to Gabii (5.49.1–6). The matrons who had given gold won funeral eulogies (5.50.7), and Caere public guest-friendship (5.50.3).

Where sources disagree. Polybius knows no rescue: threatened at home by the Veneti, the Gauls made a treaty and left with their plunder after seven months (2.18.3, 2.22.5). Diodorus has the gold paid and later recovered by Camillus from Gauls besieging Veascium (14.116.7, 14.117.5); Strabo credits the Caeretans (5.2.3); Suetonius says a Livius Drusus brought the Senones' gold back from Gaul, and that Camillus had not, as the story went, wrested it back (Tiberius 3.2). Mommsen ("Die gallische Katastrophe", 1878) took Diodorus for the oldest Roman version and Livy's rescue for patriotic growth; most since agree.

07What the Greeks heard and what the ground shows

The sack is the first Roman event recorded by outsiders near the time. Heraclides Ponticus, Plato's pupil, wrote that an army from the Hyperboreans had taken a Greek city called Rome by the great sea (Plutarch, Camillus 22.2); Aristotle knew that Celts took it and one Lucius saved it (22.4); Theopompus, says Pliny, recorded only its capture by Gauls (Natural History 3.57). None names Camillus. Justin has Gauls who had burned Rome months before seeking alliance with Dionysius I of Syracuse (20.5), and Diodorus sends them on to Iapygia (14.117.7): a war band heading south, perhaps for mercenary service, with Rome a rich stop on the way. Livy's claim that most records burned (6.1.2) sits beside his report that the treaties and the Twelve Tables were recovered (6.1.10; module 0.1).

Where sources disagree. Livy burns the whole city (5.42–43); Diodorus spares a few houses on the Palatine (14.115.6); Polybius speaks of occupation, not destruction (2.18.2). Early excavators took a layer of burnt roof tiles in the Comitium for the Gallic fire (Platner and Ashby, A Topographical Dictionary of Ancient Rome, 1929); it is now usually dated earlier, and Cornell (1995) finds no destruction horizon securely attributable to the Gauls, though traces of fire of about this date have been reported on the Palatine. On a site of timber and mud-brick rebuilt for twenty-four centuries, absence of evidence is weak evidence of absence.

08Rebuilding: the wall and the second founder

Tribunes urged migration to Veii, its buildings intact; Camillus answered, in the book's longest speech, that Rome's gods and rites were bound to its soil (Livy 5.51–54), and a centurion's order to his standard-bearer, hic manebimus optime, here we shall do best to stay, settled it (5.55.1–2). Rebuilding was hasty: public tiles, free quarrying, sewers left under private houses, a city that looked occupied rather than laid out (5.55.2–5; Diodorus 14.116.8–9).

The lasting answer was the wall. In 378 the censors let a contract for one of squared stone (6.32.1). Most of the surviving circuit, about 11 km long and enclosing some 426 hectares on the usual modern reckoning, is of Grotta Oscura tufa quarried in Veii's former territory: the conquest built the defence. Masons' marks in Greek letters suggest Greek contractors. "Servian" comes from Livy's report that Servius Tullius walled the city (1.44.3; module 1.5).

Where sources disagree. Gösta Säflund (Le mura di Roma repubblicana, 1932) dated the Grotta Oscura circuit after 390 and left the older stretches in cappellaccio tufa as fragments of earlier defences. Gabriele Cifani (Architettura romana arcaica, 2008) assigns the cappellaccio work to a sixth-century circuit on much the same line, giving the Servian name a real core. Livy has both a regal wall (1.44) and a fourth-century one (6.32).

Livy hails Camillus as a Romulus, father of his country and second founder of the city (5.49.7). Plutarch counts five dictatorships and four triumphs, yet no consulship (Camillus 1). Livy wrote book 5 when some proposed calling Octavian Romulus as a second founder (Suetonius, Augustus 7.2), and his pious, exiled, temple-restoring Camillus has an Augustan profile. His temple of Concord of 367 is probably invented (module 3.1). The man and the conquest of Veii are secure; the saviour of 390 is not.

09The Latin War and the settlement of 338

Defeat loosened the alliance: Latins and Hernici broke away (Livy 6.2) and Praeneste fought Rome. Rome answered with incorporation: in 381 Tusculum received full citizenship while keeping its own magistrates, the first municipium (6.25–26). Gallic raids drove the Latins back, and the treaty was renewed in 358 (7.12); the Carthage treaty of 348 envisaged raids on Latin towns not subject to Rome (Polybius 3.24), implying a claim to the rest. The Samnite wars are module 3.4.

In 340 the Latin praetors Lucius Annius of Setia and Lucius Numisius of Circeii demanded one consul and half the Senate (8.3–6). Latins, Campanians, Volsci, Sidicini and Aurunci fought together and lost at Veseris, the battle of Decius' devotio and of Torquatus' execution of his son (8.7–10; module 3.2), and at Trifanum (8.11). In 338 the consuls Lucius Furius Camillus and Gaius Maenius finished the war at Pedum and the Astura (8.13); the beaks of Antium's warships were fixed to the speakers' platform, thereafter the Rostra (8.14.12). Livy sets out the settlement community by community (8.14):

CommunityTreatment
Lanuviumfull citizenship; its cults returned, the grove and temple of Juno Sospita shared with the Roman people
Aricia, Nomentum, Pedumfull citizenship
Tusculumcitizenship kept; the ringleaders punished
Velitraewalls razed, senators deported across the Tiber, colonists settled on their land
Antiumcitizen colony; warships removed and the sea forbidden
Tibur, Praenesteland confiscated, partly for an old alliance with the Gauls; allies still under treaty
The other Latinsintermarriage, trade and common councils with one another forbidden
Capua, Fundi, Formiae, Cumae, Suessulacitizenship without the vote

The league ceased as a political body; only the Latin festival on the Alban Mount continued. In its place stood bilateral ties, each community bound to Rome and barred from combining with the others. Three statuses resulted: full citizens in self-governing municipia; citizens without the vote (cives sine suffragio), who served in the legions and paid tax but could not vote at Rome; and allies such as Tibur, Praeneste and the Latin colonies, keeping rights of intermarriage, trade and migration with Rome. Those Latin rights soon came loose from descent: from Cales in 334, new Latin colonies across Italy held them as a legal status (module 3.4). Rome had learned at Veii to annex and at Tusculum to incorporate; in 338 it combined both into a system it could extend.

Where sources disagree. Gellius (16.13.7) makes Caere the first community without the vote, rewarded for sheltering the sacred objects in 390; Strabo (5.2.3) treats the status as a slight, and the censors' "Caeretan tablets" became a list of disfranchised citizens. A. N. Sherwin-White (The Roman Citizenship, 1939) read it as an honour grown from reciprocal hospitality that hardened into subjection; Michel Humbert (Municipium et civitas sine suffragio, 1978) saw it from the start as a means of incorporation carrying the burdens of tax and levy.

10Self-test

  1. What did the Foedus Cassianum provide, and what does it imply about Rome's standing among the Latins in 493?
    Answer

    Perpetual peace, mutual aid, no passage for enemies, equal shares of booty and quick settlement of private contracts (Dionysius 6.95). It is a treaty between Rome and the Latins as equal parties, not an instrument of Roman rule, which is why the growth of Rome after Veii strained it.

  2. Why is Livy's annual chronicle of Volscian and Aequian wars suspect, and what supports its broad pattern?
    Answer

    It repeats stock scenes, and Livy himself wondered how the enemy kept finding soldiers (6.12.2–5). The pattern of Sabellic pressure is supported by the Volscian text from Velitrae, the new burials at Satricum and the joint colonies on the front.

  3. Which elements of the fall of Veii stand in Diodorus' spare account, and which appear only in the fuller tradition?
    Answer

    Diodorus has the dictator, the underground passage, the sale of the population and the bowl for Delphi (14.93). The Alban lake prophecy, the snatched entrails and the nodding Juno belong to the fuller annalistic version in Livy and Plutarch.

  4. Which details of the siege of Veii are probably sound, and what shaped the rest?
    Answer

    Pay for citizen soldiers from 406, funded by a war tax (Livy 4.59–60), and winter campaigning (5.2) are institutional details. The ten years and the Trojan comparison drawn by Livy's own speaker (5.4.11) are literary shaping; Cornell accepts the fall in 396 but doubts the ten years.

  5. What did the annexation of Veii's territory change for Rome?
    Answer

    In Oakley's judgement it was the largest single increase in Roman territory, and on Taylor's reckoning it added about half: allotments of seven iugera per plebeian (Livy 5.30.8), four new tribes in 387 (6.5.8) and colonies at Sutrium and Nepet. It also made Rome larger than any Latin combination, undermining the treaty of equals.

  6. What does the Portonaccio sanctuary show, and why is the Apollo's attribution to Vulca uncertain?
    Answer

    A rich extra-mural cult of Menerva, a temple of about 510 BC with life-size terracotta figures on its roof, and votive use continuing after 396. Pliny (35.157) names Vulca only as maker of the Capitoline Jupiter; linking him to the Apollo is a modern guess.

  7. Compare how Polybius, Diodorus and Livy have the Gauls leave Rome.
    Answer

    Polybius: they made a treaty and went home because the Veneti threatened their land (2.18.3). Diodorus: they took a thousand pounds of gold, which Camillus later recovered at Veascium (14.116–117). Livy: Camillus arrived during the weighing and defeated them on the spot (5.49).

  8. What do the Greek notices of the sack tell us, and what do they leave out?
    Answer

    Heraclides, Aristotle and Theopompus show that the capture of Rome by Celts was known in Greece within a generation or two (Plutarch, Camillus 22; Pliny 3.57). None mentions Camillus; Aristotle's saviour is a Lucius.

  9. Why is the "Servian" wall so called, and what does its stone reveal?
    Answer

    Livy credits Servius Tullius with walling the city (1.44.3), but the main circuit matches the contract of 378 (6.32.1). It is built of Grotta Oscura tufa from Veii's former territory, so the conquest supplied the stone for the post-sack defence.

  10. Name the three statuses produced by the settlement of 338, with an example of each.
    Answer

    Full citizenship (Lanuvium, Aricia, Tusculum); citizenship without the vote (Capua, Fundi, Formiae); allied Latin status under treaty (Tibur, Praeneste and the Latin colonies). The remaining Latins were forbidden intermarriage, trade and councils with one another (Livy 8.14).

11Carry forward

  1. For Rome before 300 BC the nearer witnesses (the Greek notices, Polybius, Diodorus) are the sparer ones; prodigies, rescues and saviours accumulate in the later annalists, so test each Livian set-piece against the barest version.
  2. Veii set the pattern of Roman expansion: conquered land became allotments, tribes and colonies for citizens, and each gain moved Rome further from partner to master of its allies.
  3. The settlement of 338 turned peoples into legal statuses: full citizenship, citizenship without the vote and Latin rights detached from Latium, each tied to Rome alone. That template governs Italy until the Social War.

Cover: the Foedus Cassianum and the wars with the Aequi, Volsci and Sabines; the ten-year siege of Veii and its fall to Camillus in 396 BC (the tunnel, the evocatio of Juno, which module 2.5 explains, the draining of the Alban lake), what Veii's territory did for Rome, and the archaeology of Veii (the Portonaccio sanctuary, the Apollo of Veii); the Gauls in Italy, the Allia and the sack of Rome (390 BC in Varro, 387/6 in Polybius), Brennus and 'woe to the conquered', the geese and Marcus Manlius, the senators in their chairs, Camillus' rescue and the gold, and the early Greek notices of the sack in Aristotle and Heraclides; whether the archaeology shows a destruction; the rebuilding, the 'Servian' wall in tufa from Veii's quarries, Camillus as second founder; and the Latin War of 340–338 BC and the settlement that dissolved the league into separate treaties, citizenship without the vote and colonies. Format: about 2500 words, headed sections, name sources, flag disagreements, end with a 10-question self-test with answers and the three ideas I must carry forward.
