01The land question as Appian and Plutarch tell it

The continuous narratives are late and Greek: Plutarch's paired Lives, early in the second century AD, and Appian's Civil Wars, about AD 160, some 290 years after 133. Both drew on lost Latin historians, and both inherited a story the Gracchi first told. Contemporary evidence is thin: fragments of Gaius' speeches, the land commission's boundary stones, an extortion law on bronze.

Appian (1.7) gives the problem in one movement. As Rome conquered Italy it confiscated land, and it opened what war had left uncultivated to anyone who would farm it, for a tenth of the grain and a fifth of the fruit. The rich took most of this public land (ager publicus), added their poor neighbours' plots by purchase or by force, and worked the whole with slaves, who were exempt from the levy and multiplied, while the Italians dwindled under poverty, taxes and service. An old cap of 500 iugera a holder was ignored (1.8); Livy dates such a cap to 367 (module 3.1), and module 4.4 describes the slave-run estate itself.

Plutarch (Tiberius Gracchus 8) adds that the dispossessed lost interest in serving and in raising children, and quotes a pamphlet of Gaius: crossing Etruria on his way to Numantia in 137, Tiberius found the land worked by foreign slaves and almost empty of free men (8.7). Tiberius' own words survive in paraphrase: Italy's wild beasts have their lairs, but the men who fight and die for Italy have air and light and nothing else, masters of the world without a clod of earth of their own (9.4–5). Appian's Tiberius argues from manpower: a kindred people was sinking into poverty and fewness, and slaves, useless in war, had lately risen in Sicily (1.9). Appian speaks of Italians, Plutarch of Romans; whether allies could receive land is uncertain.

02The modern revision: a vanishing peasantry?

Twentieth-century historians built this into a model. Arnold Toynbee (Hannibal's Legacy, 1965), P. A. Brunt (Italian Manpower, 1971) and Keith Hopkins (Conquerors and Slaves, 1978) argued that long service overseas ruined soldiers' farms, that the rich bought them with the profits of empire and stocked them with captives, and that the pool of propertied men liable for the legions shrank.

The model has been dismantled from several sides. Nathan Rosenstein (Rome at War, 2004) argued that the levy fell on young men who married late and whose labour their families could spare, so that service did not in itself wreck farms; the crisis of the 130s came from population growth and partible inheritance, too many heirs for too little land. Luuk de Ligt ("Poverty and demography: the case of the Gracchan land reforms", Mnemosyne, 2004; Peasants, Citizens and Soldiers, 2012) likewise traced the rural misery to continuing population growth rather than to slaves replacing citizens. Survey archaeology has found small rural sites persisting through the second century in many districts, though Dominic Rathbone ("Poor peasants and silent sherds", 2008) has pressed the question of how far the rural poor are visible to survey at all. What survives of the old picture is real poverty and a recruiting problem; its cause, slaves displacing citizens or too many sons for too few fields, is open.

Where sources disagree. Appian (1.7) and Plutarch (Tiberius Gracchus 8) describe a shrinking free population, but the census totals in Livy's Periochae barely move, 317,933 citizens in 136/135 (56) and 318,823 in 131/130 (59), then jump to 394,736 in 125/124 (60). The traditional reading, Brunt's among them, credits the jump to the commission's allottees, who now had property to declare; another reads the flat figures as men evading the census to escape the levy, corrected in 125/124.

03Tiberius' law and the deposition of Octavius, 133

Tiberius was the son of a twice-consul of the same name and of Cornelia, daughter of Scipio Africanus. As quaestor in 137 he negotiated the Numantine treaty that the Senate repudiated (module 4.4), the root of his breach with the Senate according to Cicero (De haruspicum responso 43) and Velleius (2.2.1). Plutarch (8) adds the Stoic Blossius of Cumae and Cornelia's complaint that she was still called Scipio's mother-in-law, not mother of the Gracchi.

As tribune for 133 he drafted his bill with his father-in-law Appius Claudius Pulcher, leader of the Senate, with Publius Licinius Crassus Mucianus and with the consul Publius Mucius Scaevola (Plutarch 9.1). It touched public land only. Each holder kept 500 iugera, now secure, and 250 for each son; the excess went to poor citizens in allotments that could not be sold, assigned by three commissioners (Appian 1.9–10). He took it straight to the plebeian council, legal since 287 (module 3.1) but without the customary prior approval of the Senate.

His colleague Marcus Octavius vetoed it. Tiberius suspended public business by edict and sealed the treasury (Plutarch 10.5), then asked the tribes to depose Octavius. When seventeen of the thirty-five had voted, he begged his colleague to yield; Octavius refused, the eighteenth tribe made a majority, and he was dragged from the Rostra (Appian 1.12; Plutarch 12). The law passed, with Tiberius, Gaius and Appius as commissioners. A tribune who works against the people, Tiberius said, is no tribune (Plutarch 15); Plutarch himself, comparing the Gracchi with Agis and Cleomenes, calls the deposition the gravest charge against him (Comparison 5.1).

Where sources disagree. Appian (1.9) gives 500 iugera plus 250 a son; the Periochae (58) give a ceiling of 1,000, perhaps the same rule with two sons counted. Only Plutarch has a first, milder draft that paid the occupiers the value of what they surrendered (9.2), replaced after the veto by a harsher bill (10.3). Whether that draft existed, or was invented to make Octavius the aggressor, is uncertain.

04Pergamon, a second tribunate and the killing on the Capitol

That spring Attalus III of Pergamon died and left his kingdom to the Roman people. Tiberius proposed that the royal money stock the new farms and that the people, not the Senate, decide about the kingdom's cities (Plutarch 14.1), striking at the Senate's control of finance and foreign affairs; Quintus Pompeius claimed that the envoy Eudemus had brought Tiberius a diadem and a purple robe (14.2). Then he stood again for 132, which his enemies called unlawful (Appian 1.14).

On election day the Senate met in the temple of Fides. Tiberius touched his head to signal danger to his life; his enemies said he was asking for a crown (Plutarch 19.2). The consul Scaevola refused to kill a citizen without trial. Publius Cornelius Scipio Nasica, pontifex maximus and Tiberius' cousin, called on all who would save the state to follow him (19.3), and senators and their attendants went up the Capitol with clubs and bench legs. A fellow tribune, Publius Satureius, struck Tiberius down (19.6). Plutarch counts more than three hundred dead, none killed with iron (19.6); Appian (1.16) says only that many died. The bodies went into the Tiber. For Plutarch this was the first civil strife since the kings to end in bloodshed (20.1).

A special inquiry in 132 executed the rhetorician Diophanes (Plutarch 20.3); Blossius said he would have done anything Tiberius asked, even burned the Capitol (Cicero, On Friendship 37). Scipio Aemilianus, at Numantia, quoted Athena on Aegisthus, "so perish any other who does such things" (Odyssey 1.47; Plutarch 21.4), and later said that Tiberius was justly killed if he had meant to seize the state (Velleius 2.4).

Where sources disagree. Appian (1.16) and Plutarch have Nasica draw his toga over his head, and Appian offers three explanations: a signal to followers, a battle sign, or shame before the gods. Velleius (2.3.1) and Valerius Maximus have it wrapped round his left arm. Donald Earl read the veiling as the dress of sacrifice; Jerzy Linderski ("The Pontiff and the Tribune", 2002) argued instead for consecratio capitis, the pontiff handing a would-be king over to the gods, with human hands executing their anger.

05The land commission and its boundary stones

The commission outlived Tiberius. Crassus took his place, and after Crassus and Appius died Marcus Fulvius Flaccus and Gaius Papirius Carbo joined Gaius (Appian 1.18). Its title survives on boundary stones from Campania, Lucania and Apulia: IIIviri agris iudicandis adsignandis, three men for judging and assigning land. Judging came first, since every holder had to prove what was private and what public. No source gives the number or size of the allotments.

A road inscription from Polla in Lucania has an unnamed magistrate claim to be the first to make shepherds give way to ploughmen on public land: primus fecei ut de agro poplico aratoribus cederent paastores. It has been assigned to Publius Popillius Laenas, consul in 132 and Tiberius' persecutor, and to Titus Annius Rufus, praetor by 131; its date and its link to the Gracchan scheme are disputed.

The allies, whose leading men held much of the land under review, complained. In 129 Scipio Aemilianus had the commission's judicial power transferred to a consul, who promptly left for Illyricum (Appian 1.19). Scipio was found dead soon after, and suspicion fell on his wife Sempronia, the Gracchi's sister (Periochae 59; Appian 1.20).

06Gaius Gracchus' programme, 123–122

Gaius had served on the commission and as quaestor in Sardinia; Cicero reports, from Coelius Antipater, his dream of Tiberius warning that he would die the same death (On Divination 1.56). The allies' grievance had sharpened: as consul in 125 Flaccus proposed citizenship for them (Appian 1.21), and that year the Latin colony of Fregellae revolted and was razed by the praetor Lucius Opimius (Periochae 60). Tribune for 123 and 122, Gaius was the greatest orator of his day. The order of his laws is uncertain.

  • Protection. No citizen was to be tried on a capital charge without the people's order (Cicero, Pro Rabirio perduellionis reo 12); Popillius went into exile.
  • Grain. Every citizen might buy a monthly ration at 6⅓ asses a modius (Periochae 60) from public granaries; Appian (1.21) wrongly makes it free. Peter Garnsey and Rathbone (Journal of Roman Studies, 1985) saw it as giving Rome's supply regularity and storage after shortages in 142, 138 and 129, not as a response to Mediterranean-wide prices. Piso Frugi, who had opposed it, queued for his share (Cicero, Tusculan Disputations 3.48).
  • Land and colonies. The agrarian law was renewed; colonies went out to Scolacium and Tarentum (Velleius 1.15), and the tribune Rubrius' law founded Junonia at Carthage, the first colony overseas, with 6,000 settlers from all Italy, more than the law allowed (Appian 1.24).
  • Soldiers and provinces. Soldiers were clothed at public expense and none conscripted under seventeen (Plutarch, Gaius Gracchus 5); the tithe of Asia was auctioned by the censors at Rome (Cicero, Verrines 2.3.12), a vast contract for the publicani (module 4.4).
  • Juries. The extortion court passed to the equites (Appian 1.22). Gaius said he had broken the Senate; Varro said he made the state two-headed, a source of civil discord (in Nonius).

The citizenship bill of 122 broke him. Appian (1.23) has full citizenship for the Latins and the vote for the other allies; Velleius (2.6) has citizenship for all Italians. The consul Gaius Fannius warned the citizens that the Latins would leave them no room at assemblies and games, and non-citizens were ordered out of the city for the vote (Appian 1.23). The tribune Marcus Livius Drusus vetoed it and outbid Gaius with twelve colonies of 3,000 poor citizens each, in Plutarch's figure (9), apparently never founded. After seventy days in Africa (Plutarch 11), Gaius failed to win a third tribunate. Deep dive module 11.3 follows the grain laws to Clodius.

Where sources disagree. Appian (1.22) and the extortion law on the bronze Tabula Bembina, usually identified with the Acilian law of 123 or 122, give the juries to non-senators: 450 jurors a year, senators and their relatives excluded. Plutarch (Gaius Gracchus 5) has 300 equites joined to the 300 senators, and the Periochae (60) have 600 equites enrolled in the Senate itself. Most follow the inscription; Plutarch may preserve an earlier proposal.

07The final decree and the death of Gaius, 121

Opimius was consul for 121, and a bill was brought to repeal the Junonia law. During a sacrifice on the Capitol a consular attendant, Antyllius, was killed by Gracchans (Plutarch 13; Appian 1.25). The Senate decreed that the consul should see to it that the state suffer no harm (Cicero, Against Catiline 1.4); Caesar later called this the extreme and final decree (Civil War 1.5). It gave no new legal power, only the Senate's backing for whatever the consul did: where Scaevola in 133 had refused to kill without trial, Opimius now had cover.

Gaius and Flaccus held the Aventine. Opimius attacked with armed senators, equites and Cretan archers (Plutarch 16); Flaccus was killed, and Gaius fled across the Tiber to a grove of Furrina, where a slave killed him at his order and then himself. Opimius had promised the heads' weight in gold (Appian 1.26), strangled accomplices in prison and built a temple of Concord (module 3.1); someone wrote under its inscription that mad discord had produced a temple of Concord (Plutarch 17.6). He was acquitted in 120 (Periochae 61). Within a few years, Appian says (1.27), the allotments were made saleable and distribution stopped.

Where sources disagree. Plutarch counts 3,000 dead in 121 (17.5), names the slave Philocrates (17.2) and has Septimuleius fill Gaius' head with lead so that it weighed 17⅔ pounds (17.4); Valerius Maximus (9.4.3) also tells the Septimuleius story. Appian (1.26) gives no total and leaves the slave unnamed; Velleius (2.6) calls him Euporus. Deep dive module 11.3 treats the decree as legalised violence.

08Cornelia

Cornelia bore the elder Tiberius Gracchus twelve children, three of whom grew up: Tiberius, Gaius and Sempronia. Widowed, she refused a king of Egypt (Plutarch, Tiberius Gracchus 1). Her letters were read as prose: Cicero (Brutus 211) judged that her sons were raised less in their mother's lap than in her speech. Pliny saw her seated statue (Natural History 34.31), and a base survives inscribed Cornelia Africani f. Gracchorum, daughter of Africanus, mother of the Gracchi. Deep dive module 11.4 goes further.

Two extracts of a letter to Gaius survive among the fragments of Cornelius Nepos. Written, it seems, before his first tribunate, it begs him not to stand: apart from Tiberius' killers no enemy has caused her such grief; when she is dead he may invoke her as a parent-god; will the family's madness never end?

Where sources disagree. Plutarch (Gaius Gracchus 13.2) knew letters of Cornelia that some read as hinting that she sent hired men to Rome disguised as harvesters to back Gaius, while others said she disapproved of him; the Nepos extracts are pure disapproval. Hans Ulrich Instinsky (Chiron, 1971) warned that genuine or forged is too crude a choice: the letter may be a fiction in the work of a historian close to the Gracchi's time, deceiving only once detached from its context.

09Heroes or demagogues

Cicero's verdict follows his audience. To the people the brothers were two most distinguished and gifted men, most devoted to the plebs (De lege agraria 2.10). In the philosophical works Tiberius tried to seize kingship, or rather reigned a few months (On Friendship 41); Nasica served the state as well as Scipio did at Numantia (On Duties 1.76); and the brothers are counted among the justly killed (2.43). Sallust (Jugurtha 42) has them assert the liberty of the plebs against a guilty nobility, yet lack moderation in their desire to win. Velleius gives Tiberius a blameless life and upright intentions, then has him desert the good cause (2.2), praises Nasica (2.3), and calls Gaius' overseas colonies his most pernicious measure (2.7). Plutarch's pairing with Agis and Cleomenes, Spartan kings who died trying to restore land to citizens, makes them noble natures spurred by love of honour, and his comparison ranks Tiberius first of the four in virtue (Comparison 5.6). Tacitus files them among the agitators of the plebs (Annals 3.27).

10Why 133 looked like the beginning of the end

Appian (1.1–2) states the thesis: earlier quarrels of people and Senate had ended in concession, Coriolanus apart, and the sword was never carried into the assembly until Tiberius Gracchus fell. Velleius (2.3.3) calls 133 the beginning of civil bloodshed and of swords unpunished, after which right was overwhelmed by force; Cicero's Laelius says that Tiberius' death, and before it his whole tribunate, split one people into two parts (On the Republic 1.31). The date is a construction: the theories of moral decline put the rot earlier (module 4.5), module 5.6 reads Sallust's, and deep dive module 11.8 weighs the explanations. Yet it names something real. A tribune used the plebeian council's legal sovereignty against senatorial custom, and senators answered with force, authorised by 121. Annalists writing after 133 also carried the quarrel back into the early Republic (module 3.1).

11Self-test

  1. How do Appian and Plutarch explain the agrarian problem, and why should the picture of an empty Etruria be read with caution?
    Answer

    The rich occupied public land and absorbed small farms, worked them with slaves exempt from service, and the free population dwindled (Appian 1.7; Plutarch, Tiberius Gracchus 8). The Etruria scene comes from a pamphlet by Gaius (8.7): partisan testimony relayed by Plutarch some two and a half centuries later.

  2. What do Rosenstein and de Ligt put in place of the decline model?
    Answer

    Rosenstein (2004): late-marrying young men could be spared for the army, so service did not ruin farms; the crisis came from population growth and partible inheritance. De Ligt (2004, 2012): rural misery from continuing population growth, not slaves displacing citizens.

  3. What did Tiberius' law provide according to Appian, and how do Livy's epitome and Plutarch differ?
    Answer

    Holders of public land kept 500 iugera plus 250 per son; the surplus went in inalienable allotments to poor citizens through a board of three (Appian 1.9–10). The Periochae (58) give a ceiling of 1,000; Plutarch alone has a first draft with compensation, withdrawn after Octavius' veto (9.2, 10.3).

  4. Which actions of Tiberius in 133, beyond the land law, broke with senatorial custom?
    Answer

    Deposing his colleague Octavius by vote of the tribes (Appian 1.12; Plutarch 12); proposing that Attalus' money fund the new farms and that the people decide on the Pergamene cities (Plutarch 14.1), invading the Senate's control of finance and foreign affairs; and standing for a second consecutive tribunate (Appian 1.14).

  5. Who led the attack on Tiberius, how many died, and how has Nasica's gesture been read?
    Answer

    Scipio Nasica, pontifex maximus, after the consul Scaevola refused to act. Plutarch counts more than 300 dead (19.6); Appian says only that many died (1.16). The veiled head (Appian, Plutarch) has been read as the dress of sacrifice (Earl) or as consecratio capitis of a would-be king (Linderski, 2002); Velleius has the toga wrapped round his arm.

  6. What do the boundary stones and the Polla inscription show about the commission?
    Answer

    The stones, from Campania, Lucania and Apulia, give the board's title, IIIviri agris iudicandis adsignandis, and its changing membership, so it was surveying and judging land across the south. The Polla stone's claim to have made shepherds yield to ploughmen on public land may refer to the same policy, but its author (Popillius Laenas or Annius Rufus) and date are disputed.

  7. Name four of Gaius' measures and the group each served.
    Answer

    Any four of: grain at 6⅓ asses a modius for citizens (Periochae 60); colonies, including Junonia at Carthage, for the landless; extortion juries for the equites (Appian 1.22); the Asian tithe let at Rome for the publicani (Cicero, Verrines 2.3.12); clothing and no conscripts under seventeen for soldiers (Plutarch, Gaius Gracchus 5); citizenship for the Latins (Appian 1.23).

  8. What was the Senate's decree of 121, and what changed between 133 and 121?
    Answer

    That the consul see to it that the state suffer no harm (Cicero, Against Catiline 1.4), Caesar's "extreme and final decree". In 133 the consul refused to kill without trial and Nasica acted as a private citizen; in 121 the Senate gave the consul Opimius cover to use force, and his acquittal in 120 (Periochae 61) confirmed it.

  9. What does the letter attributed to Cornelia say, and why is its authenticity uncertain?
    Answer

    Preserved among Nepos' fragments, it begs Gaius not to seek the tribunate and says that no enemy except Tiberius' killers has caused her such grief. Plutarch knew letters read as showing her complicity (Gaius Gracchus 13.2), and Instinsky (1971) suggested a fiction in the work of a historian close to the Gracchi's time.

  10. Which ancient writers made 133 the beginning of the end, and on what grounds?
    Answer

    Appian (1.1–2): the first time the sword entered the assembly. Velleius (2.3.3): the beginning of civil bloodshed and unpunished violence. Cicero (On the Republic 1.31): one people split into two. Plutarch (Tiberius Gracchus 20.1): the first civil strife since the kings to end in bloodshed.

12Carry forward

  1. The agrarian crisis as Appian and Plutarch tell it is Gracchan rhetoric relayed by imperial Greek writers; modern work (Rosenstein, de Ligt) makes it a crisis of rural poverty and population rather than of a vanishing peasantry. Either way the landless citizen soldier became the problem that later generals solved with land for their veterans.
  2. The Gracchi showed that the tribunate and the plebeian council could legislate over the Senate's head on land, money, courts and empire; the Senate answered with force, private in 133 and authorised by the final decree in 121. Every later crisis of the Republic repeats both moves.
  3. Whether the Gracchi were martyrs or demagogues depended on who was speaking and to whom: Cicero praised them to the people and damned them in his philosophical works. Read every source on them, and every source that starts the fall in 133, for its side.
