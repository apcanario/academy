The emperor at work: petition and response

Module 6.1 showed what the princeps was: a bundle of powers voted by the Senate, with the army beneath. Fergus Millar's The Emperor in the Roman World (1977) asked what he did all day, and answered from inscriptions, papyri and the law codes rather than from the court historians. His formula is that the emperor was what he did, and what he did was mostly respond. Cities sent embassies with loyal decrees and requests for privileges; individuals handed up petitions (libelli); governors wrote for rulings; litigants appealed. The emperor heard, judged and replied. Answers to petitions were written beneath them as subscriptions and posted in public; answers to cities and officials went as letters, which the cities cut in stone. Initiative came from below, and the rhythm of government was the queue.

Dio gives the norm in an anecdote. A woman stopped Hadrian on a journey; he said he had no time; she cried that then he should stop being emperor, and he turned and heard her (69.6.3). About 181 the tenants of an imperial estate in Africa, the saltus Burunitanus, petitioned Commodus that the lessee and the procurators had sent soldiers to beat them, Roman citizens among them; his reply, inscribed on the estate, ordered the procurators to exact no more labour days than the standing rules allowed (CIL VIII 10570). Antoninus Pius wrote to Ephesus in 145 rebuking the citizens for undervaluing Vedius Antoninus, a benefactor who preferred buildings to shows. Embassies were constant and costly: Byzantium, Pliny found, paid 12,000 sesterces a year to send a man to greet the emperor with a decree (Letters 10.43).

The paperwork passed through palace offices: ab epistulis for letters, a libellis for petitions, a rationibus for accounts. Under the Julio-Claudians imperial freedmen held them (module 7.1). Vitellius gave palace posts to knights (Tacitus, Histories 1.58), and the Historia Augusta makes Hadrian the first to put equestrians in charge of letters and petitions (Hadrian 22.8); the biographer Suetonius was his ab epistulis.

Where sources disagree. Keith Hopkins ("Rules of Evidence", Journal of Roman Studies 1978) attacked the book's method, an accumulation of evidence without an explicit model, and its picture of the emperor as administrator and judge, which left war, the army and money at the margins. Millar's case rests on the documents, which overwhelmingly record requests and replies. The reactive model fits the civil sphere best: Trajan's Dacian conquest and Hadrian's Wall answered no petition.

Senators, knights and government without bureaucracy

The emperor's officers came from two orders. Augustus cut the Senate to about six hundred (Dio 54.14) and fixed a property qualification of a million sesterces in Dio's account, 1.2 million in Suetonius' (Augustus 41.1). Pliny's career, set out on an inscription from his home town of Comum (CIL V 5262), shows the senatorial ladder: a minor judicial board, military tribune in Syria, quaestor, tribune of the plebs, praetor, prefect of the military treasury and then of the treasury of Saturn, consul in 100, augur, curator of the Tiber's banks and the city's sewers, and finally legate of Bithynia. Each post lasted a year or a few. The consulship, now shared among several pairs of suffects each year, was the rank that qualified a man for the great military provinces; the proconsulships of Asia and Africa crowned a career.

Knights, with a fortune of 400,000 sesterces, served as prefects and tribunes of auxiliary units, then as procurators graded by salary at 60,000, 100,000 and 200,000 sesterces. They managed imperial revenues and estates, governed small provinces such as Judaea before 66, and filled the great prefectures of the watch, the grain supply, Egypt and the guard. H.-G. Pflaum's count of procuratorial posts (Les procurateurs équestres, 1950) rises from about 25 under Augustus to about 136 under Commodus. Promotion ran on recommendation. Pliny asks Trajan for senatorial rank for Voconius Romanus, whose mother had made over four million sesterces to qualify him (10.4), and Richard Saller (Personal Patronage under the Early Empire, 1982) argued that patronage, not seniority, moved careers.

Set these men against Bruce Frier's estimate of some 61 million people by AD 164 (Cambridge Ancient History XI, 2000): a few dozen governors with their legates and quaestors, a little over a hundred procurators, the imperial slaves and freedmen who kept the accounts. Peter Garnsey and Richard Saller called the chapter on it "Government without bureaucracy" (The Roman Empire, 1987). Amateurs on short terms, advised by their friends, presided; the cities and the army did the work.

The governor on the spot: Pliny in Bithynia

Module 4.4 described the Republican governor: a magistrate for a year with a quaestor, a few legates and his friends, answerable only on his return. Module 6.4 showed Augustus dividing the provinces between proconsuls chosen by lot and his own legates. What changed afterwards was supervision. Legates of Caesar served for several years under written instructions (mandata); procurators reported separately on revenue; provincials could go over the governor's head. The Senate still judged extortion: Pliny prosecuted Marius Priscus, proconsul of Africa, in 100 (Letters 2.11).

Book 10 of Pliny's letters is the only surviving file of a governor's correspondence with an emperor. Bithynia-Pontus was a public province, but about 110 Trajan sent Pliny as his own legate with consular power to put the cities' finances in order. Module 2.5 reads the letters on the Christians (10.96–97); the rest show the work.

  • Money. Nicomedia had spent 3,318,000 sesterces on an aqueduct left unfinished and 200,000 more on a second, also abandoned (10.37). Nicaea's theatre had swallowed more than ten million and was cracking. Trajan replies that Pliny is on the spot and should decide, and adds that the little Greeks love their gymnasia (10.39–40).
  • Experts. Asked for a surveyor, Trajan says he barely has enough for works at Rome and near it, and every province has its own (10.17b–18).
  • Associations. After a fire at Nicomedia Pliny proposes a guild of 150 firemen. Trajan refuses: whatever the name, men gathered for a common purpose soon become political clubs (10.33–34).
  • Local law. Apamea, a colony, claims the right to run its own accounts (10.47–48); Amisus, free by treaty, may keep its benefit societies under its own laws (10.92–93); on entry fees for town councillors no general rule can be laid down (10.112–113).
  • Feuds. When Dio of Prusa is accused of setting the emperor's statue near the graves of his wife and son, Trajan refuses to let reverence for his name be won by fear and treason charges (10.81–82).

The emperor's style is consistent: refer to local law, refuse precedents, keep soldiers with the standards. So is the governor's poverty of means. Pliny had no engineers, police or fire service of his own; his staff was his friends, a few freedmen and soldiers seconded from the army.

The cities as the government

The empire was a federation of self-governing cities, each responsible for its territory, its courts of first instance, its public works and the collection of its taxes. In the Greek East the cities were old. In the West Rome made them, by founding colonies and by turning tribal centres into towns with charters.

The fullest charter was found in 1981, by men with metal detectors, near El Saucejo in the province of Seville: six of the ten bronze tablets of the lex Irnitana, the law of the small municipium Flavium Irnitanum. Vespasian had given the Latin right to all Spain (Pliny, Natural History 3.30), and Irni's law is a copy of a standard Flavian municipal statute, known before only in fragments from Malaca and Salpensa found in 1851. A letter of Domitian of AD 91, pardoning marriages contracted irregularly under the Latin right, is appended. The law sets out a council of decurions (it assumes sixty-three), annual elections of duoviri, aediles and quaestors, the oath, public slaves, finances, festivals, manumission and guardianship. Chapter 21 gives Roman citizenship to those who have held a magistracy, with their parents, wives and children. Chapter 84 caps the magistrates' civil jurisdiction at 1,000 sesterces; bigger cases went to the governor. Its courts follow the edict of the provincial governor (ch. 85), itself modelled on the praetor's at Rome. A small Spanish town ran itself on Roman law, and its leading men became Roman citizens by doing so.

The system rested on local wealth. Councillors paid an entry fee (summa honoraria), and magistrates were expected to give: buildings, oil for the baths, games, feasts. Paul Veyne made "euergetism", a word coined by French scholars before him, the standard name for the habit (Le pain et le cirque, 1976). Pliny gave Comum a library, baths and a fund for the children of its plebs (CIL V 5262); in Lycia Opramoas of Rhodiapolis paid for rebuilding after the earthquake of 141 and had the emperor's letters of thanks inscribed on his tomb. Generosity bought honour and kept the cities solvent, but competition led to the half-built aqueducts Pliny inspected. From about Trajan's time emperors appointed curators (curatores rei publicae) to supervise individual cities' finances, a sign that the model strained.

Taxes, census and coin

Provincials paid two direct taxes, on land (tributum soli) and on persons (tributum capitis); Italy paid neither. Indirect taxes included customs dues (portoria), at a fortieth in the Gauls, the five per cent on the freeing of slaves, and the inheritance and sales taxes that funded the military treasury (module 6.4). Cities collected the direct taxes under procurators' eyes; companies of publicani still farmed customs. In 58 Nero proposed abolishing all indirect taxes, was talked out of it by older advisers who warned that the empire would dissolve, and ordered the tax regulations published instead (Tacitus, Annals 13.50–51).

Direct tax needed a census, and censuses were provincial. Augustus held one in Gaul in 27 BC (Dio 53.22.5); the form Ulpian describes required each field's name, district and neighbours, its arable sown in the last ten years, and the number of vines and olive trees (Digest 50.15.4). Quirinius' census of Judaea in AD 6, when it became a province, provoked Judas the Galilean's revolt (Josephus, Jewish Antiquities 18.1–10). Luke places Jesus' birth at this census, ordered, he says, for the whole world (Luke 2.1–2), while Matthew puts it under Herod, who died in 4 BC; no empire-wide census is attested. In Egypt households declared every fourteen years, and Roger Bagnall and Bruce Frier built The Demography of Roman Egypt (1994) from the surviving returns. In 127 Babatha, a Jewish woman of Maoza, registered her date groves in the census of the new province of Arabia (P.Yadin 16).

The coinage tied the system together: an aureus of 25 denarii, a denarius of four sesterces, a sestertius of four asses, struck chiefly at Rome. Nero cut the weight of gold and silver in 64, and the denarius lost silver again under Marcus and Severus. The East kept its own silver at Alexandria and Antioch, and hundreds of cities struck bronze with the emperor's head and their own gods. Reverse types announced victories, virtues and provinces, Hadrian's tours among them. A. H. M. Jones ("Numismatics and History", 1956) doubted that users read them, against C. H. V. Sutherland's view of the types as instruments of policy (Coinage in Roman Imperial Policy, 1951).

Where sources disagree. Keith Hopkins ("Taxes and Trade in the Roman Empire", Journal of Roman Studies 1980) built a model: money taxes raised in the rich inner provinces were spent in Rome and on the frontier armies, so the taxed provinces had to sell goods to earn the coin back, and taxation drove trade and monetisation. Richard Duncan-Jones (Structure and Scale in the Roman Economy, 1990) found taxation in money less general than this assumes, and taxation in kind, Egypt's grain above all, more widespread.

The frontiers and the army in peacetime

Most soldiers in most years fought no battle. The army built roads and forts, guarded governors and prisons, escorted grain, policed roads and markets and kept the governor's office; its clerks and orderlies were the nearest thing the provinces had to a civil service. Trajan resisted every drain on it. Prisons should be guarded by public slaves, not soldiers (Pliny, Letters 10.19–20). Byzantium had a legionary centurion because of the crowds passing through, but Juliopolis would not, for every town would then ask (10.77–78). Agrippa, in the speech Josephus gives him in AD 66, says the Gauls were held by 1,200 soldiers, hardly more than their cities (Jewish War 2.345–401): rhetoric, but the point was that obedience did not depend on garrisons. Pay, service and discharge are module 9.2's.

Hadrian, the Historia Augusta says, was the first to build a wall, eighty miles long, to separate barbarians from Romans (Hadrian 11.2). Begun about 122, under the governor Aulus Platorius Nepos named on its building inscriptions, it ran from Wallsend on the Tyne to Bowness on the Solway: stone in the east, turf in the west, a fortlet with a gate at every Roman mile and two turrets between, forts added during construction, and the ditch and mounds of the Vallum behind. Legionaries of the Second, Sixth and Twentieth built it; auxiliaries manned it. Under Antoninus Pius the governor Lollius Urbicus advanced to a turf wall between Forth and Clyde (Historia Augusta, Antoninus Pius 5.4); within a generation the army was back on Hadrian's line. David Breeze and Brian Dobson (Hadrian's Wall, 1976) read it as a means of controlling movement, a line of gates, not a fighting platform. Module 0.2 holds the argument over frontier strategy.

Vindolanda, a fort on the road behind the later Wall, has since 1973 produced hundreds of ink tablets of about 85 to 130, excavated by Robin Birley and edited by Alan Bowman and David Thomas. They show the army running a province. A strength report of the First Cohort of Tungrians (Tab. Vindol. II 154) counts 752 men, of whom 456 were absent, 46 as the governor's guards and 337 at Coria (probably Corbridge); of the 296 present, 31 were sick, wounded or had inflamed eyes. A civilian contractor writes that he has bought some 5,000 modii of grain and needs cash, and that the roads are too bad to move hides (II 343). A memorandum notes that the "little Britons", Brittunculi, do not mount to throw javelins (II 164). A man from overseas, beaten with rods, petitions "your majesty" for redress (II 344): petition and response reached the edge of the province.

"Romanisation" and the quarrel over the word

Tacitus describes his father-in-law in Britain about 79 urging the Britons to build temples, fora and houses, educating chiefs' sons in the liberal arts, until they took to the toga, porticoes, baths and elegant dinners (Agricola 21). He ends:

idque apud imperitos humanitas vocabatur, cum pars servitutis esset.

"The innocent called it civilisation, when it was part of their slavery." Module 7.2 has Agricola's campaigns. The passage frames a modern argument over what happened when provincials took up Roman towns, villas, Latin, bath-houses and gods, and over whether "Romanisation" is the right word for it. Archaeology supplies most of the evidence, and the West, where Latin replaced local languages, is its main field.

Where sources disagree. Francis Haverfield, lecturing in 1905, described a civilisation spreading from Rome through which western provincials, Britons included, came to share Roman speech and ways; Richard Hingley (Roman Officers and English Gentlemen, 2000) showed how closely this mirrored the British Empire's view of itself. Martin Millett (1990) made native elites the agents: with little administration, Rome governed through tribal aristocracies who adopted Roman culture to keep their status. Greg Woolf (Becoming Roman, 1998), working on Gaul, treated Roman culture as a changing structure of differences rather than a package, remade as provincials joined it. David Mattingly (Imperialism, Power, and Identity, 2011) wants the term dropped: it implies one culture and a benign process, and hides power, exploitation and the discrepant identities of soldiers, townsmen, peasants and slaves; Jane Webster (2001) proposed creolisation instead.

The argument changes the question rather than the evidence. A bath-house in a Gallic town is a fact; whether it marks emulation, coercion or a new provincial culture is the dispute. Citizenship advanced by grant, army service and municipal office until the edict of 212 (module 7.4).

The Greek East under Rome

Greek module 7.4 covers Achaea, Athens as a university town, Hadrian's Panhellenion and the Second Sophistic. The administrative point is that Rome governed the East in Greek. Governors and emperors wrote to Greek cities in Greek, and the cities kept their councils, assemblies and magistrates. Plutarch tells a Greek magistrate to remember the Roman shoe above his head and not to run to the governor with every local quarrel (Precepts of Statecraft 813–815). Rivalry was channelled into status: in AD 26 eleven cities of Asia competed before the Senate for the right to build a temple to Tiberius, and Smyrna won (Tacitus, Annals 4.55–56); module 6.2 has the imperial cult's provincial forms. Woolf's formula for the East is "becoming Roman, staying Greek" (Proceedings of the Cambridge Philological Society, 1994): the elites took citizenship, office and senatorial rank, and kept Greek culture as their claim to distinction.

The limits of the system

Government by consent of local elites failed where its levers bit too hard, and the great revolts read as a list of them. In Britain in 60–61 the annexation of a client kingdom, a veterans' colony on native land, a temple of the deified Claudius seen as the citadel of perpetual domination (Tacitus, Annals 14.31), a procurator reclaiming Claudius' gifts and Seneca calling in his loans (Dio 62.2) all fed Boudica's revolt, which module 7.1 tells. In Judaea in 66 a procurator's raid on the Temple treasury and the ending of the sacrifices for the emperor began the war of module 7.2. The diaspora rising of 115–117 in Cyrene, Egypt and Cyprus (Dio 68.32) and the Bar Kokhba war of 132–135 (module 7.3) followed.

Banditry was the chronic limit. Ulpian tells the governor to purge his province of the sacrilegious, kidnappers, thieves and bandits (Digest 1.18.13). Pliny writes of a knight who vanished on the road near Ocriculum and of a townsman of Comum who disappeared with his slaves (Letters 6.25), and epitaphs record men killed by latrones. Under Commodus the deserter Maternus led a band through Gaul and Spain (Herodian 1.10). Under Severus Bulla Felix ran six hundred men in Italy for two years; captured, he was asked by the prefect Papinian why he was a bandit, and answered: why are you a prefect? (Dio 77(76).10). Josephus calls the Jewish rebels "bandits"; the word was a label as much as a trade.

Where sources disagree. Brent Shaw ("Bandits in the Roman Empire", Past and Present 1984) argued that banditry was endemic and structural, a sign of how thinly the state reached the countryside, and that latro was a relation to power that could fit a rustler, a local strongman, a rebel or a usurper. Thomas Grünewald (Räuber, Rebellen, Rivalen, Rächer, 1999; English 2004) sorted the evidence into types and treated the noble robber of the stories, Bulla among them, as a literary figure rather than Hobsbawm's social bandit.

Self-test

  1. What does Millar mean by government through petition and response, and give two pieces of evidence for it.
    Answer

    That the emperor mostly reacted to initiatives from below: embassies, petitions, governors' questions and appeals, answered by subscription or letter. Evidence: Dio's woman who told Hadrian to stop being emperor if he had no time (69.6.3); the African tenants' petition to Commodus and his inscribed reply (CIL VIII 10570); Pliny's file of questions to Trajan.

  2. What did Hopkins object to in Millar's book?
    Answer

    Its method, an accumulation of evidence without an explicit model, and its emphasis on the emperor as administrator and judge, which pushed war, the army and money to the margins ("Rules of Evidence", 1978).

  3. How did senatorial and equestrian careers differ?
    Answer

    Senators climbed the magistracies (quaestor, praetor, consul) and alternated them with legionary commands and governorships, ending in great consular provinces or the proconsulships of Asia and Africa. Knights served in auxiliary commands, then as salaried procurators graded at 60,000 to 200,000 sesterces, rising to the prefectures of the watch, the grain supply, Egypt and the guard.

  4. Why is "government without bureaucracy" a fair description of the second-century empire?
    Answer

    Some 61 million people on Frier's estimate were governed by a few dozen governors, about a hundred procurators on Pflaum's count, and imperial slaves and freedmen; the cities collected taxes and judged small cases, and soldiers did the clerical and police work (Garnsey and Saller, 1987).

  5. What do Pliny's letters on the Nicomedia fire brigade and the surveyor reveal?
    Answer

    Trajan feared associations as political clubs even when their purpose was useful (10.33–34), and the centre had no technical staff to spare: provinces must find their own surveyors (10.17b–18). Security outranked efficiency, and expertise was local.

  6. What did the lex Irnitana provide, and how did it bind local elites to Rome?
    Answer

    A standard Flavian constitution for a Latin town: council, elections, magistrates, finances, festivals, and procedure following the governor's edict, with civil jurisdiction capped at 1,000 sesterces (ch. 84). Chapter 21 made ex-magistrates and their families Roman citizens, so local office became the road to Roman status.

  7. Why is Luke's census a problem, and what do Babatha and the Egyptian returns show about real censuses?
    Answer

    Luke 2.1–2 has a census of the whole world under Quirinius, which Josephus dates to AD 6 in Judaea alone, while Matthew puts the birth under Herod, dead by 4 BC. Real censuses were provincial: Babatha registered her groves in Arabia in 127 (P.Yadin 16), and Egyptian households declared every fourteen years.

  8. State Hopkins's taxes-and-trade model and Duncan-Jones's objection.
    Answer

    Money taxes from the inner provinces were spent in Rome and on the frontiers, so the taxed provinces had to export goods to earn coin back, driving trade (1980). Duncan-Jones (1990) found money taxation less general and taxation in kind more widespread than the model assumes.

  9. What does the Tungrian strength report show about the army in peacetime?
    Answer

    Of 752 men only 296 were at Vindolanda; 46 guarded the governor and 337 were at Coria (Tab. Vindol. II 154). A unit was a pool of manpower dispersed on guard, escort and administrative duties, not a garrison waiting for battle.

  10. How do Haverfield, Millett, Woolf and Mattingly differ on "Romanisation"?
    Answer

    Haverfield saw a civilisation spreading from Rome; Millett made native elites the agents, adopting Roman culture to keep status; Woolf saw Roman culture itself as a changing structure remade as provincials became Roman; Mattingly rejects the term for hiding power and the discrepant identities of different groups.

Carry forward

  1. The Roman state governed through others: an emperor who answered petitions, amateur governors on short terms, and self-governing cities that collected taxes and judged small cases. When the cities' elites failed or the levers bit too hard, there was little machinery behind them.
  2. The army was the empire's civil service as well as its defence: soldiers guarded, escorted, policed, built and kept records, and the frontier armies were the largest market for taxes and goods. Changes in the army are therefore changes in government.
  3. "Romanisation" is a modern word for many local bargains; ask of each piece of evidence who adopted what, for whose benefit, and under what pressure, and remember that the Greek East became Roman while staying Greek.
