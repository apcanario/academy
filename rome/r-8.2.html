01Sources and the rise of Diocles

No narrative history of the reign survives. The Historia Augusta stops with Carus and his sons, Zosimus' account is lost in a gap in his text, and the Latin breviaries give a few pages (Aurelius Victor 39; Eutropius 9.19–28; Epitome de Caesaribus 39); module 8.1 weighs all three. The fullest witness is hostile. Lactantius, a teacher of rhetoric whom Diocletian had summoned to Nicomedia (Jerome, On Illustrious Men 80), wrote On the Deaths of the Persecutors about 314–315 to show that every persecutor died horribly; it is cited here by chapter alone. Beside it stand Eusebius, the Latin panegyrics, and an unusual wealth of documents: edicts on stone, papyri, and rescripts in the law codes.

Diocles was a Dalmatian of obscure birth, a scribe's son or the freedman of a senator Anullinus (Eutropius 9.19), who rose to command the household troops of the emperor Numerian (Aurelius Victor 39.1). In November 284 the army returning from Persia found Numerian dead in his closed litter. At Nicomedia on 20 November the officers chose Diocles, who swore he had no part in the death and killed the praetorian prefect Arrius Aper, Numerian's father-in-law, with his own hand before the troops (Eutropius 9.20). The Historia Augusta's druidess, who foretold that he would reign once he had killed a boar, aper (Carus 14–15), is the sort of tale module 8.1 warns against. In 285 Numerian's brother Carinus fought him at the river Margus and was killed by his own officers, men whose wives he had seduced on Victor's account (Aurelius Victor 39; Epitome 38.8), and Diocletian kept Carinus' prefect in office, a clemency Victor praises.

02Two Augusti and two Caesars

Diocletian made Maximian, an old comrade born to labouring parents near Sirmium (Epitome 40.10), Caesar in 285 and Augustus in 286. Pressures multiplied: Carausius, commander of the Channel fleet, seized Britain and struck coins of himself with the two emperors as CARAVSIVS ET FRATRES SVI, "Carausius and his brothers", and wars on every frontier needed an emperor on several fronts at once (Eutropius 9.21–22). On 1 March 293 two Caesars were added, each adopted by an Augustus and married into his house. Lactantius saw greed: the world divided into four, each ruler wanting more soldiers than earlier emperors had had ruling alone (7.2).

It was not a partition. Laws went out in all four names, and the Caesars fought where they were needed. Constantius recovered Britain in 296 and is hailed on a gold medallion found near Arras as REDDITOR LVCIS AETERNAE, "restorer of the eternal light". Galerius, beaten by the Persian king Narseh, was made to walk before Diocletian's carriage in his purple, for a mile in Ammianus (14.11.10), for several in Eutropius (9.24); in 298 he routed Narseh and won a peace that carried the frontier beyond the Tigris.

Diocletian took the name Jovius and Maximian Herculius, and each Caesar inherited his Augustus' name. The panegyric of 289 (Panegyric 10(2)) makes Maximian Hercules to Diocletian's Jupiter: the elder plans, the younger labours. The names claimed divine agency, not divinity, and fixed a hierarchy, Jovius above Herculius. Aurelius Victor says the other three looked up to Diocletian as to a father or a great god (39.29).

Two pairs of embracing rulers in porphyry, a stone quarried only at Mons Porphyrites in Egypt, are set into the corner of the Treasury of St Mark's in Venice, looted from Constantinople after 1204; a missing foot found in Istanbul in the 1960s suggests they stood at the Philadelphion. Each pair clasps shoulders, hands on sword hilts, one figure bearded and one not, usually read as Augustus and Caesar, though which college they show is debated. Module 10.3 reads them as art.

03A rebuilt state: provinces, dioceses, army and court

Module 7.5 described government without bureaucracy: a few dozen governors and about a hundred procurators, with the cities doing the work. Diocletian built something of another order. The Verona List, a register of about 314, names some hundred provinces, roughly double the Severan number, grouped into twelve dioceses under vicarii, deputies of the praetorian prefects. Italy was divided into provinces and taxed, a great evil for part of Italy in Aurelius Victor's phrase (39.31). Governors became civil judges and assessors, while frontier troops passed to military commanders, the duces; Constantine completed the separation. Lactantius saw soldiers so multiplied that those who drew pay outnumbered those who paid, and provinces chopped into fragments with officials piled on every region (7.3–4).

Where sources disagree. Lactantius already mentions vicars of the prefects (7.4), and most accounts give the dioceses to Diocletian: William Seston dated them to 297, Timothy Barnes to 293. Constantin Zuckerman (Travaux et Mémoires, 2002) argued that the Verona List is the first evidence for the whole grid, that earlier evidence for diocesan vicars is thin, and that Constantine and Licinius created the dioceses in 314. The date of the dioceses is open.

John the Lydian, a sixth-century official, gives Diocletian's army as 389,704 men and his fleets as 45,562 (On the Months 1.27), a figure that does not suggest the fourfold increase Lactantius implies. New legions, apparently smaller than the old, and rebuilt forts, as on the Syrian desert road later called the Strata Diocletiana, held the frontiers; the formal division into field and frontier troops is usually credited to Constantine, and module 9.2 takes the late army as an institution.

The court travelled. Each ruler had his comitatus of guards, secretaries and treasury officials, and residences rose near the frontiers, at Nicomedia, Sirmium, Milan, Trier and Thessalonica. Rome received buildings instead of an emperor, among them the Curia still standing in the Forum, Diocletian's rebuilding after a fire of 283 (module 9.5). Yet the reactive government of module 7.5 survived inside the new machine. More rescripts survive from Diocletian than from any other emperor, collected by Gregorius about 292 and Hermogenianus about 295 in codes that module 9.1 follows to the Theodosian Code.

04Taxes, coinage and the Edict on Maximum Prices

Module 8.1 told how the silver coinage collapsed. The state had come to feed its soldiers by requisition, and Diocletian made requisition a regular tax. Land was assessed in units called iuga and people in capita by census, and each year a rate per unit was announced to supply the army and court in kind, the annona. On 16 March 297 the prefect of Egypt, Aristius Optatus, proclaimed that the emperors had fixed what each aroura of land, by its quality, and each head of the rural population owed, so that everyone could know his burden (P.Cair.Isid. 1). The units varied: the fifth-century Syro-Roman Law Book counts as one iugum five iugera of vineyard, or twenty, forty or sixty of arable by grade. Reassessment produced the indiction of module 0.3.

The coinage was rebuilt about 294: gold at sixty to the pound, a silver argenteus at Nero's standard of ninety-six, some pieces marked XCVI, and a silvered bronze nummus struck across the empire. Prices kept rising, and late in 301 came the Edict on Maximum Prices. Its preamble, in all four names, denounces profiteers who in a single purchase strip a soldier of his donative and pay. It fixes ceilings, in denarii of account, on well over a thousand goods, wages and freight charges, with death for exceeding them or hoarding: an army modius of wheat 100 denarii, a farm labourer 25 a day with his keep, a teacher of rhetoric 250 a month for each pupil, a pound of purple silk 150,000. Copies come from Phrygia, Caria, mainland Greece, Crete and Cyrenaica, almost all in the eastern provinces. Lactantius gives the outcome: much blood shed over trifles, goods withdrawn from sale for fear, prices worse than before, and the law lapsing of necessity after many deaths (7.6–7).

Where sources disagree. The edict blames merchants' greed; Lactantius blames the emperor, whose armies, officials and buildings drove prices up (7.6–8). Simon Corcoran (The Empire of the Tetrarchs, 1996) judged that the edict was probably not widely published outside the East, so whether Maximian or Constantius enforced it is unknown.

05Lord and god? Adoration and the "Dominate"

Eutropius says Diocletian ordered himself to be adored where earlier emperors had been saluted, put gems on his robes and shoes, and

regiae consuetudinis formam magis quam Romanae libertatis invexit

"brought in the form of royal custom rather than of Roman liberty" (9.26). Aurelius Victor makes him the first since Caligula and Domitian to be called lord in public and adored as a god (39.2–4). The visitor knelt and kissed the hem of the purple, a gesture Greeks had long associated with Persian kings (Greek module 11.4). At Rome for his twentieth anniversary in 303, Diocletian could not bear the people's free speech and left early (Lactantius 17.1–3). Theodor Mommsen made the change a divide, setting the Principate, a magistrate among citizens, against the Dominate, from dominus, a master of subjects; module 0.3 notes that most historians now say "late empire".

Where sources disagree. Victor and Eutropius make adoration Diocletian's innovation, yet Suetonius has Domitian styled "lord and god" (Domitian 13.2), Pliny addresses Trajan as domine throughout Letters 10, and the panegyric of 291 describes officials admitted to adore both emperors at Milan (Panegyric 11(3).11). Andreas Alföldi's studies of imperial ceremonial (1934–1935) traced its elements back through the Principate; Jochen Bleicken (Prinzipat und Dominat, 1978) argued that Mommsen's division rests on no genuine constitutional transformation; Frank Kolb (Herrscherideologie in der Spätantike, 2001) sees Diocletian formalising trends already present.

06The ancestral gods defended: incest law and the Manichaeans

Diocletian's laws speak the old religion's language. An edict against incestuous marriages, issued at Damascus in 295, says that the immortal gods will stay favourable to the Roman name only if all its subjects live pious, religious, peaceful and chaste lives (Collatio 6.4). The same late antique compilation, which sets Roman law beside the law of Moses, preserves his rescript on the Manichaeans. Mani, a Babylonian prophet who died in prison under the Persian king Bahram I in the 270s, taught a dualism of light and darkness. Writing from Alexandria on 31 March to Julianus, proconsul of Africa, Diocletian called the sect a recent growth among the Persians, a nation hostile to Rome, that threatened to infect the modest and tranquil Roman people with Persian customs. Its leaders were to be burned with their books, obstinate followers executed and men of rank sent to the mines (Collatio 15.3), on the principle that it is the greatest crime to undo what the ancients once settled. The year is lost from the text: most scholars, Corcoran and Samuel Lieu among them, prefer 302; others have argued for 297.

07The Great Persecution

Lactantius was at Nicomedia in 303; Eusebius, at Caesarea, wrote during and just after the events, from what he saw in Palestine and Egypt. The persecutions of Decius and Valerian are module 8.1's; Trajan's rule that Christians were not to be sought out is module 2.5's.

Lactantius makes Galerius the driving force. In the East, probably at Antioch, when the haruspices could not read the entrails because Christian attendants crossed themselves, Diocletian ordered the palace to sacrifice and soldiers to sacrifice or be discharged (10; Eusebius, Church History 8.4). In the winter of 302–303 at Nicomedia Galerius, urged on by his mother, pressed for more; a council and Apollo's oracle at Didyma were consulted, and the answer was hostile (11).

On 23 February 303, the Terminalia, the church at Nicomedia, in sight of the palace, was sacked and levelled (12). Next day the first edict ordered churches destroyed and scriptures burned, stripped Christians of rank and of the right to bring actions at law, and re-enslaved Christian imperial freedmen (13.1; Eusebius, Church History 8.2.4). A Christian who tore it down was roasted alive (13.2–3). Eusebius records three further edicts: the arrest of the clergy; their release if they sacrificed, torture if they refused (8.2.5, 8.6.8–10); and in 304 an order that the whole population sacrifice (Martyrs of Palestine 3.1).

Enforcement followed the map of the Tetrarchy. Lactantius says the whole world suffered except Gaul, where Constantius let church buildings be pulled down but spared the true temple of God, its people (15.7, 16.1). In Maximian's Africa the first edict bit. The minutes of the search at Cirta on 19 May 303, preserved in the record of a hearing of 320 (the Gesta apud Zenophilum), list the chalices, lamps and clothing for the poor surrendered by Bishop Paul's clergy; the martyrs of Abitina, arrested at a forbidden service, told the proconsul sine dominico non possumus, "we cannot live without the Lord's day" (Acts of the Abitinian Martyrs). Those who handed over scriptures were branded traditores, the seed of the Donatist schism of module 8.3. In the East persecution ran, with pauses, until 311.

The dead cannot be counted. Gibbon found ninety-two martyrs in the Martyrs of Palestine and, taking Palestine as a sixteenth of the East, put the empire's judicial executions at somewhat less than two thousand (Decline and Fall, chapter 16). Geoffrey de Ste. Croix ("Aspects of the 'Great' Persecution", Harvard Theological Review, 1954) showed how unevenly the edicts were applied from region to region.

Galerius ended it. Dying of a wasting disease that Lactantius describes with relish (33), he conceded in an edict posted at Nicomedia on 30 April 311 that, since many Christians persisted and now worshipped neither the gods nor their own god, they might be Christians again and rebuild their meeting places, provided they kept public order and prayed for the emperors (Lactantius 34–35; Eusebius, Church History 8.17). Maximinus Daia resumed in the East; the settlement of 313 is module 8.3's.

Where sources disagree. Lactantius makes Galerius and his mother the instigators and Diocletian a reluctant old man (10–11). Constantine, as Eusebius reports him, blamed Diocletian himself, moved by Apollo's complaint that the just on earth kept him from prophesying truly (Life of Constantine 2.50–51); Eusebius' own history explains the persecution as God's chastisement of a church grown lax in the long peace (Church History 8.1.7–8). Lactantius also names Sossianus Hierocles, governor of Bithynia and a writer against the Christians, as an author of the persecution (16.4); the incest edict and the Manichaean rescript suggest Diocletian needed little prompting.

08Abdication, Split and the collapse of the system

On 1 May 305 Diocletian at Nicomedia and Maximian at Milan abdicated on the same day. Galerius and Constantius became Augusti, with Severus, a soldier close to Galerius, and Maximinus Daia, Galerius' nephew, as Caesars. Lactantius has the troops expecting Constantine, who stood on the platform, as Diocletian, weeping, put his purple on a man nobody knew (19). Constantine and Maxentius, Maximian's son, were passed over.

Where sources disagree. Lactantius says Galerius bullied Diocletian, weakened by a near-fatal illness in 304, into retiring and chose the Caesars himself (17–18). The panegyrist of 307 says Maximian had sworn in the temple of Capitoline Jupiter to retire with his colleague (Panegyric 7(6)), implying a plan agreed years before, and Eutropius has Maximian obey unwillingly (9.27). Whether the Tetrarchy was meant to renew itself by merit cannot be settled from witnesses this partisan.

Diocletian retired to his palace at Split, beside Salona: a fortified rectangle laid out like a legionary camp, enclosing his residence, a temple and an octagonal mausoleum, now the cathedral of St Domnius, a bishop of Salona martyred in the persecution. The medieval town grew inside the walls; Robert Adam's Ruins of the Palace of the Emperor Diocletian at Spalatro (1764) carried its forms into Georgian architecture. Asked in 308 to rule again, Diocletian replied that if they could see the cabbages he had planted with his own hands at Salona they would never suggest it (Epitome 39.6).

The system did not long survive him. Constantius died at York on 25 July 306 and his army proclaimed Constantine, where module 8.3 begins; Galerius made Severus Augustus and allowed Constantine only the rank of Caesar. On 28 October the praetorians and people of Rome, angered at being brought into the census (Lactantius 26), proclaimed Maxentius, and Maximian left retirement to join his son. Severus invaded Italy and died a prisoner. At Carnuntum on 11 November 308 a conference attended by Diocletian made Licinius Augustus, yet by 310 six men claimed the title. Maximian died in 310, Galerius in 311, Diocletian at Split on 3 December 311 or 312, his statues thrown down with Maximian's (Lactantius 42); he alone, Eutropius notes, was deified though he died a private man (9.28). The Milvian Bridge in 312 and the war of 313 left two Augusti and no Caesars.

09Self-test

  1. Why does a historian of Diocletian lean on documents more than on narratives, and what is the bias of the fullest narrative?
    Answer

    No connected history survives: the Historia Augusta stops with Carus, Zosimus has a gap, and the breviaries are brief, so edicts, papyri, rescripts and panegyrics carry the weight. Lactantius' On the Deaths of the Persecutors (about 314–315) was written to show that persecutors died horribly and is hostile above all to Galerius.

  2. How was the college of 1 March 293 held together, and why was it not a partition of the empire?
    Answer

    By adoption, marriage (Galerius to Valeria, Constantius to Theodora) and the shared names Jovius and Herculius. Each ruler had a region and a residence, but laws went out in all four names and the Caesars campaigned where needed, as Constantius did in Britain and Galerius against Persia.

  3. What did the names Jovius and Herculius claim, and what did they not claim?
    Answer

    That the rulers acted for Jupiter and Hercules, the elder planning and the younger labouring (Panegyric 10(2)), and that Jovius ranked above Herculius. They did not claim that the emperors were themselves gods.

  4. How did Diocletian's administration differ from the "government without bureaucracy" of module 7.5, and what of the old system survived?
    Answer

    Some hundred provinces on the Verona List against about fifty, grouped in dioceses under vicars, civil governors separated from military duces, a larger army and travelling courts; Lactantius complains that those paid outnumbered those paying (7.3). Petition and response survived: more rescripts survive from Diocletian than from any other emperor.

  5. What did the edict of Aristius Optatus of 297 announce, and why did it matter to taxpayers?
    Answer

    Assessment by units of land graded by quality and by heads of the rural population, with published rates, so that everyone could know what he owed (P.Cair.Isid. 1). Requisition became a predictable annual tax in kind, and its reassessments gave rise to the indiction (module 0.3).

  6. What did the Edict on Maximum Prices attempt, and what is the evidence on its success?
    Answer

    To fix ceilings on well over a thousand goods, wages and freight charges, on pain of death, blaming profiteers who robbed soldiers. Lactantius says it caused bloodshed, emptied the markets, made prices worse and lapsed (7.6–7); its copies come almost all from the East, and Corcoran doubted it was widely published in the West.

  7. Why do many historians avoid the term "Dominate"?
    Answer

    Mommsen's opposition implies a constitutional break, yet the elements were older: Domitian as "lord and god" (Suetonius, Domitian 13.2), Pliny's domine, adoration already in the panegyric of 291. Alföldi traced the ceremonial's gradual growth, Bleicken denied a genuine constitutional transformation, and Kolb sees Diocletian formalising trends already present.

  8. Give the four edicts of the Great Persecution and explain why its severity varied by region.
    Answer

    February 303: churches destroyed, scriptures burned, rank and legal rights lost; then the arrest of clergy; then their release if they sacrificed; and in 304 universal sacrifice (Martyrs of Palestine 3.1). Each ruler enforced in his own territory: Constantius only pulled down buildings (Lactantius 15.7), Maximian applied the first edict in Africa (Cirta, Abitina), and Galerius and Maximinus persisted in the East until 311.

  9. How does the Manichaean rescript bear on the question of who drove the persecution?
    Answer

    It shows Diocletian himself holding that undoing what the ancients settled is the greatest crime and treating a new cult from hostile Persia as contagion (Collatio 15.3), as his incest edict ties the gods' favour to piety (6.4). That weakens Lactantius' picture of a reluctant Diocletian pushed by Galerius.

  10. What happened on 1 May 305, and why did the arrangement collapse?
    Answer

    Diocletian and Maximian abdicated together; Galerius and Constantius became Augusti, with Severus and Maximinus Daia as Caesars, passing over Constantine and Maxentius. Armies and the city of Rome backed the passed-over sons in 306, Maximian returned, Carnuntum (308) failed, and by 310 six men claimed the title of Augustus.

10Carry forward

  1. The late Roman state was Diocletian's answer to the third-century crisis: more emperors, more provinces, more soldiers and officials, paid for by a census-based tax in kind. Its machinery of prefects, vicars, governors and indictions frames the West to 476 and the East long after.
  2. The Tetrarchy solved the problem of presence, an emperor on every front, but not of succession: armies were loyal to sons, and Constantine and Maxentius broke the system within eighteen months of the abdication. Watch heredity return as the principle of imperial legitimacy.
  3. The Great Persecution was the last and most systematic attempt to secure the gods' favour by compulsion; it was uneven because enforcement followed the map of the Tetrarchy, Galerius conceded its failure in 311, and its victims, Lactantius and Eusebius, wrote the story the Christian empire remembered.
