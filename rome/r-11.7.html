01Five narratives and a witness

Module 5.4 told the murder in outline; this report takes it source by source. No full narrative was written by anyone who was there. Cicero was in the Senate that morning but not in the plot, and he left letters and speeches, not an account. The five continuous narratives were written between a generation and two and a half centuries after the event, and each had its own reasons for telling it as it did.

Plutarch, Appian and Suetonius agree so often in detail and order that a shared source behind them is generally assumed; the candidate most often named is Asinius Pollio's lost history of the civil wars, and Christopher Pelling's commentary (Plutarch: Caesar, 2011) weighs the case. Nicolaus is independent of that tradition and closer to Augustus' circle, which makes him the most useful control on it.

02The honours of 45–44 and the kingship question

Module 5.4 lists the honours voted after Munda (Suetonius, Julius 76.1), the diadem on the statue, the reply that he was Caesar, not Rex, and the Lupercalia of 15 February 44, with the ancient versions of the diadem in its dispute box. Three further episodes show why the honours mattered to the men who killed him.

First, the sitting. When the whole Senate came to the temple of Venus Genetrix to present a decree of honours, Caesar received them seated (Julius 78.1). Plutarch says that Cornelius Balbus stopped him when he made to rise, and that his friends later pleaded an attack of his illness (Caesar 60); Dio reports both excuses and the offence (44.8). Second, the oath: the senators swore to protect his safety, and his person was declared sacrosanct, which is why Antony could later read the oath over his body (Appian 2.144–145; Suetonius, Julius 84.2). Third, the departure. Caesar was to leave for Parthia on 18 March, and the rumour ran that Lucius Cotta, one of the keepers of the Sibylline books, would propose the title of king at the Senate's meeting on the Ides, because the books said only a king could conquer Parthia (Julius 79.3). Suetonius says this rumour was the reason the conspirators hurried (80.1).

Whether Caesar wanted the name, he already had the substance. A dictator for life with the right to name magistrates and dispose of armies held what a king held. Cicero, writing On Duties later in 44, put it flatly: here was a man who desired to be king of the Roman people and master of all nations, and achieved it (3.83).

Where sources disagree. Andreas Alföldi (Caesar in 44 v. Chr., 1985) read the Lupercalia as a coronation Caesar engineered and the honours as the stages of a planned monarchy. John North ("Caesar at the Lupercalia", Journal of Roman Studies, 2008) argued that a carnival rite of near-naked runners, in the season of the festival of the dead, was a poor setting for a coronation. Robert Morstein-Marx (Julius Caesar and the Roman People, 2021) rejects the picture of an aspiring autocrat altogether and reads Caesar as an exceptionally successful republican leader judged by republican standards. The ancient witnesses who say he aimed at kingship, Cicero above all, were writing after the Ides to justify it.

03The conspirators and their motives

Suetonius counts more than sixty conspirators (Julius 80.4), Nicolaus more than eighty (19). Five names carry the story.

  • Marcus Junius Brutus, about forty, pardoned after Pharsalus, governor of Cisalpine Gaul for Caesar, and urban praetor in 44, preferred to Cassius by Caesar's own decision (Plutarch, Brutus 7). He was Cato's nephew and, from 45, his son-in-law through Porcia; he claimed descent from the Brutus who expelled the kings (module 1.6). "Would you were alive" was written on the statue of that Brutus (Suetonius, Julius 80.3), and on the younger man's tribunal "you are asleep, Brutus" and "you are not really Brutus" (Plutarch, Brutus 9). His mother Servilia and the rumour of Caesar's paternity are deep dive module 11.4.
  • Gaius Cassius Longinus, Crassus' quaestor who saved the remnant from Carrhae, a Pompeian pardoned in 48, praetor in 44 and Brutus' brother-in-law. Plutarch makes him the first mover and gives him personal grievances as well as a hatred of tyrants (Brutus 8). He was an Epicurean (Plutarch, Brutus 37).
  • Decimus Junius Brutus Albinus, Caesar's commander of the fleet against the Veneti (Caesar, Gallic War 3.11), consul designate for 42 and named in the will among the heirs in the second degree (Suetonius, Julius 83.2). He owned a troupe of gladiators, which made him useful (Plutarch, Brutus 12). Deep dive module 11.4 notes that Sempronia may have been his mother.
  • Gaius Trebonius, Caesar's legate in Gaul and at the siege of Massilia, consul in 45, who had already sounded Antony about killing Caesar at Narbo in 45 (Plutarch, Antony 13; Cicero, Philippics 2.34).
  • Publius Servilius Casca, tribune-elect for 43, who struck first.

The pattern is the point. Two leaders were pardoned enemies; two were Caesar's own marshals, rewarded and promoted. Nicolaus gives the widest list of reasons: some hoped to lead in Caesar's place; some resented what the war had cost them in property or office; many resented his clemency, which put the pardoned on a level with his own men; some invoked the Republic and the name of Brutus (19). Dio puts envy and hatred first and the slogan of liberty second (44.1). Plutarch, by contrast, builds the conspiracy round Brutus' reputation: men joined because he led (Brutus 12), and after Philippi Antony said that Brutus alone had attacked Caesar for the glory and apparent nobility of the deed, the rest from envy and hatred of the man (Brutus 29).

Recruitment was tested by argument. Brutus sounded Favonius, Cato's follower, who held civil war worse than an illegal monarchy, and the Epicurean Statilius, who held that a wise man should not risk himself for fools; Labeo argued against both and was brought in (Brutus 12). Cicero was left out because his age and caution would blunt the plan (Brutus 12). No oath was sworn.

Where sources disagree. Plutarch's Brutus acts from principle, and Antony's verdict (Brutus 29) is the root of Shakespeare's "noblest Roman"; Nicolaus (19) and Dio (44.1) give the conspirators mixed and mostly personal motives, and Nicolaus wrote for Augustus, whose interest was to show the killers as ingrates. David Sedley ("The Ethics of Brutus and Cassius", Journal of Roman Studies, 1997) looked to Brutus' philosophy for the conspiracy's reasoning; his case on Cassius' recent Epicureanism is weighed in deep dive module 13.3.

04Omens and warnings

Suetonius opens his account of the murder by saying that it was foretold by unmistakable signs (Julius 81.1), and lists them:

  • At Capua, colonists building on Caesar's land opened the tomb of Capys and found a bronze tablet in Greek: when his bones were disturbed, a descendant of Iulus would be killed by kinsmen. Suetonius cites Cornelius Balbus, Caesar's intimate, for the story (81.1).
  • The horses Caesar had dedicated at the Rubicon refused food and wept (81.2).
  • The haruspex Spurinna warned him to beware a danger that would come no later than the Ides of March (81.2). On the morning, Caesar mocked him that the Ides had come; Spurinna answered that they had come but not gone (81.4; Plutarch, Caesar 63; Valerius Maximus 8.11.2).
  • The day before, a small bird carrying a sprig of laurel flew into the Curia of Pompey and was torn apart by birds from a nearby grove (81.3).
  • That night Caesar dreamed he flew above the clouds and clasped the hand of Jupiter; Calpurnia dreamed that the gable of the house fell and that her husband was stabbed in her arms, and the bedroom doors flew open by themselves (81.3).

Plutarch gives Calpurnia's dream two forms: she held his murdered body in her arms, or, as "some" say and Livy among them, the ornamental pediment the Senate had voted for the house fell (Caesar 63). At dinner with Lepidus on the 14th, when talk turned to the best kind of death, Caesar chose a sudden and unexpected one (Suetonius, Julius 87; Plutarch, Caesar 63). On the way, a Greek teacher, Artemidorus of Cnidus, pressed on him a note revealing the plot, which Caesar carried unread into the meeting (Plutarch, Caesar 65; Suetonius, Julius 81.4, without the name). At the sacrifice before the session, Appian says, the first victim had no heart (2.116).

Where sources disagree. The heartless victim moves. In Cicero's On Divination, written within months of the murder, Quintus places it on the day Caesar first sat in the golden chair and wore purple (1.119), not on the Ides; in book 2 Marcus answers that no animal can live without a heart (2.36–37), so the omen was a fault of observation. Appian puts it on the morning of the Ides (2.116). The omens were gathered afterwards and grew with telling: Suetonius' list is longest, Nicolaus has the bad sacrifices and Calpurnia's fears (23–24), and Plutarch opens his list with the remark that fate, it seems, is not so much unexpected as unavoidable (Caesar 63).

05The morning and the hall of Pompey

Caesar was unwell and inclined to stay at home. Decimus Brutus, Plutarch says, mocked the seers and warned that the Senate, ready to vote him the title of king outside Italy, would take an adjournment as an insult; he took Caesar by the hand and led him out (Caesar 64; Suetonius, Julius 81.4). Nicolaus names only "Brutus", thought to be one of his most intimate friends (23–24). A senator, Popilius Laenas, spoke privately to Caesar at length as he left his litter, and the conspirators, who had seen him speak to Brutus and Cassius earlier, thought themselves betrayed until his manner showed a petition (Appian 2.116; Plutarch, Brutus 16).

The Senate house had burned at Clodius' funeral in 52 (deep dive module 11.3) and its replacement was unfinished, so the Senate met in the hall that Pompey had built into the portico of his theatre on the Campus Martius, where his statue stood. Plutarch saw in the place a sign that some divine power was leading the deed there (Caesar 66). Suetonius says they weighed attacking him at the elections on the Campus Martius, on the Sacred Way or at the entrance to the theatre before choosing the Senate (Julius 80.4); Appian says they chose the Senate because senators not in the plot would join once they saw the deed (2.114). Nicholas Horsfall ("The Ides of March: Some New Problems", Greece & Rome, 1974) asked why the date and the Curia of Pompey were chosen and showed how many of the sources' details fail to fit together.

06The murder

Trebonius kept Antony talking outside, since the conspirators had decided to spare him (Cicero, Philippics 2.34; Plutarch, Brutus 17; Appian 2.117; Dio 44.19). Plutarch's Caesar gives the task to Decimus (66), a slip within Plutarch's own work. Inside, Tillius Cimber approached as if to plead for his exiled brother, and when Caesar put him off, seized his toga at both shoulders. Caesar cried, Suetonius reports, "why, this is violence!" (Julius 82.1). One of the Cascas struck from behind, just below the throat; Caesar caught his arm and drove his stylus through it (82.2). Plutarch has Caesar shout in Latin "accursed Casca, what are you doing?" and Casca call in Greek to his brother for help (Caesar 66).

Then they closed in. Cassius struck at his face; Decimus wounded his thigh; Brutus, in the press, was cut in the hand (Nicolaus 24; Plutarch, Brutus 17). When Caesar saw Brutus with his dagger drawn, Plutarch says, he pulled his toga over his head and gave up (Caesar 66); Appian makes the turning point a wound in the thigh from Brutus (2.117); Suetonius adds that with his left hand he drew the folds down over his legs so as to fall decently (Julius 82.2). He fell at the base of Pompey's statue. Suetonius, Appian and Plutarch's Caesar count twenty-three wounds (Julius 82.3; Appian 2.117; Caesar 66), Nicolaus thirty-five (24); Plutarch's Brutus (17) and Dio (44.19) say only many; the physician Antistius judged that only the second was mortal (Julius 82.3).

The senators fled. The conspirators had planned to drag the body to the Tiber, confiscate the estate and annul the acts, and gave it up for fear of Antony and Lepidus (Julius 82.4). The body lay until three slaves carried it home on a litter, one arm hanging down (82.3; Nicolaus 26).

The last words

Suetonius states that Caesar was stabbed twenty-three times and uttered only a groan at the first blow, without a word, "though some have written" that as Brutus rushed at him he said in Greek kai su, teknon, "you too, child?" (Julius 82.2). Dio gives the same split: the truest account is that he could say nothing, but some add the Greek words to Brutus (44.19). Plutarch has no words for Brutus, only the covered head (Caesar 66), and Nicolaus records none. Latin et tu, Brute is not ancient. It appears in English drama before Shakespeare, in The True Tragedy of Richard Duke of York (printed 1595), and it has been traced, without direct evidence, to Richard Edes's lost Latin play Caesar Interfectus (about 1582); Shakespeare made it famous (Julius Caesar 3.1).

Where sources disagree. Both ancient reporters of the Greek words doubt them, and what they would mean is disputed. The old reading is a father's reproach, which needs the paternity rumour deep dive module 11.4 sets aside. James Russell ("Julius Caesar's Last Words: A Reinterpretation", 1980) pointed to kai su as a formula on mosaics and inscriptions turning the evil eye back on its sender, so that Caesar died with a curse: "you too", lad. The same Greek opening begins the words with which Augustus, in Suetonius, told the boy Galba that he too would one day taste power (Galba 4.1), and Ioannis Ziogas ("Famous Last Words: Caesar's Prophecy on the Ides of March", Antichthon, 2016) reads them through the funerary epigram, where "you too" tells the passer-by that he will share the dead man's fate: an oral epitaph and a dying man's prophecy that Brutus too would die.

07The aftermath: Capitol, amnesty, will

The killers went out with bloody daggers calling the people to liberty and found the city shut. They occupied the Capitol with Decimus' gladiators (Nicolaus 26; Appian 2.119–120); Brutus addressed the people (Plutarch, Brutus 18). Cicero later recalled that Brutus, raising his dagger, had called out his name (Philippics 2.28). Appian explains the failure in a passage of social analysis: the conspirators expected the spirit of the elder Brutus in a plebs now mixed with freedmen and foreigners, living on the grain dole, and counted at once on lovers of liberty and on men who took bribes, two incompatible things (2.120).

The amnesty of 17 March in the temple of Tellus, the confirmation of Caesar's acts, the will and its legacy of 300 sesterces a man are module 5.5's. Two details belong here. The will, made on the Ides of September 45, named Decimus Brutus among the heirs in the second degree and several of the assassins among the guardians of any son who might be born (Suetonius, Julius 83.1–2): the generosity to his killers was read out before the funeral. And Brutus had refused to kill Antony with Caesar, which Plutarch counts his first great mistake (Brutus 18, 20).

08Antony's funeral speech

Module 5.5 left the versions of the speech to this report. They differ in kind, not only in detail.

  • Suetonius (Julius 84.2): in place of a eulogy, Antony as consul had a herald read the Senate's decree voting Caesar all honours human and divine, and the oath by which all had bound themselves to his safety, and added very few words of his own. At the funeral games lines were sung to stir pity and anger, among them, from Pacuvius' Judgement of Arms, "did I save them, that they might destroy me?"
  • Appian (2.143–147): Piso brought the body to the Forum. Antony read the decrees, stressing "sacrosanct" and "father of his country", and the oaths; he chanted a lament, praised Caesar as a god, recited his wars, stripped the bloody robe from the body and raised it on a spear. In Caesar's own voice he spoke the line about saving his murderers. Then a wax image of Caesar was raised above the bier and turned by a device to show twenty-three wounds on body and face; the crowd burned the Senate house where he had died and hunted the killers.
  • Plutarch (Antony 14; Brutus 20): a customary eulogy that turned to pity when Antony saw the crowd moved, ending with the bloody robe shaken out and the murderers named.
  • Dio (44.36–49): a long, formal oration on Caesar's family, deeds and clemency, composed by Dio in the manner of his other speeches.
  • Cicero, the one contemporary: a month later he wrote that Caesar was burned in the Forum after a pitiable eulogy and slaves and paupers were sent against the conspirators' houses with torches (Letters to Atticus 14.10.1); in the Second Philippic he charged Antony that his fine eulogy, his pity and his exhortation lit those torches (2.90–91).

The burning followed. Two men with swords set fire to the bier; the crowd heaped on benches and tribunals, musicians and actors their robes, veterans their arms, women their jewellery; foreigners mourned at the pyre, the Jews above all, who came for nights on end (Suetonius, Julius 84.3–5). The crowd killed the tribune Helvius Cinna, a poet, taking him for the praetor Cornelius Cinna who had attacked Caesar, and carried his head on a spear (Suetonius, Julius 85; Plutarch, Caesar 68).

Where sources disagree. Suetonius' Antony lets documents speak and says little (Julius 84.2); Appian's stages a performance with robe, song and a turning wax effigy (2.144–147); Dio's delivers a set oration (44.36–49). Appian makes the crowd burn the Senate house of the murder, apparently confusing Pompey's hall with the Senate house burned in 52, and puts into Antony's mouth the verse Suetonius has sung at the games. Cicero (Letters to Atticus 14.10.1; Philippics 2.90–91) confirms only that the eulogy was moving and that Antony was blamed for the riot. Shakespeare built chiefly on Plutarch's, though Aldo Setaioli ("Antony's speech in Shakespeare's Julius Caesar and the ancient sources", Prometheus, 2017) argued that the Tudor English Appian (1578) shaped both funeral speeches.

09Afterlife: from the feast of the Ides to Shakespeare

Cicero's verdicts came in private first. The Ides of March consoled him (Letters to Atticus 14.4.2), yet the tyranny lived though the tyrant was dead (14.9.2), and the deed had been done with the courage of men and the planning of a child (14.21.3). In February 43 he wrote to Trebonius in Asia that he wished he had been invited to that finest feast on the Ides of March: there would have been no leftovers (Letters to Friends 10.28.1), the leftover being Antony; he made the same joke to Cassius (12.4.1). In On Duties he defended killing a tyrant, even one who was a friend, as honourable (3.19, 3.32). Brutus struck the claim on his coinage in 42: his portrait, the cap of the freed slave between two daggers and EID MAR, which Dio describes as showing that he and Cassius had freed the fatherland (47.25.3).

The heir answered. The Senate voted to wall up the hall where Caesar fell, to call the Ides the Day of Parricide, and never to meet on it (Suetonius, Julius 88); Augustus counted it a duty that he had driven his father's killers into exile by lawful courts and then beaten them twice in battle (Res Gestae 2), and Mars the Avenger was vowed at Philippi (module 6.2). Suetonius ends his life with the killers' fates: hardly any survived three years or died a natural death, and some used on themselves the daggers they had used on Caesar (Julius 89). In 2012 Antonio Monterroso of the Spanish National Research Council announced that a block of Roman concrete about three metres wide in the remains of the Curia of Pompey, under the Largo di Torre Argentina, was Augustus' sealing of the spot; the identification rests on the ancient report of the closure and is not certain. Under Tiberius praising Brutus and Cassius became a charge, and Cremutius Cordus died of it in AD 25 (module 7.1). Dante's Brutus and Cassius in Lucifer's mouths are module 10.4's.

Shakespeare's play (1599), which module 10.4 places among the Roman plays, follows North's Plutarch scene by scene and changes it for the stage.

The play kept the murder political. When John Wilkes Booth shot Lincoln in April 1865 he shouted, by witnesses' accounts, Virginia's motto sic semper tyrannis, and his diary set him beside Brutus; the previous November he had played Mark Antony in New York in a benefit performance of Julius Caesar with his brothers Edwin and Junius.

10Could the murder have restored the Republic?

The conspirators acted as if removing one man would restore the old order of its own accord. They had no plan for the day after: no armed force beyond gladiators, no measure to put before the people, and no decision on Antony, consul and Caesar's colleague, whom they spared. The magistrates and governors of 44 and 43 held their posts by Caesar's appointment, so the Senate had to confirm his acts to keep them, and the amnesty bought peace by leaving his system in place. Caesar's veterans, settled and waiting to be settled, wanted his grants honoured; Lepidus' legion stood by the city; and the urban crowd, as the funeral showed, was Caesar's. Caesar's friend Matius saw it at once: if Caesar with all his genius could find no way out, who would find one now? (Cicero, Letters to Atticus 14.1.1).

Whether a better-planned conspiracy could have succeeded is a counterfactual; whether the Republic could have been restored at all is the structural question of deep dive module 11.8, which weighs Dio's view that monarchy was the only government for an empire (44.2) among the explanations.

Where sources disagree. Cicero believed the deed half done: had Antony died with Caesar, there would have been no leftovers (Letters to Friends 10.28.1), and the Senate should have been summoned at once. Matius (Letters to Atticus 14.1.1) and Appian (2.120) judged the obstacle to be the state of Rome itself, a crowd and an army attached to Caesar's name. Among modern historians Barry Strauss (The Death of Caesar, 2015) gives Decimus and the conspiracy's military side more weight than most and sympathises with the view that the Republic could have survived with reform; one reviewer, G. Matthew Adkins, objected that the Republic had been in decline for at least ninety years before the Ides, since the killing of Tiberius Gracchus in 133.

11Self-test

  1. Which narrative of the murder is closest in time to the event, and why does its author's position matter?
    Answer

    Nicolaus of Damascus' Life of Augustus, written in the Augustan period by a Greek courtier of Herod and surviving in tenth-century excerpts. It is independent of the Plutarch–Appian–Suetonius tradition, but written for Augustus' side, so its picture of the killers as men with personal grievances (19) has a purpose.

  2. What three episodes besides the Lupercalia made Caesar's honours a provocation in early 44?
    Answer

    He received the Senate seated before the temple of Venus Genetrix (Julius 78.1; Plutarch, Caesar 60); the senators swore an oath to protect him and declared him sacrosanct (Appian 2.144–145); and a rumour said Cotta would propose the title of king on the Ides because only a king could conquer Parthia (Julius 79.3).

  3. What did Decimus Brutus and Trebonius have in common, and why does it complicate the idea of a republican conspiracy?
    Answer

    Both were Caesar's own officers, rewarded with high office: Decimus was consul designate for 42 and named among the heirs in the second degree (Julius 83.2), Trebonius consul in 45. Their part suggests that grievances and calculation among Caesar's own men mattered as well as principle among pardoned Pompeians.

  4. How did Brutus test potential recruits, and why was Cicero left out?
    Answer

    By philosophical argument: Favonius held civil war worse than illegal monarchy, Statilius that a wise man should not risk himself for fools, and Labeo, who argued against both, was brought in. Cicero was excluded because his age and caution would blunt the plan (Plutarch, Brutus 12).

  5. What did Spurinna say on the morning of the Ides, and how does the heartless victim show the omens growing after the event?
    Answer

    When Caesar mocked him that the Ides had come, he answered that they had come but not gone (Julius 81.4). Cicero's Quintus dates the heartless victim to the day Caesar first sat in the golden chair (On Divination 1.119); Appian moves it to the morning of the Ides (2.116).

  6. Who kept Antony outside, and what does the disagreement show about Plutarch?
    Answer

    Trebonius, according to Cicero (Philippics 2.34), Appian (2.117), Dio (44.19) and Plutarch's own Brutus 17. Plutarch's Caesar 66 names Decimus, so even one author telling the event in different lives can contradict himself.

  7. How many wounds did Caesar receive?
    Answer

    Twenty-three according to Suetonius (Julius 82.3), Appian (2.117) and Plutarch's Caesar (66), thirty-five according to Nicolaus (24); Plutarch's Brutus (17) and Dio (44.19) say only many. Suetonius adds that Antistius judged only the second mortal.

  8. What are three readings of kai su, teknon, and why is the phrase uncertain in the first place?
    Answer

    A father's reproach (which needs the paternity rumour); Russell's curse against the evil eye, "you too"; and Ziogas' opening of a prophecy that Brutus too would die, with Augustus' Greek words to the boy Galba as a parallel (Galba 4.1). Both reporters, Suetonius (Julius 82.2) and Dio (44.19), give it as what some say against a version in which Caesar said nothing.

  9. How do Suetonius and Appian differ on Antony's funeral speech, and what does Cicero confirm?
    Answer

    Suetonius has a herald read the decrees and oath with very few words from Antony (Julius 84.2); Appian has a lament with the bloody robe on a spear and a turning wax effigy with twenty-three wounds (2.144–147). Cicero confirms a moving eulogy and blames it for the torches (Letters to Atticus 14.10.1; Philippics 2.90–91).

  10. Name three things Shakespeare changes from Plutarch in Julius Caesar.
    Answer

    Any three: the triumph and the Lupercalia on one day; the crown offered thrice; Calpurnia's dream of a bleeding statue; "Et tu, Brute", which is not in Plutarch; Brutus' and Antony's speeches back to back at a funeral on the day of the murder; the apparition at Philippi made Caesar's own ghost.

12Carry forward

  1. The Ides are known through a contemporary who was not a narrator (Cicero) and narrators who were not contemporaries; most of the famous details (the omens, the last words, the funeral theatre) enter the record generations later and grow with each telling.
  2. The conspiracy joined pardoned enemies and Caesar's own marshals, and the motives the sources assign follow their authors' loyalties: liberty for Plutarch's Brutus, envy and grievance for Nicolaus, writing for Augustus.
  3. The murder removed the man and left his system: acts confirmed, offices held by his appointment, veterans and crowd loyal to his name. The question it leaves, whether the Republic could be restored at all, is the one deep dive module 11.8 answers.
