01The consulship of 59: Bibulus watches the sky

Module 5.3 ended with the private alliance of 60; the consulship of 59 made it public. Caesar's colleague was Marcus Calpurnius Bibulus, Cato's son-in-law. Caesar had the proceedings of Senate and people published daily (Suetonius, Julius 20.1), then brought an agrarian law for Pompey's veterans and the urban poor. When Cato talked it out in the Senate, Caesar had him led towards prison (20.4) and took the bill to the people. Bibulus came to the Forum to block the vote; a basket of dung was emptied over his head and his fasces were broken (Plutarch, Pompey 48; Dio 38.6).

For the remaining eight months Bibulus stayed at home and issued edicts that he was watching the sky (servare de caelo) on every assembly day (Julius 20.1): the augural weapon of module 2.1, by which a magistrate's report of adverse signs halted public business. Caesar ignored him and legislated through the people. Wits dated documents to the consulship of Julius and Caesar (20.2).

The laws served all three partners. Pompey's eastern settlement was ratified; the tax-farmers of Asia, Crassus' clients, had their contract cut by a third (Julius 20.3); the Campanian land went to fathers of three or more children; a law on extortion by governors outlasted the Republic. Pompey married Caesar's daughter Julia, and Caesar married Calpurnia, daughter of Lucius Piso, his successor as consul (21). The tribune Vatinius' law gave Caesar Cisalpine Gaul and Illyricum for five years, and the Senate added Transalpine Gaul (22.1). As pontifex maximus Caesar presided over the adoption that made Publius Clodius a plebeian; his tribunate of 58 and Cicero's exile are deep dive modules 11.3 and 11.5.

Where sources disagree. Whether Bibulus' edicts voided the year's laws was never settled. Cicero argued that Clodius' adoption, carried while Bibulus watched the sky, was invalid (De domo sua 39–40), an argument that reached every law of 59; in 58 two praetors demanded an inquiry into Caesar's acts, which the Senate would not take up (Julius 23.1). Many modern scholars doubt that a notice sent from home, rather than announced in person, counted as obnuntiatio at all, and Robert Morstein-Marx (Julius Caesar and the Roman People, 2021) argues that Bibulus stretched obstruction past its customary limits and that the acts of 59 stayed formally valid. The open question is one reason Caesar would never face Rome as a private citizen.

02Gaul in outline, 58–50

The Gallic War, seven books by Caesar and an eighth by his officer Aulus Hirtius, is almost the only narrative, so the outline below is Caesar's account of Caesar, with his own figures. The campaigns, the ethnography and the archaeology of Alesia are deep dive module 11.6.

The Rhine bridge of 55, built in ten days and broken down after eighteen (4.17–19), and the British landings were addressed to Rome as much as to the enemy: the Senate voted fifteen days of thanksgiving for 57, more than anyone had received (2.35), and twenty after 55 and 52 (4.38, 7.90). In 52 Vercingetorix of the Arverni united most of Gaul and repulsed Caesar at Gergovia. At Alesia Caesar ringed him with siege lines facing both inwards and outwards, beat off the relief army and took his surrender (7.68–89); Vercingetorix walked in the triumph of 46 and was then executed (Dio 43.19). Gaul paid 40 million sesterces a year in tribute (Suetonius, Julius 25.1).

Later writers gave totals. Velleius Paterculus: more than 400,000 killed and more captured (2.47.1). Plutarch: three million fought, a million killed, a million captured (Caesar 15). Appian: over four million fought, a million killed and as many captured (Gallic History 1). Pliny the Elder: 1,192,000 killed in Caesar's battles outside the civil wars, which he refuses to count as glory, calling it a great wrong to the human race (Natural History 7.92). Romans objected at the time too: when Caesar attacked the Usipetes and Tencteri while their envoys were in his camp, Cato proposed handing him over to the Germans (Plutarch, Caesar 22).

Where sources disagree. Every count goes back to Caesar, who had reasons to inflate, and historians since Hans Delbrück have cut his Helvetian and German figures on grounds of supply and terrain. Pliny's precise total looks like a sum of official reports and covers wars outside Gaul; the round millions of Plutarch and Appian are two or three times Velleius' figure. The charge of genocide built on these numbers is weighed in deep dive module 11.6.

03Luca, Julia and Carrhae

By 56 the alliance was fraying, and Lucius Domitius Ahenobarbus, candidate for 55, promised to take Caesar's army from him (Suetonius, Julius 24.1). Caesar met Crassus at Ravenna and Pompey at Luca in April; Plutarch counts 200 senators come to pay court (Caesar 21). Pompey and Crassus were consuls again in 55. Their law extended Caesar's command for five years, and the tribune Trebonius gave Pompey the Spains, which he governed through legates while staying near Rome, and Crassus Syria. Cicero's forced change of course is deep dive module 11.5.

Two deaths followed. Julia died in childbirth in 54 (Plutarch, Pompey 53); Caesar offered Pompey his great-niece Octavia (Julius 27.1), but Pompey married Cornelia, daughter of Metellus Scipio. In 53 Crassus invaded Parthia. At Carrhae the general Surena's horse archers, resupplied from a camel train, and his armoured lancers destroyed the army; Crassus' son Publius died fighting, and Crassus was killed at a parley. Plutarch gives 20,000 dead and 10,000 captured (Crassus 31). Lucan's image is the ancient verdict: Crassus had been the isthmus that kept two seas apart, and Julia might have held husband and father together (Civil War 1.98–120).

04The breakdown with Pompey and the Rubicon

In January 52 Clodius was killed by Milo's men and his followers burned the Senate house as his pyre (deep dive module 11.3). On Bibulus' motion Pompey became consul without a colleague (Plutarch, Pompey 54). A law of the ten tribunes let Caesar stand for the consulship in absence, and Pompey's law requiring candidates to appear in person had an exemption for him added after it was already engraved (Julius 26.1, 28.3).

Caesar meant to pass straight from his command to a consulship in 48, since Cato had sworn to prosecute him once he dismissed his army (Julius 30.3). In 50 the tribune Curio, his debts paid by Caesar, vetoed every move to recall him and proposed that both dynasts disarm. On 1 December the Senate agreed by 370 votes to 22 (Appian, Civil Wars 2.30), but the consul Gaius Marcellus went out to hand Pompey a sword and the defence of the state (Plutarch, Pompey 59). On 7 January 49 the Senate passed the final decree, and the tribunes Antony and Quintus Cassius fled to Caesar (Civil War 1.5).

Caesar crossed the Rubicon, the stream at the edge of his province, with one legion (Civil War 1.7.8) a few days later. Suetonius has him hesitate, see an apparition piping on the bank, and say "the die is cast" (Julius 31–33); Plutarch says he spoke it in Greek, with Asinius Pollio beside him (Pompey 60; Caesar 32); the Greek tag is a line of Menander. Caesar's own Civil War never mentions the river and moves straight to Ariminum (1.8). His case, his dignitas, the tribunes' rights and the people's freedom from a faction (1.9, 1.22), is read as propaganda in module 5.6.

Where sources disagree. No ancient source states when Caesar's command legally ended, and the question Mommsen posed in Die Rechtsfrage zwischen Caesar und dem Senat (1857) has yielded dates across 50 and 49 without agreement. The ancient verdicts divide as sharply. Pollio's Caesar blamed his enemies; Cicero, trying to mediate, wrote that each side had sought domination (Letters to Atticus 8.11.2); Lucan, that Caesar could bear no superior and Pompey no equal (1.125–126).

05Civil war: Italy, Spain, Dyrrachium, Pharsalus

Pompey had boasted that he need only stamp on the ground for legions to rise in Italy (Plutarch, Pompey 57). Instead he abandoned Rome and then Italy, crossing from Brundisium in March 49. At Corfinium Caesar released the captured Domitius and his officers (Civil War 1.16–23) and wrote that this would be a new way of conquering, to make oneself safe by mercy and generosity (Cicero, Letters to Atticus 9.7C). To the pardoned, clemency was a master's gift. Caesar took the reserve treasury from the temple of Saturn over a tribune's protest (Plutarch, Caesar 35), then marched on Pompey's legions in Spain. At Ilerda he forced Afranius and Petreius to surrender by cutting them off from water, without a pitched battle (Civil War 1.37–87). In Africa Curio died with his army fighting King Juba of Numidia (2.23–44).

In January 48 Caesar crossed to Epirus past Bibulus' fleet. At Dyrrachium he tried to wall in a larger army, his men living on bread made from a root (3.48), until Pompey broke out in July; the enemy, Caesar said, would have won that day had they had a commander who knew how to win (Plutarch, Caesar 39). At Pharsalus in Thessaly, on 9 August 48 by the unreformed calendar, cohorts held back as a fourth line routed Pompey's cavalry and turned his flank (Civil War 3.88–99). Viewing the dead, Caesar said, by Pollio's report, "they wanted it": after such deeds he would have been condemned had he not turned to his army (Suetonius, Julius 30.4).

Where sources disagree. Caesar gives Pompey 45,000 men to his own 22,000 (Civil War 3.88–89) and claims about 15,000 Pompeians killed and more than 24,000 surrendered, for a loss of no more than 200 soldiers and about 30 centurions (3.99). Asinius Pollio, an officer on Caesar's side, put the Pompeian dead at 6,000, as Plutarch reports him (Caesar 46). Caesar's own losses look improbably low, and the gap lies within the Caesarian camp itself.

06Civil war: Egypt, Africa, Munda

Pompey fled to Egypt, where the advisers of the boy king Ptolemy XIII had him stabbed as he landed at Pelusium in September 48, by the Egyptian Achillas and a Roman who had served under him (Plutarch, Pompey 77–80); Caesar, arriving days later, wept over his signet ring (Caesar 48). Drawn into Ptolemy's quarrel with his sister Cleopatra VII, he was besieged in the palace quarter of Alexandria until March 47. Cleopatra reached him carried in a bedding sack, not the carpet of later legend (Caesar 49). Fire from the burning ships spread into the city; whether it reached the Library is argued in Greek module 7.2. Ptolemy drowned after a battle by the Nile, and Cleopatra was restored. She toured the Nile with Caesar (Suetonius, Julius 52.1) and bore a son, Caesarion, whom Caesar's friend Oppius later wrote was not his (52.2). The queen herself belongs to module 5.5 and Greek modules 7.1 and 7.3.

Caesar beat Pharnaces of Pontus at Zela in August 47: veni, vidi, vici, in a letter according to Plutarch (Caesar 50), on a triumphal placard according to Suetonius (Julius 37.2). After a mutiny in Italy (deep dive module 11.2) he defeated Metellus Scipio and King Juba at Thapsus on 6 April 46. At Utica Cato saw the others safe, read Plato's Phaedo and stabbed himself, tearing open the wound when it was bound (Plutarch, Cato Minor 66–70). Caesar said he begrudged Cato his death, as Cato had begrudged him the saving of his life (72). Dead, Cato became a cause: Cicero's Cato and Caesar's Anticato fought over him (module 5.6).

Four triumphs in 46, over Gaul, Egypt, Pontus and Africa, paraded Vercingetorix and Cleopatra's sister Arsinoe (Julius 37; Dio 43.19). Pompey's sons Gnaeus and Sextus then raised Spain. At Munda on 17 March 45 Caesar had often fought for victory, he said, but this time fought for his life (Plutarch, Caesar 56); the Spanish War and Plutarch agree on about 30,000 Pompeian dead to 1,000 Caesarian (Spanish War 31; Caesar 56). Sextus escaped (module 5.5). A triumph over fellow citizens followed, and offended.

07The dictator's reforms

Caesar was dictator for eleven days in 49, for a year after Pharsalus, for ten years from 46, and from early 44 for life, dictator perpetuo on his coins; he was consul in 48, 46, 45 and 44. In the months between campaigns he legislated more widely than anyone since Sulla.

  • Calendar. The Julian year from 1 January 45 (module 2.3) replaced the pontiffs' political discretion with a rule.
  • Colonies. Some 80,000 citizens were settled overseas (Suetonius, Julius 42.1), among them at Carthage and Corinth, both destroyed in 146 (Plutarch, Caesar 57); the charter of one, Urso in southern Spain, survives on bronze tablets.
  • Rome. The free grain list was cut from 320,000 to 150,000 (Julius 41.3), the collegia dissolved except the ancient ones (42.3), debts settled at pre-war valuations (42.2), and the Forum of Caesar with its temple of Venus Genetrix dedicated in 46.
  • Senate and citizens. The Senate grew to about 900 (Dio 43.47), the quaestors to forty and the praetors to sixteen (43.51.3–4); the Transpadanes became citizens (Dio 41.36).

The watchword was clemency. The Senate voted a temple to Caesar's Clemency, and he restored Pompey's fallen statues, which, said Cicero, fixed his own (Plutarch, Caesar 57). Pardoned Pompeians, Marcus Brutus and Gaius Cassius among them, were praetors in 44.

Where sources disagree. The hostile tradition filled the new Senate with soldiers, freedmen's sons and Gauls: Suetonius quotes a lampoon of Gauls who took off their trousers to put on the senator's broad stripe (Julius 80.2), and Dio names soldiers and freedmen's sons (43.47). Ronald Syme ("Caesar, the Senate and Italy", Papers of the British School at Rome 1938) examined the known names and found mostly landed men of the Italian towns, knights and officers. The "Gauls" were most likely from Cisalpine or Narbonese Gaul, and the jokes were propaganda against a Senate opened to Italy.

08Honours that looked like kingship

After Munda Caesar accepted honours that Suetonius calls too great for a mortal: the dictatorship for life, the prefecture of morals, Father of his Country, a statue among the kings, a golden chair in the Senate house, his image carried with the gods' at the games, temples, a priest of his own, and a month, Iulius (Julius 76.1). Antony was named his flamen (Cicero, Philippics 2.110), and early in 44 Caesar became the first living Roman portrayed on Rome's coins. Stefan Weinstock (Divus Julius, 1971) read the whole as a planned cult of the living ruler; the reconstruction has been widely doubted, notably in John North's review "Praesens divus" (Journal of Roman Studies 1975).

He received the Senate without rising from his seat (Julius 78.1). In January 44 the tribunes Marullus and Flavus removed a diadem placed on his statue and arrested the man responsible, and Caesar had them deposed (79.1); hailed as king, he answered that he was Caesar, not Rex, a Roman surname (79.2). At the Lupercalia on 15 February (module 2.3) Antony, consul and running with the Luperci, twice offered him a diadem; Caesar twice refused and sent it to Jupiter on the Capitol (Plutarch, Caesar 61; Julius 79.2). A rumour followed that the Sibylline books allowed only a king to conquer Parthia, and that the title would be proposed on 15 March, three days before Caesar left for a Parthian war (79.3; Appian, Civil Wars 2.110). He had already dismissed his bodyguard (Julius 86.1).

Where sources disagree. Cicero, attacking Antony, makes the offer Antony's own act and says Caesar had his refusal entered in the calendar (Philippics 2.84–87). Nicolaus of Damascus, writing in Augustus' circle, has a Licinius and Cassius present the diadem before Antony, implicating a future assassin; Plutarch says the applause for the offer was thin and planted (Caesar 61); Dio reports the suspicion that Caesar arranged it all (44.11). A test of opinion, Antony forcing the issue, or a trap: the sources allow each.

09The Ides of March in outline

More than sixty conspired (Julius 80.4), led by two pardoned Pompeians, Marcus Brutus and Gaius Cassius, and two of Caesar's own officers, Decimus Brutus and Gaius Trebonius. Their motives are deep dive module 11.7, Servilia, Brutus' mother and Caesar's lover, deep dive module 11.4. The omens, the haruspex Spurinna's warning and Calpurnia's dream, were collected afterwards (Julius 81). On 15 March 44 the Senate met in the hall of Pompey's theatre, the Senate house still being rebuilt. Trebonius kept Antony talking outside (Cicero, Philippics 2.34). Tillius Cimber seized Caesar's toga and Casca struck first. Caesar took twenty-three wounds, of which, the physician Antistius judged, only the second was mortal; Suetonius says he only groaned, though some wrote that he said to Brutus, in Greek, "you too, child?" (Julius 82.2–3). He covered his head and fell at the base of Pompey's statue (Plutarch, Caesar 66). The senators fled. The conspirators went out with drawn daggers, calling the citizens to liberty, and made for the Capitol (Caesar 67).

10Caesar as author and as legend

The Gallic War and the unfinished Civil War are commentarii, notes nominally left for historians to work up, told in the third person; the Alexandrian, African and Spanish War were added by his officers. Lost is On Analogy, a treatise on correct Latin written while crossing the Alps (Suetonius, Julius 56.5). What Cicero and Asinius Pollio thought of the Commentaries, and how their plain style argues Caesar's case, is module 5.6; deep dive module 11.6 tests them against the conquest.

The legend began at once. Cicero's On Duties made Caesar the man who overturned all law, divine and human, for a supremacy he had imagined (1.26). Octavian made him a god: the comet at the games of July 44 was read as his soul received into heaven (Julius 88), and he was deified in 42. Velleius praised a spirit raised above human nature (2.41); Lucan made him a thunderbolt beside Pompey's withered oak (Civil War 1.129–157); Pliny admired a mind that could dictate four letters at once (7.91); Plutarch paired him with Alexander; Suetonius judged that he abused his power and was justly killed (Julius 76.1). His name became the emperors' title and, as Kaiser and tsar, outlived Rome; Shakespeare's Caesar and his successors are deep dive module 11.7 and module 10.4.

11Self-test

  1. What was Bibulus doing when he "watched the sky" in 59, and why did it still matter after his consulship?
    Answer

    He announced by edict from his house that he was observing the heavens on assembly days, a form of obnuntiatio that, if valid, halted public business (Suetonius, Julius 20.1). Caesar legislated anyway, so his acts were open to the charge of invalidity (Cicero, De domo sua 39–40; Julius 23.1), one reason he feared returning to Rome as a private citizen.

  2. Why should Caesar's figures for the Gallic dead be treated with caution, and how far apart are the later totals?
    Answer

    They come almost entirely from Caesar, who had reasons to inflate, and modern historians cut his largest counts (about 368,000 Helvetii, 430,000 Usipetes and Tencteri). Velleius gives over 400,000 killed (2.47.1); Plutarch (Caesar 15) and Appian (Gallic History 1) a million killed and a million captured; Pliny 1,192,000 killed in all Caesar's foreign wars (Natural History 7.92).

  3. What was agreed after Luca, and which two deaths broke the alliance?
    Answer

    Pompey and Crassus took the consulship of 55, Caesar's command was extended for five years, and Pompey received the Spains and Crassus Syria. Julia's death in 54 cut the marriage tie, and Crassus' death after Carrhae in 53 removed the third partner, Lucan's isthmus between two seas (1.98–120).

  4. What did Caesar need in 50–49, and what did the Senate vote on 1 December 50?
    Answer

    He needed to pass straight from his command to a second consulship in 48, as the law of the ten tribunes allowed, because as a private citizen he faced prosecution (Julius 30.3). The Senate voted 370 to 22 that both he and Pompey give up their armies (Appian, Civil Wars 2.30), but the consul Gaius Marcellus ignored the vote and turned to Pompey.

  5. How do Suetonius, Plutarch and Caesar himself treat the crossing of the Rubicon?
    Answer

    Suetonius has hesitation, an apparition and "the die is cast" (Julius 31–33); Plutarch has it spoken in Greek, with Pollio present (Pompey 60; Caesar 32). Caesar's Civil War omits the river altogether and moves straight to Ariminum (1.8).

  6. Compare Caesar's and Pollio's figures for Pharsalus.
    Answer

    Caesar claims about 15,000 Pompeians killed and more than 24,000 surrendered for a loss of no more than 200 men and about 30 centurions (Civil War 3.99). Pollio, also on Caesar's side, put the Pompeian dead at 6,000, as Plutarch reports (Caesar 46).

  7. Why did Cato's death at Utica make him more dangerous to Caesar than he had been alive?
    Answer

    By choosing death over Caesar's pardon (Plutarch, Cato Minor 66–72) he exposed clemency as a master's gift and became a martyr of liberty. Cicero's Cato and Caesar's Anticato made his memory a political battleground.

  8. Name three reforms of the dictatorship and the problem each addressed.
    Answer

    Any three of: the Julian calendar, ending the pontiffs' politically driven intercalation (module 2.3); 80,000 citizens sent to overseas colonies such as Carthage and Corinth, providing for veterans and the urban poor (Julius 42.1); the grain list cut from 320,000 to 150,000 (41.3), curbing the cost and abuse of free grain; debts settled at pre-war valuations; a Senate enlarged to about 900 with more magistracies, rewarding supporters and admitting Italian notables.

  9. What happened at the Lupercalia of 44, and why is its meaning disputed?
    Answer

    Antony, consul and Lupercus, twice offered Caesar a diadem, and Caesar refused and sent it to Jupiter on the Capitol. Cicero makes it Antony's act with the refusal recorded in the calendar (Philippics 2.84–87); Nicolaus implicates Cassius; Dio reports the suspicion that Caesar staged it (44.11).

  10. How did Romans judge Caesar after the Ides, from Cicero to Suetonius?
    Answer

    Cicero made him the man who overturned all law, divine and human, for a supremacy he had imagined (On Duties 1.26); Octavian had him deified in 42, with the comet of 44 read as his soul in heaven (Julius 88); Velleius praised a spirit raised above human nature (2.41); Suetonius concluded that he abused his power and was justly killed (Julius 76.1).

12Carry forward

  1. Caesar's career after 59 was driven by legal exposure: laws passed against the auspices and the threat of prosecution meant he could never safely become a private citizen, and the civil war began over whether a proconsul could be forced to be one.
  2. Almost every number and motive in this story comes from Caesar himself or from writers using partisan witnesses (Pollio, Oppius, Cicero) generations later; always ask whose figure or verdict you are reading before you use it.
  3. The dictatorship joined lasting reform (calendar, colonies, an Italian Senate) to honours indistinguishable from kingship and to a clemency that made the pardoned into dependants; the conspirators removed the man, not the problem of one man's supremacy, which modules 5.5 and 6.1 take up.
