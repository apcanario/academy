01Jugurtha and Sallust's case against the nobility (118–105 BC)

Micipsa of Numidia died in 118, leaving his kingdom jointly to his sons Adherbal and Hiempsal and to his adopted nephew Jugurtha, who had served under Scipio at Numantia (Sallust, Jugurtha 7–11; module 4.4). Jugurtha had Hiempsal killed and drove Adherbal out; a Senate commission under Lucius Opimius split the kingdom in 116 and gave him the richer west (16). In 112 he took Cirta and killed Adherbal with every adult Numidian and Italian trader in it (26). The tribune-elect Gaius Memmius forced a war, but the consul Bestia made a quick peace in 111 (28–29). Summoned to Rome to testify, Jugurtha was silenced by a bribed tribune and had a rival murdered; leaving, he called Rome "a city for sale, and doomed to perish quickly if it finds a buyer" (32–35). In 110 Aulus Albinus' army passed under the yoke (38), and a tribune's law of 109, the lex Mamilia, set up a court that condemned Opimius, Bestia and others for taking the king's money (40; Cicero, Brutus 128).

Quintus Caecilius Metellus, consul in 109, restored discipline and beat Jugurtha on the Muthul (44–53). His legate Gaius Marius asked leave to stand for the consulship and was told to wait until Metellus' son, then about twenty, could stand with him (64). Marius was elected for 107, and a plebiscite gave him Numidia over the Senate's prorogation of Metellus (73). The war ended by diplomacy: his quaestor Lucius Cornelius Sulla persuaded Bocchus of Mauretania to hand Jugurtha over in 105 (102–113). Jugurtha walked in Marius' triumph on 1 January 104 and died in prison (Plutarch, Marius 12). Sulla wore a signet ring showing the surrender, and Plutarch dates their feud from it (Marius 10).

Sallust wrote about 41–40 BC, some sixty-five years later, as a Caesarian ex-governor of Africa accused of plundering it (Cassius Dio 43.9). He chose the war, he says, because it was great and savage, and because "then for the first time the arrogance of the nobility was opposed" (5.1). Bribery drives his plot, and his digression on parties traces the rot to the removal of the fear of Carthage (41; module 5.6). His chronology is loose, and the Senate's reluctance to send legions to Africa while the Cimbri threatened the north needs no bribe to explain it.

02Marius, the new man, and the "Marian reforms"

Marius was born about 157 near Arpinum to an equestrian family with no senatorial ancestors (Velleius 2.11.1); Plutarch makes his parents poor labourers (Marius 3). As tribune in 119 he narrowed the gangways voters crossed to cast their ballots, making them harder to pressure (Cicero, On the Laws 3.38), and he married Julia, sister of Julius Caesar's father (Marius 6). Sallust gives him a new man's manifesto as consul-elect: the nobles despise his newness, he their idleness; he has no ancestral masks, only decorations and scars (Jugurtha 85). He was consul seven times: in 107, in five successive years from 104 to 100, the first in his absence and all against the rules on repeated office, and in 86.

The "Marian reforms" are a modern bundle. Sallust says that in 107 Marius enrolled volunteers, most of them from the capite censi, citizens registered by head alone because they owned too little for a property class (Jugurtha 86). Plutarch adds soldiers carrying their own kit, "Marius' mules" (Marius 13), and a pilum whose wooden pin snapped on impact (25); Pliny makes the eagle the sole legionary standard from 104 (Natural History 10.16). No ancient source credits Marius with the cohort, which Polybius already describes in 206 (11.23). Whether a client army followed is deep dive module 11.2; the army as an institution is module 9.2.

Where sources disagree. Emilio Gabba (1949) read the falling property minimum, 11,000 asses for the fifth class in Livy (1.43), 400 drachmas in Polybius (6.19.2), 1,500 asses in Cicero (On the Republic 2.40), as reductions that had already filled the legions with the poor, so that Marius only completed a process. John Rich (1983) denied the manpower shortage behind it, and François Cadiou (L'armée imaginaire, 2018) calls the post-Marian proletarian army a historiographical mirage. Sallust describes one emergency levy, not a law.

03The Cimbri and Teutones

The Cimbri and Teutones, migrating peoples from the north, routed the consul Carbo at Noreia in 113 (Livy, Periochae 63); in 107 their allies the Tigurini killed the consul Lucius Cassius and sent his army under the yoke (Caesar, Gallic War 1.7, 1.12). In 106 Quintus Servilius Caepio took Tolosa, whose temple gold vanished and became a proverb (Strabo 4.1.13). On 6 October 105 at Arausio on the Rhône, Caepio as proconsul refused to combine with the consul Gnaeus Mallius Maximus, a new man, and both armies were destroyed (Plutarch, Lucullus 27; Periochae 67).

The Cimbri turned to Spain, and Marius trained a new army in southern Gaul. In 102 he destroyed the Teutones and Ambrones near Aquae Sextiae, where the Massiliots later fenced their vineyards with the bones (Marius 18–21). In 101 he and Quintus Lutatius Catulus annihilated the Cimbri, who had crossed the eastern Alps, on the Raudine Plain near Vercellae, a site still uncertain (Velleius 2.12.5; Marius 25–27). Marius was hailed as Rome's third founder (27).

Where sources disagree. Livy's epitomator, citing Valerius Antias, gives 80,000 soldiers and 40,000 servants and camp followers dead at Arausio (Periochae 67). For Aquae Sextiae the Periochae give 200,000 enemy dead and 90,000 captured (68), Plutarch more than 100,000 taken or killed (Marius 21); for Vercellae, 160,000 dead and 60,000 captured (Periochae 68) against Plutarch's 60,000 captured and twice as many dead (Marius 27). Livy distrusted Antias' numbers (33.10); none can be checked.

04Saturninus and 100 BC

Marius' veterans needed land. Lucius Appuleius Saturninus, tribune in 103, carried allotments in Africa for them. In 100, tribune again, with Marius consul for the sixth time and Gaius Servilius Glaucia praetor, he passed a law distributing land in Gaul and founding colonies, and made every senator swear within five days to obey it; Metellus Numidicus refused and went into exile (Appian, Civil Wars 1.29–31). At the elections for 99 his men killed Gaius Memmius, Glaucia's rival for the consulship (1.32). The Senate passed its final decree (module 5.1), and Marius as consul besieged his allies on the Capitol and accepted their surrender. Appian has a crowd stone them with roof tiles in the Senate house (1.32), Plutarch kill them in the Forum (Marius 30). Marius had saved an order he had bypassed and pleased no one. He went east, and told Mithridates of Pontus to be stronger than Rome or obey it in silence (31). Saturninus' grain law and the politics of the street are deep dive module 11.3.

05Livius Drusus and the Social War (91–88 BC)

The Italian allies fought Rome's wars and shared little of the profit. The lex Licinia Mucia of 95 expelled Italians passing as citizens; Asconius calls it perhaps the chief cause of the war (on Cicero, Pro Cornelio). In 92 an equestrian jury in the extortion court (module 4.4) condemned Publius Rutilius Rufus, who had protected Asia from the tax-farmers (Velleius 2.13.2). In 91 the tribune Marcus Livius Drusus proposed juries drawn from a Senate enlarged by three hundred equestrians, colonies, and citizenship for the Italians (Appian, Civil Wars 1.35). The Senate annulled his laws "by one little line" (Cicero, On the Laws 2.14), and an assassin stabbed him in his house (1.36).

The revolt began at Asculum late in 91 with the killing of every Roman in the town (1.38). Marsi, Paeligni, Samnites, Lucanians and others formed a state with its capital at Corfinium, renamed Italia, a senate of five hundred, two consuls and twelve praetors (Diodorus 37.2; Strabo 5.4.2). Its denarii, struck on the Roman standard (Alberto Campana, La monetazione degli insorti italici durante la guerra sociale, 1987), read ITALIA in Latin or Víteliú in Oscan; one type shows warriors swearing an oath over a pig, another the Italian bull goring the Roman wolf, and some name the Samnite consul Papius Mutilus in Oscan as embratur, imperator.

In 90 a consul was killed and Marius, serving as a legate, withdrew pleading age (Plutarch, Marius 33). Rome conceded. The lex Julia of 90 enfranchised Latins and allies who had stayed loyal (Civil Wars 1.49); the lex Plautia Papiria of 89 any man enrolled in an allied community who registered with a praetor at Rome within sixty days (Cicero, Pro Archia 7). Force did the rest. Pompeius Strabo took Asculum in 89, with the young Pompey on his council (ILS 8888), and Sulla, as legate, won the grass crown at Nola (Pliny, Natural History 22.12). Samnites and Lucanians held out until 82. The new citizens were confined to a few tribes, ten new ones voting last in Appian (1.49), eight existing ones in Velleius (2.20.2), and their distribution became the issue of 88.

Where sources disagree. Appian presents the war as a fight for citizenship (Civil Wars 1.34–39), and Velleius calls the allies' cause most just, since they asked for the citizenship of the empire they defended (2.15). P. A. Brunt ("Italian Aims at the Time of the Social War", 1965) argued that they wanted citizenship as protection and a share of empire; Henrik Mouritsen (Italian Unification, 1998) that they fought for independence, a state called Italia with its own coins being no lobby for enfranchisement.

06Mithridates and the march on Rome (88 BC)

Mithridates VI Eupator of Pontus had built an empire round the Black Sea. In 89 the Roman envoy Manius Aquillius pushed Nicomedes IV of Bithynia to raid Pontus; in 88 Mithridates overran the province of Asia, welcomed by cities squeezed by tax-farmers, and killed Aquillius by pouring molten gold down his throat (Appian, Mithridatic Wars 11–21). From Ephesus he ordered the cities to kill on a single day every Roman and Italian, with their wives, children and Italian freedmen (22–23).

Where sources disagree. Valerius Maximus (9.2 ext. 3) and Memnon of Heraclea (22.9) give 80,000 dead; Plutarch gives 150,000 (Sulla 24). Appian, who describes the killing city by city, gives no total. No one could have counted, and the figures measure Roman outrage better than they measure people.

At Rome the consul Sulla held the command. The tribune Publius Sulpicius proposed to spread the new citizens through all thirty-five tribes and, to carry it, allied with Marius, who wanted the war. When the consuls suspended public business, Sulpicius' gangs rioted and drove Sulla to take refuge, Plutarch says, in Marius' house; the law passed and the command went to Marius (Plutarch, Sulla 8–10; Appian, Civil Wars 1.55–56). Sulla told his six legions at Nola that the eastern plunder would go to other men. They stoned Marius' officers and marched; all Sulla's officers but one quaestor refused to follow, and he told envoys he came to free Rome from tyrants (1.57). His men fought their way in by the Esquiline, the first Roman army to take the city as an enemy's (1.58–60). Marius, Sulpicius and ten others were outlawed. Sulpicius was killed; Marius escaped through the marshes of Minturnae to Africa and bade a messenger report that he had seen Marius sitting among the ruins of Carthage (Marius 37–40). Sulla let Lucius Cornelius Cinna be elected consul for 87 on an oath to respect his settlement, and sailed for Greece (Sulla 10).

07Marius, Cinna and the peace of Dardanus (87–84 BC)

Cinna broke his oath, was driven out by his colleague Gnaeus Octavius after a battle in the Forum, and was deposed by the Senate (Civil Wars 1.64–65). He won over the army at Nola; Marius landed in Etruria and joined him, and Rome, blockaded, surrendered late in 87. Octavius' head was displayed on the Rostra, the first consul's to be (1.71). The orator Marcus Antonius was beheaded, Catulus suffocated himself with charcoal fumes, and a slave bodyguard killed whomever Marius did not greet (Plutarch, Marius 43–44). Marius entered his seventh consulship in January 86 and died within the month (45). Cinna was consul every year until his own troops killed him at Ancona in 84 (Civil Wars 1.78); his regime also let debtors pay a quarter of what they owed (Velleius 2.23.2).

Sulla's war, the sack of Athens in 86 and the victories at Chaeronea and Orchomenus, is told in Greek module 7.3. The Roman point is that he fought it as an outlaw: Cinna's government declared him a public enemy and sent its own army east, whose commander Flaccus was murdered by his legate Fimbria; in 85 Fimbria's army went over to Sulla (Appian, Mithridatic Wars 51–60). Needing to go home, Sulla made peace with Mithridates at Dardanus in 85. The king gave up his conquests and kept Pontus; Plutarch says he paid 2,000 talents and surrendered seventy ships (Sulla 22), Memnon 3,000 talents and eighty ships (25.2). Asia was fined 20,000 talents (Sulla 25); its later debts belong to module 5.3.

08Civil war, the Colline Gate and the proscriptions (83–81 BC)

Sulla landed at Brundisium in 83. Metellus Pius, Crassus and the young Pompey, with three legions raised in Picenum on his own authority, joined him (Plutarch, Pompey 6; module 5.3 follows his rise). He beat one consul, Norbanus, and took over the army of the other, Scipio, without a fight (Civil Wars 1.84–85); on 6 July the Capitol burned (1.86; Julius Obsequens 57 for the day). In 82 he shut Marius' son, consul at under thirty, in Praeneste. On 1 November the Samnite Pontius Telesinus attacked Rome at the Colline Gate, saying the wolves would prey on Italy until their forest was cut down (Velleius 2.27.1–2). Sulla's left broke; Crassus won on the right (Plutarch, Crassus 6). Afterwards the prisoners were butchered while Sulla addressed the Senate in the nearby temple of Bellona, telling startled senators that some criminals were being corrected (Sulla 30): 6,000 in Plutarch, 8,000 who had surrendered, killed in the Villa Publica, in Livy's epitomator (Periochae 88). Praeneste fell, young Marius killed himself, and Sulla took the name Felix (Velleius 2.27.5).

The proscriptions made vengeance a procedure. Sulla posted eighty names, then 220 on each of the next two days (Sulla 31). Killers were paid two talents, property was auctioned, and the victims' sons and grandsons were barred from office (Sulla 31; Velleius 2.28). A law closed the lists on 1 June 81 (Cicero, Pro Roscio Amerino 128), but names were added for profit: Cicero's client Sextus Roscius had lost his father to the list after the deadline, so that Sulla's freedman Chrysogonus could buy an estate worth six million sesterces for two thousand (6). Crassus built his fortune at the auctions (Crassus 2).

Where sources disagree. Plutarch's lists total 520 (Sulla 31). Appian starts with about forty senators and 1,600 equestrians (Civil Wars 1.95) and sums the victims as ninety senators, fifteen ex-consuls and 2,600 equestrians, exiles included (1.103); Valerius Maximus gives 4,700 proscribed (9.2.1). François Hinard (1985) put the formal lists at a little over 520 and could name about seventy-five victims; the larger totals probably include the war dead.

09The dictator's settlement, retirement and death

With both consuls of 82 dead, the interrex Lucius Valerius Flaccus carried a law making Sulla dictator for writing laws and setting the state in order, without time limit (Civil Wars 1.98–99); Cicero called it the most unjust of laws (De lege agraria 3.5). The aim was to make the Senate the government again and to close the routes by which the Gracchi, Saturninus, Sulpicius and Sulla himself had gone round it.

  • Senate. About three hundred equestrians enrolled (1.100); twenty quaestors a year, entering it automatically (Tacitus, Annals 11.22).
  • Courts. Juries back to senators (Velleius 2.32.3); standing courts for extortion, treason, bribery, embezzlement, murder and poisoning, and forgery.
  • Magistrates. Eight praetors; quaestorship, praetorship and consulship in that order, with ten years between repeated tenures (1.100); governors forbidden to leave their province or make war without authorisation (Cicero, Against Piso 50).
  • Tribunes. No right to propose laws (Periochae 89), a veto only to protect individuals, no higher office afterwards: in Cicero's words, the power to help without the power to harm (On the Laws 3.22).
  • Priests and people. Colleges enlarged to fifteen and filled by co-optation (module 2.3); grain distributions ended (Sallust, Histories 1, speech of Lepidus).
  • Veterans. Twenty-three legions settled on confiscated land in Etruria and Campania, 120,000 men in Appian (1.100, 1.104); ten thousand slaves of the proscribed freed as Cornelii (1.100).

Sulla was consul again in 80 and laid down the dictatorship at a date modern scholars place between 81 and 79; Appian puts it in 79 and has him offer in the Forum to account for his acts (1.103–104). He retired to Puteoli and died in 78, aged sixty, two days after he stopped writing the twenty-second book of his memoirs (Plutarch, Sulla 37). His epitaph claimed that no friend had outdone him in kindness and no enemy in harm (38). Lepidus' attack on his system is where module 5.3 begins.

Where sources disagree. Appian marvels that a man with a tyrant's power gave it up unasked (Civil Wars 1.3, 1.104); Caesar said that Sulla did not know his ABC when he laid down the dictatorship (Suetonius, Julius 77). Arthur Keaveney (Sulla: The Last Republican, 1982) reads the retirement as the act of a conservative who wanted the Senate restored, not a monarchy. Either way the method outlived the system, whose main pieces were gone by 70 (module 5.3).

10Sulla's memoirs and Plutarch

Livy's books on the period survive only in the Periochae, Sallust's Histories begin in 78, and Appian gives the one continuous narrative (module 0.1). Sallust thought Sisenna's lost history the best account of Sulla, though not frank enough (Jugurtha 95.2). Much of the rest goes back to Marius' enemies: Rutilius Rufus, whose quarrel with Marius Plutarch notes (Marius 28); Catulus, in a book on his consulship (Cicero, Brutus 132); and Sulla, in twenty-two books of memoirs dedicated to Lucullus (Plutarch, Lucullus 1).

Plutarch read the memoirs and cites them often. Sulla presented himself as fortune's favourite: his boldest unplanned ventures went best and the gods spoke in dreams (Sulla 6); Plutarch adds, as hearsay, that a goddess from Cappadocia handed him a thunderbolt before the march on Rome (9); hence Felix, and Epaphroditus in Greek (34). He kept score too. At Chaeronea only fourteen of his men were missing, and two of them returned that evening (19); at Vercellae Marius' army lost its way in the dust while the Cimbri fell on Catulus' troops, with whom Sulla served (Marius 26–27). The grass crown at Nola comes from the same books. Plutarch notes that Sulla credits fortune more than ability and reports Vercellae as a claim, and for Marius' last days he names Posidonius and the historian Gaius Piso (Marius 45). Sallust's sketch is the counterweight: learned, eloquent, a man whose luck never outran his industry until the civil war, after which Sallust could not decide whether shame or disgust kept him silent (Jugurtha 95.3–4).

11Self-test

  1. What two reasons does Sallust give for writing about the Jugurthine War, and how does the Mamilian court fit his theme?
    Answer

    The war was great and savage, and it was the first time the arrogance of the nobility was opposed (Jugurtha 5.1). The lex Mamilia of 109 set up a court that condemned Opimius, Bestia and others for taking Jugurtha's money (40; Cicero, Brutus 128), the popular counter-attack his bribery plot leads up to.

  2. How did Marius obtain the Numidian command in 107, and why was the method dangerous?
    Answer

    Elected consul despite Metellus' contempt, he was given Numidia by a plebiscite overriding the Senate's prorogation of Metellus (Jugurtha 73). It made a command something the assembly could transfer, the precedent Sulpicius used against Sulla in 88.

  3. Which of the "Marian reforms" have ancient attestation, and what do modern critics doubt?
    Answer

    Volunteers from the capite censi in 107 (Jugurtha 86), soldiers carrying their own kit (Marius 13), the modified pilum (25) and the eagle as sole standard (Pliny 10.16); the cohort is not credited to him. Gabba saw the change as the end of a long slide; Rich denies the manpower shortage behind it, and Cadiou doubts that the army became proletarian at all.

  4. Why did Arausio happen, and what did it do to the constitution?
    Answer

    The proconsul Caepio refused to cooperate with the consul Mallius, a new man, and both armies were destroyed on 6 October 105. Rome responded by electing Marius consul five years running, 104–100, against the rules on repeated office.

  5. What did the Italian rebel state look like, and what does its coinage suggest?
    Answer

    Its capital was Corfinium, renamed Italia, with a senate of five hundred, two consuls and twelve praetors (Diodorus 37.2). Its denarii carry ITALIA or Oscan Víteliú, an oath scene and the bull goring the wolf, which Mouritsen reads as a claim to independence rather than a demand for Roman citizenship.

  6. Which laws enfranchised the Italians, and how was the value of the vote limited?
    Answer

    The lex Julia of 90 (loyal Latins and allies) and the lex Plautia Papiria of 89 (registration with a praetor within sixty days; Cicero, Pro Archia 7). New citizens were confined to a few tribes: ten new ones voting last in Appian (1.49), eight existing ones in Velleius (2.20.2).

  7. What made it possible for Sulla to march on Rome in 88, and how did his own side react?
    Answer

    Sulpicius' law transferred the Mithridatic command to Marius, and Sulla persuaded his six legions at Nola that the eastern plunder would go to others; they stoned Marius' officers. All his officers except one quaestor refused to follow him (Appian, Civil Wars 1.57).

  8. How did the Sullan proscriptions work as a procedure?
    Answer

    Lists of names were posted (Plutarch's three total 520), killers were paid two talents, property was auctioned and descendants barred from office (Sulla 31). A law closed the lists on 1 June 81, though Cicero's Pro Roscio Amerino shows names added later for profit.

  9. What did Sulla do to the tribunate and the courts?
    Answer

    Tribunes lost the right to propose laws, kept the veto only to protect individuals, and were barred from higher office: power to help without power to harm (Cicero, On the Laws 3.22). Juries went back to senators (Velleius 2.32.3), and standing courts were created for the main crimes.

  10. How do Sulla's memoirs shape what we know, and give one example of Plutarch using them.
    Answer

    Plutarch drew on them, with the hostile accounts of Rutilius and Catulus, for much of Marius' and Sulla's careers, so the record leans to Marius' enemies. Examples: Sulla's claim that only fourteen men were missing at Chaeronea (Sulla 19), or that Marius lost his way in the dust at Vercellae (Marius 26).

12Carry forward

  1. Between 107 and 82 the decisive political fact became who held a command and whether his soldiers would follow him: the plebiscite of 107, the transfer of 88 and Sulla's march turned armies into political actors, and every later dynast started from that precedent.
  2. The Social War made Italy Roman in law at the cost of a war against Rome, and the fight over how the new citizens would vote drove the violence of 88–87; Sulla's colonies and proscriptions then remade the landowning class of Italy.
  3. Sulla's settlement, the most thorough attempt to restore senatorial government, was imposed by the first march on Rome and secured by proscription; the method outlived the system, and the record of it comes largely from the memoirs of the men who won.

The prompt this answers

Give me an in-depth report on Marius, the Social War and Sulla, 112 to 78 BC. Cover: the Jugurthine War as Sallust tells it and his theme of senatorial corruption; Marius the new man, his seven consulships and the 'Marian reforms' with the modern doubts about them; the Cimbri and Teutones, Arausio, Aquae Sextiae and Vercellae; Saturninus and 100 BC; the Social War of 91–88 BC, Livius Drusus, the Italian state with its capital at Corfinium and its coins, and the citizenship grants; Mithridates and the massacre of Romans in Asia; Sulla's march on Rome in 88 BC, the first by a Roman army, Marius' return and the terror of 87, Cinna; Sulla's war in the East (point back to Greek module 7.3 for the sack of Athens), his return, the Colline Gate, the proscriptions, the dictatorship and its reforms, his retirement and death; and Sulla's lost memoirs and how Plutarch used them. Format: about 3000 words, headed sections, name sources, flag disagreements, end with a 10-question self-test with answers and the three ideas I must carry forward.
