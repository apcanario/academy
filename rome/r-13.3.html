01The other choices

A Roman who wanted philosophy in the first century BC had more than one door to knock on. Module 5.6 names the Latin Epicureans, Lucretius and Amafinius, Philodemus under Piso's patronage and Cassius as an Epicurean, each in a sentence; deep dive module 13.1 shows how Greek teachers got into Roman houses; Greek module 7.2 outlines the Hellenistic systems, and Greek modules 12.5, 12.6 and 12.8 tell the Cynics, the Garden and the sceptical Academy on their own ground. This module follows the schools other than the Stoa after they reached Rome, from Cicero's generation to Marcus Aurelius, and asks why the governing class, given the choice, settled on the Stoa.

The evidence is lopsided. Cicero's dialogues stage the schools against each other with Roman nobles as speakers, so that most of what is known of Republican Epicureans, Academics and Peripatetics comes through one Academic's pen. Under the empire the witnesses are Seneca, a Stoic who borrowed freely from rivals, and Lucian, a satirist who mocked everyone. The charred library at Herculaneum is the one body of evidence written by a rival school for its own readers.

02Philodemus, Piso and the villa

Philodemus of Gadara, in the Decapolis, came to Italy as a trained Epicurean and attached himself to Lucius Calpurnius Piso Caesoninus, consul in 58 and Caesar's father-in-law. The best-known portrait is hostile. Attacking Piso in 55, Cicero describes an unnamed Greek, learned in philosophy and in other studies, who took up with Piso when he was young, could not refuse him, and wrote elegant verses on his patron's lusts (Against Piso 68–72). Asconius, commenting on the speech a century later, names him: Cicero means Philodemus, the most distinguished Epicurean of the day, whose poems were lascivious (on Against Piso 68). In the same speech Cicero hails Piso as an Epicurean led out of the pigsty, not the school (37). In his own voice elsewhere he calls Siro and Philodemus excellent men and most learned (On Ends 2.119): the target was the patron.

Philodemus' own work tells another story. One of his epigrams invites "dearest Piso" to a modest house for the annual feast of the twentieth, the day Epicureans kept for their founder (Palatine Anthology 11.44; Greek module 12.6 gives the will that founded it). A treatise On the Good King according to Homer was dedicated to a Piso, probably a young relative of the consul, reading the poet as a manual for rulers. At Naples he and Siro taught a circle of young poets: books of his On Vices are addressed to Virgil, Varius, Plotius Tucca and Quintilius Varus, whose names survive in the papyri. A poem among Virgil's juvenilia announces a voyage to the blessed harbours of great Siro's learned sayings (Catalepton 5), though its authenticity is doubted.

The papyri come from a villa outside Herculaneum buried by Vesuvius in 79, where Bourbon excavators tunnelling underground found them from 1752. They yielded, on a common estimate, about 800 carbonised rolls, mostly Greek, many of them Philodemus' treatises on music, poems, rhetoric, piety, death, anger and frank speech, besides books of Epicurus' On Nature (Greek module 12.6). It is the only library to survive from antiquity in place, and it is an Epicurean's working library. Imaging and "virtual unwrapping" are now reading rolls that cannot be opened.

Where sources disagree. Domenico Comparetti and Giulio De Petra (La villa ercolanese dei Pisoni, 1883) gave the villa to Piso, and the identification is still the working assumption: Philodemus' library in his patron's house. No inscription names the owner. In Buried by Vesuvius (ed. Kenneth Lapatin, 2019) Valerio Papaccio dates the marble floors to 40–20 BC and Domenico Esposito the paintings to 40–25; if the villa was built then, Philodemus cannot have taught in it in the 50s, and the books came later, perhaps to Piso's son.

03Epicureans in public life: Cassius, Atticus and the pig

The Garden told the wise man to stay out of politics (Greek module 12.6), unless, Seneca adds, something intervened (On Leisure 3.2). Roman Epicureans did not all listen. Cassius, the tyrannicide, became one in the 40s. In January 45 Cicero teased him about the "spectres" of the Latin Epicurean Catius (Letters to Friends 15.16.1), and Cassius replied in earnest: Catius and Amafinius were bad translators, and pleasure and tranquillity are won by virtue and justice, since, as Epicurus says, one cannot live pleasantly without living honourably and justly (15.19.2–3). Fourteen months later he helped kill Caesar. Plutarch shows the doctrine at work twice: when Brutus sounded out his friends, the Epicurean Statilius answered that a wise man should not run into danger and turmoil for the sake of fools (Brutus 12); and when Brutus saw his ghost before Philippi, Cassius explained it away by the Epicurean theory of images (Brutus 37).

Atticus is the other pole. Cicero's letters treat him as an Epicurean, and in a dialogue Cicero makes him concede that the gods govern nature, since the birdsong and the noise of the river, he says, will keep his fellow students from overhearing (On the Laws 1.21). In On Ends he says he could not forget Epicurus if he wished, since his friends kept his likeness in pictures and even on cups and rings (5.3). Nepos' life (module 5.6) shows the practical form: refuse office, help every side, survive. Lucius Manlius Torquatus, who expounds the ethics in On Ends 1, and Gaius Velleius, the Epicurean of On the Nature of the Gods 1, show that the school had spokesmen in the Senate; Cicero teased the lawyer Trebatius on his conversion (Letters to Friends 7.12).

The poets kept the label alive as a joke. Horace quotes Lucretius, "for I have learned that the gods lead a carefree life" (Satires 1.5.101), and ends a verse letter to Albius, usually taken to be Tibullus, by calling himself, fat and sleek, a pig from Epicurus' herd, Epicuri de grege porcum (Epistles 1.4.16). The pig was the hostile tradition's emblem and Cicero's insult to Piso; Horace wore it as a badge.

Where sources disagree. Arnaldo Momigliano, in a review in the Journal of Roman Studies for 1941, gathered the evidence for Epicureans in politics, on both sides of the civil war, and read the tyrannicide as Epicureanism turned political. David Sedley ("The Ethics of Brutus and Cassius", Journal of Roman Studies, 1997) answers that Cassius' withdrawal from the war in 48 fits Epicurean quietism, so that his school did not drive him to the conspiracy of 44, though Sedley shows how Cassius could justify it in Epicurean terms. Statilius' refusal in Plutarch shows that an Epicurean could reason either way.

Under the empire the school persisted, less visible in the Senate. In 121 Trajan's widow Plotina asked Hadrian to let Popillius Theotimus, head of the Garden at Athens, name a successor who was not a Roman citizen and make his will in Greek; Hadrian granted it (IG II² 1099), and Plotina wrote to the "friends" that they had what they wanted. Lucian dedicated his Alexander to an Epicurean Celsus and makes the false prophet's two enemies the Christians and the Epicureans; Alexander burned a copy of Epicurus' Principal Doctrines in the market (Alexander 25, 38, 47). Seneca's long borrowing from Epicurus is deep dive module 13.5's.

04Cicero the sceptic

Philo of Larissa, head of the Academy, fled to Rome in 88 (Cicero, Brutus 306), and the young Cicero heard him (Greek module 12.8 has his doctrine; module 13.1 the student years). Cicero called himself a follower of the New Academy to the end, and said what it gave him. Because the Academic suspends judgement, he is freer: his power of judging is intact, and he is bound to no master's system (Academica 2.8). Those who ask what he himself thinks are more curious than they need be; in philosophy weight of argument, not authority, should count (On the Nature of the Gods 1.10). He held that some things are persuasive and others not, and that this is enough to act on (On Duties 2.7–8).

This scepticism let him do three things. It let him argue both sides, the method he took from the Academy and Aristotle (Tusculan Disputations 2.9), which suited an orator trained to argue in utramque partem. It let him write dialogues in which Epicurean, Stoic and Antiochean each state their case at length and the reader judges: Torquatus is answered in On Ends 2, Velleius and Balbus by the Academic Cotta, though Cicero himself found the Stoic case nearer the likeness of truth (On the Nature of the Gods 3.95). And it let him borrow the most persuasive doctrine for each task without contradiction: Stoic ethics in On Duties, where his Academy, he says, gives him licence to defend whatever seems most probable (3.20), and Stoic natural law in On the Laws, where he begs the New Academy of Arcesilaus and Carneades, the disturber of all these questions, to keep silent (1.39). His arguments on fate as an Academic are in deep dive module 13.2.

Where sources disagree. John Glucker ("Cicero's Philosophical Affiliations", in The Question of "Eclecticism", 1988) found three phases: Philo's pupil in youth, a follower of Antiochus through the 50s, when On the Republic and On the Laws speak with dogmatic confidence, and a return to the New Academy about 45. Woldemar Görler ("Silencing the Troublemaker: De legibus 1.39 and the Continuity of Cicero's Scepticism", in Cicero the Philosopher, ed. J. G. F. Powell, 1995) reads the plea to the Academy to be silent as a sceptic's tactical truce, not a conversion, and keeps Cicero an Academic throughout.

05Antiochus and the Old Academy: Lucullus, Varro, Brutus

Antiochus of Ascalon broke with Philo and claimed to restore the Old Academy of Plato, Xenocrates, Polemo and Aristotle, a philosophy Zeno had only renamed (Greek module 12.8). His Roman career ran through a patron. He was with Lucullus at Alexandria in the Mithridatic war when Philo's Roman Books arrived and provoked his reply (Academica 2.11–12). Plutarch says Lucullus opened his library to all Greeks, admired every school, but favoured the Old Academy, whose champion was then Antiochus (Lucullus 42). Cicero heard Antiochus at Athens for six months in 79 (Brutus 315; module 13.1).

The school suited Romans who wanted doctrine without the Stoa's paradoxes: virtue sufficed for a happy life but external goods made it happiest, a graded view that sat easily with a career. Its Roman adherents were men of weight. Cicero cast Lucullus as Antiochus' spokesman in the first version of the Academica and, when Catulus and Lucullus seemed to him too little versed in such subtleties, rewrote it with Varro, a declared follower, in their place (Letters to Atticus 13.12–19). Augustine preserves Varro's arithmetic in his lost On Philosophy: 288 possible schools, reduced to the one he chose, the Old Academy (City of God 19.1–3). Marcus Pupius Piso expounds Antiochus' ethics in On Ends 5. Brutus admired Antiochus' school and made his brother Aristus a friend and companion (Plutarch, Brutus 2); his lost On Virtue, which Seneca quotes on Marcellus in exile at Mytilene (Consolation to Helvia 9.4–8), was sent to Cicero (On Ends 1.8). After Antiochus the school had no head of his stature, and its ethics passed into Middle Platonism (Greek module 12.8).

Where sources disagree. Brutus has often been called a Stoic, and his uncle Cato's example and his suicide at Philippi seemed to confirm it; John Moles (A Commentary on Plutarch's Brutus, 2017) finds Stoic characteristics in Brutus that Plutarch plays down. Sedley (1997) argues from Cicero and Plutarch that he was an Antiochean, whose view of happiness admitted degrees, as no Stoic's could, and that his politics and death show no conversion. Plutarch's own label, admirer of the Old Academy, supports Sedley.

06The Peripatetics and Cratippus

Aristotle's school was the least distinct at Rome, because Antiochus had claimed its ethics for his Old Academy. It had its house philosophers, Staseas with Pupius Piso and Alexander with Crassus (module 13.1), and one teacher of note. Cratippus of Pergamum taught at Mytilene, where Pompey, fleeing after Pharsalus, talked with him about providence (Plutarch, Pompey 75), and then at Athens. Cicero obtained Roman citizenship for him from Caesar and persuaded the Areopagus to ask him to stay and teach the young (Plutarch, Cicero 24). Cicero's son Marcus studied with him: On Duties, written for the young man in 44, opens by noting that he has heard Cratippus for a year at Athens (1.1) and commends him again (3.5–6). Marcus himself wrote to Tiro that he was with Cratippus constantly, as a son rather than a pupil (Letters to Friends 16.21.3). In 44 Brutus heard Cratippus at Athens and drew the young Romans there, Marcus among them, into his army (module 13.1).

The texts were the school's Roman story. Sulla, taking Athens in 86, seized the library of Apellicon of Teos, which held Aristotle's and Theophrastus' books, and shipped it to Rome; the grammarian Tyrannion worked on it, and Andronicus of Rhodes published the treatises and drew up their lists (Plutarch, Sulla 26; Strabo 13.1.54). Strabo adds that the Peripatetics after Theophrastus, lacking the books, could only declaim generalities. About 200 Alexander of Aphrodisias dedicated his On Fate to Septimius Severus and Caracalla, thanking them for his post as a teacher of Aristotle's philosophy.

Where sources disagree. Strabo and Plutarch make the corpus lost in a cellar at Scepsis until Apellicon and Sulla, and Andronicus its first editor. Jonathan Barnes ("Roman Aristotle", in Philosophia Togata II, 1997) doubts that the treatises were unknown before him and that his edition shaped Cicero's Aristotle, whom Cicero praises for a golden stream of speech (Academica 2.119) that suits the published dialogues better than the treatises.

07Nigidius, the Sextii and the Pythagoreans

Pythagoras had an old Roman legend, as Numa's teacher, which Livy rejects on chronology (1.18.2) and module 1.5 weighs. Revived Pythagoreanism had a Roman champion. Publius Nigidius Figulus, praetor in 58 and a Pompeian, was in Cicero's words the man who revived the Pythagoreans' teaching after it had almost died out (Timaeus 1). His books on the gods, divination, the sphere and grammar survive in fragments; Gellius ranks his learning next to Varro's (4.9.1); Suetonius says he read Octavian's greatness in the hour of his birth (Augustus 94.5), and Lucan gives him a prophecy of civil war (1.639–672). Cicero wrote to console him in exile (Letters to Friends 4.13), and Jerome's Chronicle records his death there in 45. The same chronicle, after Eusebius, records the expulsion from Rome in 28 of Anaxilaus of Larissa, a Pythagorean charged with magic. Pythagoreanism at Rome carried a scent of the occult: Cicero sneered at Vatinius for calling himself a Pythagorean while he practised unheard-of rites (Against Vatinius 14).

The one school founded by a Roman in Rome was Pythagorean in diet and Stoic in tone. Quintus Sextius refused the broad stripe of senatorial rank when Caesar offered it (Seneca, Letters 98.13) and founded a sect continued by his son. Seneca says Sextius philosophised in Greek words with Roman morals, Graecis verbis, Romanis moribus (Letters 59.7), and that a book of his read at dinner had a vigour Greek philosophy lacked, though Sextius denied he was a Stoic (64.2–3). Sextius forbade meat; Seneca's teacher Sotion gave Pythagoras' reasons and Sextius' own, and the year the young Seneca kept off meat is module 13.5's; it ended at a time when abstaining from certain meats was taken as a sign of foreign cults, though his father, Seneca says, did not fear slander but hated philosophy (108.17–22). Sextius' nightly examination of the soul, which Seneca adopted, is deep dive module 13.5's (On Anger 3.36). By Seneca's old age the sect was gone: the school of the Sextii, new and of Roman vigour, began with great impetus and was extinct (Natural Questions 7.32.2).

Where sources disagree. Seneca treats Sextius as a near-Stoic who denied the name (Letters 64.2). Claudianus Mamertus, a Christian writer of the fifth century, says the Sextii taught that the soul is incorporeal and without place (On the State of the Soul 2.8), a Platonist and Pythagorean doctrine no Stoic could hold. Whether the school was a Roman Stoicism or a Roman Pythagoreanism depends on which witness one trusts, and if, as many think, the son is the Sextius Niger whose pharmacology Pliny used, he adds a third face.

Under the empire the Neopythagoreans were Greeks of the East. Apollonius of Tyana became, through Philostratus' Life, a wandering sage and wonder-worker; Moderatus of Gades and Nicomachus of Gerasa wrote on number; Numenius belongs to Greek module 12.8. None founded a Roman school.

08The Cynics under the empire

Greek module 12.5 gives the Cynic way of life; the imperial Cynics show what it became at Rome. Demetrius is known chiefly through his friend Seneca, who calls him a man of consummate wisdom (On Benefits 7.8) and says that he takes Demetrius, the best of men, about with him, leaves the men in purple and talks with that half-naked one (Letters 62.3). Caligula offered him two hundred thousand sesterces; Demetrius laughed it off, saying that to tempt him the emperor would have had to offer his whole empire (On Benefits 7.11). Seneca quotes his maxims: it is better to hold a few precepts ready for use than to learn many (7.1.3), and nothing is unhappier than a man who has never met adversity (On Providence 3.3). Philostratus says Nero's minister Tigellinus banished him for denouncing Nero's baths (Life of Apollonius 4.42). He was with Thrasea Paetus at his death in 66 (Tacitus, Annals 16.34), which deep dive module 13.4 tells with the expulsions of philosophers that followed. Under Vespasian, condemned and exiled, he met the emperor on the road, would not rise and barked at him, and Vespasian thought it enough to call him a dog (Suetonius, Vespasian 13).

Where sources disagree. Seneca's Demetrius is a sage. Tacitus' is less clean: in 70, when Musonius Rufus prosecuted the Stoic informer Egnatius Celer, who had betrayed his patron Barea Soranus, Demetrius defended Celer, more for show, Tacitus says, than with honour (Histories 4.40). Juvenal saw little to choose between the schools: Stoics differ from Cynics by a tunic (13.121–122).

Peregrinus, surnamed Proteus, is the subject of Lucian's On the Death of Peregrinus, a hostile eyewitness account; Greek module 7.4 names the work. Born at Parium on the Hellespont, he left home under suspicion of strangling his father (10); in Palestine he joined the Christians, rose to lead them and was jailed, and they cared for him in prison until a governor of Syria let him go (11–14). Back at Parium he gave his estate to the city (15), trained in Egypt under the Cynic Agathobulus (17), and was expelled from Rome by the city prefect for abusing the emperor (18). At Olympia he reviled Herodes Atticus (Greek module 7.4) and was nearly stoned (19–20). At the games of 161 he announced that he would burn himself at the next, to teach men to despise death (20). At the games of 165, at night as the moon rose, he leapt into a pyre at Harpina, twenty stades from Olympia, calling on the spirits of his mother and father (35–36). Lucian, who was there, laughed when he called on his father, recalling the murder (37), then invented a vulture rising from the flames and later heard his fiction told back to him by a grey-haired witness (39–40). His verdict is love of glory.

Where sources disagree. Gellius, a student at Athens, often visited Peregrinus in his hut outside the city and calls him a man of dignity and constancy, who taught that the wise man would not do wrong even if gods and men would never know (12.11). Ammianus names him with honour among those who chose death by fire (29.1.39), and Athenagoras reports that a statue of him at Parium gave oracles (Embassy 26). Lucian alone makes him a charlatan, and Lucian mocked every philosopher he wrote about.

09Why the Stoa won, and the chairs of 176

Seneca, surveying the schools in old age, found most of them dead: the Old and New Academy without a head, no one to hand on Pyrrho's teaching, the Pythagorean school without a teacher, the Sextii extinct (Natural Questions 7.32.2). He wrote as a Stoic, and the Epicureans and Peripatetics he did not list were alive. But his diagnosis fits the senatorial class, whose philosophy by the first century AD was overwhelmingly Stoic. The rivals each failed it at one point.

  • The Epicureans counselled abstention and pleasure. A senator could be one, as Piso and Cassius were, but the doctrine gave no reason to hold office and an enemy a word to throw: Cicero's pig.
  • The New Academy gave method, not a rule of life. It served an orator and a writer of dialogues; it gave no one a reason to die for anything.
  • The Old Academy and the Peripatetics gave doctrine close to the Stoa's ethics, by Antiochus' own account, without its single standard. After Antiochus they had no teacher at Rome to match the Stoics.
  • The Pythagoreans carried diet, number and magic, all suspect to Roman custom, and the Roman version, the Sextii, lasted two generations.
  • The Cynics rejected the social order a senator lived by.

The Stoa offered what the others did not: a duty for every office, in Cicero's Latin of officium and the personae (module 13.2); a rule that the wise man takes part in public life unless something prevents (Seneca, On Leisure 3.2); and, from Cato on, an example of how to die (module 13.4). Under emperors who punished dissent it supplied both a reason to serve and a dignified exit, which is why it became the philosophy of senators and, at last, of an emperor.

Yet the schools did not die, and the endowment of 176, recorded in Greek module 7.4, shows how they stood. Visiting Athens after Avidius Cassius' revolt, Marcus gave teachers to every branch of learning and an annual salary (Dio 72(71).31.3). Philostratus says Marcus chose the rhetor Theodotus himself and left the choice of the Platonists, Stoics, Peripatetics and Epicureans to Herodes Atticus (Lives of the Sophists 566). Lucian's Eunuch gives the salary, ten thousand drachmas a year, the same for Stoics, Platonists, Epicureans and Peripatetics, and a contest for a Peripatetic vacancy judged by the city's oldest and most eminent men (3). Three points follow. The emperor who wrote Stoic notes to himself paid Epicureans on equal terms: by the Antonines the four schools were institutions of Greek culture, like rhetoric, not sects competing for the Senate. The Academy now meant Plato taught as doctrine, a Platonist chair, not Cicero's scepticism. And there was no chair for Cynics or Pythagoreans, the schools that were a way of life without a curriculum.

10Self-test

  1. How do Cicero and Asconius between them identify Piso's philosopher, and why should Cicero's portrait be read with care?
    Answer

    Cicero describes an unnamed Greek companion who wrote verses on Piso's lusts (Against Piso 68–72); Asconius names him as Philodemus. The speech is invective against Piso, and elsewhere Cicero calls Siro and Philodemus excellent and most learned men (On Ends 2.119).

  2. Why is the Villa of the Papyri important, and what makes its link to Piso uncertain?
    Answer

    It is the one ancient library found in place, about 800 rolls, largely Philodemus' Epicurean treatises with books of Epicurus' On Nature. No inscription names the owner, and dating of the floors and paintings to about 40–20 BC would put its building after Philodemus' years with Piso in the 50s.

  3. How did Cassius reconcile Epicureanism with public virtue in his letter of 45?
    Answer

    He called Catius and Amafinius bad translators and quoted Epicurus that one cannot live pleasantly without living honourably and justly, so that pleasure and tranquillity are reached through virtue and justice (Letters to Friends 15.19.2–3).

  4. What does Horace's "pig from Epicurus' herd" show about how the school was seen?
    Answer

    The pig was the hostile emblem, the insult Cicero threw at Piso. Horace applies it to himself in a verse letter to Albius, usually taken to be Tibullus (Epistles 1.4.16), turning the slur into an ironic badge of a contented life.

  5. What three things did Academic scepticism allow Cicero to do?
    Answer

    Argue both sides of a question (Tusculan Disputations 2.9); write dialogues in which each school states its case and the reader judges; and adopt the most persuasive doctrine for each task, Stoic ethics in On Duties (3.20) and Stoic natural law in On the Laws, where he asks the Academy to keep silent (1.39).

  6. On what passage do Glucker and Görler disagree, and what does each make of it?
    Answer

    On the Laws 1.39, the plea to the New Academy to be silent. Glucker reads it as evidence that Cicero followed Antiochus in the 50s before returning to the New Academy about 45; Görler as a sceptic's tactical truce within a consistent Academic career.

  7. Why did Antiochus' Old Academy appeal to Romans such as Lucullus, Varro and Brutus?
    Answer

    It offered firm doctrine without the Stoic paradoxes: virtue sufficed for happiness, but external goods made it fuller, a graded view that suited a career. It came with a patron, Lucullus, and an author, Varro, who chose it from 288 possible schools (Augustine, City of God 19.1–3).

  8. What does Seneca's account of the Sextii show about Roman attempts at a native philosophy?
    Answer

    Quintus Sextius refused senatorial rank, taught in Greek words with Roman morals (Letters 59.7) and forbade meat. The sect began with great impetus and was extinct within Seneca's lifetime (Natural Questions 7.32.2), and its vegetarianism already looked like a suspect foreign rite under Tiberius (Letters 108.22).

  9. How do Lucian and Gellius differ on Peregrinus, and why does the difference matter?
    Answer

    Lucian makes his burning at Olympia in 165 a stunt driven by love of glory, and admits inventing the vulture himself. Gellius, who heard him at Athens, calls him a man of dignity and constancy (12.11). The only full narrative is a satirist's, so the Cynic's motives cannot be read from it straight.

  10. What do the chairs of 176 show about the standing of the schools under the Antonines?
    Answer

    Marcus paid Platonists, Stoics, Peripatetics and Epicureans alike, ten thousand drachmas a year by Lucian's figure (Eunuch 3), so the four had become civic institutions of Greek learning rather than rivals for the Senate's allegiance. The Academy now meant Platonism, and Cynics and Pythagoreans had no chair.

11Carry forward

  1. Romans chose among Greek schools as they chose patrons and careers, and a school's success at Rome depended less on its arguments than on what it let a senator do: hold office, argue a case, refuse a tyrant or die well.
  2. Most of what is known of the rival schools comes through hostile or partial reporters: Cicero the Academic on Epicureans, Seneca the Stoic on the Sextii and Demetrius, Lucian the satirist on Peregrinus. Read each school through its reporter, and prefer its own texts, the Herculaneum papyri or an inscription, where they survive.
  3. By the Antonines the schools had settled into a curriculum, four salaried chairs at Athens, while the Stoa held the inner life of the governing class; the philosophies that were a way of life rather than a syllabus, Cynic and Pythagorean, lived on as individual sages outside the institutions.
