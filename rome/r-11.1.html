The ladder of office after 180

By 200 BC the magistracies were fixed in rank; module 3.1 gives their origins. All were annual, collegiate and unpaid. Consuls and praetors held imperium, the power to command armies and to judge; the others held only potestas. What the Republic lacked was a statutory order. Titus Quinctius Flamininus stood for the consulship of 198 before he was thirty, without the aedileship or praetorship, and two tribunes objected in vain (Plutarch, Flamininus 2).

From 197 six praetors were elected each year (Livy 32.27.6), but still only two consuls, so the contest sharpened at the top. In 180 the tribune Lucius Villius carried the first law fixing the ages at which each office could be sought and held, and his family took the name Annalis from it (Livy 40.44.1); the same chapter records that four praetors were elected that year under a Baebian law ordering four and six in alternate years (40.44.2), but six were elected every year after. Livy gives no figures. They are inferred from the ages of known office-holders, the method of A. E. Astin (The Lex Annalis before Sulla, 1958): most reconstructions give an interval of two years between curule offices and minimum ages of about 39 for the praetorship and 42 for the consulship. Those ages are best attested for Sulla's law of 81, so whether Sulla repeated Villius' rules or raised them is uncertain. Ten campaigns were already required before any office (Polybius 6.19.4; module 9.2 has the levy). Cicero later said the ancestors had no such laws, which ambition brought in long afterwards to set steps of candidacy among equals (Philippics 5.47). The law regulated rivalry inside the nobility; it did not restrain the people.

The shape was a pyramid: of six praetors a year, at most two could reach the consulship. The extension of commands beyond the year, which turned the pyramid into a base for generals, is deep dive module 11.2.

Voting by centuries and by tribes

Consuls, praetors and censors were elected by the centuriate assembly on the Campus Martius; its 193 centuries and their Servian legend are module 1.5. At some date after 241, when the thirty-fifth tribe was created, it was reformed: Livy notes that the centuries of his day, doubled with the tribes into juniors and seniors, no longer matched Servius' figures (1.43.12). The first class now had seventy centuries, a senior and a junior for each tribe. With the eighteen equestrian centuries it held 88 of 193, nine short of the 97 that made a majority.

The order was fixed. A century of the first class chosen by lot, the praerogativa, voted first and its result was announced. Romans treated it as an omen (Cicero, On Divination 1.103; Pro Murena 38), and Cicero claims that no candidate who carried it had failed to become consul at that election or the next (Pro Plancio 49). Then came the rest of the first class, then the six old equestrian centuries, then the second class; Cicero describes the sequence for an election of 44 (Philippics 2.82). Each century voted for as many candidates as there were places; a candidate was declared elected on reaching 97 centuries, and voting stopped once every place was filled. Cicero praised the design for ensuring that the greatest number did not have the greatest power (On the Republic 2.39).

The tribal assembly, under a consul or praetor, elected quaestors, curule aediles and some military tribunes; the plebs alone, under a tribune, elected tribunes and plebeian aediles. The thirty-five tribes counted equally, and thirty-one were rural. A rural tribe's vote was cast by whichever members came to Rome, chiefly men who could afford the journey, while freedmen were kept to the four urban tribes. In legislation the tribes voted in turn until eighteen made a majority, as at Octavius' deposition in 133 (module 5.1); in elections they probably voted together, results being read out in an order drawn by lot (Lily Ross Taylor, Roman Voting Assemblies, 1966). Voting was oral until the ballot laws, the Gabinian of 139 for elections and the Cassian of 137 for most trials (Cicero, On the Laws 3.35–37).

Where sources disagree. Livy says the vote hardly ever descended to the lowest classes (1.43.11), and Cicero treats the weighting as decisive (On the Republic 2.39). Alexander Yakobson ("Petitio et largitio", 1992; Elections and Electioneering in Rome, 1999) argued that in contested elections the first class split among several candidates for several places, so the count often ran into the middle classes, which is why candidates courted humbler voters.

The Senate: authority without legal power

The Senate of about three hundred was enrolled by the censors, in practice from former magistrates (module 3.1). It met only when a consul, praetor or tribune summoned it; it could not legislate, elect or judge, and its decrees bound no one in law. A senatus consultum was advice, typically that the consuls act if it seemed good to them. The presiding magistrate asked opinions in order of rank, the leader of the Senate first, then the former consuls and on down; Varro wrote a handbook of this procedure for Pompey (Gellius 14.7). A tribune's veto reduced a decree to an auctoritas, recorded but without force (Caelius in Cicero, Letters to Friends 8.8).

The leader, princeps senatus, was the man the censors placed first on the roll. Custom gave the place to the earliest surviving ex-censor. In 209 the censor Sempronius Tuditanus, who had drawn the lot to choose, set custom aside for Fabius Maximus, the first man of the state, he said, as even Hannibal would admit (Livy 27.11.9–12). Scipio Africanus followed; Marcus Aemilius Lepidus was chosen by six successive pairs of censors (Periochae 48); Appius Claudius Pulcher held the place in 133 (module 5.1). The leader had only the first word, but in a house that decided by moving to one side or the other, the first word framed the question.

Practice gave the Senate what law did not. It assigned provinces and armies, extended commands, controlled the treasury, received embassies and handled prodigies; Polybius' account of its powers is module 4.5. Its decree on the Bacchanalia in 186 reached into allied communities across Italy (module 2.5). Mommsen called its auctoritas more than advice and less than command, advice one could not ignore (Römisches Staatsrecht, 1871–88). Magistrates complied because they held office for a year and sat in the Senate for life, because it voted the money, supplies, extensions and honours they needed, and because their peers' judgement was the tribunal that counted.

Nobles, new men and the pursuit of glory

The nobiles were families with a consul among their ancestors (Gelzer's definition; module 3.1), a matter of repute, not law. A man with no senatorial forebears who entered the Senate was a novus homo, a new man; one who reached the consulship was rare. Cato, consul in 195, called himself new to office but old in ancestral deeds of courage (Plutarch, Cato Maior 1). Sallust, writing in the 40s, has the nobility pass the consulship from hand to hand, a new man however distinguished being thought unworthy and almost polluted (Jugurtha 63.6–7); Marius' reply is in module 5.2.

The counts are less closed than the rhetoric. Keith Hopkins and Graham Burton (Death and Renewal, 1983) found that over the last two centuries of the Republic only about 40 per cent of consuls had consular fathers and only a third had consular sons. The nobility dominated the top office without reproducing itself in it: families dropped out as others rose, and each generation competed afresh.

The currency was glory (gloria, laus), office (honos) and standing (dignitas), won in the state's service and displayed before the people. In 221 Quintus Metellus said in his father's eulogy that his father had achieved the ten greatest goods that wise men seek: to be foremost warrior, best orator and bravest general, to win great victories under his own auspices, hold the highest office, show the greatest wisdom, be the leading senator, gain wealth honourably, leave many children and be the most famous man in the state (Pliny, Natural History 7.139–140). The epitaph of Gnaeus Cornelius Scipio Hispanus, praetor in 139, says he added to his family's virtues, strove to match his father's deeds and kept his ancestors' praise, and ends stirpem nobilitavit honor, "office ennobled my stock" (CIL I² 15). The funeral with masks and eulogies is modules 2.4 and 4.5. The ethic also demanded restraint. Annual office, colleagues, ages and intervals spread the honours, and sumptuary laws on banquets, the Orchian of 182 and the Fannian of 161, limited display (Macrobius, Saturnalia 3.17). The nobility rationed glory so that no member could monopolise it.

Patrons and clients

Tradition made patronage as old as the city: Dionysius has Romulus give the plebeians to patricians as clients (2.9–10; module 3.1), and the Twelve Tables cursed a patron who cheated his client. By the second century the bond took many forms. Freedmen owed duties to their former owners by law; conquered cities and provinces had patrons in the Senate; humbler men sought a great man's help in court and attended his morning greeting (salutatio). Plutarch says Gaius Herennius refused to testify against Marius in 116 because custom excused patrons from witnessing against clients (Marius 5). The canvassing handbook ascribed to Quintus Cicero, of 64 BC, tells the candidate to mobilise friends, clients, freedmen and tribes.

The older modern model built politics on these ties. Matthias Gelzer (Die Nobilität der römischen Republik, 1912) explained noble power by networks of personal obligation; Friedrich Münzer (Römische Adelsparteien und Adelsfamilien, 1920) and H. H. Scullard (Roman Politics 220–150 BC, 1951) turned them into family alliances that delivered blocs of votes. Three doubts weigh against it. Clients had several patrons, and patrons competed for them; after 139 a written ballot could not be checked; and great men with great followings lost elections often enough for Valerius Maximus to give such defeats a chapter (7.5).

Where sources disagree. Gelzer and Scullard read clientship as the machinery of the vote. P. A. Brunt ("Clientela", in The Fall of the Roman Republic and Related Essays, 1988) found little evidence that clients voted to order, the word covering loose and overlapping attachments; Millar and Henrik Mouritsen (Plebs and Politics in the Late Roman Republic, 2001) both rejected the clientela model, from opposite directions, Millar for a sovereign people, Mouritsen for an electorate too small and propertied to need herding.

Canvassing, bribery and the laws against it

Ambitus, "going round", meant canvassing before it meant a crime. The candidate (candidatus) whitened his toga, toured the Forum with escorts, greeted voters by name with a slave name-caller (nomenclator) at his ear and courted the tribes. Livy, writing three to four centuries later, records a law of 432 against whitening clothes for candidacy (4.25.13) and the Poetelian law of 358 against new men touring markets and country gatherings (7.15.12–13). Gifts were the danger. In 181 the consuls, on the Senate's authority, carried a law on bribery (Livy 40.19.11), and another followed in 159 (Periochae 47). The ballot cut both ways: it freed the voter from his patron's eye and from the briber's. Later laws, the Calpurnian of 67 and Cicero's own of 63, mark an escalation that the Pro Murena shows at its height.

Where sources disagree. Polybius, writing about 150, says that at Rome bribery for office carried the death penalty, unlike Carthage (6.56.4); yet laws of 181 (Livy 40.19.11) and 159 (Periochae 47) show the problem already real, and no execution for it is recorded. Yakobson (1999) reads the growth of bribery as proof that ordinary votes were worth buying; Mouritsen (2001) as proof that poorer citizens had little other reason to come and vote.

The contio

A contio was a meeting called by a magistrate to address the people, with no vote. The augural writer Messalla, quoted by Gellius, distinguishes holding a contio, speaking to the people without a proposal, from dealing with the people, asking them to order or forbid by vote (13.16). The convening magistrate decided who spoke; a private citizen addressed the crowd only if brought forward by him. Every bill was posted for three market days and argued in contiones before the vote, so argument happened in the contio and the assembly, once divided into its units, only voted. Magistrates also used it to report news, publish decrees and deliver funeral eulogies.

The setting was the Rostra. Cicero and Varro credit the tribune Gaius Licinius Crassus, in 145, with first turning from the Comitium to face the open Forum (On Friendship 96; On Agriculture 1.2.9); Plutarch gives the gesture to Gaius Gracchus (Gaius Gracchus 5). The contio could change outcomes: in 200 the centuries rejected war with Philip V almost unanimously, and the consul Sulpicius Galba carried the second vote after addressing a contio (Livy 31.6–8; module 4.4 tells the episode). Its late-Republican uses, Clodius included, are deep dive module 11.3.

Where sources disagree. Francisco Pina Polo (Contra arma verbis, 1996) and Robert Morstein-Marx (Mass Oratory and Political Power in the Late Roman Republic, 2004) treat the contio as the arena where opinion formed and the elite had to persuade. Mouritsen (2001) argues that audiences were small and, until the late Republic, largely propertied; Karl-Joachim Hölkeskamp (Reconstructing the Roman Republic, 2010) reads the meeting as a ritual that displayed the hierarchy of speaker above hearers.

Was Rome a democracy?

Fergus Millar attacked the orthodoxy of Gelzer, Münzer and Syme, an oligarchy managing its assemblies through clientship and family alliance, in "The Political Character of the Classical Roman Republic, 200–151 B.C." (1984), a sequel on 150–90 (1986) and The Crowd in Rome in the Late Republic (1998). Only the people could pass laws, and every law was passed by them; office came only by popular election, competitive and uncertain; the Senate had no legislative power; and politicians spent their lives addressing crowds. Rome, he concluded, belonged among ancient democracies, a direct democracy of its own kind. The second century supplies his cases: the war vote of 200; tribunes jailing the consuls of 151 over a harsh levy (Periochae 48); the people's insistence on electing Scipio Aemilianus consul for 147 when he stood, under age, for the aedileship, which made the Senate have the Villian law suspended for a year (Appian, Punica 112; Periochae 50); and the ballot laws of 139 and 137.

John North ("Democratic Politics in Republican Rome", 1990) welcomed the attack on what he called the "frozen waste" theory of an inert electorate, but argued that popular choice became decisive only when the elite divided and offered alternatives, as the united nobility of the early second century seldom did. Mouritsen (2001) separated the people as an ideal from the crowds who came. He estimated that the Comitium held some 3,600 to 3,800 people and the Forum perhaps 10,000, and that electoral assemblies seldom exceeded 10,000, against a census of 337,022 citizens in 164 (Periochae 46): sovereignty exercised by a self-selected few, mostly propertied, was no democracy. Hölkeskamp (2010; German 2004) judged the question unfruitful and studied instead a political culture of consensus, in which the people's votes, applause and presence at rituals confirmed a hierarchy of merit embodied by the nobility. Morstein-Marx (2004) took up Millar's oratory: the people were arbiters the elite had to persuade, but since every speaker claimed to serve them, they heard no real alternative and judged whose claim was credible. Most historians now stand between the poles: the people held real powers, used them seldom and on terms the elite set, and the system held while the nobility agreed. What changed after 133 is deep dive modules 11.3 and 11.8.

Where sources disagree. Polybius found a democratic element in the people's control of honours, punishments, laws and war (6.14). Sallust, writing a century later of the years after 146, has affairs at home and abroad run at the will of a few, who held the treasury, provinces, offices and triumphs (Jugurtha 41.7). Syme sided with Sallust, holding that an oligarchy lurks behind the façade of every government (The Roman Revolution, 1939); Millar wrote against Syme, his own teacher, and for Polybius (module 4.5).

Self-test

  1. What did the lex Villia annalis of 180 do, and what about it is inferred rather than attested?
    Answer

    It fixed the ages at which each magistracy could be sought and held (Livy 40.44.1). The figures, usually given as a two-year interval between curule offices and minimum ages of about 39 for the praetorship and 42 for the consulship, are reconstructed from known careers, and are best attested only for Sulla's law of 81.

  2. Why did the voting order of the reformed centuriate assembly matter beyond its arithmetic?
    Answer

    The first class and the equites held 88 centuries, nine short of the 97 needed, so the second class decided close contests and the poorest rarely voted. The praerogativa, voting first by lot, was announced and read as an omen, so it swayed those who followed (Pro Plancio 49; On Divination 1.103).

  3. How did the tribal assembly favour the propertied when every tribe counted equally?
    Answer

    Thirty-one of the thirty-five tribes were rural, and their votes were cast by members present in Rome, chiefly men who could afford to travel; freedmen were confined to the four urban tribes.

  4. On what did the Senate's authority rest, if not on law, and what did its leader contribute?
    Answer

    On practice and interest: magistrates were senators for life, and the Senate controlled provinces, money, extensions of command and honours, so its advice could not safely be ignored; Mommsen called it more than advice and less than command. The princeps senatus spoke first and so framed the debate; in 209 Fabius was chosen by merit over custom (Livy 27.11.9–12).

  5. How do Sallust's picture of the nobility and Hopkins and Burton's counts differ?
    Answer

    Sallust has the nobles hand the consulship among themselves and treat new men as polluted (Jugurtha 63.6–7). Hopkins and Burton found only about 40 per cent of consuls with consular fathers and a third with consular sons: a dominant but self-renewing elite, in constant competition.

  6. What do the Metellus eulogy and the Hispanus epitaph show about aristocratic values, and how was competition kept within bounds?
    Answer

    Both measure a life by office, military glory, fame and the matching of ancestors (Pliny, Natural History 7.139–140; CIL I² 15). Annual and collegiate office, the age and interval rules of 180, and sumptuary and bribery laws rationed glory among the nobility.

  7. Why do modern historians doubt that clientship decided elections?
    Answer

    Clients had several patrons who competed for them; the ballot after 139 hid the vote; and great men often lost (Valerius Maximus 7.5). Brunt (1988) found little evidence of clients voting to order, and Millar and Mouritsen rejected the model from opposite sides.

  8. What does the sequence of bribery laws suggest, and why is Polybius 6.56.4 hard to square with it?
    Answer

    Laws in 181 and 159 show electoral gifts were already a problem, and the later laws of 67 and 63 an escalation. Polybius claims bribery for office was a capital crime at Rome, yet no execution is recorded and the repeated legislation implies the practice persisted.

  9. What was a contio, and what does the war vote of 200 show about it?
    Answer

    A meeting summoned by a magistrate to address the people without a vote (Gellius 13.16), where every bill was argued and only invited citizens spoke. In 200 the centuries rejected war with Philip, but after Galba's speech in a contio a second vote carried it (Livy 31.6–8): persuasion before the vote could reverse a result.

  10. State Millar's case and the replies of North and Mouritsen.
    Answer

    Millar: the people alone passed laws and elected magistrates, the Senate had no legislative power, and oratory to crowds was central, so Rome was a kind of direct democracy. North: popular choice mattered only when the elite divided. Mouritsen: assemblies of seldom more than 10,000, mostly propertied, against a census of over 300,000, made formal sovereignty no democracy.

Carry forward

  1. The Republic gave formal power to the people and structured its use: weighted units, a fixed voting order, and a presiding magistrate who controlled proposals and speakers. Ask of every later crisis not who held the powers but who could use them.
  2. The Senate ruled by authority and the nobility by competition policed among peers: age rules, collegiality, sumptuary and bribery laws were the aristocracy's self-discipline. When individuals escaped it through long commands, armies or popular tribunates, the informal restraints failed, which is the story of this deep dive.
  3. The democracy debate turns on which evidence counts: formal rules (Millar), numbers present (Mouritsen), elite unity (North), ritual and consensus (Hölkeskamp), persuasion (Morstein-Marx). Test each later episode, from the Gracchi to Caesar, against all five.
