The sons of Constantine, 337–361

Module 8.3 ends with Constantine's death near Nicomedia on 22 May 337. That summer the soldiers at Constantinople killed nearly every male descendant of his father's second marriage, including his half-brothers Julius Constantius and Dalmatius (Eutropius 10.9; Zosimus 2.40). Julius Constantius' sons Gallus and Julian were spared; Julian later blamed Constantius openly (Letter to the Athenians). On 9 September 337 Constantine's three sons took the title Augustus and divided the empire.

Constantius II is the hinge of the period. A committed Christian of the homoian party, which called the Son "like" the Father and set aside the Nicene formula of module 8.3, he imposed that creed through councils in 359. Against the old cults he legislated more than he enforced: cesset superstitio, sacrificiorum aboleatur insania, "let superstition cease, let the madness of sacrifice be abolished" (Codex Theodosianus 16.10.2, of 341). Yet visiting Rome in 357 he admired its temples (Ammianus 16.10), and Symmachus recalled that he left the Vestals their privileges and filled the priesthoods with nobles (Relatio 3). He removed one object: the altar of Victory from the senate house (Ambrose, Letter 18). Gallus, Caesar from 351, was executed in 354 (Ammianus 14.11); Julian became Caesar in 355.

Julian's restoration of the gods

Julian, born at Constantinople in 331 or 332, was raised a Christian and kept for six years with Gallus on the imperial estate of Macellum in Cappadocia. As a student he found the Neoplatonists; Eunapius says Maximus of Ephesus drew him into theurgy (Lives of the Sophists 474–475). In Gaul the bookish Caesar proved a general, beating the Alamanni at Strasbourg in 357 (Ammianus 16.12). When Constantius demanded his best troops for Persia in 360, the army at Paris proclaimed him Augustus (20.4), and Constantius' death on 3 November 361 averted civil war.

Julian ordered the temples opened and the sacrifices restored (Ammianus 22.5.2). He recalled the bishops Constantius had exiled and summoned the quarrelling parties to the palace, knowing, Ammianus says, that no wild beasts are as dangerous to men as most Christians are to one another (22.5.3–4). His law of 17 June 362 made teachers subject to approval by their city councils and the emperor (Codex Theodosianus 13.3.5); a rescript explained that men who disbelieved in Homer's gods should not teach Homer but go to the churches of the Galileans and expound Matthew and Luke (Letter 36). Ammianus, an admirer, calls it harsh and fit to be buried in eternal silence (22.10.7; 25.4.20).

His most original project was a pagan church. He set high priests over provinces, with authority over city priests, and wrote them pastoral letters: priests were to keep out of theatres and taverns and give to strangers and the poor. To Arsacius, high priest of Galatia, he wrote that the Galileans had grown by kindness to strangers, and that it was a disgrace when they fed "not only their own poor but ours as well" (Letter 22).

Where sources disagree. The letter to Arsacius survives only because the Christian historian Sozomen copied it (5.16). Peter Van Nuffelen (2002) argued that it is a Christian forgery, citing anachronistic uses of "Hellenism"; Jean Bouffartigue (2005) and others defend it on philological grounds.

Antioch, a Christian city devoted to theatre and racing, met him with mockery. At Apollo's festival at Daphne he found one priest with a goose brought from his own house (Misopogon). In October 362 the temple there burned; Julian blamed the Christians and closed the city's great church (Ammianus 22.13.1–2). The Antiochenes jeered at his beard and his hecatombs, and early in 363 he answered with the Misopogon, "Beard-Hater": a satire on himself, the shaggy, ink-fingered emperor with lice in his beard, and through himself on a city that preferred pleasure to virtue and Christ to the gods.

Persia, 363: the death of Julian

Julian left Antioch on 5 March 363 to finish the Persian war (Ammianus 23.2.6), and Ammianus went with him. Module 0.1 introduced him in outline, a Greek of Antioch writing Latin at Rome around 390; here he is the eyewitness, a staff officer who had escaped from besieged Amida in 359 (book 19). Books 23–25 slip at times into the first person plural, and their detail is a participant's: the burning of the fleet (24.7.4), the retreat up the Tigris under attack. On 26 June, hurrying to a skirmish without his breastplate, Julian was struck by a cavalry spear that lodged in his liver (25.3.3–6). Ammianus gives him a philosopher's death, discussing the soul with Maximus and Priscus until about midnight (25.3.23). The army chose the officer Jovian, who bought its escape by ceding Nisibis and five regions beyond the Tigris (25.7.9–11).

The obituary (25.4) shows Ammianus' independence. He praises Julian's chastity, learning, courage and justice, then lists faults: garrulous, vain, superstitious rather than truly religious, slaughtering victims so freely that people said there would be no cattle left if he returned (25.4.17).

Where sources disagree. Ammianus says no one knew where the spear came from (25.3.6). Libanius, in his funeral speech, insists the killer was a Christian in Julian's own army (Oration 18.274–275); Sozomen thinks this probably true and the deed blameless (6.1–2). Theodoret has the dying emperor cry "You have won, Galilean" (Church History, book 3). Ammianus' uncertainty, from a man in the column, outweighs them.

Valens, the Goths and Adrianople

Jovian died in February 364. The officers chose Valentinian, who gave the East to his brother Valens (Ammianus 26.4.3); on Valentinian's death in 375 his sons Gratian and the four-year-old Valentinian II held the West.

In 376 Gothic refugees reached the lower Danube, driven by the Huns (31.2–3). The Tervingi under Fritigern asked to be received as settlers and soldiers, and Valens, wanting recruits, agreed. Ammianus will not give their number: whoever wants it might as well count the sands of the Libyan plain (31.4.6). The reception was a scandal. The commanders Lupicinus and Maximus let the refugees starve and traded them dogs, one for each slave (31.4.11). At Marcianople Lupicinus tried to kill the Gothic leaders at a banquet; Fritigern escaped and raised his people (31.5.5–7), and for two years they ravaged Thrace.

On 9 August 378, outside Adrianople, Valens attacked without waiting for Gratian's approaching army. His scouts had put the Goths at 10,000 (31.12.3); the general Victor urged delay, Sebastianus attack, and courtiers urged the emperor not to share the glory with his nephew (31.12.6–7). The army marched eight miles in the noon heat, and as Fritigern played for time with envoys his cavalry returned and struck (31.12.11–17). The Roman left collapsed. Valens died, of an arrow with his body never found, or burned in a farmhouse (31.13.12–16). Scarcely a third of the army escaped, and Ammianus knows no slaughter in the annals to match it save Cannae (31.13.18–19). There he ends, signing himself miles quondam et Graecus, a former soldier and a Greek (31.16.9). The late army as an institution is module 9.2's subject.

Where sources disagree. Ammianus explains the disaster by human failings: the greed of Lupicinus and Maximus and Valens' jealousy of Gratian (31.4.11; 31.12.1, 7). Orosius, a generation later, sees divine justice: Valens had sent Arian teachers to the Goths and was burned alive by the people he had condemned to burn forever (7.33).

Theodosius: the Goths, Thessalonica and Ambrose

On 19 January 379 Gratian made Theodosius, a Spanish officer, Augustus in the East. He could not destroy the Goths. On 3 October 382, by the date in the Constantinopolitan consular list, they were received by treaty, settled in the Balkans as a body and bound to send soldiers; the orator Themistius sold the peace as victory by clemency (Oration 16). Whether this was something new, an undefeated people inside the empire on its own terms, is debated: Peter Heather (Goths and Romans, 1991) thinks so, while Malcolm Errington (Chiron, 1996) sees a surrender followed by patronage; module 8.5 follows these Goths under Alaric.

His match was Ambrose, a former governor made bishop of Milan in 374. In 388 Ambrose preached at the emperor in church until he withdrew an order that Christians at Callinicum rebuild the synagogue they had burned (Letters 40–41). In 390 a crowd at Thessalonica lynched the general Butheric, who had jailed a popular charioteer (Sozomen 7.25), and imperial troops massacred the people in the circus; Theodoret gives 7,000 dead (5.17). Ambrose wrote privately that he could not offer the sacrifice in the emperor's presence until he had done penance (Letter 51). Theodosius laid aside his insignia and wept for his sin in church (Ambrose, On the Death of Theodosius 34).

Where sources disagree. Theodoret, writing some sixty years later, has Ambrose bar Theodosius at the church door and exclude him for eight months (5.17–18); Ambrose's own letter is private and tactful. Peter Brown (Power and Persuasion in Late Antiquity, 1992) finds no confrontation at the door, and Neil McLynn (Ambrose of Milan, 1994) reads the penance as a negotiated ceremony that served the emperor's image as much as the bishop's.

The altar of Victory, 384

The altar stood in the senate house before the statue of Victory from Tarentum that Octavian set up there in 29 BC (Dio 51.22.1–2), and senators swore their oaths on it (Symmachus, Relatio 3). Removed by Constantius, it was back by the 380s. In 382 Gratian removed it again and confiscated the endowments of the Vestals and the public priesthoods. A senatorial embassy under Quintus Aurelius Symmachus was turned away, and Pope Damasus forwarded a counter-petition from Christian senators (Ambrose, Letter 17).

In 384, after Gratian's murder, Symmachus was prefect of the city and tried again. His third Relatio, to the thirteen-year-old Valentinian II, is the finest defence of the old religion in Latin. It argues from custom: Rome herself asks to keep the rites that drove Hannibal from her walls and the Gauls from the Capitol (3.9). It argues from the limits of knowledge:

uno itinere non potest perveniri ad tam grande secretum (Relatio 3.10)

"Not by one road can one come to so great a mystery." And it argues from consequence: since the Vestals' revenues were seized the harvest had failed (3.11–15).

Ambrose answered in two letters (Letters 17 and 18; 72 and 73 in the CSEL edition), the second a reply to the Relatio point by point: Rome's victories were won by courage, not rites; a few paid Vestals compared poorly with the Church's unpaid virgins; and antiquity was no argument, because all things improve with time, as the day grows from darkness and the crop from seed. Valentinian refused, and the altar returned only under the usurper Eugenius (Paulinus, Life of Ambrose 26).

Law, the Serapeum and the Frigidus

Theodosius' laws against cult came late. In the East the prefect Maternus Cynegius had already let monks wreck temples, which Libanius protested in Oration 30 (Greek module 7.4). Then came three laws: on 24 February 391, to the prefect of Rome, no sacrifice, no visiting shrines (Codex Theodosianus 16.10.10); in June 391 the same for Egypt (16.10.11); and on 8 November 392 every form of cult forbidden, down to incense before household gods: sacrifice and reading entrails counted as treason, incense cost the house where it burned (16.10.12). Greek module 7.4 tabulates the two main laws; the Code that preserved them is module 9.1's subject.

At Alexandria the bishop Theophilus was turning a disused temple into a church. When cult objects found in it were paraded in mockery, pagans rioted, killed Christians and barricaded themselves in the Serapeum, the great temple of Serapis, led by the philosopher Olympius (Rufinus 11.22; Sozomen 7.15). An imperial letter pardoned them but ordered the cult destroyed. A soldier with an axe struck the colossal statue; it was broken up and burned, and Theodoret adds that mice ran out of the hollow head (Rufinus 11.23; Theodoret 5.22). Eunapius, the pagan, says the monks who took the site were men in appearance who lived like swine (Lives of the Sophists 472).

Where sources disagree. Gibbon (Decline and Fall, chapter 28) charged Theophilus with destroying the library of Alexandria, citing Orosius' empty book-chests in the temples (6.15.32). But Rufinus, who describes the precinct in detail, mentions no books, and Ammianus, writing shortly before, speaks of the Serapeum's libraries in the past tense (22.16.12–13). Roger Bagnall ("Alexandria: Library of Dreams", 2002) doubts that any great collection survived to be lost in 391.

In the West Valentinian II, dominated by his Frankish general Arbogast, was found hanged at Vienne on 15 May 392. Arbogast raised Eugenius, a Christian teacher of rhetoric. The pagan senator Virius Nicomachus Flavianus, prefect of Italy, rallied to him. The armies met on the river Frigidus below the Julian Alps on 5–6 September 394. Theodosius' Gothic allies, Alaric among them, bore the first day's losses, which Orosius counts as a second victory for Rome (7.35.19). On the second day a gale, the bora, blew into the faces of Eugenius' army. Ambrose saw God's hand (Explanation of Psalm 36.25); the pagan Claudian sang it for Theodosius' son:

o nimium dilecte deo … cui militat aether / et coniurati veniunt ad classica venti (On the Third Consulship of Honorius 96–98)

"O dearly loved of God, for whom the sky fights and the leagued winds come at your trumpet's call." Augustine quotes the lines and adds that Theodosius gave his runners the golden thunderbolts from the statues of Jupiter set up against him on the passes (City of God 5.26). Eugenius was beheaded; Flavianus and Arbogast killed themselves. Theodosius died at Milan on 17 January 395, and module 8.5 begins with his sons.

Where sources disagree. Rufinus (11.33) and, after him, Theodoret (5.24) tell the Frigidus as a war of gods, Jupiter and Hercules against the Cross. Herbert Bloch ("A New Document of the Last Pagan Revival in the West", 1945) built on them a pagan senatorial revival crushed in 394. Alan Cameron (The Last Pagans of Rome, 2011) answers that Eugenius was a Christian usurper, that the pagan colouring derives almost wholly from Rufinus, and that there was no organised pagan party to defeat.

The last Vestals and the last Games

Module 2.3 described the Vestals at their height; their end went unrecorded. In 384 or 385 the chief Vestal Coelia Concordia set up a statue to the dead Praetextatus, and his widow returned the honour (CIL VI 2145); Symmachus, their champion, thought it improper for Vestals to honour a man (Letters 2.36). The last story of a Vestal is Zosimus', a century later: an old woman, the last of the Vestals, cursing Stilicho's wife Serena for taking a necklace from the statue of the Mother of the Gods (5.38). No source records when the flame went out. The end of the Olympic Games belongs to the Greek East and to Greek module 7.4; at Rome the Lupercalia lasted into the 490s (module 2.3).

The slow conversion of the senatorial aristocracy

Laws closed temples; they did not convert senators. The Roman aristocracy was the last pagan stronghold in the West and changed family by family. The Calendar of 354, a luxury codex made for a Christian aristocrat named Valentinus, lists the festivals of the gods beside the burial days of Rome's bishops and martyrs (Michele Salzman, On Roman Time, 1990). Marius Victorinus, the rhetorician honoured with a statue in Trajan's forum (Jerome, Chronicle, s.a. 354), asked whether walls made Christians, then professed his faith in public (Augustine, Confessions 8.2.4–5). Praetextatus, priest of many cults (module 2.5), told Pope Damasus, Jerome says, that he would become a Christian at once if they made him bishop of Rome (Against John of Jerusalem 8). Ammianus shows why the joke stung: after Damasus' contested election of 366, 137 bodies lay in a basilica, and Rome's bishops, he adds, rode in carriages, enriched by matrons' gifts (27.3.13–14). By 402 Prudentius could name the great houses, the Anicii first, as Christian (Against Symmachus 1).

Where sources disagree. Peter Brown (Journal of Roman Studies 1961) described a quiet conversion carried by marriage and household, in which Christian wives and mothers did much of the work. Salzman (The Making of a Christian Aristocracy, 2002), studying several hundred known aristocrats, found men converting as readily as women, drawn by imperial service and by a Christianity that adapted itself to aristocratic ideals of status and honour.

Self-test

  1. Why does Ammianus' account of Julian carry special weight, and where does he part company with his hero?
    Answer

    He marched on the Persian campaign and writes parts of books 23–25 as a participant. Though a pagan admirer, he calls Julian superstitious rather than religious, repeats the joke about his endless sacrifices (25.4.17) and condemns the teaching law as fit to be buried in silence (22.10.7).

  2. What was Julian's "pagan church", and what did it borrow from Christianity?
    Answer

    Provincial high priests set over city priests, moral rules for priests and organised charity to strangers and the poor. Julian's letter to Arsacius names the Galileans' kindness to strangers as the model to imitate (Letter 22), though Van Nuffelen has argued the letter is forged.

  3. What did Julian's teaching law require, and why did even a pagan admirer object?
    Answer

    Teachers needed approval from their councils and the emperor (Codex Theodosianus 13.3.5), and the rescript (Letter 36) barred Christians who disbelieved in the gods from teaching the classics. Ammianus thought it harsh because it turned the shared education of the elite into a religious test.

  4. Why is the identity of Julian's killer uncertain, and how did the question become partisan?
    Answer

    Ammianus, who was with the army, says no one knew where the spear came from. Libanius blamed a Christian soldier and Sozomen accepted and excused the deed, while Theodoret's "You have won, Galilean" turned the death into a Christian legend.

  5. On Ammianus' account, what turned the Gothic crossing of 376 into the disaster of 378?
    Answer

    Roman officials starved and exploited the refugees, trading dogs for slaves (31.4.11); the attempted murder of the leaders at Marcianople started open war; and Valens attacked on a false estimate of 10,000 Goths without waiting for Gratian, unwilling to share the glory (31.12).

  6. What does the Thessalonica affair show about Ambrose and Theodosius, and which source exaggerates it?
    Answer

    That a bishop could make a Christian emperor do public penance: Theodosius laid aside his insignia and wept in church (Ambrose, On the Death of Theodosius 34). Theodoret's confrontation at the church door is a later dramatisation; Ambrose's own letter is private and tactful.

  7. Set out the three main arguments of Symmachus' third Relatio and how Ambrose answered the appeal to antiquity.
    Answer

    Custom (Rome's rites had saved her from Hannibal and the Gauls, 3.9), the limits of knowledge (not by one road to so great a mystery, 3.10) and consequence (the famine after the Vestals lost their revenues). Ambrose replied that all things improve with time, so age was no argument for keeping a rite.

  8. What did the laws of 391–392 forbid, and how did the Alexandrian riot lead to the fall of the Serapeum?
    Answer

    Sacrifice and visits to shrines, and then every form of cult down to household incense, with sacrifice and reading entrails treated as treason and incense punished by loss of the house (Codex Theodosianus 16.10.10–12). When cult objects from a temple being made into a church were paraded in mockery, pagans rioted and occupied the Serapeum; an imperial letter pardoned them but ordered the cult destroyed, and a soldier's axe began the destruction of the statue.

  9. Why does Alan Cameron deny that the Frigidus was a last stand of paganism?
    Answer

    Eugenius was a Christian, the war was a usurpation like any other, and the pagan colouring comes almost entirely from Rufinus and the writers who followed him; Cameron finds no organised pagan party, against Bloch's pagan revival.

  10. How do Brown and Salzman differ on how the Roman aristocracy became Christian?
    Answer

    Brown stressed a quiet process through marriage and household, with Christian women prominent. Salzman, studying several hundred aristocrats, found men converting as readily as women, drawn by imperial careers and by a Christianity that accommodated aristocratic ideals of status.

Carry forward

  1. Law ended public sacrifice; society ended paganism as an elite identity. The edicts of 391–392 forbade sacrifice and temple visits, but the old religion lost the senatorial families through marriage, career and fashion over two or three generations, not by decree.
  2. Almost every narrative of these years is Christian and reads events as judgement: Valens burned for heresy, the wind at the Frigidus, the penance at Thessalonica. Ammianus, a pagan officer who saw much of what he describes and still criticised his hero, is the control to test them against for the years he covers, to 378.
  3. After Adrianople and the treaty of 382 an undefeated Gothic people lived and fought inside the empire, and after Thessalonica a bishop could discipline an emperor. Both precedents shape the West of module 8.5 and the relations of emperors and popes long after.
