Greek writers give Rome a past

Rome's first foundation stories were written by Greeks, two centuries before any Roman wrote history. Dionysius of Halicarnassus, publishing in 7 BC, gathered them in one catalogue (Roman Antiquities 1.72–73); Plutarch (Romulus 1–2) and Festus preserve others.

  • Hellanicus of Lesbos (late fifth century), Priestesses of Hera at Argos: Aeneas came into Italy from the Molossians with Odysseus and named the city after Rhome, a Trojan woman who led the others in burning the ships (Dionysius 1.72.2). Damastes of Sigeum agreed.
  • Aristotle: Achaeans returning from Troy were blown off course at Cape Malea to "Latinion" in the land of the Opici; their Trojan captives burned the ships, and they stayed (Dionysius 1.72.3–4). Here the settlers are Greeks.
  • Heraclides Ponticus, Plato's pupil, wrote that an army "from the Hyperboreans" had taken "a Greek city called Rome" near the Great Sea (Plutarch, Camillus 22.2): the Gallic sack of about 390, reported within a generation.
  • Callias, historian of Agathocles of Syracuse (about 300): Rhome married Latinus, king of the Aborigines, and bore Rhomos, Rhomylos and Telegonos, who named the city after her (Dionysius 1.72.5).
  • Timaeus of Tauromenium (about 350–260), the first Greek to treat Rome at length: he dated its foundation, with Carthage's, to 814/3 (Dionysius 1.74.1), read the October Horse as a memorial of the Wooden Horse (mocked by Polybius 12.4b–c), and learned from locals that the holy objects at Lavinium were iron and bronze heralds' staffs and Trojan pottery (Dionysius 1.67.4).
Where sources disagree. The manuscripts make Aeneas come "with" Odysseus; some editors emend to "after", while Friedrich Solmsen (1986) defended "with" as a story of reconciled enemies founding a city together. Others doubt the ascription, since Hellanicus' own Troika left Aeneas at Pallene (Dionysius 1.47–48). If it stands, a Greek put Aeneas at Rome over seven centuries after the war's traditional date.

Rival founders: Rhomos, the sons of Circe, Latinus

The Trojan founder had Odyssean rivals. The oldest is in the closing catalogue of Hesiod's Theogony: Circe bore Odysseus "Agrios and Latinos, blameless and strong", who "far off in a recess of holy islands" ruled the Tyrsenians (1011–1016). Xenagoras gave Odysseus and Circe three more sons, Rhomos, Anteias and Ardeias, eponyms of Rome, Antium and Ardea (Dionysius 1.72.5); Plutarch adds a Rhomanos son of Odysseus and Circe and a Rhomos sent by Diomedes (Romulus 2.1). Telegonus, Circe's son, founded Tusculum, whose Octavius Mamilius Livy still calls a descendant of Ulysses (1.49.9). Latinus has four fathers: Odysseus (Hesiod), Telemachus (Hyginus, Fabulae 127), Hercules (Dionysius 1.43; Justin 43.1) and Faunus, the version Virgil fixed.

Where sources disagree. M. L. West and most editors date the catalogue containing Theogony 1011–1016 to the sixth century, well after Hesiod, and bracket line 1014, on Telegonus, as a further addition. "Tyrsenians" may mean the Etruscans or, loosely, the peoples of western Italy. The lines are the earliest Greek mention of Latinus, not evidence for the eighth century.

Saturn, Janus, Picus and Faunus

Latium's divine prehistory took its canonical form in Augustan poets and late antiquarians. Saturn, driven from heaven by Jupiter, hid in Latium:

dicta quoque est Latium terra latente deo (Ovid, Fasti 1.238)

There he ruled a Golden Age, Hesiod's golden race (Works and Days 109–126) moved to Italy: no one was a slave and nothing was private property (Justin 43.1.3), the logic of the Saturnalia, when masters served slaves. His town Saturnia stood on the Capitol, once the Saturnian hill (Varro, On the Latin Language 5.42), and his temple below it held the state treasury.

Janus received him. Macrobius (Saturnalia 1.7.19–24) makes Janus the earlier king who shared his realm with the newcomer, and the prow on the bronze as, behind the two-faced head, was explained as Saturn's ship (Ovid, Fasti 1.229–240). Ovid says Janus has no Greek counterpart (Fasti 1.90). Picus, son of Saturn, is the woodpecker, bird of Mars and of augury, whom Circe transformed for refusing her (Ovid, Metamorphoses 14). His son Faunus, a prophetic woodland god later identified with Pan, was king of the Aborigines when Evander landed (Dionysius 1.31.2); Virgil's chain of Saturn, Picus, Faunus and Latinus (Aeneid 7.45–49) became standard.

Where sources disagree. Ancient writers could not decide whether these were gods or men. Ennius' Euhemerus made Saturn a mortal king deified for his benefits; Varro derived his name from satus, sowing (On the Latin Language 5.64), a native seed-god. Most modern scholars see a Latin agricultural god overlaid by the Greek Kronos; where the native layer ends is uncertain.

Evander's Arcadians, Hercules and Cacus

Evander is the Greek who came first: an Arcadian from Pallantion, son of Mercury and the prophetic nymph Carmenta, who arrived sixty years before the Trojan War (Dionysius 1.31.1). Faunus gave him land, and his settlement named the Palatine, Palatium from Pallantion (Livy 1.5.1). He brought letters (Livy 1.7.8), the Lupercalia for Pan Lycaeus (Livy 1.5.1–2) and shrines of Victory and Ceres (Dionysius 1.32–33). Livy and Dionysius wrote more than twelve centuries after the date they give him.

Hercules, driving Geryon's cattle from Spain, rested by the Tiber. Cacus stole some, dragging them backwards into his cave so that the tracks pointed outwards; their lowing betrayed him, and Hercules clubbed him. Evander recognised the god his mother had foretold, and an altar was vowed (Livy 1.7.4–11; Dionysius 1.39–40; Virgil, Aeneid 8.185–275; Propertius 4.9). Dionysius adds a rationalised version: Hercules a general, Cacus a local chieftain (1.41–42).

Where sources disagree. Livy's Cacus is a fierce shepherd, Virgil's a fire-breathing son of Vulcan; Diodorus (4.21.2) has a Cacius who, with Pinarius, entertained Hercules on the Palatine and named the Steps of Cacus. An Etruscan mirror from Bolsena shows Cacu as a young lyre-playing seer ambushed by the Vibenna brothers. Which form is oldest is uncertain.

The Ara Maxima of Hercules Invictus stood in the Forum Boarium, the cattle market and river harbour. Its rite was Greek:

sacra dis aliis Albano ritu, Graeco Herculi (Livy 1.7.3)

The sacrificer stood bareheaded, women were excluded (Plutarch, Roman Questions 60), and merchants and generals vowed a tithe (Roman Questions 18). The Potitii and Pinarii served it; the Pinarii, late to the first feast, were barred from the entrails for ever (Livy 1.7.12–14). In 312 the censor Appius Claudius handed the rite to public slaves, and the Potitii died out within the year (Livy 9.29.9–11). The nearby sanctuary of Sant'Omobono, with Greek pottery of the eighth century and a terracotta Hercules and Minerva of about 530, confirms early Greek contact.

Where sources disagree. Jean Bayet (1926) derived this Hercules from the Greek Herakles through the Etruscan Hercle and the Greeks of Campania; Denis van Berchem (1959) argued for the Phoenician Melqart, whose cult at Tyre and Gades also had an open altar, a tithe and no women. Many now see a market cult shared by Greek, Phoenician and Etruscan traders.

Aeneas in Greek tradition

Aeneas was available because Homer had saved him. At Iliad 20.307–308 Poseidon rescues him from Achilles because he will rule the Trojans, "and his sons' sons, who will be born hereafter". Strabo (13.1.53) knew a reading that made him rule "over all", an adjustment for Rome, but himself kept Aeneas' heirs in the Troad. The Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite repeats the promise (196–197) and derives the name from the goddess's "dread grief", ainon achos (198–199). These texts stand some five centuries after the war's traditional date.

The Epic Cycle took him out of the city: in the Sack of Ilion, as Proclus summarises it, Aeneas withdraws to Ida before the fall. Hellanicus' Troika sent him to Pallene in Thrace (Dionysius 1.46–48), and nearby Aineia put Aeneas carrying Anchises on its coins around 500. Thucydides (6.2.3) makes Trojan refugees the Elymians of Eryx and Segesta. Hostile versions circulated too: Menecrates of Xanthus said Aeneas betrayed Troy (Dionysius 1.48.3).

Where sources disagree. The Tabula Iliaca Capitolina, an early imperial relief, labels its sack of Troy "after Stesichorus" and shows Aeneas "departing for Hesperia". Karl Galinsky (Aeneas, Sicily and Rome, 1969) took this as evidence that a sixth-century poet already sent Aeneas west; Nicholas Horsfall ("Stesichorus at Bovillae?", 1979) argued it reflects Augustan taste, and most now share his doubt.

Etruscan images and the tomb at Lavinium

The image of Aeneas with his father on his back was popular in Etruria before Rome claimed it. Dozens of Attic black-figure vases of about 520–470 show it, most of those with known findspots from Etruscan tombs, and terracotta statuettes of the group were dedicated at Veii. Andreas Alföldi (Early Rome and the Latins, 1965) concluded that Rome took Aeneas from the Etruscans.

Where sources disagree. The Veii statuettes were once dated about 500, the date behind the "sixth-century" statuettes of Greek module 7.3, but their date is disputed: Martinelli and Paolucci (2006) put them early in the fifth century, Sybille Haynes (2000) after Rome took Veii in 396, which would reverse the inference. Horsfall adds that the vases show a taste for a Greek image of piety, not an ancestor cult.

Dionysius (1.64.4–5) says Aeneas vanished in battle by the river Numicus and the Latins raised him a mound inscribed "to the father and god of the place"; Livy (1.2.6) calls him Jupiter Indiges. From 1968 Paolo Sommella excavated at Lavinium a seventh-century tumulus remodelled as a shrine in the fourth century, and identified it as that heroon. Lavinium kept Rome's Penates (Varro, On the Latin Language 5.144): consuls sacrificed there on taking office (Macrobius, Saturnalia 3.4.11), and after the Latin War Rome renewed its treaty with Lavinium every year (Livy 8.11.15). Varro saw bronze images of the white sow and her thirty piglets, and her body kept in brine (On Agriculture 2.4.18).

Where sources disagree. Nothing on the site names Aeneas: Dionysius' inscription names only a "father and god", and Jupiter Indiges may be an older Latin power later equated with him. The fourth-century remodelling fits the settlement of 338, when Rome had a motive to honour Lavinium's founder. Sommella's identification is attractive, not proven.

The Roman version: Fabius Pictor to Varro

Romans began writing their own past around 200, nearly a thousand years after the supposed landing, and followed the Greek arrangement. Fabius Pictor wrote in Greek; a library catalogue painted on a wall at Tauromenium summarises him as recording Hercules' arrival in Italy, Lanoios, ally of Aeneas and Ascanius, and "much later" Romulus and Remus. Cicero cites a dream in Fabius that foretold all Aeneas' deeds (On Divination 1.43). Naevius and Ennius made Romulus Aeneas' grandson through a daughter (Servius on Aeneid 1.273), with no Alba and no gap; Greek module 10.12 covers the poets themselves.

Cato's Origines (168–149) fixed the Latin geography. Aeneas lands at Laurentum; Latinus grants land; Aeneas marries Lavinia and founds Lavinium; Turnus of the Rutuli makes war; Mezentius of Caere joins him and demands the first fruits owed to the gods, which the Latins vow to Jupiter instead, the origin of the Vinalia (Macrobius, Saturnalia 3.5.10). Cato also made the Aborigines Greeks (Dionysius 1.11.1) and set Rome 432 years after Troy (Dionysius 1.74.2). Varro systematised: Lavinium is named after Lavinia, and thirty years later Alba is founded, named from the white sow (On the Latin Language 5.144). Livy 1.1–3 and Dionysius 1.45–71 give the settled sequence: landing, alliance or war with Latinus (Livy offers both, 1.1.6–7), marriage, war with Turnus and Mezentius, Aeneas' disappearance, and Ascanius' foundation of Alba Longa (Livy 1.3.3–4).

Where sources disagree. In Cato, as Servius reports him, Latinus falls in the first battle, Turnus and Aeneas in the second, and Ascanius later kills Mezentius. In Dionysius (1.64–65) and Livy (1.3.4) Ascanius makes peace with Mezentius instead; Virgil's Latinus survives and Aeneas kills Turnus. No version has authority over the others.

The Alban kings: four centuries filled

Once Eratosthenes put Troy's fall at 1184/3 and Fabius and Varro put Rome's foundation in the eighth century, Aeneas' grandson could not found Rome. About 430 years had to be filled, and kings of Alba Longa filled them. Livy's list (1.3.6–10) runs Silvius, Aeneas Silvius, Latinus Silvius, Alba, Atys, Capys, Capetus, Tiberinus, Agrippa, Romulus Silvius, Aventinus, Proca, then Numitor and Amulius (module 1.4). Dionysius (1.70–71) adds a reign length for each; Ovid has other names (Fasti 4.39–56). The method shows: Alba, Capys, Tiberinus and Aventinus are eponyms of the city, of Capua, of the river in which Tiberinus drowned and of the hill where Aventinus was buried (Livy 1.3.8–9); Romulus Silvius, Dionysius' Allodius, mimicked Jupiter's thunder and died by lightning (1.71.3). No modern historian treats the list as other than a learned construction.

Where sources disagree. Livy makes Silvius the son of Ascanius (1.3.6). Dionysius makes him the posthumous son of Aeneas and Lavinia, chosen king over Iulus, Ascanius' son, who received a priesthood instead and founded the Julii (1.70.3–4). Both answer one awkward question: why Aeneas' Julian heirs were not Alba's kings.

Why a Trojan and not a Greek

Rome had Greek founders on offer and kept Greeks as precursors: Evander and Hercules arrive first, as hosts and benefactors, but the blood is Trojan. Homer had promised Aeneas a ruling line. A Trojan ancestor put Rome inside the Homeric world, as old as any Greek city, without making it a Greek colony owing a mother city deference. In Sicily Trojan descent already marked non-Greeks against Greek colonists. Lavinium's Penates belonged to all the Latins, so to claim Aeneas was to claim the Latin past, above all after 338. And a son carrying his father and his gods matched the virtue Romans claimed first, pietas. The cost was descent from the defeated.

Where sources disagree. Erich Gruen (Culture and Identity in Republican Rome, 1992) reads the choice as Roman self-fashioning, entry to Hellenic culture at a distance; Andrew Erskine (Troy between Greece and Rome, 2001) stresses that Greeks built and exploited the legend and did not feel it as anti-Greek before Augustus. Dionysius denied the dilemma: the Trojans descended from the Arcadian Dardanus, so Rome was a Greek city (1.61–62, 1.89.1).

The claim at work: diplomacy and the Julii

The legend was first turned against Rome. Pyrrhus, descended from Achilles' son Neoptolemus, invaded Italy in 280 hoping to repeat the capture of Troy, "a descendant of Achilles making war on a colony of Trojans" (Pausanias 1.12.1). Thereafter Rome and its friends used it the other way.

The Julii, an Alban family (Tacitus, Annals 11.24), claimed descent from Iulus. Livy keeps his distance:

Iulum eundem Iulia gens auctorem nominis sui nuncupat (Livy 1.3.2)

Their clan altar at Bovillae honoured Vediovis "by Alban law". In 69 Caesar told the mourners of his aunt Julia that the Julii descended from Venus (Suetonius, Julius 6.1), and his denarius of 47–46 shows Aeneas carrying Anchises and the Palladium. Varro's lost De familiis Troianis catalogued the other Trojan clans. Self-test

  1. What did Hellanicus, as Dionysius 1.72 reports him, say about Rome's foundation, and why is the phrase "with Odysseus" a problem?
    Answer

    Aeneas came from the Molossians into Italy with Odysseus and named the city after Rhome, a Trojan woman who led the burning of the ships. Making two enemies co-founders is odd, so some editors emend to "after"; Solmsen defended the text, and others doubt the ascription because Hellanicus' Troika left Aeneas at Pallene.

  2. How does Aristotle's version differ from Hellanicus', and what motif do they share?
    Answer

    Aristotle's settlers are Achaeans blown to "Latinion", not Trojans, so the city would be Greek (Dionysius 1.72.3–4). Both use Trojan women burning the ships to explain why wanderers stayed.

  3. Name three things Timaeus contributed to the Greek picture of early Rome.
    Answer

    A foundation date of 814/3, synchronised with Carthage (Dionysius 1.74.1); the October Horse as a memorial of the Wooden Horse (Polybius 12.4b–c); and the report of heralds' staffs and Trojan pottery among the holy objects of Lavinium (Dionysius 1.67.4).

  4. Give three different fathers ancient sources assign to Latinus, and say what the variety shows.
    Answer

    Odysseus (Hesiod, Theogony 1011–1016), Hercules (Dionysius 1.43) and Faunus (Virgil); Hyginus adds Telemachus. Latinus was an eponym of the Latins, attached to whichever founding story a writer favoured.

  5. What marked the cult of the Ara Maxima as foreign, and what happened to it in 312?
    Answer

    A Greek rite with the head bare, exclusion of women, a tithe, and service by two families, the Potitii and Pinarii; Livy calls it the only foreign rite Romulus adopted (1.7.3, 1.7.15). In 312 Appius Claudius transferred it to public slaves and the Potitii died out (Livy 9.29.9–11).

  6. Which Homeric passage made Aeneas usable as a founder, and how was it bent for Rome?
    Answer

    Iliad 20.307–308: Aeneas and his sons' sons will rule the Trojans. Strabo (13.1.53) reports a reading in which he rules "over all", adjusted to fit Roman power, while he himself kept Aeneas' heirs in the Troad.

  7. What did Sommella find at Lavinium, and why is its identification as Aeneas' heroon uncertain?
    Answer

    A seventh-century tumulus remodelled as a shrine in the fourth century, matching Dionysius' heroon by the Numicus (1.64.4–5). Nothing names Aeneas; the inscription Dionysius quotes names a "father and god", and the remodelling may reflect Rome's settlement of 338 rather than an older cult.

  8. Why did the Alban king list become necessary, and how do Livy and Dionysius differ over its first link?
    Answer

    Naevius and Ennius made Romulus Aeneas' grandson, but once Troy's fall was set at 1184/3 and Rome's foundation in the eighth century, some 430 years had to be filled. Livy makes Silvius Ascanius' son; Dionysius makes him Aeneas' son by Lavinia, preferred over Iulus, ancestor of the Julii (1.70.3–4).

  9. Give two reasons Rome preferred a Trojan ancestor to a Greek one, and one risk.
    Answer

    Troy gave Rome Homeric antiquity without colonial dependence on a Greek mother city, and it tied Rome to the Latin cult at Lavinium and to western peoples such as the Elymians. The risk was descent from the losers, and Greek versions in which Aeneas betrayed Troy (Dionysius 1.48.3).

  10. How did Pyrrhus and Flamininus use the same legend from opposite sides?
    Answer

    Pyrrhus, descended from Achilles, cast his war as a new sack of Troy against a Trojan colony (Pausanias 1.12.1). Flamininus called himself "great leader of the Aeneadae" at Delphi while proclaiming Greek freedom, so Trojan descent became a Roman credential in Greece (Plutarch, Flamininus 12).

Carry forward

  1. Rome's prehistory was a Greek product before it was a Roman one. For any "Roman" legend, ask which Greek first wrote it, how many centuries after the event, and what Roman writers from Fabius to Varro chose to keep.
  2. Rome took Greeks as precursors (Evander, Hercules) and Trojans as ancestors: kinship with the Homeric world without subordination to it, which is why the claim worked in diplomacy with Greek cities as well as against Greek kings.
  3. Chronology drives myth. Once the foundation was fixed in the eighth century, invented Alban kings filled the gap, and the Julii's Iulus shows genealogy staying open to political adjustment into the Augustan age.
