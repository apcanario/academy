Maximinus and the soldier emperors

Module 7.4 ends with the recruits at Mainz proclaiming their trainer in 235. Maximinus was a Thracian of humble, half-barbarian origin, Herodian says (7.1.1–2); the Historia Augusta makes him a giant. He never entered Rome as emperor. He fought on the Rhine and Danube, and the Harzhorn battlefield in Lower Saxony, found in 2008 and dated by coins to the 230s, bears out the Historia Augusta's campaign deep in Germany (Maximini duo 12). To pay the troops he seized the property of the rich and the funds of cities and temples (Herodian 7.3).

In 238 landowners at Thysdrus in Africa killed a procurator and proclaimed the aged proconsul Gordian and his son (Herodian 7.4–5); within weeks the governor of Numidia had killed the son and the father had hanged himself (7.9). The Senate chose two of its own, Pupienus and Balbinus, and the crowd forced it to add the boy Gordian III (7.10). Maximinus stalled before Aquileia and was killed by his soldiers (8.5); the praetorians then murdered Pupienus and Balbinus (8.8). Herodian ends there, and no contemporary narrative of the next forty-six years survives. Gordian III died on the Persian front in 244; his prefect Philip, an Arab from the Hauran, celebrated Rome's thousandth birthday in 248 (Eutropius 9.3) before Decius overthrew him.

The pattern held: armies made emperors, the Senate ratified them, few died in bed. Many of these soldier emperors came from the Danubian provinces, as would Diocletian and Constantine. The table leaves out the Gallic and Palmyrene rulers and a swarm of usurpers.

Shapur I, Valerian and the rock of Naqsh-e Rustam

Module 7.4 introduced Ardashir. His son Shapur I, crowned about 240, made the Sasanian claim good. The best witness is Shapur himself: a trilingual inscription in Middle Persian, Parthian and Greek on the Ka'ba-ye Zardosht at Naqsh-e Rustam, near Persepolis, known as the Res Gestae Divi Saporis. It narrates three campaigns. Gordian III fell in a great battle at Misiche in 244, and Philip bought peace for 500,000 denarii, by Shapur's figure. Most Roman writers blame Philip instead: Zosimus says he engineered a grain shortage and turned the troops against Gordian (1.18–19), Eutropius that his treachery killed him (9.2); the Byzantine chroniclers Zonaras and Malalas have Gordian die after a fall from his horse in battle. About 252–253 Shapur destroyed a Roman army of 60,000 at Barbalissos (his figure) and took Antioch; Dura-Europos fell about 256, its synagogue and house church preserved under the siege embankment. In 260 Valerian came with 70,000 men, again by Shapur's count, and beyond Carrhae and Edessa Shapur took him with his own hands, with his praetorian prefect and senators; the captives were settled in Persian lands.

On the cliff below the Achaemenid tombs at Naqsh-e Rustam Shapur carved himself on horseback, one Roman kneeling, another held by the wrist: usually read as Philip suing for peace and the captive Valerian. A relief at Bishapur adds a third Roman, dead beneath the horse, taken as Gordian. No emperor had been taken alive before.

Where sources disagree. Shapur claims a battle. Zosimus says plague had wasted the Roman army and that Valerian, hoping to buy peace, went to a parley with a small escort and was seized (1.36). Lactantius has Shapur use him as a mounting block and hang his flayed, dyed skin in a temple (On the Deaths of the Persecutors 5), the end a persecutor deserved; Eutropius says only that he grew old in shameful servitude (9.7).

Decius: the order to sacrifice and the death at Abritus

The order of 249–250

Decius, a senator from Budalia near Sirmium (Eutropius 9.4), was proclaimed by the Danube army and defeated Philip near Verona in 249 (Zosimus 1.21–22; the place from Eutropius 9.3). Late in 249 or early in 250 he ordered every inhabitant of the empire to sacrifice, pour a libation and taste the sacrificial meat before local commissioners, who signed a certificate (libellus). Some four dozen survive from Egypt, dated June to July 250, in one formula: the applicant has always sacrificed, has now done so in the commissioners' presence according to the order, and asks them to sign. Whole households applied, and one certificate comes from a priestess of the crocodile god Petesouchos.

For Christians this replaced Trajan's rule of not seeking them out (module 2.5) with a test all had to pass. Fabian, bishop of Rome, died in January 250 and Origen was tortured (Eusebius, Church History 6.39); Dionysius of Alexandria saw Christians come forward pale and trembling, some to sacrifice, some to refuse (6.41). Cyprian's On the Lapsed addresses the readmission of those who yielded or bought certificates, the issue behind Novatian's schism at Rome. Valerian struck at clergy and Christians of rank in 257–258 (Cyprian, Letters 80), and Cyprian was beheaded at Carthage on 14 September 258; Gallienus restored church property (Eusebius, Church History 7.13). Module 8.2 takes the persecution of 303.

Where sources disagree. Cyprian and Dionysius present the order as an assault on the church, and Lactantius leaves his naked corpse to the beasts and birds in punishment (On the Deaths of the Persecutors 4). J. B. Rives ("The Decree of Decius and the Religion of Empire", 1999) read it as a general religious act that for the first time treated the empire's inhabitants as one religious community; the priestess's certificate shows pagans were tested too. The order's text is lost, so its purpose is inferred.

The Goths and Abritus

In 250 the Gothic king Kniva crossed the Danube, surprised Decius at Beroe and took Philippopolis (Jordanes, Getica 101–103). In the summer of 251, at Abritus (Razgrad in Bulgaria), Decius and his son died with their army in a marsh, the first emperor killed by a foreign enemy. Zosimus blames Trebonianus Gallus for luring him into the fen (1.23), but the army proclaimed Gallus and he adopted Decius' surviving son, which tells against betrayal. Gallus let the Goths go home with plunder and captives and promised a yearly payment (Zosimus 1.24). Sea raids followed across the Black Sea and the Aegean, and Claudius II won the name Gothicus at Naissus in 268 or 269 against a host Zosimus puts at 320,000 men in 6,000 ships (1.42).

The plague of Cyprian

An epidemic reached Alexandria about 250 and lasted more than a decade. Cyprian, bishop of Carthage, lists its symptoms: flux, fever, sores in the throat, vomiting, burning eyes, and in some the loss of limbs to putrefaction, or of hearing or sight (On Mortality 14). Dionysius wrote that Alexandrians aged fourteen to eighty on the grain register now numbered fewer than those aged forty to seventy once had, and praised Christians who nursed the sick (Eusebius, Church History 7.21–22). The Historia Augusta's 5,000 dead in a day at Rome (Gallienus 5.5) cannot be checked; Claudius II died of the disease in 270. Kyle Harper (The Fate of Rome, 2017) argues from the symptoms for a viral haemorrhagic fever like Ebola; smallpox and influenza have also been proposed. Turning such reports into mortality is the method dispute of module 7.3.

Gallienus and the Gallic empire

Valerian made his son Gallienus co-Augustus in 253 and left him the West. After 260 the empire split three ways. The Rhine army proclaimed Postumus, who forced Cologne to surrender Gallienus' son Saloninus and killed him (Zosimus 1.38). Postumus and successors such as Victorinus and Tetricus ruled Gaul, the Germanies and Britain, at first Spain and Raetia too, with their own consuls and mints. An altar found at Augsburg in 1992 records a victory of 24–25 April 260 over the Semnones or Iuthungi, returning from Italy with many thousands of Italian captives; it was dedicated in September under Postumus' consuls, so Raetia had already gone over. Postumus never marched on Rome, and his troops killed him in 269 for refusing them the sack of Mainz (Aurelius Victor 33.8).

The Latin tradition remembers Gallienus as a voluptuary (Eutropius 9.8). Yet he held Italy and the Balkans, ended the persecution, honoured Plotinus (Porphyry, Life of Plotinus 12) and built up mobile cavalry under Aureolus at Milan. Aurelius Victor says he barred senators from army commands by edict (33.34); inscriptions show a slower change over decades (module 9.2, and module 0.2 for the dispute over Luttwak's grand strategy). In 268, marching against the rebel Aureolus at Milan, he died in a plot devised by his prefect Heraclianus and known to Claudius (Zosimus 1.40).

Odaenathus and Zenobia of Palmyra

Palmyra, a caravan city between Roman Syria and the Euphrates, had long been Roman; its leading man, Septimius Odaenathus, held consular rank. After Valerian's capture he harried Shapur's retreating army, destroyed the usurper Quietus at Emesa in 261 and kept the East for Gallienus; Zosimus says he retook Nisibis and twice reached Ctesiphon (1.39). Palmyrene inscriptions after his death call him king of kings, turning the Persian title on Persia, but no coin makes him Augustus. He was murdered at Emesa in 267 or 268 (Zosimus 1.39).

His widow Zenobia ruled for their son Vaballathus. In 270 her general Zabdas took Egypt, with 70,000 men against 50,000 by Zosimus' figures (1.44). The mints of Antioch and Alexandria paired Aurelian on one face with Vaballathus, bearing Roman titles, on the other; in 272 mother and son became Augusta and Augustus. Aurelian beat Zabdas near Antioch and at Emesa, besieged Palmyra and caught Zenobia at the Euphrates, fleeing on a fast camel to seek Persian help (Zosimus 1.50–55); her adviser, the rhetorician Longinus, was executed (1.56). When Palmyra rose again in 273 it was sacked (1.60–61).

Where sources disagree. Zosimus says Zenobia died on the road to Europe, of illness or by refusing food (1.59). The Historia Augusta parades her in golden chains in Aurelian's triumph and retires her to an estate near Tibur (Tyranni triginta 30.24–27), with an invented descent from Cleopatra and a defiant letter to Aurelian (Aurelian 27). Her aims are read from the coins: the step from Vaballathus beside Aurelian to Vaballathus Augustus looks like a claim to share the Roman empire, not a separate kingdom.

Aurelian: reunification, the wall and the Sun

After Claudius and his brother Quintillus, the cavalry commander Aurelian took power in 270 and in five years reversed the division. Invaders of Italy were driven out after a near-disaster; Dacia beyond the Danube was given up and its name moved to a new province south of the river (Eutropius 9.15). Palmyra fell in 272; in 274 Tetricus, last of the Gallic emperors, deserted his army near Châlons and was made governor of Lucania (Eutropius 9.13). Aurelian's coins hailed him restorer of the world.

He began to wall Rome, unwalled for centuries, and Probus finished the work (Zosimus 1.49): about 19 km by modern measurement, some 8 m high at first, taking in the praetorian camp and the pyramid of Cestius. The Historia Augusta's nearly fifty miles (Aurelian 39.2) is fiction; much still stands.

In 274 he dedicated a temple of Sol in Rome, adorned with Palmyrene spoils, statues of Helios and Bel among them (Zosimus 1.61), with a new college of senatorial pontiffs and four-yearly games. The Sun was a god every army could share, the emperor's companion rather than his rival; Constantine's coins kept him into the 320s (module 8.3). The Calendar of 354 marks 25 December as the birthday of the Unconquered; that Aurelian fixed the date is a modern inference. In 275, outside Perinthus in Thrace, officers deceived by a secretary's forged order for their own execution killed him (Zosimus 1.62).

Where sources disagree. The Historia Augusta has Aurelian see the god of Emesa aid him against Zenobia (Aurelian 25), and Gaston Halsberghe (The Cult of Sol Invictus, 1972) took his Sol for the Syrian Elagabal; others prefer Palmyrene Malakbel. Steven Hijmans (Sol: The Sun in the Art and Religions of Rome, 2009) argued from coins and monuments that Sol had been a Roman god continuously since the Republic, and that Aurelian gave an old god a new priesthood.

The coin and the prices

Caracalla's antoninianus of 215 (module 7.4) weighed about one and a half denarii and was probably tariffed at two, a hidden debasement from the start. On the assays summarised by Kenneth Harl (Coinage in the Roman Economy, 300 B.C. to A.D. 700, 1996), it held about half silver at its launch and a few per cent by the late 260s: bronze with a silver wash. Mints multiplied near the armies, hoards of thousands of pieces fill museum stores, and in Britain and Gaul local copies circulated beside them.

Trust failed at the counter. In 260 the strategos of the Oxyrhynchite nome ordered bankers who had shut their exchanges rather than take the divine coin of the emperors to reopen and accept all but plain forgeries (P.Oxy. 12.1411). The state increasingly levied and paid in kind, the road to Diocletian's taxes (module 8.2). Rome's mint workers, who had adulterated the coin, rose about 271: Eutropius has them kill the finance officer Felicissimus (9.14), and the Historia Augusta counts 7,000 soldiers dead (Aurelian 38.2). Aurelian's reform of 274 struck a heavier coin marked XXI, read as twenty parts copper to one of silver or as a mark of value. Dominic Rathbone ("Monetisation, not price-inflation, in third-century AD Egypt?", 1996) found Egyptian prices broadly steady through most of the debasement, then a sudden multiplication after the reform.

Lost and garbled sources

No half-century of imperial history is worse documented. Herodian stops in 238. The best contemporary historian, Dexippus of Athens, wrote a Chronicle to 270 and a Scythica on the Gothic wars, and led the Athenians against the Heruli in 267 (Historia Augusta, Gallienus 13.8; Greek module 7.4 notes the damage to Athens). He was known only in fragments until a Vienna palimpsest, identified between 2007 and 2009 and published by Gunther Martin and Jana Grusková from 2014, yielded new pages on Kniva's war and the Herulian invasion.

The rest is late. Zosimus, a pagan writing at Constantinople about 500, follows Dexippus in book 1 of his New History and blames Rome's decline on neglect of the gods. The Latin breviaries of the 360s, Aurelius Victor and Eutropius, with the later Epitome de Caesaribus, compress a lost common source (module 0.1) into a few lines a reign; Byzantine chroniclers such as Zonaras keep Greek material otherwise lost. The Historia Augusta lacks its lives for 244–260 and fills the rest with forgery: the Thirty Tyrants, padded to echo the Thirty at Athens, and the letters of the Life of Aurelian (module 0.1 makes the case against it). The controls are contemporary: Cyprian's letters, Dionysius in Eusebius, the Thirteenth Sibylline Oracle, Shapur's inscription, papyri, the Augsburg altar and coins, which fix the order and length of reigns.

Was it a crisis?

The losses are plain: one emperor killed by the Goths and another a Persian prisoner, three regimes for a decade, Antioch sacked, Athens burned, Dacia given up, the silver coin turned to bronze. The curve of Latin inscriptions that Ramsay MacMullen traced ("The Epigraphic Habit in the Roman Empire", 1982) peaks under Severus and falls steeply after; Gallic towns shrank behind new walls. Cyprian told a pagan critic that the world had grown old (To Demetrianus 3). Yet Diocletian in 284 inherited the old frontiers, less Dacia and the angle between the upper Rhine and Danube, and the cities of Africa and much of the East kept building. What broke was how power worked: equestrian commanders, emperors from the camp, taxes in kind, a court that moved with the army. Module 8.2 shows Diocletian making a system of the century's improvisations.

Where sources disagree. Michael Rostovtzeff (The Social and Economic History of the Roman Empire, 1926) explained the century as a war of peasant soldiers against the urban classes; Géza Alföldy ("The Crisis of the Third Century as Seen by Contemporaries", 1974) argued that contemporaries themselves wrote of a world in crisis. Karl Strobel (Das Imperium Romanum im "3. Jahrhundert", 1993) and Christian Witschel (Krise, Rezession, Stagnation?, 1999) answered that the evidence shows regional variety and accelerated change, not one empire-wide collapse. Wolf Liebeschuetz ("Was there a crisis of the third century?", 2007) replied that a crisis need not be uniform, and that the convergence of usurpation, invasion, plague and monetary collapse in the 250s and 260s earns the word.

Self-test

  1. What did the events of 238 show about how emperors were made, and why does the narrative evidence thin out after that year?
    Answer

    An African tax revolt, the Senate's own candidates, the Roman crowd and finally the armies and praetorians each made or unmade emperors, and the army's choice proved decisive. Herodian, the last surviving contemporary narrative, ends with Gordian III's accession in 238.

  2. What does Shapur's inscription at Naqsh-e Rustam claim about Gordian III and Valerian, and how do Roman writers tell the same events?
    Answer

    It claims Gordian fell in battle at Misiche and that Shapur took Valerian with his own hands beyond Carrhae and Edessa. Zosimus and Eutropius blame Philip's intrigue for Gordian's death (Zosimus 1.18–19; Eutropius 9.2), and Zosimus has Valerian seized at a parley after plague had wasted his army (1.36).

  3. What exactly did Decius order, and what do the Egyptian certificates show about its scope?
    Answer

    That every inhabitant sacrifice, pour a libation and taste the offering before commissioners and obtain a signed libellus. Some four dozen survive, dated June to July 250; whole households and even a priestess of Petesouchos applied, which supports Rives' view that it was a general religious act, though Christians felt it as persecution.

  4. Why was Abritus a landmark, and why is Zosimus' charge against Trebonianus Gallus doubted?
    Answer

    Decius was the first emperor killed by a foreign enemy, dying with his son and army in a marsh in 251. Zosimus says Gallus lured him into the fen (1.23), but the army then proclaimed Gallus and he adopted Decius' surviving son, which a traitor would hardly have managed.

  5. What do Cyprian and Dionysius contribute to our knowledge of the plague, and what has Harper proposed?
    Answer

    Cyprian lists the symptoms, including flux, fever, throat sores and putrefying limbs (On Mortality 14); Dionysius gives the shrinking Alexandrian grain register and Christian nursing (Eusebius, Church History 7.21–22). Harper (2017) argued for a viral haemorrhagic fever; the diagnosis remains uncertain.

  6. What does the Augsburg altar reveal about the Gallic empire?
    Answer

    Dedicated in September 260 for a victory over the Semnones or Iuthungi returning from Italy with captives, it is dated by Postumus' consuls, so Raetia had joined him within months of his proclamation.

  7. How did Palmyra move from defending Gallienus' East to claiming imperial titles?
    Answer

    Odaenathus beat Shapur's retreating army, destroyed Quietus and reached Ctesiphon in Rome's name. After his murder Zenobia ruled for Vaballathus, took Egypt in 270, and the coins moved from pairing Vaballathus with Aurelian to naming mother and son Augusta and Augustus in 272, before Aurelian's reconquest.

  8. What did Aurelian do in Rome itself, and how does the Historia Augusta distort it?
    Answer

    He began a city wall of about 19 km, finished under Probus (Zosimus 1.49), and built a temple of Sol with senatorial pontiffs and games. The Historia Augusta gives the wall nearly fifty miles (Aurelian 39.2) and derives his Sol from a vision of the god of Emesa (25).

  9. What does P.Oxy. 12.1411 show, and how does Rathbone's price evidence complicate the link between debasement and inflation?
    Answer

    In 260 Oxyrhynchite bankers refused the emperors' coin until the strategos ordered them to accept it. Rathbone (1996) found Egyptian prices broadly steady during most of the debasement, with a sudden jump only after Aurelian's reform of 274.

  10. State the case for and against calling the years 235–284 a crisis, with a scholar on each side.
    Answer

    For: usurpation, defeat, plague and monetary collapse converged in the 250s and 260s, contemporaries spoke of a world in decline (Géza Alföldy, 1974), and Liebeschuetz (2007) defends the word. Against: Strobel (1993) and Witschel (1999) stress regional variety and accelerated change, with Africa and much of the East prospering.

Carry forward

  1. From 235 the emperor was whoever an army could make and keep; the men who survived were Danubian officers ruling from the camp with equestrian commanders, and the late Roman state of Diocletian and Constantine is their improvisation made permanent.
  2. The century is told by late, thin and partisan witnesses (the Historia Augusta, Zosimus, the breviaries, Christian writers judging persecutors), so the contemporary documents, Shapur's inscription, the libelli, papyri, altars and coins, are the controls on every story.
  3. Decius' universal sacrifice and Aurelian's Sol show emperors seeking one religion for the whole empire, a single test of loyalty or a single supreme god; the Great Persecution and Constantine's turn answer the same demand with opposite gods.
