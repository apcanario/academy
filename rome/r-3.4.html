The sources and the Samnites

The conquest has one continuous narrative: Livy books 7 to 10, written in the 20s BC, about three centuries after the events, and ending in 293. After that come the summaries of his lost books (Periochae 11–15), fragments of Dionysius and of Appian's Samnite History, and Polybius' digression on the Gallic wars (2.18–23). Diodorus books 19 and 20 keep a thinner annalistic line that sometimes contradicts Livy. Livy's sources, from Fabius Pictor about 200 BC to Valerius Antias in the first century, embroidered battles and casualty figures; the skeleton of consuls, triumphs, colonies and treaties is sound. S. P. Oakley's commentary on books 6–10 separates the two.

Module 1.1 traced the Samnites' Sabellic origins. By 343 they held the central and southern Apennines, the largest state in Italy by area, and it was no city-state. The unit was the touto, "people", under an annual magistrate, the meddix tuticus, with rural districts, villages and hill-forts such as Monte Vairano beneath it. Four peoples formed the core: the Pentri round Bovianum, the Caudini in the west, the Hirpini round Maleventum, and the small Carricini. They shared a name, a language and sanctuaries such as Pietrabbondante, where captured arms were dedicated. Transhumance to the winter plains of Apulia and Campania kept them in contact, and in conflict, with the lowlands.

Where sources disagree. E. T. Salmon (Samnium and the Samnites, 1967) reconstructed a Samnite League with a federal council and a single war-leader. Emma Dench (From Barbarians to New Men, 1995) and Rafael Scopacasa (Ancient Samnium, 2015) answer that it rests mainly on Livy's habit of writing "the Samnites" as one state, while the archaeology shows a looser, regionally varied society. Whether Samnium had federal institutions or only wartime coalitions is uncertain.

The First and Second Samnite Wars, 343–304

A treaty of 354 (Livy 7.19.4; Diodorus 16.45.8) broke down in 343 when, Livy says, the Samnites attacked the Sidicini, Capua sent help and was beaten, and the Campanians surrendered themselves to Rome outright, so that Rome would be defending its own (7.29–31). Peace came in 341, and within a year Samnites were fighting beside Rome against the Latins (module 3.3). Diodorus says nothing of this war, and the surrender of Capua looks like a later legal justification.

The real contest began with Rome's push to the Liris and into Campania: a Latin colony at Fregellae (328) on land the Samnites claimed (8.22–23), and the siege of Neapolis in 327–326, during which Publilius Philo's command was extended beyond his year, the first prorogation (8.23.12). War followed in 326.

In 321 both consuls, Veturius Calvinus and Postumius Albinus, marching for Luceria, were trapped in a wooded defile near Caudium by the Samnite general Gavius Pontius (Livy 9.2–6). His father, Livy says, advised releasing them with kindness or killing them all (9.3); Pontius chose the middle course. The army gave up its arms and 600 equites as hostages; the consuls promised peace and the evacuation of Samnite land; then every man passed, in his tunic, under the yoke, two spears upright and one across (9.5–6). The rite unmade an army: the defeated passed beneath the victor's weapons as through a gate. The Senate repudiated the terms as a personal pledge of the commanders, not a treaty of the people, and handed Postumius over bound; he struck the fetial and declared himself a Samnite, so that a renewed war would be just (9.8–11).

Where sources disagree. Livy insists the peace was made by pledge (sponsio), "not, as is commonly believed and as even Claudius writes, by treaty" (9.5.2), and then avenges it at once at Luceria in 320 (9.12–15). Salmon and T. J. Cornell (The Beginnings of Rome, 1995) think the peace was real and kept until 316, and that the pledge and the revenge were built to clear Rome of perjury and shame.

When war resumed, Rome lost again at Lautulae in 315, a defeat Diodorus reports plainly (19.72) and Livy blurs (9.23). Recovery came by method: Latin colonies at Luceria behind Samnium (314) and along its western edge (313–312), the Via Appia (312), alliances in Apulia. Bovianum fell in 305 and peace in 304 restored the old treaty (9.44–45). By 302 the Marsi, Paeligni, Marrucini, Frentani and Vestini were allies (9.45; 10.3), which cut Samnium off from the north.

Sentinum and the end of Samnite independence, 298–264

The Third War began in 298, and the Samnite strategy was not to fight Rome alone. In 296 Gellius Egnatius took an army north to join the Etruscans, Umbrians and Senonian Gauls (Livy 10.16–18). In 295 the consuls Fabius Rullianus and Decius Mus met the coalition at Sentinum in Umbria, after raids on Clusium had drawn the Etruscans and Umbrians home (10.27). On the left Decius' line broke before the Gallic chariots, and he devoted himself and the enemy to the gods below, as his father had done at Veseris in 340, and rode to his death; Fabius won on the right and Egnatius fell (10.28–29). Livy gives 25,000 enemy dead against 8,700 Romans (10.29). The devotio as moral example is module 3.2.

Where sources disagree. Polybius (2.19.5–6), probably following Fabius Pictor, names only Samnites and Gauls. Duris of Samos, a contemporary Greek, put the dead at 100,000 (Diodorus 21.6.1), while Livy rejects writers who swelled the enemy to fantastic numbers (10.30). The epitaph of Scipio Barbatus, consul in 298, contradicts Livy's provinces for that year (module 0.1).

Livy ends at Aquilonia (293), where the Samnite elite had sworn in a linen-screened enclosure never to flee (10.38–42). Curius Dentatus forced peace in 290 and conquered the Sabines (Periochae 11). The Senones were expelled from the Adriatic coast and a citizen colony planted at Sena Gallica; Boii and Etruscans were beaten at Lake Vadimon in 283 (Polybius 2.19–20). Samnites, Lucanians and Bruttians joined Pyrrhus (module 3.5) and were reduced by 272. The ring of Latin colonies closed round Samnium: Venusia (291), Paestum and Cosa (273), Beneventum and Ariminum (268), Aesernia (263). Picenum fell in 269–268 and Volsinii in 264. Everything south of the Arno and Ariminum was bound to Rome.

Appius Claudius Caecus

The Second War's most consequential Roman won no great battle. Appius Claudius, later Caecus, "the Blind", was censor in 312 (Livy 9.29–34, 9.46; Diodorus 20.36). His road ran straight across the Pontine plain to Capua, giving armies a route to Campania clear of the exposed inland Via Latina. His aqueduct, the Aqua Appia, ran 11,190 paces from springs off the Via Praenestina, all but about sixty of them underground; Frontinus dates it to "the thirtieth year of the Samnite war" (On Aqueducts 5). He enrolled freedmen's sons in the Senate and spread the humble (humiles) through all the tribes, until the censors of 304 confined them to the four urban tribes (9.46.10–15). He transferred the cult of the Ara Maxima from the clan of the Potitii to public slaves (9.29.9–11). His speech against Pyrrhus is module 3.5.

Where sources disagree. Mommsen read Appius as a democratic reformer arming the urban poor; E. S. Staveley (1959) as an aristocrat building a clientele, which suits a man who opposed opening the priesthoods to plebeians in 300 (Livy 10.7–8). T. P. Wiseman (Clio's Cosmetics, 1979) traced Livy's arrogant Claudii to late-Republican invention, and the elogium of the Forum of Augustus, known from a copy at Arretium, lists his offices, Samnite towns taken, a Sabine and Etruscan army routed, peace with Pyrrhus prevented, the road, the water and the temple of Bellona, but nothing of freedmen or the Potitii.

Colonies and roads as instruments of control

Module 0.2 surveyed the roads. Rome planted two kinds of colony.

  • Citizen colonies: about 300 Roman families as a coastal garrison (Livy 8.21.11, for Tarracina), at Antium (338), Tarracina (329), Minturnae and Sinuessa (296) and Sena Gallica. Guarding the shore was their service, and they still claimed exemption from the legions in 207 (Livy 27.38).
  • Latin colonies: 2,500 to 6,000 men with their families (2,500 at Cales, Livy 8.16; 6,000 at Alba Fucens, 10.1), drawn from Romans, Latins and other allies. A Roman who enrolled became a Latin. The colony was a separate state with its own magistrates, laws and often coinage, linked to Rome by the Latin rights (intermarriage, trade, migration to Rome) and by the duty to send soldiers. About nineteen were founded between 334 and 263.

The sites state the purpose: Fregellae on the Liris crossing, Luceria behind Samnium, Sora and Interamna on its western edge, Alba Fucens and Carseoli on the route through the Marsi, and Venusia, Beneventum and Aesernia round the rest. Each stood on confiscated land, so the defeated paid for their own surveillance; to Cicero they seemed not towns of Italy but bulwarks of empire (De lege agraria 2.73). Individual allotments did the same work without a colony, organised into new rural tribes until the total reached thirty-five in 241. The roads, built by censors and consuls, joined the colonies to Rome: a colony in hostile country had only to hold out until an army came down them.

Where sources disagree. Aulus Gellius called colonies "little images and likenesses" of the Roman people (16.13.9), and Salmon (1969) saw a planned state strategy. Edward Bispham (2006) and Guy Bradley argue that this projects later practice backwards: early colonies had mixed populations, and the monumental centres excavated at Cosa and Alba Fucens are largely later than the foundations.

The alliance system and the formula togatorum

By 264 Rome had a separate treaty with nearly every community in the peninsula. The settlement of 338 (module 3.3) supplied the categories.

The allies owed men. None paid tribute. Each sent a contingent at its own expense, under its own officers and Roman prefects; Rome fed it free, where legionaries had grain deducted from their pay (Polybius 6.39.15). Allied infantry equalled the Roman and allied cavalry outnumbered it (3.107.12); Greek coastal cities sent ships, and lent them to Rome for the crossing of 264 (1.20.14). The allies surrendered their foreign policy, sometimes under a clause binding them to uphold the majesty of the Roman people (Cicero, Pro Balbo 35–36), but kept their laws, magistrates, cults and languages. In return they had security, a share of booty and, through the Latin colonies, of land, and Roman backing for their ruling families, as at Arretium in 302 (Livy 10.3–5) and at Volsinii in 264, where former slaves had seized power. Cornell draws the consequence: since the only obligation was to fight, the system worked only when Rome was at war.

The register was the formula togatorum, the list of "men of the toga" from whom Rome could demand soldiers; the phrase survives in the agrarian law of 111 BC. Its working shows in 209, when twelve of the thirty Latin colonies refused their contingents, while eighteen, led by Fregellae, said their men were ready according to the formula and more besides (Livy 27.9–10). In 204 the twelve were made to supply double their largest levy (29.15).

Where sources disagree. Arnold Toynbee (Hannibal's Legacy, 1965) took the formula to fix a maximum each community could be asked for. P. A. Brunt (Italian Manpower, 1971) argued for a sliding scale, each year's demand set in proportion to the legions raised and to each ally's registered manpower.

The manipular legion

The instrument was the manipular legion, described by Polybius in the mid second century (6.19–42; module 9.2) and confusedly by Livy for 340 (8.8). Behind light-armed velites stood three lines, the hastati, the principes and the veteran triarii, each of ten maniples ("handfuls") with gaps between them, the second line covering the gaps of the first. The front lines threw heavy javelins (pila) and closed with sword and oval shield (scutum); the triarii knelt in reserve with the thrusting spear. The hoplite phalanx was one deep line that won by cohesion and push, with no reserve and no way to turn. A legion could relieve a tired line through the gaps, detach maniples to a flank and cross broken ground in order. Polybius, comparing it with the Macedonian phalanx after Cynoscephalae, says the phalanx needed level ground while the Romans could fight anywhere (18.28–32). Valleys, passes and hill-forts were the ground of the Samnite Wars.

Where sources disagree. In the anonymous Greek Ineditum Vaticanum a Roman tells a Carthaginian in 264 that Rome fought the Etruscans in their own phalanx, then took the oblong shield and javelin from the Samnites; Sallust's Caesar says the Romans took their arms from the Samnites (Catiline 51.38). Livy instead ties the change to the scutum to the introduction of pay at Veii (8.8.3). Such schematic sources cannot settle whether the system was borrowed or evolved.

Citizens of both grades, in the field and on the lists, make about 325,000 of some 770,000: three-fifths of Rome's strength was allied. The Samnites alone listed 77,000 men; Rome had not destroyed its most dangerous enemy but enrolled it. Hannibal invaded with fewer than 20,000 (2.24); he could destroy armies but not the lists, unless the allies left Rome, which is why his strategy aimed at them (module 4.3). Brunt took the lists as genuine but incomplete: the Bruttians and Greek cities are missing.

Why Rome united Italy and no Greek city united Greece

Module 0.2 gave the geographical half of the answer. The institutional half lies in the treatment of the conquered.

  1. Citizenship. Rome made defeated peoples Roman by grades (Tusculum in 381, much of Latium and Campania in 338, the Sabines in 290 and 268) and made citizens of freed slaves. Athens confined citizenship to children of two Athenians in 451/0 (Greek module 6.2), and Sparta's citizens fell from about 8,000 (Herodotus 7.234) to under a thousand (Aristotle, Politics 1270a). Aristotle's polis had to be small enough for citizens to know one another (Politics 1326b). Philip V, urging Larisa about 215 to enrol new citizens, cited the Romans, who admit freed slaves and have sent colonies to nearly seventy places (Syll.3 543); Dionysius (2.16–17) and Tacitus' Claudius (Annals 11.24) say Sparta and Athens fell by treating the conquered as aliens.
  2. Colonies. A Greek colony was an independent polis from birth (Greek module 5.2), and Corcyra fought Corinth; a Latin colony was defined by its rights at Rome and its duty to Rome. The nearest Greek equivalent, the Athenian cleruchy, was resented as confiscation, renounced in the charter of the Second Athenian League in 377 and revived at Samos in 365.
  3. Men, not money. Delian tribute paid Athenian rowers and Athenian buildings (Greek module 6.2), and every Athenian defeat invited revolt. Rome's allies paid nothing and fought beside Romans for shared spoils: a heavy burden, but a partnership in conquest.
  4. Separate treaties and local elites. Athens imposed democracies and drew allied lawsuits to its own courts; Rome left internal government alone, backed the propertied everywhere, and gave the allies no common council in which to combine.
Where sources disagree. Salmon (The Making of Roman Italy, 1982) described a gradual, largely consensual unification. Henrik Mouritsen (Italian Unification, 1998) argues that "unification" was written after the Social War made the allies citizens; in 264 Italy was a hegemony of unequal treaties, and the defections after Cannae (module 4.3) and the revolt of 91 (module 5.2) show how conditional consent was. Both sides agree that the Latin colonies, none of which defected after Cannae, were the system's spine.

Self-test

  1. Why can Livy books 7–10 not be read as a plain record of the Samnite Wars, and which parts of them are most trustworthy?
    Answer

    Livy wrote about three centuries after the events, from annalists who elaborated battles and inflated figures. The skeleton of consuls, triumphs, colonies and treaties is sound; battle narratives and casualty totals are not, and the Scipio Barbatus epitaph contradicts Livy 10.11–12 on 298.

  2. What was a touto, and why do scholars disagree over a Samnite League?
    Answer

    The Samnite "people", the basic state under an annual meddix tuticus, with districts, villages and hill-forts beneath it. Salmon reconstructed a federal league with a war-leader; Dench and Scopacasa argue the evidence is mostly Livy's habit of treating "the Samnites" as one state.

  3. What did the yoke at the Caudine Forks signify, and how did Rome escape the agreement?
    Answer

    Passing disarmed beneath the victor's spears was a rite of submission that unmade the army. The Senate called the terms a personal sponsio of the commanders and handed them over, and Postumius struck the fetial to make a new war just (Livy 9.5–11); Livy's denial that it was a treaty (9.5.2) shows the tradition was contested.

  4. Who fought at Sentinum, and how do Polybius and Duris complicate Livy's account?
    Answer

    Rome under Fabius Rullianus and Decius Mus against Samnites and Gauls, the Etruscans and Umbrians having been drawn away (Livy 10.27–29). Polybius (2.19.5–6) names only Samnites and Gauls; Duris' 100,000 dead (Diodorus 21.6.1) dwarfs Livy's 25,000.

  5. Name three acts of Appius Claudius' censorship and say why his road mattered in the war.
    Answer

    Any three of: the Via Appia, the Aqua Appia, freedmen's sons in the Senate, the humble spread through all the tribes, the transfer of the Ara Maxima cult (Livy 9.29, 9.46). The road gave armies a direct, all-weather route to Campania clear of the exposed Via Latina.

  6. How did a Latin colony differ from a citizen colony?
    Answer

    A citizen colony was some 300 Roman families guarding a coast, who claimed exemption from the legions; in 207 the Senate upheld the claim only for Antium and Ostia (Livy 27.38). A Latin colony had 2,500 to 6,000 men, whose Roman members gave up citizenship, and formed a separate state with Latin rights that owed troops to Rome.

  7. What did Rome's allies owe and not owe, and what does the episode of 209 show about the formula togatorum?
    Answer

    They owed soldiers or ships at their own expense and gave up independent foreign policy; they paid no tribute and kept their own laws. In 209 twelve Latin colonies refused their contingents while eighteen declared men ready according to the formula (Livy 27.9–10): a register of obligations that Rome enforced on the twelve in 204 (29.15).

  8. How did the manipular legion differ from the hoplite phalanx, and why did that suit Samnite warfare?
    Answer

    Three lines of small units with gaps, pila and scutum, and veterans in reserve, against a single deep line relying on cohesion. The legion could relieve tired troops, detach units and move over rough ground, which valleys and hill-forts demanded; Polybius makes the comparison at 18.28–32.

  9. What do Polybius' figures for 225 show about the composition of Rome's strength?
    Answer

    Of some 770,000 men able to bear arms (2.24), about three-fifths were allies, and the Samnites alone listed 77,000. Rome's power was the alliance, which is why Hannibal had to detach allies rather than simply win battles.

  10. Give two institutional reasons, each with a Greek counterpart, why Rome could unite Italy when no Greek city united Greece.
    Answer

    Rome extended citizenship by grades and even to freed slaves, while Athens closed it in 451/0 and Sparta's shrank (Greek module 6.2; Philip V in Syll.3 543). Rome took soldiers, not the tribute that made Athens' allies resent paying for another city, and its colonies extended the state rather than becoming independent poleis (Greek module 5.2).

Carry forward

  1. Rome conquered Italy by turning defeated enemies into allies who owed soldiers, not tribute; since the only obligation was war, the system needed war to work, and its manpower (three-fifths allied in 225) is the reserve that outlasted Pyrrhus and Hannibal.
  2. Colonies on confiscated land, roads that tied them to Rome, and a ladder of statuses (ally, Latin, citizen without the vote, full citizen) were instruments of control: each community had something to hope for and no common council in which to combine.
  3. The difference from Greece was institutional, an elastic citizenship and dependent colonies against the closed polis and the independent colony; but "unification" is partly a later story, and the allies' exclusion from citizenship is the fault line that opens in the Social War.
