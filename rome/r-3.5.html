01A Greek war in Roman memory

No Roman narrative of the war survives whole. Livy's books 12–15 are lost, and the Periochae give a line or two per event. The one connected account is Plutarch's Pyrrhus (about AD 100, nearly four centuries later), which follows Pyrrhus' contemporary Hieronymus of Cardia and the king's own memoirs; Greek module 7.3 summarises the campaign from that side. The Roman tradition survives in pieces: Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Roman Antiquities books 19–20, in Byzantine excerpts; Appian's fragmentary Samnite History; Cassius Dio, in fragments and in Zonaras' twelfth-century epitome (8.2–6); and the handbooks of Valerius Maximus, Florus and Eutropius. Behind them stand Ennius, who gave the war book 6 of his Annales about a century after it, and annalists such as Claudius Quadrigarius and Valerius Antias, two centuries after, whom Aulus Gellius sets against each other (3.8). The battles reach us from Greek witnesses; the moral legends were made by Romans long afterwards.

02Tarentum and the soiled toga (282)

The road to Tarentum ran through Thurii. Pressed by the Lucanians, that Greek city appealed to Rome, and in 282 Gaius Fabricius relieved it (Livy, Periochae 11; Valerius Maximus 1.8.6). The same year ten Roman ships appeared off Tarentum. The Tarentines, watching from their theatre, cited an old treaty barring Roman ships from sailing beyond the Lacinian promontory, put out, sank several and killed the commander; then they expelled the Roman garrison from Thurii (Livy, Periochae 12; Appian, Samnite History 7; Zonaras 8.2).

Rome sent envoys under Lucius Postumius Megellus, who were received in the theatre. Postumius spoke Greek, and his mistakes were laughed at; then a drunk named Philonides soiled his toga. Postumius told the Tarentines they would wash it out with much blood (Dionysius 19.5; Appian, Samnite History 7). The story does Roman work: a violated envoy made the war just in fetial terms (modules 2.3 and 4.4), and the setting pits Greek wine and theatre against the Roman toga. It also shows a senator able to address Greeks in Greek.

Where sources disagree. Appian names the admiral Cornelius and says he was sightseeing along the coast; Dio and Zonaras call him Lucius Valerius. The Lacinian treaty is known only from Appian and is commonly placed about 303, when the Spartan Cleonymus was in Italy, but that is inference. Whether the fleet's entry was a tour or a deliberate breach is uncertain; the Roman tradition answers by shifting the weight of the story to the theatre.

03Heraclea and Asculum as Rome remembered them (280–279)

Pyrrhus landed in 280 after a storm scattered his fleet (Plutarch, Pyrrhus 15); the size of his army is in Greek module 7.3. The Roman tradition kept moments, not tactics. Before Heraclea the consul Publius Valerius Laevinus caught a spy of Pyrrhus, paraded the army before him and sent him back to invite the king to come and see Roman strength openly (Dionysius 19.11). Pyrrhus, viewing the Roman camp, judged that the order of these barbarians was not barbarian (Plutarch, Pyrrhus 16). The elephants, which no Roman had seen, panicked the Roman horse and decided the day (Plutarch, Pyrrhus 17). Romans called them Lucanian oxen (Varro, On the Latin Language 7.39; Pliny, Natural History 8.16). Defeat was turned into credit: Pyrrhus found every Roman dead wounded in front and said that with such soldiers he could have ruled the world (Eutropius 2.11).

At Asculum in 279 the consuls brought an answer to the elephants. Dionysius (20.1) describes three hundred ox-wagons fitted with iron spikes, scythes and fire-bearing grapnels; his narrative covers a single day, while Plutarch, following Hieronymus, has two (Pyrrhus 21). The cost of the second day produced the remark behind "Pyrrhic victory" (Plutarch, Pyrrhus 21), quoted in Greek module 7.3.

Where sources disagree. Plutarch gives two sets of figures for Heraclea: Dionysius' nearly 15,000 Roman and 13,000 Epirote dead, Hieronymus' 7,000 and under 4,000 (Pyrrhus 17). For Asculum he gives Hieronymus' 6,000 Romans and, from Pyrrhus' memoirs, 3,505 of the king's men (21). The Roman-derived figures inflate both sides and so make each victory costlier for Pyrrhus; the Greek ones are lower and precise, which does not make them neutral.
Where sources disagree. Did a third Decius Mus devote himself at Asculum, as his grandfather had at Veseris in 340 and his father at Sentinum in 295 (modules 3.2 and 3.4)? Cicero counts the consul of 279 among the Decii who died for Rome (Tusculan Disputations 1.89). Dio and Zonaras (8.5) say Pyrrhus ordered his men to take alive any Roman in the ritual dress, and that Decius survived; Dionysius mentions no devotio. Patrick Kent (A History of the Pyrrhic War, 2020) treats it as family legend completing a triad.

04Cineas, the blind censor and the assembly of kings

After Heraclea Pyrrhus sent Cineas, a Thessalian orator whose words, the king said, had won him more cities than his arms (Plutarch, Pyrrhus 14). His gifts were refused by the senators and, Livy's Cato later boasted, by their wives (Livy 34.4.6). The terms were moderate: friendship with Pyrrhus and Tarentum, freedom for the Italian Greeks, and the return of what Rome had taken from the Samnites, Lucanians, Bruttians and Daunians (Appian, Samnite History 10). The Senate inclined to accept.

Appius Claudius Caecus, censor in 312 (module 3.4), old and blind, had his sons carry him into the Curia. He wished he were deaf as well as blind: Romans who had boasted that they would have beaten Alexander now trembled before Chaonians and Molossians, and there could be no talk of peace while Pyrrhus stood in Italy (Plutarch, Pyrrhus 18–19). Ennius put the speech into verse, and Cicero quotes two of Ennius' lines (On Old Age 16); Cicero also read a prose text of it (Brutus 61), the earliest Roman speech known to have circulated in writing. Cineas reported that the Senate seemed a council of many kings, and that fighting Rome was like fighting the Lernaean hydra (Plutarch, Pyrrhus 19). Appius' principle, no negotiation with an invader on Italian soil, became doctrine; the Senate's refusal to ransom the prisoners of Cannae is its sequel (module 4.3).

Where sources disagree. Plutarch and Appian place the embassy after Heraclea. Justin (18.2) narrates it after Asculum and the arrival of a Carthaginian fleet of 120 ships under Mago, which offered help at Ostia (Valerius Maximus 3.7.10) and belongs to 279/8. Modern accounts therefore either posit two embassies or move the decisive refusal later. The text Cicero read may also have been a later composition under Appius' name.

05Fabricius: gold, an elephant and a poisoner

Gaius Fabricius Luscinus, consul in 282 and 278 and censor in 275, became the war's Roman hero without beating Pyrrhus in battle. Sent to ransom the prisoners after Heraclea, he was offered enough gold to outdo any Roman in wealth; Dionysius gives him a speech on honourable poverty in reply (19.13–18). Plutarch adds an elephant trumpeting from behind a curtain, at which Fabricius smiled (Pyrrhus 20). Pyrrhus let the prisoners home for the Saturnalia on parole, and all came back.

In 278 the king's physician offered to poison him. The consuls Fabricius and Quintus Aemilius Papus warned Pyrrhus by letter, and he freed the prisoners in return (Plutarch, Pyrrhus 21). Cicero made the episode his proof that the honourable and the advantageous coincide (On Duties 3.86–87); Eutropius (2.14) has Pyrrhus say the sun could sooner be turned from its course than Fabricius from honour.

Where sources disagree. Gellius (3.8) shows the annalists at odds. Valerius Antias named the would-be poisoner Timochares of Ambracia, a friend of the king; Claudius Quadrigarius insisted it was Nicias and quoted the consuls' letter, in which Rome wishes to win by arms, not bribes or tricks. Plutarch's version, a letter from the king's physician, is closer to Quadrigarius'. The letter is rhetoric, not an archive document; that some offer was made is possible and unprovable.

The enemy was ennobled to make the virtue worth more. Ennius' Pyrrhus refuses ransom, proposing that both sides decide by iron, not gold, a sentiment Cicero calls worthy of the Aeacids (On Duties 1.38). The domestic half followed. As censor Fabricius expelled Publius Cornelius Rufinus, twice consul, from the Senate for owning ten pounds of silver plate (Gellius 4.8), and Manius Curius was found cooking turnips when he refused Samnite gold (Plutarch, Cato Maior 2). These portraits were fixed in the second century, as eastern wealth poured in and Livy's older senators could deplore a "new wisdom" of deceit (Livy 42.47; Greek module 7.3). Fabricius was the past the age of Cato needed, and Valerius Maximus filed him under abstinence and justice (4.3, 6.5; module 3.2).

06Sicily, Beneventum and elephants at Rome (278–275)

In 279/8 Rome and Carthage renewed their treaty with clauses against Pyrrhus (Polybius 3.25; module 4.1). Pyrrhus spent 278–276 in Sicily (Greek module 7.3) and, leaving, called it a wrestling ground for Carthaginians and Romans (Plutarch, Pyrrhus 23), the cue for module 4.2. On his way back he stripped Persephone's treasury at Locri; a storm wrecked the ships carrying it and he restored it in fear (Dionysius 20.9). In 204 the Locrians retold the story in the Senate against a Roman legate who had robbed the same temple (Livy 29.18), so a Greek tale of royal sacrilege became a charge against Romans.

In 275 Manius Curius Dentatus met Pyrrhus near Maleventum. A night march to surprise the Roman camp lost its way; at dawn Curius attacked, and the elephants were driven back through Pyrrhus' own lines (Plutarch, Pyrrhus 25). Dionysius (20.12) adds a wounded elephant calf that threw the beasts into confusion. A painted plate from Capena, now in the Villa Giulia, shows a war elephant with a tower followed by its calf, and is usually connected with the battle. When a colony was planted in 268 the ill-omened Maleventum became Beneventum (Pliny, Natural History 3.105). Curius' triumph showed Rome its first elephants (Pliny, Natural History 8.16), and his spoils paid for the Anio Vetus aqueduct (Frontinus, On Aqueducts 6).

Where sources disagree. Frontinus says Pyrrhus first enclosed a whole army in one camp and that the Romans learned the method from his camp captured near Maleventum (Stratagems 4.1.14); Livy's Hannibal agrees that Pyrrhus first taught camp-making (35.14.8). Plutarch's Pyrrhus instead admires the Roman camp at Heraclea (Pyrrhus 16), and Polybius describes the camp as a Roman institution (6.27–42). The Roman fortified camp was probably older; the story flatters Rome as a pupil of the best.

07Tarentum, Rhegium and the settlement of the south (272–264)

Pyrrhus left his officer Milo in the citadel of Tarentum. After the king's death at Argos in 272 Milo surrendered it to the consul Lucius Papirius Cursor for safe passage, and the city gave up its arms and ships and became an ally (Zonaras 8.6). A Carthaginian fleet appeared off the harbour at the same moment (Livy, Periochae 14; Orosius 4.3.1–2), later read as a breach of treaty (module 4.2). Locri, back on the Roman side, struck a silver stater showing a seated Roma crowned by Pistis, Good Faith, both named in Greek: among the earliest images of Roma, and a Greek city's statement of what the new relationship should be.

Rhegium was the scandal. It had asked for a Roman garrison, and a legion of Campanians under Decius Vibellius was sent. Imitating the Mamertines across the strait, they killed or expelled the men and took the women and property (Polybius 1.7.6–8; Appian, Samnite History 9). In 270 Rome retook the city. The prisoners were flogged and beheaded in the Forum, burial was refused, and Rhegium was returned to its citizens (Polybius 1.7.10–13; Valerius Maximus 2.7.15). Polybius notes that Rome, having so punished its own men, then helped the Mamertines who had done the same (1.10.4).

Where sources disagree. Polybius says more than 300 were taken alive and executed (1.7.12); Livy's Scipio, rebuking mutineers in 206, makes it 4,000 beheaded (28.28), a number that suits a deterrent speech. Polybius ties the garrison to Pyrrhus' crossing in 280 (1.7.6–7); Dionysius ties it to a consulship of Fabricius (20.4). The year is uncertain; Polybius is the soberer witness.

08What Greek Italy gave Rome before 240

Between Heraclea and the first Latin play Rome took the south's equipment as well as its cities.

Money. Rome's first silver coins were didrachms on the standard of Naples, inscribed ROMANO. The earliest, with a helmeted Mars and a horse's head, was probably struck at a southern Greek mint; its date is disputed, about 310–300 for Andrew Burnett ("The first Roman silver coins", Numismatica e Antichità Classiche 1978) and Historia Numorum: Italy (2001), 280–276 for Crawford (Roman Republican Coinage, 1974, no. 13). A later issue shows Hercules and the she-wolf suckling the twins, the foundation story's first appearance on a coin (module 1.4). One series of cast bronze bars carries an elephant on one face and a sow on the other, usually read as a memory of Pyrrhus' beasts and of the squealing pigs said to have routed them (Aelian, On Animals 1.38).

Where sources disagree. Pliny dates Roman silver to 269 and attaches it to the denarius (Natural History 33.44); Livy, Periochae 15 says the people first used silver that year. The excavations at Morgantina showed the denarius began about 211, and the didrachms are earlier than 269. Most numismatists connect Pliny's year with the Hercules and wolf issue (Crawford, Roman Republican Coinage, 1974, no. 20, dated 269–266), so that it marks a new phase, perhaps the first silver minted at Rome itself, rather than a beginning.

Cults and clocks. Ceres' Greek rites were served by priestesses chosen from Naples or Velia and given citizenship (Cicero, Pro Balbo 55). During a Samnite war Rome set up statues of Pythagoras and Alcibiades, wisest and bravest of the Greeks, in the Comitium at Apollo's command (Pliny, Natural History 34.26), the same prestige that made Numa a pupil of Pythagoras (module 1.5). In 263 Manius Valerius Messalla brought a sundial from Catana; it was set for Sicily, and Rome kept time by it for ninety-nine years (Pliny, Natural History 7.214).

Ships and letters. The crossing of 264 went in fifty-oared ships and triremes lent by Tarentum, Locri, Velia and Naples (Polybius 1.20.14), allies whose service was ships rather than soldiers. Livius Andronicus, a Greek from Tarentum, staged the first Latin plays in 240 (Cicero, Brutus 72); Accius' claim that he came as a captive in 209 is refuted there, and the usual link with 272 is inference. Ennius, born at Rudiae in Calabria in 239, wrote this war into Latin epic. Their work belongs to module 4.5.

09Achilles against Troy, and the Greek world looks west

Module 1.3 has Pyrrhus' framing of the war as Achilles' descendant against a colony of Trojans (Pausanias 1.12.1). His Italian coinage made it visible: a didrachm probably struck at Locri shows a helmeted Achilles and Thetis on a sea-horse bringing his shield. Rome kept the epic frame and reversed its outcome. Ennius gave Pyrrhus his ancestral name in the Delphic answer:

aio te, Aeacida, Romanos vincere posse (Cicero, On Divination 2.116)

The Latin means either that Pyrrhus can conquer the Romans or that the Romans can conquer him; Cicero objects that Apollo never spoke Latin. Virgil completed the reversal: a Roman will overthrow Argos, Mycenae and the Aeacid himself, usually taken as Perseus of Macedon, avenging his Trojan ancestors (Aeneid 6.836–840; deep dive module 12.5).

The war also made Rome a Greek subject. Pyrrhus dedicated spoils "from the Romans and their allies" to Zeus at Dodona (Syll.³ 392). Dionysius names Hieronymus as the first Greek to touch on Roman antiquities and Timaeus as the next, in his general history and in a separate work on the wars with Pyrrhus (1.6.1); Timaeus ended where Polybius begins, in 264 (Polybius 1.5.1). In 273 Ptolemy II sought friendship; the Roman envoys sent in return handed his gifts to the treasury and were allowed to keep them (Dionysius 20.14; Valerius Maximus 4.3.9). When young Romans struck envoys from Apollonia, the Senate surrendered them to that city (Valerius Maximus 6.6.5): the insult of 282, reversed for display.

Lycophron's Alexandra puts the new power into Cassandra's prophecy: Aeneas' descendants will take the sceptre and monarchy of land and sea (1226–1230), and a kinsman will later end the strife of Europe and Asia (1435–1450).

Where sources disagree. If the poet is Lycophron of Chalcis at Ptolemy II's court, these lines are Greek recognition of Rome in the 270s; Arnaldo Momigliano ("Terra marique", Journal of Roman Studies 1942) took the silence about the First Punic War as a sign of that date. Beloch and Simon Hornblower (edition, 2015) date the poem to about 190, and Hornblower, like Gruen, makes the kinsman Flamininus. On that view the Alexandra answers Cynoscephalae, not Pyrrhus.

10Self-test

  1. What happened to Lucius Postumius at Tarentum, and what work does the story do in the Roman tradition?
    Answer

    Received in the theatre, he was mocked for his Greek and his toga was soiled by Philonides; he promised it would be washed in blood (Dionysius 19.5; Appian, Samnite History 7). A violated envoy made the war just, and the setting contrasts Greek levity with Roman gravity, displacing the question of whether the Roman fleet broke the Lacinian treaty.

  2. Why does Plutarch give two sets of casualty figures for Heraclea, and what is the pattern?
    Answer

    He cites Dionysius (nearly 15,000 Roman and 13,000 Epirote dead) and Hieronymus (7,000 and under 4,000) side by side (Pyrrhus 17). The Roman-derived figures inflate both sides, making Pyrrhus' victories look ruinous.

  3. Why is the devotio of a third Decius at Asculum doubted?
    Answer

    Dionysius' full account omits it, and Dio and Zonaras (8.5) have Decius survive after Pyrrhus prepared against the rite; only vaguer notices such as Cicero's make him die. It looks like family tradition completing the deaths at Veseris and Sentinum.

  4. What did Appius Claudius Caecus argue, and why is his speech important to Latin literature?
    Answer

    That Rome must not negotiate while Pyrrhus remained in Italy (Plutarch, Pyrrhus 18–19). Cicero read a written text of it (Brutus 61), making it the earliest Roman speech known to have circulated in writing; Ennius versified it (Cicero, On Old Age 16).

  5. What did Cineas report about the Senate, and what chronological problem surrounds his embassy?
    Answer

    That it seemed a council of many kings, and Rome a hydra (Plutarch, Pyrrhus 19). Plutarch and Appian put the embassy after Heraclea; Justin narrates it after Asculum and the arrival of Mago's fleet at Ostia in 279/8, so some posit two embassies.

  6. How do Quadrigarius and Antias differ over the poisoning offer, and what did Cicero make of it?
    Answer

    Antias named Timochares of Ambracia; Quadrigarius named Nicias and quoted the consuls' letter (Gellius 3.8). Cicero used it to show that the honourable and the advantageous coincide (On Duties 3.86–87).

  7. Why did Roman writers ennoble Pyrrhus himself?
    Answer

    A chivalrous enemy made Roman virtue worth more: Ennius' Pyrrhus will decide by iron, not gold (Cicero, On Duties 1.38), and his praise of Fabricius and of the Roman dead (Eutropius 2.11, 2.14) turns battlefield defeats into moral victories.

  8. What did the Campanian legion do at Rhegium, and how was it punished?
    Answer

    Sent as a garrison, it killed or expelled the citizens and seized the city in imitation of the Mamertines. In 270 Rome retook Rhegium, flogged and beheaded the survivors in the Forum (more than 300 in Polybius 1.7.12, 4,000 in Livy 28.28), and restored the city to its citizens.

  9. What is wrong with Pliny's date for Roman silver, and what do the coins show?
    Answer

    Pliny (Natural History 33.44) puts silver and the denarius in 269, but the denarius dates to about 211 (Morgantina), and ROMANO didrachms on the Neapolitan standard are earlier than 269. His year probably marks the Hercules and wolf issue, a new phase rather than a beginning.

  10. How do Pyrrhus' Achilles coins and Ennius' oracle show Rome answering his claim?
    Answer

    Pyrrhus advertised Achilles and Thetis on his Italian silver; Ennius kept the epic frame but gave the Aeacid an ambiguous oracle that could predict his defeat (Cicero, On Divination 2.116), and Virgil turned the conquest of Greece into Troy's revenge (Aeneid 6.836–840).

11Carry forward

  1. The Pyrrhic war comes in two registers: battle accounts from Greek witnesses such as Hieronymus, and moral legends (Postumius, Appius, Fabricius, Curius) fixed by Roman annalists one to two centuries later. Read the first for events and the second for what second-century Romans wanted to have been.
  2. Rome's method in the south was to refuse terms while an invader stood in Italy, absorb defeats with manpower, and bind Greek cities by fides, ships and colonies, punishing breaches of faith even by its own soldiers; that is the posture it carried to Messana in 264.
  3. By 264 Rome had become a Greek subject and a Greek-equipped state: Greek coins, cults, clocks and ships at home, and Greek historians and poets abroad placing it in the Trojan scheme that Pyrrhus had tried to turn against it.
