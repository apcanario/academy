01Cato as Plutarch draws him

Marcus Porcius Cato (95–46 BC), great-grandson of the censor, is the one Roman of the Republic whom later Stoics treated as something close to a sage. The fullest portrait is Plutarch's Cato Minor, written about a century and a half after his death; modules 5.3 and 5.4 tell his politics, and this module keeps to the philosophy he was taken to embody.

Plutarch gives him two Stoic teachers. As a young man he attached himself to Antipater of Tyre and gave himself above all to the ethical and political doctrines (Cato Minor 4); Cicero mentions Antipater's recent death at Athens in a work of 44 (On Duties 2.86). The second was Athenodorus Cordylion, whom Cato, on leave from his military tribunate, fetched from Pergamum to live in his house (Cato Minor 10; module 13.1 tells the capture). Plutarch also shows the training. When over-bright purple was the fashion, Cato wore the dark; he went out after meals without shoes or tunic, not for notoriety but to accustom himself to be ashamed only of what deserved shame (6). The Stoic indifferents, practised in public, became a style.

The style carried into office. As quaestor he mastered the treasury's books and prosecuted the clerks who had run it (16–18); in the Senate he spoke against Catiline's associates in December 63, and Sallust gives him the speech (module 5.6). Cicero mocked his paradoxes before a jury the month before (Pro Murena 61–66), and module 13.2 tells that scene. The end, at Utica in April 46, is module 5.4's: the sword, the Phaedo read twice, the bandaged wound torn open (Cato Minor 66–70). Two details belong here. At his last dinner the talk turned to the Stoic paradox that only the good man is free and every bad man a slave, and Cato argued it against the Peripatetic, Demetrius (65) with a vehemence that told his companions what he meant to do (67). And he kept the Stoic Apollonides and the Peripatetic Demetrius with him to the last night (65, 70): the philosophers were his witnesses.

Where sources disagree. Cicero in On Duties 1.112 judged that Cato, given his unbending nature, had to die rather than look on the tyrant, and Seneca made the death the spectacle God himself admires (On Providence 2.9–12). Augustine answered that Cato's friends, learned men, thought the act weakness rather than strength, a refusal to bear Caesar's glory, and noted that he urged his son to trust Caesar's clemency (City of God 1.23). Whether Cato died as a Stoic or as a defeated aristocrat was argued from the start.

02The pamphlet war: Cicero's Cato and Caesar's Anticato

Cato dead was a text to be fought over, and the fighting began within months. Modules 5.4 and 5.6 name the two works in a sentence each; here is the exchange.

Cicero wrote a Cato in 46. He knew the danger: to praise the man honestly was to praise his politics, and that, under the victor, was what Cicero called an Archimedean problem (Letters to Atticus 12.4.2). Brutus wrote a Cato of his own, which Cicero thought gave Cato too much of the credit for the Senate's decision of 5 December 63 (12.21.1). Caesar's officer Hirtius circulated a hostile pamphlet first, which Cicero read as a sample of what Caesar's own attack would be (12.40.1). Then, from Spain in 45, came Caesar's Anticato, in two books by Juvenal's count (6.338) and lost like its target (Suetonius, Julius 56.5).

Plutarch frames the pair: Cicero wrote an encomium titled Cato, Caesar answered with the Anticato, and Cicero's was eagerly read as the ablest orator's work on the noblest theme, and both found eager readers, for Caesar's sake and for Cato's (Caesar 54). Caesar was careful with Cicero, praising his style and life and likening him to Pericles and Theramenes (Cicero 39), and he told a correspondent, Cicero reports, that reading Cicero's Cato made him more fluent, while reading Brutus' made him think himself eloquent (Letters to Atticus 13.46.2). Against Cato he went low. Plutarch preserves the charges in order to answer them: that Cato sifted the ashes of his brother Caepio's pyre for melted gold (Cato Minor 11), and that he gave his wife Marcia to the orator Hortensius and took her back, now a rich widow, as a speculation in marriage (52). Pliny the Younger recalls the charge of drunkenness and its unintended compliment: passers-by who uncovered the drunken Cato's face blushed, as if they had been caught by Cato and not he by them (Letters 3.12.3).

The war decided the shape of the legend. Caesar's attack, being personal, could be rebutted anecdote by anecdote; Cicero's praise, being general, could be reused by anyone. Under the emperors the Cato genre persisted while the Anticato survived only in Plutarch's refutations and Pliny's anecdote.

03Seneca's Cato and Lucan's

Seneca's life and death are deep dive module 13.5's; his Cato is this module's. For Seneca, Cato is the proof that the sage is not a fiction. In On the Constancy of the Wise Man he offers Cato as a surer example than Ulysses or Hercules, and closes the case with the line that Cato did not outlive liberty, nor liberty Cato (2.2). In On Providence the gods watch Cato standing upright among the ruins of the Republic, and nothing on earth is a finer sight for Jupiter (2.9–12). In the Letters Cato is the companion of daily practice: choose a Cato and live as if he watched you, or, if he seems too stern, a gentler man such as Laelius (11.10); read how he spent his last night with Plato's book and a sword under his pillow (24.6–8); remember that he lost elections and bore it (71). Seneca's Cato is a Stoic first and a republican second. The politics are almost drained away, leaving fortitude, the free exit and an internal audience.

Lucan, Seneca's nephew, restored the politics. Module 10.2 takes the Civil War as a poem and gives its famous line, that the winning cause pleased the gods and the losing one Cato (1.128). His Cato occupies two books. In book 2 Brutus comes by night to ask whether a good man should take a side; Cato answers that he will follow the Republic as a father follows his son's corpse, and offers himself as a victim whose blood might pay for Rome's (2.234–325). He then remarries Marcia without ceremony or feast (2.326–391), and Lucan sums up the sect of unbending Cato: to keep the mean, to hold to the end, to follow nature, to spend his life for his country and to believe himself born not for himself but for the whole world (2.380–383). In book 9, after Pompey's death, Cato leads the remnant through the Libyan desert, refuses to drink before his men, endures the serpents, and declines to consult the oracle of Jupiter Ammon, since god's seat is earth, sea, air, sky and virtue, and Iuppiter est quodcumque vides, quocumque moveris (9.580): Jupiter is whatever you see and wherever you move. The narrator then calls him the true father of his country, who will be a god if Rome is ever free (9.601–604).

Where sources disagree. Frederick Ahl (Lucan: An Introduction, 1976) read Lucan's Cato as the poem's moral centre and its one true hero. Matthew Leigh (Lucan: Spectacle and Engagement, 1997) read the desert march of book 9 as a flawed Stoic allegory that exposes the impotence of philosophy in the face of civil war. Others hold that the portrait is deliberately unresolved; the poem breaks off before Utica, so Lucan never had to show the death.

04Thrasea Paetus: the life of Cato and the death in 66

Publius Clodius Thrasea Paetus, a senator from Patavium and suffect consul in 56, is the hinge between Cato and the imperial martyrs. He wrote a Life of Cato, now lost, which Plutarch used: Plutarch cites Thrasea, drawing on Cato's companion Munatius, for the Marcia affair (Cato Minor 25), and says that Munatius published a book about Cato which Thrasea chiefly followed (37). Juvenal pictures Thrasea and his son-in-law Helvidius drinking, garlanded, on the birthdays of Brutus and Cassius (5.36–37). The cult of the Republic's dead was a household practice before it was a charge.

His public career, as Tacitus tells it, was a sequence of small refusals. Module 7.1 gives the first: when Nero's letter accused Agrippina in 59, Thrasea walked out of the Senate, making danger for himself without giving the others a start towards liberty (Annals 14.12.1). In 62, when the praetor Antistius Sosianus was accused of treason for satirical verses, Thrasea carried the Senate to exile instead of the death the consul designate proposed (14.48–49). He then stayed away: by 66 his accuser Cossutianus Capito could list that he had not attended the Senate for three years, had skipped the vows for the emperor's safety, and never sacrificed for his heavenly voice (16.22).

Capito's speech is the fullest ancient statement of the "Stoic opposition" as an idea; Tigellinus had sketched it against Rubellius Plautus in 62 (Annals 14.57). The city, eager for discord, talks of Nero and Thrasea as once of Caesar and Cato; Thrasea has a following, men who copy his stern looks; the provinces and armies read the daily gazette to see what Thrasea has not done; and his sect produced the Tuberones and Favonii, names hateful even to the old Republic (16.22). The Cato comparison was the prosecution's, not Thrasea's own claim.

Tacitus frames the trial as Nero's wish to destroy virtue itself by killing Thrasea and Barea Soranus (16.21.1). Thrasea took counsel whether to defend himself; the young tribune Arulenus Rusticus offered to veto the decree, and Thrasea stopped him, saying a man starting his career should think what path to take in such times (16.26). The Senate met under armed guard, and Eprius Marcellus made the speech for death (16.27–29). Thrasea was given the choice of his death; Helvidius Priscus and Paconius Agrippinus were banished from Italy (16.33). When the quaestor came, Thrasea was in his garden with distinguished men and women, listening to the Cynic Demetrius (module 13.3) on the nature of the soul and its parting from the body (16.34). He told his wife Arria not to follow him but to live for their daughter, had the veins of both arms cut, and, sprinkling the blood, said Libamus Iovi liberatori (16.35.1): we pour a libation to Jupiter the Liberator. Then to the quaestor: look, young man, you were born into times when it helps to steady the mind with examples of constancy. The Annals break off a few lines later, as he turns to Demetrius. The scene is written as the Roman Phaedo: a philosopher, a talk on the soul, a libation. Seneca had staged the same model a year earlier, in 65 (15.60–64; deep dive module 13.5), and Lucan had died reciting his own verse (15.70; module 7.1).

05Arria and the women of the house

Thrasea's wife Arria was the daughter of a more famous Arria, and Pliny the Younger, who heard the stories from her granddaughter Fannia (Letters 3.16.2), set them down. In 42 the elder Arria's husband, Caecina Paetus, was condemned for his part in Scribonianus' revolt against Claudius. When he hesitated to die, Dio says (60.16), she stabbed herself, drew out the dagger and handed it to him with the words Paete, non dolet (3.16.6): Paetus, it does not hurt. Pliny's point is that this famous act was not her greatest. She had hidden their son's death from her sick husband, bathing her eyes and returning composed to his room (3.16.3–5); when Thrasea, trying to dissuade her, asked whether she would want her daughter to die with him if he had to die, she answered yes, if her daughter had lived with him as long and as harmoniously as she with Paetus (3.16.10). Martial turned the dagger scene into an epigram (1.13).

The line continued through the women. The younger Arria, urged by Thrasea not to die with him in 66, was exiled in 93 (Letters 3.11.3); her daughter Fannia, Helvidius' wife, followed her husband into exile twice and was banished a third time herself on his account (7.19.4). Charged in 93 with supplying her husband's journals to Herennius Senecio for his life of Helvidius, she admitted it, denied that her mother knew, and kept copies of the books the Senate had ordered burned (Letters 7.19.4–6). In this family the exemplary death was an inheritance, handed down as anecdote, and the women carried the memory that the men died for.

06Helvidius Priscus against Vespasian

Gaius Helvidius Priscus, Thrasea's son-in-law, is the most openly political of the group. Tacitus gives him a philosophical portrait. He turned to philosophy young, not to cloak idleness with a great name but to enter public life stronger against chance; he followed teachers who held that only the honourable is good and only the base evil, and that power, birth and everything outside the mind are neither; from Thrasea he took above all freedom of speech; in every role of life, as citizen, senator, husband, son-in-law and friend, he was consistent. Some thought him too eager for fame, since even for the wise the desire for glory is the last thing to be shed (Histories 4.5–6). Banished in 66, he returned under Galba and as praetor in 70 attacked Eprius Marcellus, the prosecutor of Thrasea (4.6, 4.43); module 7.2 tells the politics of Vespasian's reign (69–79).

Suetonius gives the breach with Vespasian. Helvidius alone greeted the returning emperor by his private name, and as praetor left him without honour in all his edicts; Vespasian was not angry until Helvidius all but reduced him to the rank of a private citizen. He was relegated and then ordered killed; Vespasian tried to recall the order and would have saved him had he not been falsely told that Helvidius was already dead (Vespasian 15). Dio's epitome has Helvidius abuse the emperor and praise popular government, and Vespasian leave the Senate in tears, saying his son would succeed him or no one would (Dio 65(66).12). The year of his death is not given; modern accounts place it in the mid-70s.

Epictetus kept the exchange that made Helvidius a Stoic example. Vespasian sent word that he should not attend the Senate. It is in your power, Helvidius answered, not to let me be a senator, but while I am one I must attend. Then attend, but say nothing. Do not ask my opinion and I shall be silent. But I must ask it. And I must say what seems right. If you speak, I shall kill you. When did I tell you I was immortal? You will do your part and I mine: yours to kill, mine to die without trembling; yours to banish, mine to go without grief (Discourses 1.2.19–21). Epictetus adds that one such man does good to others as an example (1.2.22–24). Module 13.6 sets the passage within his doctrine of roles; here it is the purest statement of the opposition's philosophy, which demanded nothing of the emperor except that the senator's part be left to the senator.

Around the same affair Vespasian expelled the philosophers. Dio's epitome says that Mucianus, Vespasian's chief minister, spoke at length against the Stoics: full of empty boasting, they claim that anyone with a long beard, raised eyebrows, a rough cloak and bare feet is wise, and they despise everyone else. Vespasian then drove all the philosophers from Rome except Musonius, and sent the Cynic Demetrius and Hostilianus to islands; when Demetrius went on abusing him, he sent word that he would not kill a barking dog (65(66).13). Module 13.3 gives Suetonius' version (Vespasian 13). Modern dates for the expulsion vary, from 71 into the mid-70s. Musonius' own exiles are module 13.6's.

07Domitian: the praise of Thrasea and Helvidius as a capital charge

The last act, under Domitian (81–96), was a war about texts. Tacitus opens the Agricola with it: when Arulenus Rusticus praised Thrasea and Herennius Senecio praised Helvidius, it was a capital crime, and savagery was turned not only on the authors but on their books, which officials were commissioned to burn in the Comitium and the Forum, as if the fire could destroy the voice of the Roman people; the teachers of wisdom were expelled besides and every good art driven into exile (Agricola 2.1–2). Suetonius says Domitian killed Rusticus for publishing praises of Thrasea and Helvidius and calling them most holy men, and on that occasion removed all philosophers from Rome and Italy; Helvidius' son died too, for a farce thought to glance at the emperor's divorce (Domitian 10.4). Dio adds that Senecio was charged with having sought no office after the quaestorship and with writing the life of Helvidius (67.13).

The year was 93, after Agricola's death in August. Tacitus confesses the Senate's share: our hands led Helvidius to prison, and Senecio drenched us with innocent blood (Agricola 45.1). Pliny, who says he was praetor at the time, counts seven friends killed or banished, Senecio, Rusticus and Helvidius killed, Mauricus, Gratilla, Arria and Fannia exiled, and says he visited the philosopher Artemidorus outside the city when the philosophers were expelled (Letters 3.11.2–3). Epictetus left Rome for Nicopolis because of a senatorial decree under Domitian (Gellius 15.11), which some editors date to 89 rather than 93; module 13.6 follows him. Within thirty years of Thrasea's death, writing his life was itself what got men killed.

Where sources disagree. The sources do not agree on the shape of the Domitianic expulsion. Tacitus ties it to the burning of the books and speaks of "teachers of wisdom" (Agricola 2.2); Suetonius ties it to Rusticus' death and extends it to Italy (Domitian 10.3); Gellius makes it a decree of the Senate barring them from Rome and Italy (15.11); Pliny speaks only of the city (Letters 3.11.2). Whether one measure lies behind all four accounts, or there were stages, is uncertain.

08Was there a Stoic opposition?

The emperors' agents said so. Tigellinus warned Nero in 62 that Rubellius Plautus had taken on the arrogance of the Stoics, a sect that makes men turbulent and hungry for affairs (Annals 14.57); Capito's speech of 66 built a party out of Thrasea's following; Mucianus' speech gave the same case under Vespasian. The victims and their friends answered that philosophy was loyal. Seneca insists that those who think philosophers stubborn and contemptuous of magistrates and kings are wrong, since no one is more grateful to the ruler who gives them leisure (Letters 73.1). Tacitus, who had served Domitian, drew another line: there can be great men even under bad emperors, and obedience and restraint joined to energy earn more praise than the steep path by which many won fame through an ostentatious death of no use to the state (Agricola 42.4). Withdrawal itself was read both ways: Seneca could argue that a Stoic out of office still serves the greater commonwealth (module 13.2), while Capito read Thrasea's three years away from the Senate as secession. Marcus Aurelius thanked the Severus he calls his brother for showing him Thrasea, Helvidius, Cato, Dion and Brutus, and with them the idea of a monarchy that values the freedom of the governed above all (Meditations 1.14): by the 170s the martyrs were the emperor's own models.

Modern work divides on what this adds up to. One question is doctrine: Stoic theory had no objection to kingship as such, and the sage was the true king. A second is motive: Thrasea and Helvidius defended the Senate's dignity and free speech, the values of Cato's class rather than of Zeno's school. A third is membership: many of the condemned, Soranus, Senecio, Rusticus, were charged as friends and writers more than as philosophers, and Seneca, the most prolific Stoic of the age, served Nero for years.

Where sources disagree. Ramsay MacMullen (Enemies of the Roman Order, 1966) placed the philosophers among the empire's internal enemies, in a chapter that follows one titled "Cato, Brutus, and Their Succession" and precedes others on magicians and astrologers, treating dissent as a social fact with Stoic and Cynic teachers among its carriers. P. A. Brunt ("Stoicism and the Principate", Papers of the British School at Rome 1975) argued that Stoicism was not hostile to monarchy and that what these senators defended were traditional senatorial values; the school supplied constancy in the face of death, and its doctrine of personae explained why a Thrasea and a Seneca might rightly choose differently. The ancient sources split the same way: Tigellinus and Capito saw a sect (Annals 14.57, 16.22), Seneca and Tacitus denied that philosophy or virtue need mean revolt (Letters 73.1; Agricola 42.4).

What can be said without taking a side is that the group had a canon and a liturgy. The canon ran from Cato through Thrasea's Life to the lives by Rusticus and Senecio; the liturgy was the household birthday, the anecdote told by grandmother to granddaughter, and the death performed before witnesses with a philosopher present. Its programme was abstention more than conspiracy: none of these men is known to have plotted against an emperor. Their weapon was the example, and the emperors, by burning the books, conceded that it worked.

09Self-test

  1. Who were Cato's two Stoic teachers according to Plutarch, and what does Plutarch say Cato studied with the first?
    Answer

    Antipater of Tyre, with whom he gave himself above all to ethical and political doctrine (Cato Minor 4), and Athenodorus Cordylion, whom he brought from Pergamum to live in his house (10).

  2. Why did Cicero call writing a Cato in 46 an Archimedean problem?
    Answer

    Honest praise of Cato meant praising his politics, which was dangerous under the victorious Caesar; Cicero had to celebrate the man without provoking the dictator (Letters to Atticus 12.4.2).

  3. What charges did Caesar's Anticato bring, and how do we know them?
    Answer

    Avarice (sifting his brother's ashes for gold, Cato Minor 11; trading Marcia to Hortensius and taking her back rich, 52) and drunkenness (Pliny, Letters 3.12.3). The work is lost; Plutarch preserves the charges in order to refute them.

  4. How does Seneca's Cato differ from Lucan's?
    Answer

    Seneca's is a Stoic sage first, a model of constancy and the free exit, watched by the gods and imagined as a daily witness (On Providence 2.9–12; Letters 11.10), with the politics drained away. Lucan's restores the Republic: he joins the civil war as a father follows his son's corpse and leads the remnant through Libya (books 2 and 9).

  5. What evidence links Thrasea Paetus to Cato in writing and in practice?
    Answer

    He wrote a Life of Cato, based on Munatius, which Plutarch cites and says Thrasea chiefly followed (Cato Minor 25, 37); Juvenal shows him and Helvidius celebrating the birthdays of Brutus and Cassius (5.36–37); and his accuser Capito compared Nero and Thrasea to Caesar and Cato (Annals 16.22).

  6. In what ways is Tacitus' account of Thrasea's death shaped as a Roman Phaedo?
    Answer

    He receives the sentence in his garden among friends, listening to the Cynic Demetrius on the soul's parting from the body, pours his blood as a libation to Jupiter the Liberator, and offers the quaestor his death as an example of constancy (Annals 16.34–35), the elements of Socrates' last day.

  7. Why does Pliny say that "Paete, non dolet" was not the elder Arria's greatest act?
    Answer

    Because less famous acts showed more endurance: hiding her son's death from her sick husband and returning composed to his room (Letters 3.16.3–5). The famous words, said as she handed Paetus the dagger in 42 (3.16.6), had glory to sustain them.

  8. What did Helvidius Priscus tell Vespasian when forbidden to speak in the Senate, and what principle does it state?
    Answer

    That the emperor could remove him from the Senate but while a senator he must attend and say what seemed right; the emperor's part was to kill, his to die without trembling (Epictetus, Discourses 1.2.19–21). Each keeps to his own role; the senator demands only that his be left to him.

  9. How did Domitian's victims of 93 differ from Thrasea and Helvidius in what they were killed for?
    Answer

    Rusticus and Senecio were killed for writing praise of Thrasea and Helvidius, and their books were burned (Agricola 2.1; Suetonius, Domitian 10.3; Dio 67.13): the charge was commemoration, not conduct in the Senate.

  10. State the two sides of the debate over a "Stoic opposition" and one ancient witness for each.
    Answer

    MacMullen (1966) treated philosophers as real carriers of disaffection, as Tigellinus did in calling Stoicism a sect that makes men turbulent (Annals 14.57); Brunt (1975) argued that Stoicism accepted monarchy and that the senators defended traditional senatorial values, much as Seneca denied that philosophers despise rulers (Letters 73.1).

10Carry forward

  1. Cato became a text before he became a model: in the pamphlet war of 46–45 Cicero praised the man and with him his cause, and Caesar attacked his private life; later Seneca drained off the politics to leave a sage of constancy, Lucan restored them, and every later use chose between those Catos.
  2. The Stoic senators' programme was abstention and example, not conspiracy: walking out, staying away, saying what seemed right and dying well before witnesses. Emperors who burned the books about them conceded that example was a political act.
  3. Whether a "Stoic opposition" existed depends on separating doctrine from class: Stoic theory accepted kingship, while the men it steadied defended the Senate's freedom of speech. Marcus Aurelius could revere Thrasea and Helvidius because the two were separable.
