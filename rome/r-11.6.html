01Commentarii: a genre that claims not to be history

A commentarius was a working record: the pontiffs and augurs kept commentarii of their rulings, magistrates kept them of their acts, and a governor's dispatches to the Senate were drawn from them. As a literary form it sat below history. History (historia) was a work of art, with speeches, digressions, moral judgement and the periodic rhythm Cicero demanded of it; a commentarius was the raw material a historian would later work up, the Latin equivalent of the Greek hypomnema. Cicero himself wrote a commentarius of his consulship in Greek and sent it to the Stoic Posidonius for elaboration (Letters to Atticus 2.1.1–2; deep dive module 13.1 tells his reply). Sulla had written his memoirs in some twenty-two books, still at work two days before he died (Plutarch, Sulla 37). The form was the statesman's apology, and everyone knew it.

Caesar's innovation was to make the subordinate genre do the work of the superior one while keeping the modest name. The seven books of the Gallic War and the three of the Civil War have no preface, no statement of method, no dedication. The first sentence of the Gallic War is geography: "Gaul as a whole is divided into three parts" (1.1.1). The first sentence of the Civil War is a letter from Caesar being delivered to the consuls (1.1.1). There is no narrator who says "I".

The third person is the decisive choice. Xenophon had used it in the Anabasis, and in the Hellenica refers readers to an account of the march by one Themistogenes of Syracuse (3.1.2), a name ancient readers took for his own disguise. In Caesar the effect is that "Caesar" becomes a character observed from outside, whose decisions are reported, explained and seldom questioned, while the narrator stands nowhere. The army is "our men" (nostri), so the reader is placed in the Roman ranks; the author's own interventions come in the first person plural of the report (ut supra demonstravimus, "as we showed above"). Speeches are mostly in indirect speech, condensed into the arguments a reasonable man would weigh. Direct speech is rare and placed for effect: the standard-bearer of the Tenth leaping into the British surf (4.25.3), or the Arvernian Critognatus at Alesia urging his starving comrades to eat the bodies of those useless for war, a speech Caesar says he gives for its singular and abominable cruelty (7.77).

The rhetoric lies in the absence of rhetoric. Motives are stated as facts ("Caesar, thinking that ...", "since he knew that ..."), so the reader receives Caesar's reasons as the situation's reasons. Opponents break faith, attack envoys, conspire, rebel; Caesar responds. Each year has a cause, and the cause is almost always someone else's.

02Writing, publication and Hirtius' eighth book

The Gallic War covers 58 to 52 BC in seven books, one a year. It stops after Alesia with the thanksgiving of twenty days (7.90). The eighth book, on 51 and 50, is by Aulus Hirtius, Caesar's officer and consul in 43. His preface, addressed to Lucius Cornelius Balbus, says that Balbus' insistence had made him undertake a task he had often declined: to join Caesar's commentaries on Gaul to the ones that followed, since the earlier and the later did not connect, and to finish the last, uncompleted one from the events at Alexandria "to the end, not of civil discord, of which we see no end, but of Caesar's life" (8, preface 2). The preface was therefore written after the Ides of March 44 and before Hirtius died at Mutina in April 43. It also gives the earliest testimony to how Caesar wrote: others know how well and how faultlessly he finished the books, "we know also how easily and quickly" (8, preface 6).

The Civil War, three books on 49 and 48, breaks off at Alexandria with the first moves of the Alexandrian war (3.112). The continuations, the Alexandrian War, the African War and the Spanish War, were written by officers. Suetonius reports that some gave them to Hirtius and others to Gaius Oppius (Julius 56.1); the Latin of the Spanish War, rough and full of soldiers' idiom, shows at least that one hand was not Caesar's or Hirtius'. Whether Caesar released the Civil War in his lifetime is unknown; Hirtius' word "uncompleted" suggests he did not.

Where sources disagree. Hirtius' "easily and quickly" was long read, by Matthias Gelzer (Caesar: Politician and Statesman, 1968) among others, as evidence that Caesar wrote all seven books at once, in the winter of 52 to 51, and published them as a block to support his candidacy for a second consulship. T. P. Wiseman ("The publication of De Bello Gallico", in Welch and Powell (eds.), Julius Caesar as Artful Reporter, 1998) argued instead that the books were composed and released year by year, and read aloud to wide audiences, pointing to inconsistencies a single revision would have removed: the Nervii, all but wiped out in 57 (2.28), are back in the field in strength in 54 (5.39–49). Annual publication would make each book a dispatch to the Roman people timed for the politics of that winter; publication in 51 would make the whole a single campaign document. Both readings fit Hirtius' sentence, and the question is open.

03Gauls, Germans and Britons: the ethnography and its purposes

Caesar's Gaul is his own construction. The opening division into Belgae, Aquitani and Celts, with the Belgae bravest because furthest from the province's refinement and its traders (1.1.1–3), turns a patchwork of some dozens of peoples, many already bound to Rome as allies, into a single country waiting to be conquered. The principal ethnography comes in book 6 (6.11–28), placed in 53 at the point where Caesar had crossed the Rhine a second time and achieved nothing.

Gauls (6.11–20). Every people, every canton and almost every household is split into factions, each with a leader to whom the weaker attach themselves for protection (6.11). Two classes count: the Druids and the knights; the common people are treated almost as slaves (6.13). The Druids conduct sacrifices, teach, and judge disputes, meeting each year in the territory of the Carnutes, held to be the centre of Gaul; their teaching is thought to have come from Britain (6.13). They commit nothing of it to writing, though they use Greek letters for other business, and teach that souls pass after death from one body to another, which removes the fear of death (6.14). Some peoples burn living men in huge wicker figures (6.16). The Gauls' gods are given Roman names, Mercury first (6.17; their Roman names are module 2.2). They claim descent from Dis Pater and so count time by nights (6.18); husbands have power of life and death over wives and children (6.19).

Germans (6.21–28). They have no Druids and little sacrifice, and count as gods only what they can see and use, the Sun, Vulcan and the Moon (6.21). No one owns land: magistrates assign it each year and move the holders on, so that no one grows attached to farming or to wealth (6.22). Peoples take pride in wastelands round their borders, and raiding outside them is no disgrace (6.23). Once the Gauls had been the stronger and sent colonies across the Rhine (6.24). Beyond lies the Hercynian forest, nine days' journey wide, with an ox bearing one horn and elks without joints that sleep leaning on trees (6.25–28), a passage many editors have suspected as an interpolation.

Britons (5.12–14). The interior people say they are native to the island, the coastal people came from Belgic Gaul; they use bronze or gold coins or iron bars of fixed weight, will not eat hare, hen or goose, dye themselves blue with woad, and hold wives in common among groups of ten or twelve men (5.12–14).

Much of the material is older than Caesar. Posidonius had travelled in southern Gaul a generation earlier, and his observations survive in Diodorus (5.25–32) and Strabo (4.4.5), who cites him on heads nailed to doorways. What is Caesar's is the arrangement. The Gauls have a priesthood, councils, magistrates, a centre and a law: a society with institutions, which Rome can take over. The Germans have none of these, and no fixed fields. The ethnography ends and the narrative resumes with Caesar withdrawing across the Rhine because, as he has just explained, the Germans do not farm and he feared a shortage of grain (6.29). The description is the argument for stopping at the river.

Where sources disagree. Caesar makes the Rhine the line between Gauls and Germans (1.1, 6.11–24), yet he also reports that most of the Belgae descended from Germans who had crossed the Rhine (2.4) and names peoples west of it as Germani (6.32). Tacitus cites "the deified Julius, the weightiest of authorities" for the Gauls once having been stronger than the Germans (Germania 28), and so takes Caesar's picture as fact. Andrew Riggsby (Caesar in Gaul and Rome: War in Words, 2006) reads the boundary as a construction: Gauls drawn as people who could become Roman, Germans as people who could not. Archaeology finds no sharp cultural line on the Rhine in the first century BC; how far Caesar invented the division and how far he sharpened one he found is uncertain.

Three patterns run through the table. First, the war is always defensive or retributive: the province is threatened, allies are wronged, envoys are seized, treaties broken. Caesar never says that a proconsul with ten years of command, debts and rivals at home needed a war. Second, the conquest is announced before it happens. At the end of 57 Caesar writes that all Gaul was pacified (2.35.1); the revolts of 54 to 52 then have to be presented as rebellion. Third, the moments Rome would most admire, the Rhine bridge built in ten days (4.17–18), the crossings to Britain, the shield seized at the Sambre, are told at length, while setbacks are explained. The destruction of fifteen cohorts at Atuatuca in 54 is laid at the door of the legate Sabinus, who ignored his colleague Cotta's advice and trusted Ambiorix (5.26–37). The repulse at Gergovia in 52, with almost 700 soldiers and 46 centurions lost by Caesar's count (7.51.1, 7.51.4), is blamed on soldiers who did not hear the recall and pursued too far (7.47–52).

Gergovia is the one defeat of Caesar's own in seven books, and he turns it into a lesson: he rebuked the men for their eagerness and praised their courage (7.52). Cicero's letters show that Rome had other sources. His brother Quintus served as a legate, and Cicero heard from Britain that there was no silver on the island and no hope of booty except slaves (Letters to Atticus 4.16.7). Caesar's account had to survive comparison with officers' letters home, which limited what he could invent and shaped what he chose to stress.

05Alesia, 52: the siege and the spade

After Gergovia the Aedui, Rome's oldest allies in Gaul, joined the revolt, and a council at Bibracte confirmed Vercingetorix as supreme commander (7.63). Beaten in a cavalry battle, he withdrew with 80,000 picked men, by his own reckoning as Caesar reports it (7.71.3), into Alesia, the town of the Mandubii on a hill between two rivers. Caesar enclosed it. An inner line, the contravallation, ran round a circuit of eleven miles with twenty-three forts (7.69); then an outer line of fourteen miles, the circumvallation, faced outwards against the relief army Vercingetorix had sent his cavalry to raise (7.74). Caesar describes the works in detail: a trench twenty feet wide with vertical sides, a rampart and palisade of twelve feet with towers every eighty feet, and in front of them rows of obstacles the soldiers named: cippi, sharpened branches set in trenches; lilia, "lilies", pits with a fire-hardened stake in each; and stimuli, iron hooks fixed in blocks of wood (7.72–73).

Inside, food ran short. The Mandubii, with their wives and children, were expelled to save rations; Caesar placed guards on the rampart and refused to receive them, even as slaves (7.78). He does not say what became of them; Dio says they died between the lines (40.40). The relief army arrived, Caesar says 250,000 infantry and 8,000 cavalry, 258,000 in all (7.76.3), and attacked three times. The last assault fell on a camp on a northern hill that the lines could not take in and that lay on an awkward slope (7.83), usually identified with Mont Réa. Caesar, recognisable by the colour of his cloak, led the counter-attack, the cavalry took the Gauls in the rear, and seventy-four standards were brought to him (7.88). The next day Vercingetorix surrendered (7.89). Each soldier received one captive as booty, except for the Aedui and Arverni, whom Caesar kept to win back their peoples (7.89.5).

The site became a nineteenth-century battlefield of its own. In 1855 the architect Alphonse Delacroix placed Alesia at Alaise, south of Besançon, against the tradition that put it at Alise-Sainte-Reine on Mont-Auxois in Burgundy; scholars divided into camps. Napoleon III, then writing his History of Julius Caesar, settled the matter by spade. The Commission de topographie des Gaules excavated at Alise from 1861, and from 1862 to 1865 his ordnance officer Eugène Stoffel directed the work, tracing the ditches of both lines and recovering quantities of weapons. A Gaulish inscription found at Alise names the place as Alisiia. In the 1960s André Berthier proposed Chaux-des-Crotenay in the Jura, on the ground that its terrain fitted Caesar's description better; excavation has not supported him.

Aerial photography, René Goguey's above all, showed the ditches as crop marks, and Franco-German excavations directed by Michel Reddé and Siegmar von Schnurbein between 1991 and 1997 (Alésia: fouilles et recherches franco-allemandes sur les travaux militaires romains autour du Mont-Auxois, 2001) confirmed the double line and found the pits and obstacles of the kind Caesar names. They also found that the works varied from sector to sector with the ground and the time available, rather than repeating the single uniform scheme of 7.72–73. Caesar's description is accurate in kind and idealised in detail: a model of the system, offered to a reader who would never see the hill.

Where sources disagree. Delacroix (1855) and later Berthier argued that the ground at Mont-Auxois does not fit Caesar's description of the town and its setting, and Berthier's supporters still contest the identification. Napoleon III's commission and Stoffel, and then Reddé and von Schnurbein (2001), set against them the excavated lines, the weapons in the ditches and the inscription naming Alisiia. On the location the archaeology has decided for Alise; on the lines, it shows a text that compresses many local solutions into one ideal pattern, and the debate is now over how much Caesar simplified, not whether he was there.

06Counting the dead, and the charge of genocide

Module 5.4 sets out the figures: Caesar's for each campaign and the later totals of Velleius, Plutarch, Appian and Pliny. Weighing them starts with a question the text never answers: who counted, and how?

Caesar's most exact number is his first. In the Helvetian camp, he says, tablets were found written in Greek letters, listing those who had set out: 263,000 Helvetii, 36,000 Tulingi, 14,000 Latobrigi, 23,000 Rauraci and 32,000 Boii, 368,000 in all, of whom 92,000 could bear arms; a census on their return counted 110,000 (1.29). The precision is the persuasion. Yet 92,000 is exactly a quarter of 368,000, a suspiciously tidy ratio for a migrating people, and no one but Caesar saw the tablets. Plutarch, writing a century and a half later, gives 300,000, of whom 190,000 were fighting men (Caesar 18): a different total and a different ratio, so either Plutarch had another source or the figures were never stable.

Caesar's text also contradicts itself. In 57 the surrendering Nervian elders, as Caesar reports them, say their fighting men have been cut from 60,000 to scarcely 500 (2.28); in 54 they are besieging Quintus Cicero's camp in force (5.39–49). Of the Usipetes and Tencteri he gives 430,000, men, women and children, and says that his own army lost not a man and had few wounded (4.15). At Avaricum he says his soldiers spared neither old men nor women nor children, and that of about 40,000 scarcely 800 escaped (7.28). His own losses appear only where they explain a failure, as at Gergovia. Inside Alesia and in the relief army, his figures and Plutarch's differ again.

Plutarch has 170,000 inside the town and 300,000 coming to relieve it (Caesar 27). Higher numbers of enemies make a greater victory, and the round millions of the later totals are an inflation of what was already Caesar's own advertisement. David Henige (1998) doubted that a migrating people could have taken such a census or carried its tablets, found its exact quarter of fighting men oddly convenient, and judged Caesar's Helvetian figures more likely composed than counted. What is certain is the scale of enslavement Caesar himself reports: 53,000 Atuatuci sold in one lot (2.33), the whole people of the Veneti sold (3.16), a captive for every soldier after Alesia (7.89.5). Suetonius, no friend of Caesar's reputation, adds that he plundered Gallic shrines and temples, sacked towns more often for booty than for any offence, and had so much gold that he sold it in Italy and the provinces at 3,000 sesterces a pound (Julius 54.2). Whatever the dead numbered, the war paid.

The modern charge of genocide rests on three episodes. In 55 Caesar detained the envoys of the Usipetes and Tencteri, attacked their leaderless camp, and sent his cavalry after the women and children; the rest drowned at the meeting of the Meuse and the Rhine (4.13–15). In 2015 Nico Roymans of the Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam announced finds at Kessel on the Meuse: skeletons of men, women and children with sword and spear wounds, bent weapons in the old riverbed, and isotope results showing three of the dead were not local. He identified the site with Caesar's massacre ("A Roman massacre in the far north", 2018), and the university's announcement said that today such an action would be called genocide. In 53 Caesar invited the neighbouring peoples to plunder the Eburones so that "the stock and name of the people" would be destroyed (6.34.8). In 52 came Avaricum (7.28).

Where sources disagree. Tristan Taylor ("Caesar's Gallic Genocide? A Case Study in Ancient Mass Violence", 2012, expanded in The Cambridge World History of Genocide, vol. 1, 2023) applies the modern definition, the intent to destroy a group as such, and finds only the Eburones episode genocidal, since only there does Caesar state the intent; the Meuse and Avaricum were massacres framed as retribution, though he notes that the mass enslavements could have had the same effect as genocide, by dispersing a people. Kurt Raaflaub ("Caesar and Genocide: Confronting the Dark Side of Caesar's Gallic Wars", New England Classical Journal 2021) asks whether a concept of 1948 can fairly be applied at all, and without excusing Caesar places his conduct within the norms of Roman and other ancient imperial warfare. Roman contemporaries were not silent: Cato's demand to surrender Caesar to the Germans (module 5.4) and Pliny's verdict that the killing was a great wrong to the human race (Natural History 7.92) show that the ancient norm could be contested from inside.

The Commentaries do not hide these episodes; they report them as a Roman audience would approve them. That is the most disturbing evidence they offer. Caesar expected the Senate to vote him thanksgivings for exactly the conduct a modern court would examine, and the Senate did.

07Vercingetorix: Caesar's enemy and France's ancestor

Caesar introduces him in 52 as a young Arvernian of great power, son of Celtillus, who had held the leadership of all Gaul and had been killed by his own people for seeking to make himself king (7.4.1). Expelled from Gergovia by his uncle and the nobles, he raised a band of the needy and desperate, and with them seized power; he enforced discipline by fire and torture for grave offences and, for light ones, by cutting off ears or putting out an eye (7.4). Every element marks a type Roman readers knew: the aspirant to kingship, the demagogue of the poor, the tyrant's cruelty.

Yet Caesar's Vercingetorix is also the most capable enemy in the work, and the more capable he is, the greater the victory. He grasps that Rome cannot be beaten in the field and orders the Gauls to burn their own towns and farms to starve the legions (7.14). Accused of treachery, he answers his accusers in a speech that wins them over (7.20). After Avaricum he consoles the Gauls and promises to make one plan for all Gaul, whose agreement not even the whole world could withstand (7.29.6). At the end he tells the council that he undertook the war not for his own advantage but for the common liberty, and offers himself to be killed or surrendered alive (7.89.1–2). Caesar's account of the surrender itself is a sentence: the chiefs were brought out, Vercingetorix was surrendered, the arms were thrown down (7.89.4).

The famous scene is later. Plutarch has him ride out in his finest armour on a decorated horse, circle Caesar as he sat, dismount, strip off his armour and sit silent at Caesar's feet until he was led away to be kept for the triumph (Caesar 27). Dio has him fall at Caesar's knees and Caesar reproach him with a former friendship (40.41). He was kept six years, walked in the Gallic triumph of 46 and was then put to death (Dio 43.19). Arvernian gold coins bearing his name survive, the only words of his own.

Nineteenth-century France remade him. Amédée Thierry's Histoire des Gaulois (1828) made the Gauls the ancestors of the French nation, and Vercingetorix became its first hero: a patriot who united a divided country against a foreign invader and sacrificed himself for it. Napoleon III claimed both sides at once. He excavated Alesia, and in 1865 set up Aimé Millet's colossal statue on Mont-Auxois, its base carrying a French paraphrase of the words Caesar gives Vercingetorix at 7.29.6, that Gaul united could defy the universe; yet his History of Julius Caesar (1865–66) made Caesar a providential man, and he judged that Vercingetorix's defeat, however honourable, was not to be regretted, since it brought Gaul into civilisation. After 1870, with Alsace and Lorraine lost to Germany, the defeated Gaul who had resisted an invader suited the Third Republic better than the conqueror. Camille Jullian's Vercingétorix (1901) gave him a scholarly life, Bartholdi's equestrian statue was inaugurated at Clermont-Ferrand in 1903, and schoolbooks, Ernest Lavisse's above all, taught every child that the Gauls were their ancestors. The comic afterlife, from Asterix onwards, is module 10.4.

Both portraits serve their makers. Caesar's Vercingetorix is great so that Caesar may be greater; France's is a nation's founder in a world where Gaul was never a nation. Caesar's own text supplies the patriot's lines, since it was Caesar who put the words about a united Gaul in his mouth.

08The Civil War: a case for the defence

The Gallic War justifies a conquest; the Civil War justifies a war against the Republic's own government, a harder brief. Caesar's answer is to narrate the war as a defence of legality against a faction. Book 1 opens with the Senate refusing to read his letter, the consuls overriding the tribunes' veto, and the tribunes fleeing to him (1.1–5). Each opponent then receives a private motive: Lentulus his debts and hope of provinces and kings' bribes, Scipio his fear of the courts, Cato his old enmity and electoral defeat, Pompey his refusal to let anyone stand equal to him in dignitas (1.4). Caesar's own motives are public. He addresses his soldiers on the wrongs done to him and on the tribunes' veto, which even Sulla had left intact (1.7); his dignitas was always dearer to him than life (1.9.2); he had come to restore the tribunes and to free the Roman people oppressed by the faction of the few (1.22.5). Module 5.6 sets these phrases among the period's diagnoses of crisis; here the point is how the narrative proves them.

It proves them by contrast. Caesar repeatedly offers peace, and the narrative records each offer and its rebuff (1.9–11, 1.24–26, 3.10, 3.57), so that responsibility for the war falls on whoever refused. He releases Domitius and the senators captured at Corfinium (1.23) and sends back Afranius' men unharmed at Ilerda, while Petreius has Caesarian soldiers caught in his camp killed (1.75–77). Labienus parades prisoners taken at Dyrrachium, calls them comrades, mocks them, and has them killed (3.71). Before Pharsalus the Pompeian leaders quarrel over Caesar's office of pontifex maximus and the spoils of victory (3.83), and their captured camp is full of silver plate and arbours of ivy (3.96). The enemy is cruel, greedy and divided; Caesar is merciful, patient and right.

The silences complete the case. The Civil War does not mention the Rubicon (module 5.4); it passes over the mutiny at Placentia in 49 (deep dive module 11.2); its figures for Pharsalus are contradicted by Pollio, an officer on Caesar's own side (module 5.4). It ends at Alexandria, before the Egyptian entanglement with Cleopatra and before the years of dictatorship that would have been hard to present as the restoration of anything. Whether Caesar stopped there by design or simply ran out of time is unknown.

09Bare, upright, untrue? The ancient verdicts

Two contemporaries judged the Commentaries, and Suetonius set their verdicts side by side (Julius 56). Cicero, in the Brutus of 46, praised them highly: bare, upright and graceful, stripped of all ornament of speech as of a garment. Caesar, he went on, had meant to provide material for others who wished to write history, and perhaps he had pleased the inept, who would want to curl it with tongs; but he had deterred sensible men from writing, since in history nothing is more pleasing than pure and lucid brevity (Brutus 262). The praise is double-edged. A commentarius was supposed to be raw material; Caesar's could not be improved, so it could not be rewritten, so his version would stand. Cicero, who had once sent his own commentarius to Posidonius in the hope of having it worked up, knew exactly what that meant.

Hirtius made the same point as praise: the books had been published so that historians should not lack knowledge of great events, but they were so approved by the judgement of all that Caesar seemed to have taken the opportunity from writers rather than offered it (8, preface 4–5). The plain style closed off rival accounts.

Asinius Pollio, who had served Caesar in the civil war and later wrote its history, struck at truth rather than style. He thought the Commentaries composed with too little care and too little regard for truth: Caesar had often too readily believed what others reported of their own actions, and had set down his own wrongly, either on purpose or from forgetfulness; Pollio believed Caesar would have rewritten and corrected them (Suetonius, Julius 56.4). Pollio's charge is limited and precise. He does not accuse Caesar of inventing campaigns; he accuses him of trusting legates' self-serving reports and of misremembering, or misrepresenting, his own part.

Where sources disagree. Among the ancients, Cicero (Brutus 262) and Hirtius (8, preface) praise the Commentaries as accurate material, while Pollio (Suetonius, Julius 56.4) doubts their truth. Modern readers repeat the division. Michel Rambaud (L'art de la déformation historique dans les Commentaires de César, 1953) argued that Caesar distorts systematically, less by stating falsehoods than by selection, order, emphasis and the attribution of motive. The contributors to Kathryn Welch and Anton Powell (eds.), Julius Caesar as Artful Reporter: The War Commentaries as Political Instruments (1998), took deformation for granted and asked instead how each device worked on a Roman audience; Adrian Goldsworthy's chapter there found the generalship Caesar describes conventional by Roman standards. That Caesar shaped the record is agreed; how far the shaping reached the facts is still argued case by case.

The two judgements are compatible. The style Cicero admired is the instrument of the distortion Pollio suspected. A narrative without visible argument, in which "Caesar" acts and the reader watches, persuades by seeming not to persuade, and it has done its work for two thousand years: the Gallic War became the first Latin prose most European schoolchildren read, and its first sentence the way they learned to think of Gaul.

10Self-test

  1. What was a commentarius, and why was it an advantage to Caesar to call his books by that name?
    Answer

    A working record or notebook, the raw material a historian would later work up into historia, as Cicero hoped Posidonius would work up his (Letters to Atticus 2.1.1–2). The modest label let Caesar disclaim artistry and present partisan narrative as plain report.

  2. What does the third person do to the reader's view of Caesar's decisions?
    Answer

    It makes "Caesar" a character observed from outside by a narrator who never says "I", so his motives arrive as statements of fact rather than claims. With the army as "our men", the reader stands in the Roman ranks.

  3. What does Hirtius' preface to book 8 tell us about when it was written and how Caesar wrote?
    Answer

    It speaks of continuing the commentaries to the end of Caesar's life, so it was written after March 44 and before Hirtius' death in April 43. It says Caesar finished the books easily and quickly, and that their excellence took the opportunity from historians rather than offering it (8, preface).

  4. What internal evidence did Wiseman use for year-by-year publication of the Gallic War?
    Answer

    Inconsistencies a single revision would have removed, above all the Nervii: reduced, by their own envoys' account, to scarcely 500 fighting men in 57 (2.28), yet besieging Quintus Cicero's camp in force in 54 (5.39–49).

  5. How does the ethnography of book 6 serve the narrative around it?
    Answer

    It draws the Gauls as a society with priests, councils and law, which Rome could take over, and the Germans as landless people who neither farm nor own fields. The narrative resumes with Caesar leaving Germany for fear of a grain shortage (6.29), so the description justifies stopping at the Rhine.

  6. Name two ways Caesar's own text undermines the Rhine as the line between Gauls and Germans.
    Answer

    He says most of the Belgae were descended from Germans who had crossed the Rhine (2.4), and he names peoples west of the river as Germani (6.32); he also says the Gauls once sent colonies east of it (6.24).

  7. What did the excavations at Alise-Sainte-Reine confirm about Caesar's description of the siege works, and what did they qualify?
    Answer

    Napoleon III's excavations (1861–65) traced the ditches of both lines, and those of Reddé and von Schnurbein (1991–97) confirmed the double line and found pits and obstacles of the kind Caesar names (7.72–73). The later work also showed the works varying with the ground, so his single uniform scheme is an idealised model.

  8. Why should the Helvetian census of 1.29 be read with suspicion?
    Answer

    Only Caesar saw the tablets; his 92,000 fighting men are exactly a quarter of his 368,000 total; and Plutarch gives a different total and ratio, 300,000 with 190,000 fighting men (Caesar 18).

  9. On what grounds does Taylor find only one episode of the Gallic war genocidal, and how does Raaflaub frame the question differently?
    Answer

    Taylor applies the modern test of stated intent to destroy a group and finds it only in Caesar's call for the "stock and name" of the Eburones to be wiped out (6.34.8); the Meuse massacre and Avaricum are retributive killing. Raaflaub asks whether a concept of 1948 can fairly be applied and places Caesar within ancient imperial norms without excusing him.

  10. How does the Civil War establish Caesar's case without arguing it directly?
    Answer

    By contrast and omission: it records his peace offers and their rejection, his clemency at Corfinium and Ilerda against the cruelty of Petreius and Labienus and the greed of the Pompeian leaders (1.23, 1.75–77, 3.71, 3.83), and it leaves out the Rubicon and the Placentia mutiny.

11Carry forward

  1. A text that disclaims rhetoric is still rhetoric: Caesar's plain third-person report persuades by placing his motives among the facts and his enemies' among the excuses, so read every "Caesar, thinking that" as a claim.
  2. Numbers in ancient war narratives are arguments, not counts; ask who could have counted, whether the author contradicts himself, and what a bigger number did for him, before letting any figure carry a conclusion, including a moral one.
  3. Every age rewrites a defeated enemy to its own needs: Caesar's Vercingetorix magnified Caesar, Napoleon III's justified empire, the Third Republic's founded a nation. Look for the same move when Boudica, Arminius or Hannibal reappear.
