01Four collections and how they survived

Cicero's correspondence is the largest body of private letters to survive from the ancient world, and the only one in which a leading politician can be followed week by week, sometimes day by day, across a quarter of a century. It comes in four collections, none of them arranged by Cicero.

The totals are Shackleton Bailey's: just over 900 letters, of which about 90 are written to Cicero, by Caelius, Pompey, Caesar, Cato, Brutus, Cassius, Antony, Plancus and others. Ancient grammarians quote further books, to Caesar, to Pompey, to Octavian and to others, that are lost. What survives is therefore a selection, made by others after his death, and weighted by accident towards the years when Cicero was out of Rome or out of power, since a man in the city met his friends and did not write to them.

A note on numbers. This report cites letters by the traditional book, letter and section (Letters to Atticus 4.5.1). Shackleton Bailey's editions renumber every letter in date order, and much modern work cites his numbers instead: the letter traditionally Letters to Atticus 16.5 is his 410. The two systems must never be mixed silently.

How the letters became books is partly visible. In July 44 Cicero told Atticus that no collection of his letters existed, but that Tiro had about seventy and some more could be had from Atticus; he would look them over and correct them, and then they might be published (Letters to Atticus 16.5.5). Tiro, his freedman secretary, is the probable editor of the Letters to Friends, whose grouping by correspondent looks like a secretary's files. The letters to Atticus were a different matter: Atticus kept them. Cornelius Nepos, writing his life of Atticus, says that whoever read the eleven volumes of letters sent to Atticus from Cicero's consulship to his last days would hardly need a connected history of the times (Atticus 16.3; module 5.6 quotes the verdict). By Nero's reign they were a classic: Seneca tells Lucilius that Cicero's letters do not allow the name of Atticus to perish (Letters 21.4).

Where sources disagree. When were the letters to Atticus published, and by whom? Cicero's own words of July 44 (Letters to Atticus 16.5.5) show that nothing was collected then; Nepos (Atticus 16.3) speaks of eleven volumes he had read, though our collection has sixteen books and begins in 68, five years before the consulship he names as its start. Jérôme Carcopino (Les secrets de la correspondance de Cicéron, 1947) argued that the collection was published under Octavian to blacken Cicero's memory; reviewers admired the ingenuity and rejected the thesis, and most scholars now put publication in the first century AD, no later than the early 60s, when Seneca could assume Lucilius knew the letters (Letters 21.4). Who chose and how much was left out remains unknown.

The Middle Ages kept the letters thinly. The Letters to Friends rest above all on one ninth-century manuscript now in Florence (Laurentianus 49.9), which belonged to the church of Vercelli; a copy of it was made for the Florentine chancellor Coluccio Salutati in 1392. The letters to Atticus, Quintus and Brutus travelled together. In 1345, in the chapter library of Verona, Francesco Petrarch found them and had them copied; his manuscript and its exemplar are both lost, and the text descends through later copies, one of them made for Salutati. Petrarch had already found the Pro Archia at Liège in 1333, and he knew Cicero as the philosopher of Tusculan Disputations and On Duties. The letters showed him a different man. On 16 June 1345 he wrote a letter to Cicero himself, reproaching him: he had heard him say much, lament much and change his mind on much; why had a man of his age and learning thrown away the quiet that suited him for quarrels and civil wars that could bring no good (Petrarch, Letters on Familiar Matters 24.3)? The modern argument over Cicero's character starts in that letter.

Where sources disagree. The Letters to Brutus were attacked as a forgery wholesale by Jeremiah Markland in 1745, and are now accepted as a body, but two letters remain in doubt: 1.16 and 1.17, in which Brutus denounces Cicero for flattering Octavian and calls him, in effect, a slave seeking a kinder master. D. R. Shackleton Bailey (Epistulae ad Quintum fratrem et M. Brutum, 1980) judged both to be rhetorical exercises from the schools; John Moles (1997) defended them as Brutus' own. If genuine, they are the harshest contemporary verdict on Cicero's last policy; if not, they are an early chapter of its reception.

02The new man and his consulship

Marcus Tullius Cicero was born in 106 at Arpinum, the hill town of the Volsci that had produced Marius (module 5.2), into an equestrian family with no senator in its past. He had nothing to rise by but speech. Quaestor in Sicily in 75, he made his name prosecuting Verres in 70 (module 5.3), was aedile in 69, praetor in 66 and consul for 63, elected at the first legal age. The earliest letter is of November 68 (Letters to Atticus 1.5), and from the first the reader overhears ambition: in July 65 he lists his likely rivals for 63 and says Catiline will certainly be one, if a jury rules that the sun does not shine at noon (1.1.1); in the next letter he is thinking of defending Catiline, his competitor (1.2.1).

The consulship of 63 (module 5.3) became the fixed point of his life, and the letters show him selling it. He complained to Pompey, returning from the East in 62, that Pompey's letter had offered no congratulation on what he had done for the state (Letters to Friends 5.7.3). He sent Atticus a Greek memoir of the year for circulation in the East and asked Posidonius to work it up (Letters to Atticus 2.1.1–2; deep dive module 13.1 tells Posidonius' polite refusal). He wrote a Latin poem On his Consulship, whose half-line "let arms yield to the toga" he still defended in 44 (On Duties 1.77), and whose jingle "O fortunate Rome, born in my consulship" Juvenal still mocked nearly two centuries later (10.122). In 55 he asked the historian Lucceius to write a separate monograph on the conspiracy and his return, and to set aside the laws of history and grant his affection a little more than truth would allow (Letters to Friends 5.12.3). Few documents show a politician's self-portrait being commissioned so openly.

The reason was structural, not only vanity. A new man had no ancestral masks in his atrium (module 2.4; deep dive module 11.1 on new men); his standing rested on one achievement, and the achievement, the execution of citizens without trial, was legally exposed. Plutarch says his constant self-praise made him widely hated (Cicero 24). The letters let us watch the advertising and the exposure grow together.

03Exile, return and the change of course, 58–54

The exposure came in 58. Clodius' law against anyone who had put a citizen to death uncondemned, and the street politics that carried it, belong to deep dive module 11.3. Cicero left Rome in March before the vote; a second law named him and confiscated his property, and his house on the Palatine was pulled down. His own letter gives the bounds: he was to stay more than four hundred miles from Italy (Letters to Atticus 3.4; Plutarch, Cicero 32, says five hundred). He waited at Thessalonica and Dyrrachium until August 57.

The letters of exile (Letters to Atticus book 3; Letters to Friends 14.1–4, to Terentia and the children) are the least guarded he wrote. He weeps, accuses friends whose advice and envy, he thinks, drove him out, regrets that he did not stay and fight or die, and begs Atticus not to leave him. His letters to her from exile call her his most faithful and best wife (Letters to Friends 14.4); eleven years later, from Venusia in October 47, he sends her three lines ordering a basin for the bath (14.20), and they divorced the next year. Terentia herself, holding the family together in Rome, is deep dive module 11.4's. Recalled by the centuriate assembly in August 57, he reached Rome on 4 September, and his letter to Atticus describes the crowds from Brundisium to the Capitol as a second life (Letters to Atticus 4.1). The return speeches, and the De domo sua to recover the site of his house, rebuilt the public image the letters had shown in pieces.

Module 5.4 leaves Cicero's forced change of course to this module. In April 56 he moved that the Senate discuss Caesar's Campanian land law on 15 May. Caesar met Crassus at Ravenna and Pompey at Luca; Pompey then sent word through Quintus, who had stood surety for his brother's good behaviour, that Cicero was to drop the matter (Letters to Friends 1.9.8–10). He dropped it, spoke for the extension of Caesar's command (On the Consular Provinces, 56), and confessed to Atticus that he had written a "palinode", a recantation he was a little ashamed of: since those without power would not love him, let him take care to be loved by those who had it (Letters to Atticus 4.5.1–2). In 54 he defended Vatinius, whom he had attacked two years before, and then Gabinius, the consul of his exile.

His defence is the long letter of 54 to Lentulus Spinther (Letters to Friends 1.9). The optimates, he argues, had abandoned him and courted Clodius; Pompey and Caesar had kept faith; Plato taught that a man should press his case in politics only so far as he could persuade his fellow citizens; a statesman must steer with the weather, and constancy means holding to the same end, not the same opinions. It is the most elaborate justification of political compromise in Latin, and a reader can take it as realism or as rationalisation. The private letters to Atticus of the same months, with their shame and bitterness, are the control: the public reasoning was made after the decision, not before it.

04A reluctant governor: Cilicia, 51–50

A law of 52 required ex-magistrates without a province to take one, and in 51 Cicero, who had avoided a province after his praetorship and consulship, drew Cilicia, with Cyprus attached. Books 5 and 6 to Atticus and book 15 of the Letters to Friends record a year he counted day by day. He campaigned on the Amanus, was hailed imperator by his troops (Letters to Atticus 5.20), and wanted a triumph.

The governorship produced the most revealing financial episode in the letters. The city of Salamis in Cyprus owed money to two men, Scaptius and Matinius, who demanded interest at 48 per cent a year; Cicero's edict capped interest at 12. Scaptius produced letters from Brutus asking for help, and Cicero discovered that the creditor was Brutus himself, lending through agents. Scaptius, given cavalry by Cicero's predecessor Appius Claudius, had shut the Salaminian council in its chamber until, Cicero says, five councillors died of hunger. Cicero refused to enforce the rate and tried to defer the question to his successor (Letters to Atticus 5.21.10–13, 6.1.5–7, 6.2.7–9). The future tyrannicide, champion of Stoic virtue, appears as a loan shark with a governor's troops at his disposal.

The triumph shows the Senate's politics of honour. Cato, asked for support (Letters to Friends 15.4), praised Cicero in the Senate but voted against a thanksgiving, one of only three who did (8.11.2), and wrote to explain why (15.5); Cicero then learned that Cato had voted twenty days of thanksgiving for Bibulus, Cato's son-in-law, whose achievements were less (Letters to Atticus 7.2.7). Cicero came home in late 50 still hoping for his triumph, into a civil war.

05The civil war: knowing whom to flee, 49–47

Books 7 to 10 to Atticus are the record of a mind failing to decide. Pompey abandoned Rome in January 49; a month later Cicero wrote that he knew whom to flee but not whom to follow (Letters to Atticus 8.7.2). He despised Pompey's strategy, feared Pompey's victory as much as Caesar's, and wrote that Pompey's mind had long wanted to be Sulla and to proscribe (9.10.6). Caesar courted him. His short notes ask for Cicero's counsel, influence, standing and help (Letters to Atticus 9.6A), and one answer to news that pardoned Pompeians had gone back to the war is the most quoted sentence he wrote outside his books: nothing pleased him more than that he should be like himself and they like themselves (9.16.2). Clemency was policy, and Cicero was meant to see it (module 5.4 has the letter on Corfinium).

On 28 March 49 they met at Formiae. Cicero said that if he came to the Senate he would speak against war in Spain and Greece and lament Pompey's fate; Caesar said he did not want such things said, and asked him to think it over (Letters to Atticus 9.18.1). Cicero stayed in his villas, wrote to Atticus almost daily, and in June 49 sailed to join Pompey. Caelius, by then with Caesar, had warned him in writing not to (Letters to Friends 8.16).

In Pompey's camp he was useless and sardonic (Plutarch, Cicero 38), and after Pharsalus he refused the command Cato offered him and went back to Italy, landing at Brundisium late in 48. There he waited almost a year, unable to approach Rome without Caesar's leave, quarrelling with his brother and his nephew, worrying about Terentia's management and Tullia's marriage to Dolabella (Letters to Atticus book 11). Caesar landed in September 47, met him on the road, and pardoned him with every courtesy (Plutarch, Cicero 39). Mommsen judged that the letters of these months, written when Cicero was thrown back on himself, were "stale and empty"; a modern reader is more likely to find them the most human in the collection.

06Tullia, philosophy, the Philippics and death, 45–43

Tullia died in February 45, a month after giving birth. Book 12 to Atticus follows the grief: Cicero shuts himself in his villa at Astura, reads everything written on mourning, writes himself a Consolation, and asks Atticus again and again to find and buy a garden where he can build her a shrine (Letters to Atticus book 12). Servius Sulpicius, governing Achaea, sent the most famous letter written to him: sailing home from Asia he had seen Aegina, Megara, the Piraeus and Corinth in ruins, and asked why a man should grieve so for one life when the corpses of so many cities lay before him (Letters to Friends 4.5.4). Tullia and Terentia as women of the late Republic are deep dive module 11.4's. The grief turned into work: the philosophical burst of 45–44 is listed in module 5.6, and what it did with Greek philosophy is deep dive modules 13.1 and 13.2.

The Ides of March are deep dive module 11.7, the war of Mutina module 5.5. Module 5.5 also gives the Philippics their political aim and leaves them as oratory to this module. Fourteen survive, from 2 September 44 to 21 April 43. The name is Demosthenes' and was a joke before it was a title: Brutus wrote that, having read two of the speeches, he would now allow them to be called Philippics, as Cicero had playfully suggested in a letter (Letters to Brutus 2.3.4). The Second, the longest and most savage, was never delivered: it is a pamphlet cast as Cicero's reply to Antony's attack in the Senate of 19 September 44, which Cicero sent to Atticus for correction and was afraid to release (Letters to Atticus 16.11.1–2). Like Demosthenes' On the Crown it is a defence of the speaker's life turned into an assault on the accuser's. Antony's drunken vomiting before the assembly (2.63) and his travels with the actress Cytheris (2.58) belong to invective's stock; the peroration is Cicero's own: he had defended the republic as a young man and would not abandon it as an old one, and he would offer his body if his death could bring the city freedom (2.118–119). The Third and Fourth were spoken on the same day, 20 December 44, the Third to the Senate and the Fourth to the people in a contio; the Sixth was also to the people. The cycle is the last sustained political oratory of the Republic, and later Rome canonised it: Juvenal calls the Second "divine" (10.125).

The letters stop in the summer of 43. Cicero was proscribed by the triumvirs and killed near Formiae on 7 December 43 (module 5.5 tells the death from Plutarch, Cicero 46 and 48).

Where sources disagree. The first verdicts on Cicero were written over his corpse, and Seneca the Elder collected them for declaimers (Suasoriae 6). Asinius Pollio, Caesar's officer and Antony's ally in 43, claimed that Cicero had offered to disown the Philippics to save himself (Suasoriae 6.15), and wished he had borne prosperity with more moderation and adversity with more courage (6.24). Livy, writing under Augustus, called him a great and memorable man who bore none of his misfortunes as a man should except his death, and judged that he suffered from a victorious enemy nothing crueller than he would have done himself in the same fortune (6.22). Pollio's story of the offered recantation is uncorroborated; Livy lets the good death weigh against the wavering life, and Pollio does not.

07How politics was done

Elections

The letters show the canvass from inside: the candidate's arithmetic of rivals in 65 (Letters to Atticus 1.1), the deals between candidates, the money. In 54 the consular elections broke records for bribery. Cicero reports that on 15 July the rate of interest at Rome doubled, from one-third to two-thirds of one per cent a month, that is from 4 to 8 per cent a year, because so much cash had been borrowed for buying votes (Letters to Atticus 4.15.7). The same summer two candidates, Memmius and Domitius Calvinus, made a written compact with the consuls in office: if elected they would pay each consul 4,000,000 sesterces unless they produced three augurs to swear they had been present at a law conferring the consuls' commands that was never passed, and two ex-consuls to swear to a Senate decree on their provinces that was never made. Memmius, at Pompey's urging, read the document out in the Senate (Letters to Atticus 4.17.2; the figure is Cicero's). The elections were postponed until 53. Deep dive module 11.1 gives the bribery laws; here is the practice they failed to control.

A short handbook of canvassing, the Commentariolum petitionis, is addressed to Cicero in the name of his brother Quintus for the election of 64. It advises a new man to court the nobles, the equestrian companies, the towns and the tribes; to fill his house at dawn and his escort in the Forum (34–38; module 9.4 uses it on the morning greeting); to promise rather than refuse, since a refusal makes a certain enemy and a promise only a possible disappointment (45–48); and to remind voters of his rivals' scandals.

Where sources disagree. M. I. Henderson ("De commentariolo petitionis", Journal of Roman Studies 1950) argued that the handbook is a later exercise, built from Cicero's speeches with anachronisms a brother writing in 64 would not commit. J. P. V. D. Balsdon ("The Commentariolum Petitionis", Classical Quarterly 1963) defended it as Quintus' work. If genuine, it is the only contemporary manual of Roman electioneering; if not, it is a later reader's reconstruction of how Cicero must have won, and good evidence still for what Romans believed a campaign required.

Money

Politics ran on credit. In December 62 Cicero told Sestius that he had bought Crassus' house on the Palatine for 3,500,000 sesterces and was so deep in debt that he would join a conspiracy if anyone would have him (Letters to Friends 5.6.2). By 50 he owed money to Caesar and thought it ugly to be the debtor of a political opponent (Letters to Atticus 7.8.5). Atticus managed his accounts, his purchases and his loans, as he managed his books. Brutus' Cyprian loan shows how far a governor's power could be put at a creditor's service.

Favours and friendship

The whole of book 13 of the Letters to Friends is recommendations: letters to governors on behalf of businessmen, towns, freedmen and Greek friends, each a small transfer of credit from Cicero's standing to the recommended man's affairs. Amicitia in these letters is political alliance spoken as affection, and its vocabulary of goodwill and faith is the one Catullus turned on Lesbia (module 5.6). Its obligations bound across party lines: the Pompeian Cicero recommended men to Caesarian governors and was answered.

News and gossip

Away from Rome, a senator needed information. Caelius, leaving Cicero for Cilicia in 51, hired a man to write up everything, decrees of the Senate, rumours, trials, the stories of the town, and sent the bundle with a covering letter, apologising for the bulk (Letters to Friends 8.1.1). His own letters (book 8) are the liveliest prose in the collection. Letters went by private carrier and could be read on the way, so Cicero wrote obliquely when he had to, and in 59 proposed to Atticus that in future they write under assumed names (Letters to Atticus 2.19.5). The gossip is the politics: who had dined with Pompey, who was in debt, which jury had been bought, what Clodia had said (the Bona Dea trial, reported to Atticus at 1.16, is module 5.3's).

08Atticus, Caelius, Pompey and Caesar as the letters show them

Atticus. Titus Pomponius Atticus (110–32), an equestrian who lived some twenty years at Athens and took his name from it, an Epicurean who never stood for office, was Cicero's banker, publisher, estate agent and conscience. His replies are lost, so he exists only as Cicero addressed him: someone whose advice was asked on everything, from a Greek word to whether to join Pompey. Nepos' life shows him helping each side in turn and surviving every proscription (Atticus 6–11; module 5.6). His daughter married Agrippa. That such a man kept and let circulate the letters of a friend who died on the losing side is one of the collection's quiet puzzles.

Caelius. Marcus Caelius Rufus, Cicero's pupil and client (the Pro Caelio of 56 is deep dive module 11.4's), is the only correspondent whose voice rivals Cicero's. As aedile-elect he pestered his governor for panthers for his games, and Cicero answered that the panthers were complaining of being the only creatures in his province against whom plots were laid, and had decided to leave for Caria (Letters to Friends 2.11.2). In August 50 Caelius gave the clearest analysis of the coming war: in domestic quarrels a man should take the more honourable side, but once it came to arms, the stronger (8.14.3). He took his own advice, joined Caesar, rebelled against him as praetor in 48 and was killed (Caesar, Civil War 3.20–22).

Pompey. The letters give the most unflattering portrait of him we have, because Cicero needed him and resented it. In 61 Cicero found in him nothing courteous, nothing frank, nothing brilliant, nothing brave or free (Letters to Atticus 1.13.4); in 59 he called him "Sampsiceramus", after an Arab princeling (2.14.1, 2.16.2); in 49 he despaired of his strategy and feared his vengeance (9.10.6). Pompey's own letters to Cicero and to the consuls in February 49 (8.11A, 8.11C, 8.12A–D, to Cicero, the consuls and Domitius) show a commander trying to hold an army together by correspondence. Measured against the ideal statesman of On the Republic, Pompey failed, as module 5.6 notes from Letters to Atticus 8.11.

Caesar. He appears in his own clipped notes (9.6A, 9.16.2), through Quintus' service in Gaul, and in one domestic scene. On 19 December 45 Caesar came to dinner at Cicero's villa near Puteoli with some two thousand men, by Cicero's count; he bathed, took an emetic, ate and drank with pleasure; the talk was of literature, nothing serious; and he was not a guest to whom one says "do come again on your way back": once was enough (Letters to Atticus 13.52). After the Ides Cicero reported to Atticus two remarks of Caesar's that Caesar's friend Matius had passed on to him: of Brutus, that it mattered much what he wanted, for whatever he wanted he wanted intensely; and of Cicero, that he could hardly doubt he was hated when even Marcus Cicero had to sit waiting to see him (14.1.2). Matius' own letter defending his friendship with the dead dictator is in the collection (Letters to Friends 11.28).

09The argument over Cicero's character

The letters made Cicero the best-known individual of antiquity and so the most exposed. No other Roman politician left private evidence of his fears, vanities, debts and changes of mind; judged by public speeches alone, Cicero's contemporaries would look steadier than he does. Every verdict on his character has had to decide how far to hold the private voice against him.

Petrarch, finding the letters in 1345, was the first to feel betrayed by them. The decisive modern verdict was Theodor Mommsen's, in volume 3 of his Römische Geschichte (1856), in the chapter on the culture of the late Republic (book 5, chapter 12). For Mommsen, whose hero was Caesar, Cicero was "a statesman without insight, idea, or purpose", who figured in turn as democrat, aristocrat and tool of the monarchs and was never more than a short-sighted egotist; as a writer, "a journalist in the worst sense of that term"; and his letters, clever while they mirrored the society of the capital and the villas, became "stale and empty" when he was thrown on his own resources, in exile and after Pharsalus (quoted from W. P. Dickson's English translation, The History of Rome). The judgement was political as much as historical: Mommsen, a disappointed liberal of 1848, despised the parliamentarian who talked while the man of action acted, and his authority fixed the verdict for German scholarship for generations.

The rehabilitations began in France. Gaston Boissier's Cicéron et ses amis (1865) read the correspondence as a portrait of a society, and found its writer more likeable for the weaknesses he did not hide. In English the letters' own editor, D. R. Shackleton Bailey, built a life from them (Cicero, 1971) that is unsparing but not contemptuous; the sympathetic turn came with Elizabeth Rawson's Cicero: A Portrait (1975) and with Thomas N. Mitchell's two volumes, Cicero: The Ascending Years (1979) and Cicero: The Senior Statesman (1991), which argue that beneath the shifts lay a consistent political outlook, the rule of the Senate and the concord of the propertied orders. The common ground of the rehabilitations is a change of question: not whether Cicero was brave or consistent, but what a new man without an army could have done in a republic decided by armies, and whether any of his contemporaries, examined in the same private detail, would have done better.

Where sources disagree. Mommsen (1856) read the wavering of 56 and 49 and the despair of exile as proof of a weak and vain man; Carcopino (1947) went further and drew from them a deeply hostile portrait. Rawson (1975) and Mitchell (1979, 1991) read the same letters as the record of a man thinking aloud to his closest friend, whose public choices were more consistent than his private moods. Neither side disputes the evidence; they dispute whether private doubt, which only Cicero left in writing, should count against public conduct, and the question remains open.

10Self-test

  1. Name the four collections of Cicero's letters and say how the letters to Atticus and to his friends reached the Renaissance.
    Answer

    To Atticus (16 books), to his friends (16), to Quintus (3) and to Brutus (2). Petrarch found the letters to Atticus, Quintus and Brutus in the chapter library of Verona in 1345; the Letters to Friends rest on a ninth-century manuscript from Vercelli, copied for Salutati in 1392.

  2. Why must a reader check which numbering a citation of a Ciceronian letter uses?
    Answer

    The traditional system cites book, letter and section; Shackleton Bailey renumbered every letter in date order, and much modern work cites his numbers, so the same letter has two references (Letters to Atticus 16.5 is his 410). Mixing them silently sends a reader to the wrong letter.

  3. What did Cicero say in July 44 about a collection of his letters, and why does it matter for the date of publication?
    Answer

    That none existed, though Tiro had about seventy and more could come from Atticus, to be corrected before publication (Letters to Atticus 16.5.5). So the collections were made after his death by others, which leaves open when, by whom and with what omissions; Carcopino's theory of an Octavianic publication found few followers.

  4. What did Cicero ask of Lucceius in 55, and what does the request show?
    Answer

    A monograph on the conspiracy and his return, in which Lucceius should set aside the laws of history and grant his affection a little more than truth allowed (Letters to Friends 5.12.3). It shows a new man, whose standing rested on one legally exposed achievement, commissioning his own image.

  5. What was the "palinode" of 56, and how did Cicero justify it to Lentulus Spinther?
    Answer

    After Luca, warned through Quintus to drop the Campanian land question (Letters to Friends 1.9.8–10), he supported Caesar's command and called his change of line a recantation (Letters to Atticus 4.5.1). To Lentulus he argued that the optimates had abandoned him, Pompey and Caesar had kept faith, and constancy means holding to the same end, not the same opinions (Letters to Friends 1.9).

  6. What does the Salamis affair reveal about Brutus and about provincial government?
    Answer

    Brutus, through agents, was lending to Salamis at 48 per cent against Cicero's edict of 12, and his agent had used a governor's cavalry to besiege the city council (Letters to Atticus 5.21, 6.1). Senators' private credit could command a province's military force.

  7. What was Cicero's dilemma in 49, and how did Caesar try to resolve it?
    Answer

    He knew whom to flee but not whom to follow (Letters to Atticus 8.7.2), fearing a Pompeian victory as much as Caesar's. Caesar courted him with notes asking for his counsel and help (9.6A) and met him at Formiae, where Cicero refused to come to the Senate except to speak against the war (9.18.1); in June he sailed to Pompey.

  8. What do the events of July 54 show about consular elections?
    Answer

    Borrowing for bribery doubled interest at Rome from 4 to 8 per cent a year (Letters to Atticus 4.15.7), and two candidates contracted to pay the consuls 4,000,000 sesterces each unless they produced false witnesses to a law and a decree that never happened (4.17.2). The bribery laws described in deep dive module 11.1 did not bind practice.

  9. How do the letters show Caesar as a man rather than a general?
    Answer

    In the dinner at Puteoli in December 45, with its emetic, its literary talk and Cicero's relief that once was enough (Letters to Atticus 13.52), and in remarks reported through Matius: on Brutus' intense wanting and on Cicero kept waiting (14.1.2). His own notes show clemency as deliberate policy (9.16.2).

  10. On what ground did Mommsen condemn Cicero, and how do the modern rehabilitations change the question?
    Answer

    Mommsen (1856) called him a statesman without insight, idea or purpose and a journalist in the worst sense, and found his letters stale and empty when he was on his own. Boissier, Rawson and Mitchell ask instead what a new man without an army could do, and whether any contemporary examined in the same private detail would look steadier.

11Carry forward

  1. Cicero's letters are a selection made after his death by others, weighted towards the years he was away or out of power; always ask which collection, whose numbering, and what could have been left out.
  2. The letters show Roman politics as it was practised, through credit, bribery, recommendation, news and friendship that bound across party lines, so they are the measure against which laws, speeches and later histories of the late Republic should be read.
  3. Because only Cicero left a private voice, he is judged on evidence no contemporary faced; when you weigh him against Pompey, Caesar, Brutus or Cato, compare like with like, public act with public act, before letting his doubts decide the verdict.
