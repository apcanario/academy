01The evidence: panegyrists, a bishop and a hostile historian

Constantine is the best-documented emperor since Augustus and the hardest to see, because nearly every witness wrote for or against a convert. The Latin panegyrics (Panegyrici Latini VII(6) of 307, VI(7) of 310, V(8) of 311 and XII(9) of 313, spoken before him in Gaul, and Nazarius' IV(10), spoken at Rome in his absence in 321) give the official line of each year, unrevised by hindsight. Lactantius, a rhetorician at Nicomedia who in old age tutored Constantine's son Crispus (Jerome, On Illustrious Men 80), wrote On the Deaths of the Persecutors about 314–315 to show that God destroys persecutors. Eusebius, bishop of Caesarea, revised his Church History into the 320s and left the Life of Constantine unfinished at his death in 339, quoting imperial letters in full inside a frame of encomium. Zosimus' New History (book 2, about 500), following Eunapius' lost history, blames Constantine for the empire's ruin. Beside these stand the Latin breviaries, the anonymous Origo Constantini, Optatus' dossier on the African schism, the laws in the Theodosian Code (module 9.1), coins and inscriptions.

Where sources disagree. Burckhardt (Die Zeit Constantins des Grossen, 1853) called Eusebius the first thoroughly dishonest historian of antiquity, and Henri Grégoire (1938) denied him the Life in its present form. A. H. M. Jones and T. C. Skeat (1954) then identified in a London papyrus (P. Lond. 878) a near-contemporary copy of part of the letter to the provincials of Palestine quoted at Life 2.26–29. The documents are now accepted as genuine; the frame Eusebius built round them is what remains in dispute.

02From York to Rome, 306–312

Constantine was born at Naissus (Niš) on 27 February, probably in the early 270s, the son of Constantius and Helena, and served at the eastern courts as a pledge of his father's loyalty (Origo 2.2–3). At the abdications of 1 May 305 the new Caesars were Galerius' men, Severus and Maximinus Daia; Constantine and Maximian's son Maxentius were passed over (Lactantius, Deaths of the Persecutors 18–19; module 8.2). Constantine left Galerius' court by night, taking or killing the post-horses behind him to stop pursuit (Lactantius 24; Origo 2.4), and joined his father at Boulogne for a campaign against the Picts. Constantius died at York on 25 July 306 and the army proclaimed his son, urged on, the Epitome de Caesaribus says, by the Alamannic king Crocus (41.3). Lactantius says Constantine's first act was to restore the Christians to their worship (24.9). Galerius recognised him as Caesar, not Augustus (25).

In late October 306 Rome proclaimed Maxentius, and Maximian came out of retirement to back his son. Severus' invasion of 307 collapsed when his troops defected, and Galerius' own withdrew. Maximian bought Constantine's goodwill with his daughter Fausta and the title Augustus, both celebrated by the panegyric of 307; Minervina, mother of Constantine's eldest son Crispus, vanishes from the record, dead or divorced. The conference at Carnuntum in November 308 made Licinius Augustus of the West over the heads of both Caesars; Galerius then offered Constantine and Maximinus the invented title "sons of the Augusti", which neither accepted for long (Lactantius 32). In 310 Maximian, now at Constantine's court, seized the treasury at Arles; Constantine cornered him at Massilia, and he died, by his own hand in the official version (Lactantius 29–30). The panegyrist at Trier that summer announced a new claim to rule: descent from Claudius II Gothicus (VI(7).2). Constantine no longer owed his throne to the Tetrarchy.

Galerius' edict of toleration and his death in 311 (module 8.2) left two pairs: Constantine with Licinius, betrothed to Constantine's half-sister Constantia; Maxentius with Maximinus. In spring 312 Constantine crossed the Alps with barely a quarter of his army, says the panegyrist of 313 (XII(9).3.3); Zosimus gives him 90,000 foot and 8,000 horse against Maxentius' 170,000 and 18,000 (2.15.1–2), figures modern historians treat as inflated. He stormed Segusio, broke Maxentius' armoured cavalry near Turin and took Verona after a siege. Maxentius had provisioned Rome and cut the Milvian Bridge; then, Lactantius says, the Sibylline Books told him that the enemy of the Romans would perish that day (44.8). On 28 October 312 he crossed the Tiber on a bridge of boats and fought with the river behind him. His line broke, the pontoon gave way and he drowned, a new Pharaoh in the Red Sea for Eusebius (Church History 9.9.5–8). Constantine abolished the praetorian guard (Aurelius Victor, Caesares 40.25) and the imperial horse guard, over whose barracks the Lateran basilica rose. The battle is one of module 0.3's anchor dates and the hinge of the late empire.

Lactantius describes the shield sign as "transversa X littera, summo capite circumflexo", the letter X turned with its top bent round: a Chi-Rho or a looped cross. Eusebius' own Church History, written nearer the event, mentions a statue in Rome of Constantine holding the saving sign (9.9.10–11) but no vision; the sky-sign appears only in the Life, told him by the emperor under oath long afterwards (1.28.1). In hoc signo vinces is a later Latin rendering. The panegyrist of 313 says only that Constantine shared some secret with the divine mind (XII(9).2.4–5). The coins hedge too: a Ticinum medallion of 315 shows the Chi-Rho on his helmet, while Sol Invictus, the emperor's "companion", stayed on the bronze into the 320s.

Where sources disagree. Lactantius' dream on the eve of battle and Eusebius' daylight sign, seen by an army months earlier somewhere on the march, cannot both be literally true, and neither writer knows the other's version. Peter Weiss (1993; in English 2003) argued that all three accounts go back to one experience, a solar halo with cross-like arcs seen in Gaul in 310: read first as Apollo's wreaths, then retold after 312 as the sign of Christ. Barnes (2011) adopted the thesis as explaining all the early evidence, but it moves Eusebius' story two years and a country away from Lactantius' battlefield.

04The "Edict of Milan" and what it actually was

In February 313 Constantine and Licinius met at Milan, where Licinius married Constantia, and agreed a religious policy; no edict was issued there. Licinius defeated Maximinus near Adrianople at the end of April, his troops having recited a prayer to the highest god dictated, Lactantius says, by an angel (46), and on 13 June posted at Nicomedia a letter to the governor of Bithynia (Lactantius 48; Greek version in Eusebius, Church History 10.5.2–14). It grants Christians and everyone else free choice of religion, so that whatever divinity dwells in heaven may favour the emperors and their subjects, and orders Christian churches and corporate property restored without charge. The Christians are named; the god is not.

It was thus Licinius applying in the East a policy Constantine had followed in the West since the winter of 312, with restitution, money for Caecilian's clergy at Carthage and exemptions for clergy (Church History 10.5.15–17, 10.6, 10.7). It went beyond Galerius' edict of 311 by returning property and by stating free choice as a principle for all.

Where sources disagree. Otto Seeck ("Das sogenannte Edikt von Mailand", 1891) showed that the text is a rescript posted by Licinius, not an edict issued at Milan, and most historians now speak of the Milan agreement. Milton Anastos (1967) defended the traditional name, arguing that the policy was framed by Constantine and issued in both emperors' names. The unnamed "divinity" is read either as Licinius' pagan monotheism or as a formula both rulers could sign.

05The Arch of Constantine as evidence

The Senate dedicated the arch beside the Colosseum for Constantine's tenth anniversary in 315. Its inscription (CIL VI 1139) says he avenged the state on the tyrant and his faction "instinctu divinitatis, mentis magnitudine", by the prompting of the divinity and the greatness of his mind: words a pagan senate could write and a Christian emperor accept. Much of the sculpture is reused: slabs of a Trajanic battle frieze, eight Hadrianic roundels of hunting and of sacrifice to Silvanus, Diana, Apollo and Hercules, eight panels from a monument of Marcus Aurelius and Trajanic statues of captive Dacians, with the earlier emperors' heads recut. The new frieze tells the campaign, from the departure from Milan through Verona and the bridge to the entry, a speech from the Rostra and a distribution of money. Sol and Luna fill the end roundels. There is no Christian symbol.

As evidence it says three things: the Senate's Constantine stands in the line of Trajan, Hadrian and Marcus; his victory is owed to an unnamed divinity; and in 315 the city still showed its emperor at pagan sacrifice. Its style and the meaning of the reuse belong to module 10.3; module 9.5 puts it on the walk.

06Sole emperor: Licinius, Crispus and Fausta

The partnership with Licinius broke twice. War in 316 (once dated 314) brought victories at Cibalae and in Thrace and gave Constantine the Balkans except Thrace; on 1 March 317 Crispus and the infant Constantine II became Caesars, alongside Licinius' infant son. In the 320s Licinius purged Christians from his court and army (Eusebius, Life 1.51–54). In 324 Constantine won at Adrianople, Crispus destroyed Licinius' fleet in the Hellespont, and Chrysopolis on 18 September decided it. Licinius surrendered on Constantia's plea and was promised his life; he was killed at Thessalonica in 325, against the oath, Eutropius says (10.6).

In 326 Constantine had Crispus executed at Pola (Ammianus 14.11.20); soon afterwards Fausta died in an overheated bath. No contemporary explains either death, and Eusebius dropped Crispus from the last edition of the Church History. Later accounts conflict: Zosimus says Crispus was suspected of an affair with his stepmother (2.29.2); the Epitome says Fausta accused him and was killed when Helena reproached her son (41.11–12); Philostorgius has Fausta caught in adultery (2.4).

Where sources disagree. Zosimus (2.29.3–4), after Eunapius, says Constantine turned Christian because pagan priests would not purify him of these murders and an Egyptian from Spain promised that the faith washed away every sin; Julian had already shown Constantine running to Jesus, who makes murderers clean (Caesars 336a–b). Sozomen replied that Constantine had favoured the Christians long before (Church History 1.5). The story is impossible in its chronology but shows what opponents made of a convert with blood on his hands.

07The emperor and the bishops: Donatists, Arius, Nicaea

Africa: the Donatists

The first church quarrel reached Constantine within months of the Milvian Bridge. At Carthage the bishop Caecilian had been consecrated by Felix of Abthugni, whom rigorists accused of surrendering the scriptures in the persecution of 303 (module 8.2); the Numidian bishops set up a rival, Majorinus, soon succeeded by Donatus, and appealed to the emperor. Constantine referred the case to Miltiades of Rome (Eusebius, Church History 10.5.18–20), whose council upheld Caecilian in October 313; on a further appeal he summoned western bishops to Arles, who confirmed it in August 314 (10.5.21–24). Constantine told his vicar of Africa that he feared the highest divinity might be roused against the human race and against himself while such dissension lasted (Optatus, appendix 3). He heard the case himself in 316, tried coercion from 317 and gave up in 321. The pattern was set: bishops appealed to the emperor, the emperor convened bishops, and the losers called him a persecutor.

Arius and the Council of Nicaea, 325

About 318 Arius, a presbyter of Alexandria, taught that the Son was created from nothing: "there was when he was not". His bishop, Alexander, excommunicated him; Eusebius of Nicomedia and others took his side. Fresh from Chrysopolis, Constantine sent a letter calling it an idle question that should never have been aired (Eusebius, Life 2.64–72), carried, Socrates says, by Ossius of Corduba (Church History 1.7), then summoned a general council to Nicaea.

The bishops came on the imperial post in early summer 325: more than 250 by Eusebius' count (Life 3.8), about 300 by Athanasius' (On the Decrees of Nicaea 3); the later 318 echoes Abraham's servants (Genesis 14:14). Nearly all were eastern; the West sent Ossius, two Roman presbyters and a few others. Constantine entered in purple and gold and joined the debate (3.10–13). The creed declared the Son homoousios, "of one substance", with the Father and anathematised those who said "there was when he was not". Arius and two Libyan bishops were exiled; Eusebius of Nicomedia, who signed the creed but not the anathemas, followed within months. The council also fixed a common Easter independent of the Jewish calendar (Life 3.18–20), issued twenty canons and ended at Constantine's twentieth-anniversary banquet, which Eusebius thought an image of Christ's kingdom (3.15).

The settlement did not hold. Constantine readmitted Arius about 327 and recalled Eusebius of Nicomedia; Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria from 328, refused to receive Arius, was condemned at Tyre in 335 and exiled to Trier. Arius died at Constantinople in 336. Constantine wanted unity, not a formula; the formula outlived him, and module 8.4 follows Constantius II's attempt to replace it. For how the Church's thinkers used Greek philosophy, see Greek module 7.4.

Where sources disagree. Eusebius of Caesarea, explaining his signature to his own church, says Constantine himself proposed homoousios and glossed it harmlessly (letter in Socrates, Church History 1.8); Philostorgius says Alexander and Ossius had agreed on the word beforehand at Nicomedia (1.7). Beneath lies a larger dispute: Rowan Williams (Arius: Heresy and Tradition, 1987) and R. P. C. Hanson (The Search for the Christian Doctrine of God, 1988) treat Arius as a conservative theologian and "Arianism" as a party created by Athanasius' polemic, which is also our main source for it.

08A new Rome on the Bosporus

Within weeks of Chrysopolis Constantine chose Byzantium, on the strait between his Danube and Persian fronts, and in November 324 marked out a far larger city, its land wall some fifteen stades beyond the old (Zosimus 2.30). Philostorgius has him pacing the line with a spear, saying he would go on until the one walking ahead of him stopped (2.9). The city was dedicated on 11 May 330 (module 0.3) and given a senate, a grain dole, a hippodrome joined to the palace, a forum with a porphyry column for the founder's statue, and art stripped from the Greek world: Eusebius lists the Delphic tripods set up in the hippodrome (Life 3.54), and Jerome's Chronicle under 330 says "dedicatur Constantinopolis omnium paene urbium nuditate", by stripping almost every other city bare. The church of the Holy Apostles was to be Constantine's tomb, his sarcophagus among twelve cenotaphs of the apostles (Life 4.58–60).

Where sources disagree. Eusebius says the city never saw an idol or a sacrifice (Life 3.48); Zosimus says Constantine built it temples to Rhea and to the Fortune of Rome (2.31.2–3). Gilbert Dagron (Naissance d'une capitale, 1974) traced how Constantine's city became a "New Rome" only by stages: a senate of equal rank under Constantius II, and the title itself in the third canon of the council of 381.

09The legislation

Constantine's laws survive as dated, addressed excerpts in the Theodosian and Justinianic codes (module 9.1), and their mixture shows his priorities. Some favour the Church: clergy freed from civic liturgies (Theodosian Code 16.2.2, 319), bequests to churches permitted (16.2.4, 321), manumission in church made valid (4.7.1, 321), bishops' arbitration made binding (1.27.1). Traditional cult was restrained only at the edges: private haruspicy was banned (9.16.1), while the haruspices were to be consulted by ancient custom when lightning struck the palace (16.10.1). Courts and workshops rested on "venerabili die solis", the venerable day of the Sun (Code of Justinian 3.12.2, 321). Many laws are harsh in the old manner, such as molten lead for nurses who abetted an abduction (Theodosian Code 9.24.1) and chains for fugitive tenants (5.17.1, 332); others reach for mercy, forbidding branding on the face, formed in the likeness of heavenly beauty (9.40.2), and sending criminals to the mines rather than the arena because "cruenta spectacula" displease in peacetime (15.12.1, 325). Yet the Hispellum rescript (CIL XI 5265, 333–337), cited in module 1.2 for the games at Volsinii, still allowed a gladiatorial show there, and let the Umbrians build a temple to the emperor's family provided it was not polluted by the deceits of contagious superstition, probably meaning sacrifice. The Augustan penalties on the unmarried were repealed (8.16.1, 320) and unilateral divorce confined to grave causes (3.16.1, 331). The split of the army into field and frontier troops belongs to module 9.2.

Where sources disagree. Eusebius presents the family laws as Christian reform (Life 4.26); Judith Evans Grubbs (Law and Family in Late Antiquity, 1995) found most of the marriage legislation rooted in Roman moral traditionalism, with Christian influence hard to prove. On sacrifice the witnesses collide: Eusebius says a law forbade it (Life 2.45), and a law of his sons in 341 cited their father's law against it (Theodosian Code 16.10.2), but Libanius says Constantine took the temples' money and changed nothing in the lawful worship (Oration 30.6). Barnes ("Constantine's prohibition of pagan sacrifice", 1984) accepts a general ban in 324.

10Baptism, death and the debate over the conversion

In spring 337 Constantine was preparing war on Shapur II of Persia, with bishops in his suite (Life 4.56–57). He fell ill after Easter and at a villa near Nicomedia asked for baptism, saying he had hoped to receive it in the Jordan (4.61–62); he put off the purple, wore white, and died at Pentecost, 22 May 337 (4.62–64). Eusebius does not name the baptiser; Jerome's Chronicle does: Eusebius of Nicomedia, Arius' patron. Late baptism was normal for men whose office required killing, so it is no evidence of a late conversion. He was buried among his apostles in Constantinople, yet he was enrolled among the gods (Eutropius 10.8), and his consecration coins show him veiled in a chariot, reaching for a hand from heaven (Life 4.73). The killings of his relatives that summer open module 8.4; the legend of his baptism by Pope Silvester and the forged Donation of Constantine, exposed by Lorenzo Valla in 1440, belong to module 10.4.

When, how and how sincerely he became a Christian is the oldest question in the field, and the evidence above pulls both ways.

H. A. 11Self-test

  1. Why does the panegyric of 310 matter for the history of the vision, and what modern argument builds on it?
    Answer

    It is the only account written within weeks of an experience: Apollo with Victory offering wreaths of thirty years at a Gallic temple (Pan. Lat. VI(7).21.3–5), two years before the Christian sign of 312. Weiss argued that the same event, a solar halo, was later retold as the sign of Christ.

  2. How do Lactantius' and Eusebius' accounts of the Christian sign differ, and why is Eusebius' version suspect?
    Answer

    Lactantius has a dream the night before the battle and a monogram painted on shields (44.5–6); Eusebius a midday cross of light with "by this conquer", seen by the army on an earlier march, then a dream of Christ and the labarum (Life 1.27–32). Eusebius heard it from Constantine long afterwards, and his own Church History mentions no vision.

  3. What was the "Edict of Milan" in fact?
    Answer

    A letter of Licinius to the governor of Bithynia, posted at Nicomedia on 13 June 313 (Lactantius 48; Eusebius, Church History 10.5), applying in the East the policy agreed at Milan: free choice of religion for all and restitution of Christian property. Seeck (1891) showed that no edict was issued at Milan.

  4. What do the inscription and decoration of the Arch of Constantine tell us about Rome in 315?
    Answer

    The Senate credited the victory to "instinctu divinitatis", an unnamed divinity both sides could accept. Reused Trajanic, Hadrianic and Aurelian reliefs set Constantine among the good emperors, the Hadrianic roundels show sacrifice, Sol and Luna appear, and there is no Christian sign.

  5. Why did the Donatist dispute matter beyond Africa?
    Answer

    It made the emperor the arbiter of church quarrels: a council at Rome (313), then Arles (314), his own hearing (316), coercion from 317 and surrender in 321. It set the pattern of appeal, council and coercion, and showed its limits.

  6. What did the Council of Nicaea decide, and why did it not end the dispute?
    Answer

    It declared the Son homoousios with the Father, anathematised "there was when he was not", fixed a common Easter and issued twenty canons. Constantine wanted unity rather than a formula, so he readmitted Arius about 327, recalled Eusebius of Nicomedia and exiled Athanasius in 335.

  7. Why are the deaths of Crispus and Fausta a problem of sources, and what did pagans make of them?
    Answer

    No contemporary explains them; Eusebius erased Crispus, and Zosimus, the Epitome and Philostorgius give conflicting motives. Zosimus, after Eunapius, made the murders the reason Constantine turned to a faith that forgave everything; Sozomen (1.5) answered that the favour to Christians came first.

  8. What evidence complicates the picture of Constantinople as a Christian city from its foundation?
    Answer

    Zosimus reports temples to Rhea and the Fortune of Rome (2.31.2–3), against Eusebius' claim of a city without idols (Life 3.48); even Eusebius records the Delphic tripods in the hippodrome (3.54). Dagron traced how the "New Rome" of equal rank grew only under Constantine's successors.

  9. Show with two laws how Constantine's legislation combined favour to the Church with tolerance of traditional cult.
    Answer

    Bequests to churches were permitted (Theodosian Code 16.2.4) and episcopal arbitration made binding (1.27.1), yet the haruspices were to be consulted when lightning struck the palace (16.10.1), and the day of rest was "the venerable day of the Sun" (Code of Justinian 3.12.2). The Hispellum rescript allowed a temple to his family if it was kept free of contagious superstition.

  10. Contrast Burckhardt's and Barnes's verdicts, and say what the deathbed baptism does and does not show.
    Answer

    Burckhardt saw an ambitious, essentially unreligious ruler using the Church; Barnes a committed Christian from 312 who pursued a Christian reformation after 324. Deathbed baptism was normal for men whose office required killing, so it does not show a late conversion.

12Carry forward

  1. Read every Constantinian source by its date and audience: the panegyrics of 310 and 313, Lactantius about 315 and Eusebius after 337 tell one story progressively rewritten, and the later the account, the more Christian and the more shaped it is.
  2. The Christian empire began as patronage and arbitration, not prohibition: money, exemptions, courts and councils for bishops, with the emperor as judge of their quarrels. The pattern of imperial councils and coercion set by the Donatists and Nicaea governs the rest of the late empire.
  3. Ambiguity was a method: an unnamed divinity on the arch and in the Milan letter, Sol beside the Chi-Rho, a Sunday named for the Sun, let pagans and Christians each read in their own god. Constantine's successors were forced to choose (module 8.4).
