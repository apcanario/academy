01Sources: a founder written down five centuries late

Varro's date for the foundation, 21 April 753 BC, became canonical; the rival dates belong to module 0.3. No Roman wrote the story down until about 200 BC, five and a half centuries later. Quintus Fabius Pictor, a senator writing Greek prose around the Second Punic War, gave the first Roman narrative. Plutarch says he followed the Greek Diocles of Peparethus in most points (Romulus 3.1); Dionysius says Cincius Alimentus, Cato and Calpurnius Piso followed Fabius (Roman Antiquities 1.79.4). Ennius' Annales, of the 180s and 170s BC, put the story into Latin verse. The full narratives are Augustan or later: Livy 1.3.10–16.8, written about 27–25 BC, seven centuries after the date he accepts; Dionysius, Roman Antiquities 1.76–90 and 2.1–56, published from 7 BC; and Plutarch's Romulus, about AD 100, paired with Theseus (Greek module 3.3). Ovid's Fasti ties episodes to festivals. All of them knew the versions conflicted.

02Alba, the Vestal and the wolf

Proca, king of Alba Longa, left the throne to his elder son Numitor. Amulius, the younger, drove him out, killed his male offspring and made his daughter Rhea Silvia a Vestal, ending her hope of children "under the appearance of honour" (Livy 1.3.10–11). Raped, she bore twins and named Mars as their father, "whether she believed it, or because a god was a more honourable author of the fault" (1.4.2). Dionysius (1.77.1) reports that others blamed a suitor, or Amulius himself in armour. She was chained in custody (Livy 1.4.3); in Fabius' version Amulius' own daughter Antho begged for her life (Plutarch 3).

The twins were ordered drowned, but the Tiber was in flood and the servants left the basket in the shallows by the fig tree later called Ruminalis, at the foot of the Palatine. A she-wolf coming down to drink heard the crying, suckled them and licked them (Livy 1.4.4–6). Plutarch adds the woodpecker, sacred to Mars, which brought them food, and derives the fig's name from ruma, the teat (4). The royal herdsman Faustulus took the boys to his wife Larentia (Livy 1.4.7). In Dionysius' Fabian version he watches the wolf suckling them before a cave, the Lupercal, where a bronze group "of ancient workmanship" stood in Dionysius' own day (1.79.6–8). Livy (1.5.1–2) traces the Lupercalia to Evander's Arcadian rite of Lycaean Pan (modules 1.3 and 2.3). The boys grew up hunting and raiding brigands (Livy 1.4.8–9).

Where sources disagree. The mother's name and fate vary. Livy's Rhea Silvia stays in prison; Dionysius (1.78–79) records that she was killed at once, or hidden until her sons freed her. Naevius and Ennius call her Ilia, daughter of Aeneas, and Ennius has her thrown into the river, whose god takes her as wife, the version Horace's "uxorious" Tiber presupposes (Odes 1.2.17–20). Ovid calls her simply Silvia (Fasti 3.11).

03Recognition, augury and the death of Remus

In Livy (1.5.3–7) brigands ambushed the twins during the Lupercalia and took Remus, who was handed to Numitor for punishment. Numitor, struck by his age and bearing, suspected the truth; Faustulus told Romulus; the brothers killed Amulius and restored Numitor (1.6.1–2). Dionysius (1.79–83) makes the trigger a quarrel over pasture. Plutarch (8) admits that the story looks "dramatic and fictitious", but fortune, he says, is sometimes a poet.

Wishing to found a city where they had been raised, the twins fell into "the ancestral evil, desire for kingship" (avitum malum, regni cupido, Livy 1.6.4). Neither was senior, so they asked the gods. Romulus took the Palatine for his observation post, Remus the Aventine. Remus saw six vultures first; Romulus then saw twelve. Each side hailed its man king, one claiming priority, the other number, and Remus fell in the brawl (1.6.4–7.1). "The more common story" (1.7.2) is that Remus jumped the new walls in mockery and Romulus killed him, adding:

So perish whoever else shall leap over my walls. (Livy 1.7.2)

Dionysius (1.85–87) has Romulus claim birds before he had seen any; Remus died in the fighting, Faustulus with him, and Romulus buried his brother at Remoria. Plutarch (9–10) offers two killers: Romulus, or his companion Celer, who fled to Etruria and gave his name to the celeres, the swift. In Ovid, Celer strikes with a spade and Romulus weeps (Fasti 4.837–852); Remus' ghost later gives the Lemuria its name (5.451–484). Plutarch (11) describes the rite: Etruscan experts, a pit (mundus) filled with first-fruits and soil, and a bronze ploughshare drawn by a bull and a cow to cut the line of the wall, lifted where gates would stand. The day was the Parilia, 21 April (Plutarch 12).

Where sources disagree. Ennius' augury (Annales, quoted by Cicero, On Divination 1.107–108) puts Romulus, not Remus, on the Aventine, frames the contest as Roma against Remora, and makes Romulus' twelve birds a clear sign; Livy, Dionysius and Plutarch put Romulus on the Palatine. Livy leaves the result open, while Dionysius and Plutarch (9) report that Romulus cheated. The killer is a nameless brawler in Livy's first version, Romulus in his second, Celer in Ovid and Plutarch.

04The asylum, the Sabine women and Titus Tatius

The city needed people. Romulus opened an asylum "between the two groves" on the Capitol for any man, free or slave, without question (Livy 1.8.5–6; Plutarch 9.3). It also needed women. When the neighbours refused intermarriage, Romulus held games for Neptune Equester, the Consualia, and at a signal the young Romans seized the visiting girls of Caenina, Crustumerium, Antemnae and the Sabines (Livy 1.9). Livy gives no number; Plutarch (14) reports thirty, 527 from Valerius Antias and 683 from Juba.

Romulus killed Acron, king of Caenina, with his own hand and dedicated the armour to Jupiter Feretrius, whose precinct was the first temple in Rome (Livy 1.10.4–7; Plutarch 16). These were the first spolia opima, spoils taken by a Roman commander from an enemy commander killed in single combat. Only Aulus Cornelius Cossus, against Lars Tolumnius of Veii (Livy 4.19–20), and Marcus Claudius Marcellus in 222 BC repeated the feat.

The Sabines of Cures under Titus Tatius came last. Tarpeia, daughter of the citadel's commander, let them in for "what they wore on their left arms", meaning their gold bracelets, and they crushed her under their shields (Livy 1.11.6–9). In the battle in the valley Romulus vowed a temple to Jupiter Stator, the Stayer, and the Romans held (1.12.3–7). Then the Sabine women, hair unbound, ran between their fathers and their husbands (1.13.1–3). Peace made one people: a joint kingship, Rome the seat, the citizens called Quirites after Cures (1.13.4–5); the curiae belong to module 1.5. Some years later Tatius was killed at Lavinium after his kinsmen abused Laurentine envoys, and Romulus was "less troubled than was proper" (1.14.1–3).

Where sources disagree. Tarpeia's motive was argued over from the start. Fabius and Cincius, says Dionysius, made it gold; Calpurnius Piso (in Dionysius 2.38–40) had her ask for the shields in order to disarm the enemy, which would explain the yearly libations at her tomb. Propertius (4.4) makes her a Vestal in love with Tatius; the Greek poet Simylus (in Plutarch 17) moved her treachery to the Gallic sack.

05The vanishing king and Quirinus

Romulus, dearer to the people than to the senators (Livy 1.15.8), was reviewing the army at the Goat's Marsh on the Campus Martius when a storm and thick cloud wrapped him; when it cleared he was gone (1.16.1). The senators said he had been taken up; the people hailed him a god; some muttered that the senators had torn him apart (1.16.4). Proculus Julius swore that Romulus had come down from the sky at dawn and declared that Rome would be capital of the world (1.16.5–7). Plutarch (27) sets the killing in Vulcan's precinct, each senator carrying a piece out under his robe, and has Romulus tell Proculus that he is now Quirinus (28). Ennius already had Romulus living in heaven "with the gods who begot him"; Cicero tells the eclipse and the vision in On the Republic 2.17–20. Quirinus himself was an old god with his own flamen and his festival on 17 February (Ovid, Fasti 2.475–512; module 2.2).

Where sources disagree. Livy calls the murder an obscure rumour; Dionysius (2.56) says the more plausible writers have Romulus killed by his own citizens for his harshness; Plutarch (28) argues that only souls, not bodies, rise to heaven. When Romulus became Quirinus is uncertain. Cicero is the earliest surviving author to state it outright; Ennius' prayer to "father Quirinus" may presuppose it, or may address the old god alone.

06Variants: other founders, other parents, a wolf who was a woman

Plutarch opens the Life with a catalogue (1–2). Rome was named from the Pelasgians' strength (rhōmē); from Rhome, a Trojan woman who burned the ships; from Romanus, son of Odysseus and Circe; from Romus, sent from Troy by Diomedes; or from Romis, a Latin tyrant. Those who do name Romulus make him son of Aeneas and Dexithea, or of Mars by Aeneas' daughter Aemilia. Dionysius (1.72–73) gives a parallel list; Wiseman (Remus, 1995) catalogues sixty-one Greek and Roman versions in all.

Promathion, an author of unknown date, told the strangest version (Plutarch 2). A phantom phallus rose from the hearth of Tarchetius, a cruel king of Alba; an oracle ordered a virgin to lie with it, and the king's daughter sent her maid instead. Vesta forbade Tarchetius to kill them, so he set them weaving a web to earn their freedom and had it unpicked each night. The maid's twins were left by the river, suckled by a she-wolf, fed by birds and raised by a cowherd; grown, they overthrew him. There is no Mars and no Rhea: the father is a power of the hearth, the mother a slave. The same hearth-birth is told of Servius Tullius (Dionysius 4.2; Pliny, Natural History 36.204) and of Caeculus of Praeneste, which suggests an old Latin pattern for the birth of kings.

Acca Larentia has three faces. She is Faustulus' wife. She is the wolf rationalised, since lupa also meant prostitute (whence lupanar, a brothel), and Livy (1.4.7), Dionysius (1.84.4) and Plutarch (4) report that some called her the wolf because she sold herself to the shepherds. And she is a second Larentia, a courtesan of Ancus Marcius' day, won at dice by Hercules' temple-keeper, who left her estates to the Roman people and was honoured at the Larentalia on 23 December (Plutarch 5; Gellius 7.7; Macrobius, Saturnalia 1.10.11–17). Masurius Sabinus (in Gellius 7.7.8) and Pliny (Natural History 18.6) make her the mother of twelve sons, the first Arval Brothers, with Romulus taking a dead son's place. Many scholars see an old goddess behind all three; uncertain.

07The earliest evidence

The Roman literature begins about 200 BC. Objects take the wolf and twins a century further back.

In 296 BC the brothers Gnaeus and Quintus Ogulnius, plebeian curule aediles who as tribunes in 300 had opened the pontificate and augurate to plebeians (Livy 10.6–9), set up the twins under the wolf from fines levied on moneylenders (10.23.11–12). In 65 BC lightning struck a gilded wolf with the infant Romulus on the Capitol (Cicero, Against Catiline 3.19). Neither group survives.

Where sources disagree. The mirror is the earliest image, but of what? Adam and Briquel (1982) read it as Praenestine work showing the twins in a Latin rather than Etruscan setting, and most still see Romulus and Remus with Faustulus or a Lupercus. Wiseman (1993) made the twins the Lares Praestites and the onlookers Mercury and Lara, their parents, which would leave the Ogulnii group as the first certain Romulus and Remus.

The Capitoline wolf stood at the Lateran in the Middle Ages, where courts sat "at the wolf"; Sixtus IV gave it to the people of Rome in 1471, and the twins were added soon after, perhaps by Antonio del Pollaiuolo. Winckelmann judged it Etruscan, and later scholars dated it about 480–470 BC. Restoring it in 1997, Anna Maria Carruba found it cast in a single pour, a technique she argued was medieval (La lupa capitolina: un bronzo medievale, 2006). Radiocarbon tests at the University of Salento, on organic residue in the casting core, gave a date of about 1021–1153 at 95 per cent confidence; thermoluminescence on the core agreed.

Where sources disagree. Adriano La Regina backed the medieval date on grounds of style, comparing Carolingian and Romanesque bronzes. Defenders of antiquity cited a lead-isotope study co-authored by the Capitoline curator Claudio Parisi Presicce (2005) tracing the copper to a Sardinian mine; later analyses have not confirmed the match. Most specialists now accept a medieval wolf, perhaps copying an ancient one.

08What the story is doing

Why Remus?

Remus is the puzzle: a twin whose only act is to die before the city exists. Theodor Mommsen ("Die Remuslegende", Hermes 16, 1881) read the twins as a projection of the two consuls; Mary Beard objects that consuls did not kill each other. Hermann Strasburger (1968) argued that so discreditable a tale, with its raped priestess, beast's milk, fratricide, runaways and stolen wives, must have begun as Greek anti-Roman propaganda; yet Romans displayed the wolf with evident pride. Jaan Puhvel (1975) derived Remus from *Yemo-, "twin", the being sacrificed at creation, like Norse Ymir; Jan Bremmer (1987) read exposure and wild youth as a pattern of initiation. T. P. Wiseman (Remus: A Roman Myth, 1995) dated Remus to the fourth century. The twins mirror the power-sharing of patricians and plebeians in the consulship; Remus is the latecomer, from remorari, "to delay"; and his murder enters the story about 295 BC, after Sentinum, as the memory of a human sacrifice to protect the walls, spread by plays staged at the games.

Where sources disagree. Cornell (The Beginnings of Rome, 1995) treats the twin story as old and indigenous, and in reviewing Wiseman objected that Remus' death is told as murder, not sacrifice. Beard treats his reconstruction of lost plays as largely speculative. Carandini, at the other extreme, reads the legend as the memory of a real eighth-century founding. The evidence settles none of these.

A brother's blood

In the civil wars the fratricide became the city's original sin. Horace's seventh Epode, written in the triumviral civil wars, asks what madness drives Romans to arms and answers with bitter fates "and the crime of a brother's murder" (scelusque fraternae necis, 7.18): innocent Remus' blood, shed on the earth, is a curse on his descendants (7.19–20). Lucan (1.92–95) makes the first triumvirate an image of the first walls, "wet with a brother's blood". Tacitus has many excuse Nero's murder of Britannicus by "a kingship that cannot be shared" (Annals 13.17); Augustine pairs Romulus with Cain as founders by fratricide (City of God 15.5). Virgil's answer is "Quirinus with his brother Remus" giving laws (Aeneid 1.292–293): civil war over, the brothers reconciled.

A model and a warning

Romulus offered founder, conqueror and god; he also offered king, fratricide and a ruler killed by senators. In 45 BC Caesar's statue was set up in the temple of Quirinus (Dio 43.45.3), and Cicero wrote that he would rather Caesar shared a temple with Quirinus than with Salus, Safety (Letters to Atticus 12.45), a joke about how Romulus ended. The Lupercalia of 44, where Antony offered the diadem, was Romulus' festival (module 5.4). Twelve vultures greeted Octavian's first consulship in 43, as they had Romulus (Suetonius, Augustus 95). In 27 he passed over the name Romulus for Augustus, which Suetonius glosses with Ennius' line on Rome founded "by august augury" (Augustus 7.2); Dio says he feared the suspicion of kingship (53.16.7). He lived beside the hut of Romulus, rebuilt the Lupercal and the temples of Quirinus and Jupiter Feretrius (Res Gestae 19), denied Marcus Crassus the spolia opima in 29 BC (Dio 51.24.4), and set Romulus with the spoils opposite Aeneas in his Forum (Ovid, Fasti 5.563–566; module 6.2).

09What the ground shows

The huts and the hut of Romulus

Rome was inhabited long before 753. Bronze Age pottery comes from the Capitol and the Forum Boarium, and the Forum cemetery begins in the tenth century (module 1.1). On the Cermalus, the south-western corner of the Palatine, twentieth-century excavations found Iron Age hut floors cut into the tufa, with post-holes and drainage gutters, of the eighth century in their visible phase; they match the hut-shaped urns of Latial burials. The Romans kept a casa Romuli there, repaired but never enlarged (Dionysius 1.79.11), and another on the Capitol (Vitruvius 2.1.5); Dio records fires in the Palatine hut (48.43, 54.29). Augustus' house stood alongside. The huts prove a village, not a founder.

Carandini's wall

From 1985 Andrea Carandini excavated the north slope of the Palatine above the Sacra Via, and in 1988 his team reported an earth-and-timber wall on a stone footing, rebuilt into the sixth century, its first phase in the mid- or later eighth. He read a gap as the Porta Mugonia, a girl's grave goods under its threshold as a foundation deposit, and the line as the boundary Tacitus traces round the foot of the Palatine (Annals 12.24). In La nascita di Roma (1997) and Rome: Day One (2011) it became a real founder's rite of about 750 BC.

Where sources disagree. Wiseman ("Reading Carandini", JRS 91, 2001, and later reviews) accepts the finds but not the story: turning a wall into Romulus needs texts written five centuries later, and since 753 is a construct of third-century chronographers, its match with the pottery is "no more than an accident". Adam Ziółkowski argues that the walls are too flimsy for defences and that the gate cannot be demonstrated.

The Lapis Niger and the tomb of Romulus

Festus records a black stone in the Comitium marking a place of ill omen, meant, some said, for Romulus' tomb but used for Faustulus or for Hostus Hostilius (s.v. niger lapis); Varro, in Porphyrio's note on Horace's "bones of Quirinus" (Epodes 16.13), put the tomb behind the Rostra. In 1899 Giacomo Boni found the black pavement and beneath it an archaic altar, a column stump and a sixth-century cippus naming a rex (module 1.5). Coarelli identifies the complex with the Volcanal, where Plutarch's senators killed Romulus. In 2020 a sixth-century tufa chest found beneath the steps of the Curia was hailed in the press as the tomb of Romulus; it was empty, and at most it marks a cult place.

The Lupercal of 2007

In 2007 Irene Iacopi announced a domed chamber some 15 m below the House of Augustus, encrusted with shells and mosaic and crowned by a white eagle, and it was hailed as the Lupercal that Augustus rebuilt.

Where sources disagree. Adriano La Regina, Fausto Zevi and Henner von Hesberg replied that the chamber is a nymphaeum or dining room of Nero's time, and that the ancient sources put the Lupercal at the foot of the hill (Dionysius 1.32.3–5, 1.79.8), not high inside it. Most topographers now look lower, towards Sant'Anastasia. The chamber is real; its name is not established.

10Self-test

  1. Who wrote the first Roman narrative of the foundation, when, and how long after the traditional date?
    Answer

    Fabius Pictor, in Greek, about 200 BC: some five and a half centuries after 753. Plutarch says he followed Diocles of Peparethus in most points (Romulus 3.1), and Dionysius that Cincius, Cato and Piso followed him (1.79.4).

  2. What are the competing accounts of how Remus died?
    Answer

    Livy gives a brawl after the disputed augury and, as "the more common story", Romulus killing him for leaping the new walls (1.7.1–2). Dionysius has him die in the fighting with Faustulus; Ovid and Plutarch name Romulus' companion Celer as the killer.

  3. How does Ennius' augury differ from Livy's?
    Answer

    Ennius (in Cicero, On Divination 1.107–108) puts Romulus on the Aventine, makes the contest about the name Roma or Remora, and gives Romulus a clear sign. Livy puts Romulus on the Palatine and Remus on the Aventine and leaves the result disputed between priority and number.

  4. What were the spolia opima, and why did they matter to Augustus?
    Answer

    Spoils taken by a Roman commander from an enemy commander killed in single combat, dedicated to Jupiter Feretrius; Romulus won the first from Acron (Livy 1.10). Octavian rebuilt the temple and in 29 BC denied Crassus the honour; as Augustus he displayed Romulus carrying the spoils in his Forum.

  5. Why was Acca Larentia called lupa, and who is the other Larentia?
    Answer

    Lupa meant prostitute as well as she-wolf, and Livy 1.4.7, Dionysius 1.84 and Plutarch 4 report that some explained the wolf as Faustulus' wife, who sold herself to shepherds. The other Larentia is the courtesan of Ancus' day who left her estates to the Roman people and was honoured at the Larentalia (Plutarch 5; Gellius 7.7).

  6. What is distinctive about Promathion's version, and what does it connect with?
    Answer

    The twins are born to a slave girl and a phallus risen from the hearth of the Alban king Tarchetius, with no Mars and no Rhea Silvia (Plutarch 2). The hearth-birth matches the stories of Servius Tullius and Caeculus of Praeneste, suggesting a Latin pattern for royal births.

  7. Why can the Capitoline wolf no longer serve as early evidence, and what is the earliest secure evidence instead?
    Answer

    Its single-pour casting and a radiocarbon date of about 1021–1153 make it medieval, and the twins are fifteenth-century additions. The earliest images are the later fourth-century mirror, if it shows Romulus and Remus, the Ogulnii group of 296 BC (Livy 10.23.12) and the ROMANO didrachm of about 269–266.

  8. Summarise Wiseman's explanation of Remus and one objection to it.
    Answer

    Remus entered the story in the fourth century as an image of patrician and plebeian power-sharing, the "delayer" from remorari, and his death was added about 295 BC from a human sacrifice for the walls. Cornell objects that the death is told as murder, not sacrifice; Beard, that the lost plays it relies on are largely conjecture.

  9. How do Horace, Lucan and Virgil use the fratricide?
    Answer

    Horace (Epodes 7.17–20) makes Remus' blood the curse that drives Romans to civil war; Lucan (1.95) makes the first walls wet with a brother's blood an image of Rome shared among three masters. Virgil (Aeneid 1.292–293) answers with Quirinus and Remus giving laws together under Augustus.

  10. What do Carandini's wall and the 2007 cave prove, and what do they not?
    Answer

    The wall shows an eighth-century boundary on the Palatine's north slope, not that Romulus built it; Wiseman notes that 753 is a later construct and its match with the pottery accidental. The cave is a real decorated chamber, but La Regina, Zevi and von Hesberg date it to Nero's time and place the Lupercal lower, at the foot of the hill.

11Carry forward

  1. The foundation story was first written down by a Roman about 200 BC, five and a half centuries after its date, and never settled into one version: names, parents, hills and killers all vary. For every early Roman story, ask who first tells it, when, and what was at stake in Rome at that moment.
  2. Romulus is double-edged: founder, conqueror and god, but also fratricide, king and a ruler perhaps murdered by senators. Caesar and Augustus had to claim him while avoiding his fate, and from Horace to Augustine the twins stand for civil war.
  3. Archaeology confirms an eighth-century Palatine village with huts and a boundary wall, but it cannot reach a named person, and a rough match between pottery and a chronographers' date is no proof of a legend. Module 1.5 applies the same test to the kings.

The prompt this answers

Give me an in-depth report on Romulus and Remus and the foundation of Rome. Cover: the story in full as Livy book 1, Dionysius books 1 and 2 and Plutarch's Romulus tell it (Rhea Silvia and Mars, Amulius and Numitor, the exposure, the she-wolf and the woodpecker, Faustulus and Acca Larentia, the Lupercal, the recognition, the augury contest on the Palatine and the Aventine, the wall and the death of Remus, the asylum, the rape of the Sabine women, Tarpeia, Titus Tatius and the joint kingship, the first spolia opima, the disappearance in the storm and the deification as Quirinus); the variants (Remus killed by Celer, the competing parents and names, Plutarch's catalogue of versions including Promathion's, Acca Larentia as lupa, 'she-wolf' and prostitute); the earliest evidence (the statue group set up by the Ogulnii in 296 BC, the Etruscan mirror from Bolsena, the first coins with the wolf and twins, and the Capitoline wolf, whose scientific dating made it medieval); what the story is doing (the explanations for Remus from Mommsen to Wiseman, the fratricide as an image of civil war in Horace's Epodes 7 and Lucan, Romulus as a model and a warning for Caesar and Augustus); and what the ground shows (the Iron Age huts on the Palatine and the 'hut of Romulus', Carandini's Palatine wall of the later eighth century and its critics, the Lapis Niger and the 'tomb of Romulus', the Lupercal cave claimed in 2007). Format: about 3000 words, headed sections, name sources, flag disagreements, end with a 10-question self-test with answers and the three ideas I must carry forward.
