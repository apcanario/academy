01Patricians and plebeians: what the distinction was

Every narrative of the conflict is late. Livy wrote books 2 to 10, covering 509 to 293, in the 20s BC, three to five centuries after the events; Dionysius of Halicarnassus, fuller on the fifth century in books 5 to 11, published from 7 BC (module 0.1). Both inherited from annalists of the second and first centuries BC a contest between two orders: patricians who monopolised office and priesthood, plebeians who fought their way in.

The ancient explanation is genealogical: Romulus' hundred senators were the patres, their descendants the patricians (Livy 1.8.7), and Dionysius (2.9–10) makes everyone else their clients. What the patricians demonstrably held was religious. The auspices reverted to them when the magistracies lapsed, so the interrex was patrician; their senators ratified decisions by the patrum auctoritas; the rex sacrorum, the major flamines and the Salii stayed patrician to the end. The order was open early: Attus Clausus joined it with his clients in 504 (Livy 2.16.4–5).

The plebs was never a class: it held indebted smallholders and also rich families who later reached the consulship. It is defined by exclusion and, after 494, by its own organisation. Several consuls before 486 bear names later plebeian, Junius and Cassius among them; such names then vanish for decades, and the Twelve Tables forbade patrician-plebeian marriage. Many now speak of a "closing" of the patriciate in the early fifth century rather than of a primeval caste.

Where sources disagree. Niebuhr made the plebs a conquered people, like the Latins Ancus settled on the Aventine (Livy 1.33.2); Mommsen, nearer Dionysius, derived it from clients and outsiders. Momigliano and Cornell (The Beginnings of Rome, 1995) see in the patriciate a religious and political elite that closed itself after 509, the plebs being everyone it excluded. Richard Mitchell (Patricians and Plebeians, 1990) reads the distinction as religious and military in origin and denies a conflict of hereditary orders from the start.

02The first secession and the plebeian state, 494–471

In Livy debt drives the crisis. In 495 an old centurion, scarred in front and scourged behind, showed the Forum what his creditor had done to him (2.23.3–7). Relief was promised and refused, and in 494 the army, still bound by its oath, marched to the Sacred Mount beyond the Anio and camped there (2.31–32): a refusal to fight, not an assault. The Senate sent Menenius Agrippa, dear to the plebs as a man of plebeian origin (2.32.8). He told a fable. The limbs, resenting the idle belly, refused to feed it and the whole body wasted, until they saw that the belly also served, returning food to every part as blood (2.32.9–12; Dionysius 6.86). The plebs returned with magistrates of their own, tribunes to bring help (auxilium) against the consuls (2.33.1–3).

The plebs, not the community, created that power. Tribunes were sacrosanct by a collective oath, a lex sacrata making whoever harmed them sacer, killable without guilt; the jurists of Livy's day still held that their inviolability came from the oath, not from statute (3.55.10). From the inviolable body set between magistrate and citizen grew the veto. Two plebeian aediles kept the plebs' temple of Ceres, Liber and Libera on the Aventine, dedicated in 493 (Dionysius 6.90, 6.94) and later its archive (Livy 3.55.13). From 471 the Publilian law had the tribunes elected by the plebs voting in tribes (Livy 2.56–58). With its own officers, assembly, cult centre and resolutions (plebiscita), the concilium plebis was a state within the state.

Where sources disagree. Livy notes that Calpurnius Piso put the secession on the Aventine (2.32.3). Livy gives two first tribunes who co-opted three (2.33.2–3), Dionysius five (6.89); Piso had the number raised from two to five only in 471 (Livy 2.58.1), and ten came in 457 (3.30.7). The fable is Greek in form, close to an Aesopic tale and to Socrates' brothers made to work together like hands and feet (Xenophon, Memorabilia 2.3.18).

03Debt-bondage: nexum

Nexum, "binding", was a loan made by the ritual of bronze and scales in which the borrower pledged his person; a defaulter could be chained and worked by his creditor without any judgment. Republican jurists already disagreed on the term: Manilius applied it to every bronze-and-scales transaction, Mucius Scaevola only to those creating an obligation (Varro, On the Latin Language 7.105). Debt frames the period, from the centurion of 495 to the secession of 287, also over debts (Periocha 11).

Livy dates abolition to 326. Gaius Publilius, bound for his father's debt, was flogged by the moneylender Lucius Papirius for refusing his advances; the outcry carried a law that a debtor's goods, not his body, answer for money lent (8.28), velut aliud initium libertatis, "as it were a second beginning of liberty" (8.28.1). Solon had banned loans on the person at a stroke in 594/3 (Greek module 5.5). Rome needed nearly 170 years from the first secession, and the remedy came from a plebeian counter-state, not a mediator.

Where sources disagree. Varro (On the Latin Language 7.105) puts the abolition under the dictator Gaius Poetelius Libo Visolus, in 313, against Livy's 326. Tacitus (Annals 6.16) credits the Twelve Tables with the first ceiling on interest; Livy (7.16.1) gives it to a tribunician law of 357.

04The Decemvirate and the Twelve Tables, 451–449

From 462, Livy says, tribunes demanded that consular power be defined in writing (3.9). An embassy went to Athens in 454 to copy Solon's laws (3.31.8), and for 451 consuls and tribunes gave way to ten patrician decemviri charged with writing laws. Their ten tables passed the centuriate assembly: fons omnis publici privatique iuris, the source of all public and private law (3.34.6). A second board under Appius Claudius added two (3.37.4), including the marriage ban Cicero calls "a most inhuman law" (On the Republic 2.63).

That board ruled as a tyranny and would not leave office (3.36–38). Livy tells its fall through Verginia (3.44–48). Appius had a client claim the centurion Verginius' daughter as his slave and, judging the case himself, gave interim custody to the claimant against his own law. Her father killed her in the Forum rather than surrender her; the armies mutinied, the plebs seceded again, to the Aventine and the Sacred Mount (3.50–52), and the decemvirs abdicated. Diodorus (12.24) tells the story briefly; module 3.2 treats Verginia as exemplum.

No text survives. The bronze tables (Livy 3.57.10) are reconstructed from quotations, often modernised, in Cicero, who learned them by heart as a boy (On the Laws 2.59), in Festus, Gellius, Pliny and Macrobius, and in the jurists, chiefly Gaius' commentary excerpted in the Digest. The table numbers are modern, from Jacques Godefroy (1616); Crawford's Roman Statutes (1996) reorders much.

What survives is procedure and private law for a farming community of households. Litigation begins with self-help: si in ius vocat, ito, "if he summons to court, let him go". A judgment debtor, after thirty days' grace and sixty in chains, was shown at three markets, and then (Gellius 20.1.48–49):

tertiis nundinis partis secanto. si plus minusve secuerunt, se fraude esto.

"On the third market day let them cut shares; if they cut more or less, let it be no offence"; or he was sold across the Tiber. A son sold three times is free of his father (Gaius, Institutes 1.132); a patron who cheats his client is sacer (Servius on Aeneid 6.609); assault costs twenty-five units of bronze as poena, a Greek loanword (Gellius 20.1.12); charming away a neighbour's crops is a crime (Pliny, Natural History 28.17–18); funeral display is capped, on Solon's model, Cicero says (On the Laws 2.59–64). Two clauses are constitutional: no laws against individuals, and capital cases only before the centuriate assembly (On the Laws 3.44). The plebeian gain was publication, though the forms of action stayed with the pontiffs until Gnaeus Flavius published them in 304 (Livy 9.46.5).

Where sources disagree. Ettore Pais and Édouard Lambert (1902) dismissed the Tables as a later compilation, Lambert's by Sextus Aelius Paetus about 200 BC; Wieacker, Watson and Crawford accept a fifth-century code on the evidence of its archaic language and society. The Athenian embassy is widely doubted, Greek influence much less: Pomponius (Digest 1.2.2.4) and Pliny (Natural History 34.21) name Hermodorus of Ephesus as adviser, and Gaius matched a boundary rule to Solon's (Digest 10.1.13). Most think the models came through southern Italy.

05From the Valerio-Horatian laws to the consular tribunes, 449–367

The consuls of 449, Lucius Valerius and Marcus Horatius, carried three laws (Livy 3.55): that what the plebs resolved by tribes should bind the people; that no magistracy without appeal (provocatio) be created, renewing Publicola's law of 509 (module 1.6); and that whoever harmed tribunes or aediles should forfeit his head to Jupiter.

In 445 the tribune Gaius Canuleius repealed the marriage ban against patrician warnings that the auspices would be confounded (Livy 4.1–6). A bill to open the consulship produced a compromise: from 444 military tribunes with consular power, three at first and six from 405, replaced the consuls in about two years out of three until 367, with plebeians eligible (4.6.8). The patricians kept the census in a new censorship (443; 4.8), and the quaestorship was opened in 421 (4.43). Yet plebeians first won it in 409 (4.54.2–3), and the first plebeian consular tribune, Publius Licinius Calvus, came in 400 (5.12.9): eligibility did not win elections in assemblies weighted towards the rich.

Where sources disagree. Livy gives two reasons for the consular tribunate: to keep plebeians from the consulship (4.6.8), or, in some authors, too many simultaneous wars for two consuls (4.7.2). Forsythe (2005) links it to a military reorganisation that also produced the censorship; Cornell (1995) finds neither explanation convincing, since dictators were still named for wars and plebeians appear so late. Uncertain.

06From the Licinio-Sextian laws to the lex Hortensia, 367–287

Livy's book 6 makes the decisive episode a ten-year struggle (6.34–42), sparked when Fabia, married to a plebeian, envied her sister, a consular tribune's wife, as the lictor's rod rapped at the door (6.34.5–10). In 376 the tribunes Gaius Licinius Stolo and Lucius Sextius Lateranus proposed that interest paid be deducted from debts; that no one hold more than 500 iugera of public land; and that one consul be plebeian (6.35.4–5). They blocked curule elections for five years (6.35.10), an anarchy the Varronian chronology must absorb (module 0.3). The laws passed in 367 with a half-plebeian board for the Sibylline Books. Lucius Sextius was consul in 366, and the patricians took compensation in a praetor for jurisdiction and two curule aediles (6.42).

The Ogulnian law opened the colleges that interpreted sacred law and the auspices, the patricians' last argument; Livy gives Appius Claudius the opposition and Decius Mus the reply (10.7–8). The last secession, to the Janiculum over debts, ended when the plebeian dictator Quintus Hortensius carried a law that what the plebs ordered should bind all Romans (Periocha 11; Pliny, Natural History 16.37). Livy's book 11 is lost; jurists writing four centuries and more later confirm that plebiscites had not bound patricians before (Laelius Felix in Gellius 15.27.4; Gaius, Institutes 1.3).

Where sources disagree. Three laws, of 449, 339 and 287, make plebiscites binding. Beloch, Ogilvie and Forsythe treat the first two as anticipations of the Hortensian law; Cornell argues that statutes were often re-enacted and that the earlier laws carried conditions, perhaps the patrum auctoritas, removed by stages. The consulship clause is doubted too: two patrician consuls appear in several years from 355 to 343 and never after 342, so von Fritz (1950) read 367 as an administrative reform, and many make the Genucian law the real guarantee.

07The settlement: nobility, magistrates, Senate and assemblies

The result was a merged elite, not a plebeian victory. The leading plebeian families, Licinii, Genucii, Marcii, Publilii, Decii, shared the consulship with the surviving patrician clans and soon their interests: Licinius Stolo was fined under his own law for holding a thousand iugera (Livy 7.16.9). Gelzer (1912) defined the nobiles as families with a consul among their ancestors; Hölkeskamp (Die Entstehung der Nobilität, 1987) dates the formation of this patricio-plebeian nobility to the generations after 367 (module 0.3). The tribunate became a rung for young nobles. The patricians kept the interregnum and the old priesthoods; both consuls were first plebeian in 172.

The Senate of about three hundred was enrolled by the censors under the Ovinian law, of uncertain date before 312, from the best men of every order (Festus, s.v. praeteriti senatores); in practice it became a body of former magistrates, whose decrees were advice with customary authority. Of the three assemblies, the curiate survived to confer imperium. The centuriate, weighted by wealth (module 1.5), elected consuls, praetors and censors, declared war and heard capital cases. The tribal assembly elected quaestors and curule aediles and legislated; the plebeian council was the same tribes without patricians, and after 287 its plebiscites carried most legislation. No law yet fixed the cursus honorum: the Genucian ten-year rule restrained iteration, yet Valerius Corvus held six consulships. Rank was already ordered, the censorship normally going to former consuls, but the sequence stayed a matter of custom until the lex Villia annalis of 180 set minimum ages for each office (Livy 40.44.1; deep dive module 11.1). Cato called Rome's constitution the work of many men over centuries, not of one lawgiver (Cicero, On the Republic 2.2), the reverse of Athens under Solon and Cleisthenes. Polybius' mixed constitution is module 4.5; the system at its height, deep dive module 11.1.

08Livy books 2 to 10 and the Gracchan lens

The framework is suspiciously late-Republican: agrarian bills almost yearly in books 2 to 4, debt relief, an obstructive Senate, secessions, appeals. Livy's named sources besides Fabius Pictor, Piso, Valerius Antias, Claudius Quadrigarius, Licinius Macer and Aelius Tubero, wrote after 133: Piso amid the Gracchan crisis, the rest after Sulla had curbed the tribunate (modules 5.1 and 5.2). Macer, tribune in 73, campaigned for its restoration (Sallust, Histories 3) and, Livy complains, sought praise for his family (7.9.5); Licinii supply a first tribune, the first plebeian consular tribune and the laws of 367. The appeal law was carried three times, Livy notes, semper a familia eadem, "always by the same family", the Valerii (10.9.3), and Valerius Antias is suspected of flattering his namesakes.

Specific projections show. Spurius Cassius' land bill of 486 includes Latins and Hernici (Livy 2.41), like Gaius Gracchus' programme for the allies; Mommsen read Cassius, Maelius and Manlius as Gracchan demagogues (module 3.2). The 500-iugera limit is Tiberius Gracchus' figure. Camillus' temple of Concord for 367 (Plutarch, Camillus 42) recalls Opimius' Concord after Gaius Gracchus' death in 121; Momigliano ("Camillus and Concord", 1942) judged the earlier one invented. The speeches, Canuleius' (Livy 4.3–5) or Dionysius', speak first-century idiom (Gabba, Dionysius and the History of Archaic Rome, 1991).

Against wholesale projection stand the lists: named laws, the Fasti's firsts and the odd colleges of consular tribunes, which no Gracchan pamphleteer needed, and the Twelve Tables' fifth-century world of debt and patronage. Cato's speech for the Rhodians of 167 cites a penalty for wanting more than 500 iugera (Gellius 6.3.37). Livy says the record grows clearer and surer after the Gallic sack (6.1.2–3). The skeleton is old, the flesh late.

Where sources disagree. Forsythe (A Critical History of Early Rome, 2005) treats much of the fifth-century narrative as reconstruction; Mitchell (1990) doubts a struggle of hereditary orders at all. Cornell (1995) and Raaflaub (Social Struggles in Archaic Rome, 1986; 2nd edn 2005) accept a real conflict in two phases, poorer plebeians seeking protection and debt relief in the fifth century and rich plebeians seeking office in the fourth, which the annalists fused. Module 0.1 gives Wiseman's case that such reconstruction was accepted method.

09Self-test

  1. What did the patricians demonstrably hold that plebeians did not, and why do the early Fasti matter to the debate on the orders' origin?
    Answer

    The auspices (hence the patrician interrex), the patrum auctoritas and certain priesthoods such as the rex sacrorum and major flamines. Consuls with later plebeian names before 486 suggest that the patriciate closed itself in the early fifth century rather than being a primeval caste (Cornell).

  2. On what did the tribunes' sacrosanctity rest, and why does that make the office revolutionary?
    Answer

    On the plebs' collective oath, a lex sacrata making anyone who harmed a tribune sacer; Livy says the jurists still held this in his day (3.55.10). The power was created by a part of the community against the magistrates, not granted by the community's law.

  3. What was nexum, and how and when did it end?
    Answer

    A loan by bronze and scales in which the debtor pledged his person and could be bound by the creditor without judgment. The lex Poetelia, of 326 in Livy (8.28) or 313 in Varro (On the Latin Language 7.105), made the debtor's goods, not his body, answerable.

  4. How do the Twelve Tables survive, and why should you not trust a reference such as "Table VIII.21" too far?
    Answer

    Only as quotations and paraphrases in Cicero, Festus, Gellius, Pliny, Macrobius and the jurists, Gaius above all, via the Digest. The assignment to numbered tables is a modern reconstruction from Godefroy (1616), and Crawford's 1996 edition reorders much of it.

  5. Give three provisions of the Tables and what each shows about early Roman society.
    Answer

    Creditors may cut a judgment debtor into shares or sell him across the Tiber (Gellius 20.1.48–49): debt was enforced on the body. A son sold three times is free of his father (Gaius 1.132): paternal power was near absolute. A patron who defrauds his client is sacer (Servius on Aeneid 6.609): clientship was a sacred bond.

  6. What part does Verginia play in Livy's constitutional narrative?
    Answer

    Her case is the decemvirs' fall: Appius Claudius breaks his own law on interim custody, her father kills her, the armies mutiny and the plebs secede a second time (Livy 3.44–52). The abdication leads directly to the Valerio-Horatian laws of 449, as Lucretia's death led to the consulship.

  7. Why were the military tribunes with consular power created, and what shows that eligibility did not bring plebeians office?
    Answer

    Livy offers a political motive, to keep plebeians out of the consulship (4.6.8), and a military one, too many wars for two consuls (4.7.2); moderns divide. The first plebeian consular tribune was elected only in 400 (5.12.9) and the first plebeian quaestors in 409 (4.54.2–3): forty-four and twelve years after the two offices were opened.

  8. What did the Licinio-Sextian rogations propose, and what did the patricians gain in return?
    Answer

    Relief of debts by deducting interest paid, a limit of 500 iugera of public land, and the end of consular tribunes with one consul plebeian (Livy 6.35.4–5), plus a half-plebeian board for the Sibylline Books. The patricians obtained the new praetorship and the curule aedileship (6.42.11–14).

  9. Why do three laws (449, 339, 287) each make plebiscites binding, and what did the Hortensian law certainly do?
    Answer

    Either the first two are annalistic anticipations (Beloch, Ogilvie, Forsythe) or each carried conditions, such as patrician ratification, removed by stages (Cornell). The Hortensian law made plebiscites bind all citizens, patricians included (Gellius 15.27.4; Gaius, Institutes 1.3).

  10. Give two signs that Livy's account is coloured by the Gracchan age and two arguments that its skeleton is nonetheless old.
    Answer

    Spurius Cassius' land bill including Latins and Hernici (2.41) and the 500-iugera limit mirror Gracchan proposals, and Licinius Macer and Valerius Antias promoted their own families. But the laws' names and the Fasti's firsts are list data, the Twelve Tables are fifth-century, and Cato's speech of 167 already knows a 500-iugera penalty (Gellius 6.3.37).

10Carry forward

  1. The Struggle of the Orders produced a broadened aristocracy, not a democracy: the patricio-plebeian nobility of 367–287 governed through Senate and magistracies, and the tribunate was absorbed into the state. Read Polybius' mixed constitution and the Millar debate (module 4.5 and deep dive module 11.1) against that outcome.
  2. The tribunate kept its revolutionary origin: sacrosanct by oath, armed with help, veto and the binding plebiscite, it lay dormant until the Gracchi used it in 133; and the annalists who wrote after 133 painted the early tribunes in Gracchan colours, so the traffic between 494 and 133 runs both ways.
  3. Separate skeleton from flesh: named laws, the Fasti's firsts and the Twelve Tables are early evidence; speeches, motives and the two-bloc drama were written three to five centuries after the events, through late-Republican concerns.

The prompt this answers

Give me an in-depth report on the Struggle of the Orders and the making of the Republic's constitution, 509 to 287 BC. Cover: patricians and plebeians and the debate over what the distinction originally was; the first secession of 494 BC, Menenius Agrippa's fable of the belly, the tribunes of the plebs and their sacrosanctity, the plebeian council and the aediles; debt-bondage (nexum); the Decemvirate and the Twelve Tables of 451–450 BC (what survives, how it survives, and what it shows of early Roman society), Appius Claudius and Verginia, the second secession; the Valerio-Horatian laws, the Canuleian law on intermarriage, the military tribunes with consular power; the Licinio-Sextian laws of 367 BC and the first plebeian consul; the lex Ogulnia opening the priesthoods; the lex Hortensia of 287 BC; and the result: the patricio-plebeian nobility, the consuls, praetors, censors and dictator, the Senate and the three assemblies, and the cursus honorum taking shape. Then the question of reliability: how much of Livy books 2 to 10 is the conflicts of the Gracchan age projected backwards. Format: about 2500 words, headed sections, name sources, flag disagreements, end with a 10-question self-test with answers and the three ideas I must carry forward.
