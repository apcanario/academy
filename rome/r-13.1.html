01Edicts and expulsions, 181–92 BC

Module 4.5 tells the first three episodes, and each needs only a sentence here. In 181 a praetor had Greek books of philosophy, found in a chest at the supposed tomb of Numa, burned as dangerous to religion (Livy 40.29). In 161 the Senate authorised the praetor Marcus Pomponius to see that philosophers and rhetors did not remain in Rome (Gellius 15.11.1). In 155 Carneades argued for justice one day and against it the next (Lactantius, Divine Institutes 5.14), and Cato urged the Senate to send the embassy home (Plutarch, Cato Maior 22).

Two further measures complete the series. Athenaeus records that the Romans expelled two Epicureans, Alcaeus and Philiscus, in the consulship of a Lucius Postumius, because of the pleasures they introduced (12.547a); Aelian repeats the story and makes the young their victims (Historical Miscellany 9.12). The likely consuls of that name held office in 173 and 154, so the date is uncertain.

In 92 the censors Gnaeus Domitius Ahenobarbus and Lucius Licinius Crassus, the orator, issued an edict that Suetonius (On Grammarians and Rhetoricians 25) and Gellius (15.11.2) preserve. Men had set up a new kind of teaching and called themselves Latin rhetors, and young men idled whole days in their schools; the ancestors had laid down what their children should learn and which schools they should attend; these novelties, against custom and ancestral usage, neither pleased the censors nor seemed right. The edict declared that displeasure to teachers and pupils alike and expelled no one. In Cicero's dialogue Crassus explains that the new masters could teach nothing but boldness, a school of impudence (On the Orator 3.93–94); Tacitus remembered the phrase (Dialogue on Orators 35.1).

None of these measures names a doctrine. Each targets teachers and their hold on the young, asserts that the ancestors decide what a Roman boy learns, and works by declaration with little enforcement: within a generation of 161 a Stoic lived in Scipio Aemilianus' house. Erich Gruen (Studies in Greek Culture and Roman Policy, 1990) reads the series as gestures by which the governing class displayed its authority over Hellenism rather than attempts to stop it. The edict of 92 was not even aimed at Greeks, but at Romans teaching Romans in Latin.

Where sources disagree. The edict speaks only of custom and Cicero gives Crassus only a teacher's reason. Friedrich Marx, in his edition of the Rhetorica ad Herennium (1894), saw a political act against Latin rhetoric under Lucius Plotius Gallus, which would open oratory to men outside the Greek-educated nobility and to Marius' friends. Matthias Gelzer rejected the thesis, and Jürgen von Ungern-Sternberg (Chiron, 1973) found nothing certain about Plotius' politics. Suetonius' one witness is a letter of Cicero recalling that learned men kept him as a boy from Plotius' school, because Greek exercises trained the mind better (On Grammarians and Rhetoricians 26).

02Panaetius and the Romans who heard him

Module 4.5 places Panaetius in Scipio Aemilianus' household, at home and on campaign (Velleius Paterculus 1.13.3), and weighs the modern "Scipionic circle"; Greek module 12.7 gives the doctrines he softened or dropped. What matters here is what he taught Romans. Cicero's Laelius recalls Scipio discussing the state with Panaetius in Polybius' presence, two Greeks the most expert in public affairs (On the Republic 1.34). The teaching was practical: Panaetius wrote to Quintus Aelius Tubero on enduring pain (On Ends 4.23), and Cicero praises him for avoiding the gloom and harshness of the other Stoics (4.79). Laelius heard Diogenes of Babylon as a young man and Panaetius later (2.24).

His pupils show what the school meant in a Roman career. Publius Rutilius Rufus, who had heard Panaetius (On Duties 3.10), was condemned for extortion by an equestrian jury in 92 (module 5.2); he refused to let his advocates plead for pity and preferred conviction to a defence beneath the truth (On the Orator 1.229–230), and spent his exile at Smyrna (Tacitus, Annals 4.43). When Quintus Fabius Maximus gave the funeral feast for his uncle Scipio, Tubero, another nephew, spread the couches with goatskins and set out Samian crockery, and the voters, Cicero says, punished the austerity by rejecting him for the praetorship (Pro Murena 75–76). Athenaeus, probably following Posidonius, names Rutilius, Tubero and Mucius Scaevola as the only Romans who kept Fannius' sumptuary law (6.274c–e). The lessons were endurance, frugality and clean hands in office. Panaetius' On Proper Functions, the model of Cicero's On Duties, and its four personae are deep dive module 13.2's.

03Posidonius at Rhodes

Greek module 12.7 gives Posidonius' doctrine, his travels and his embassy to Rome in 87/86, when he saw Marius in his last illness (Plutarch, Marius 45). At Rhodes he became a fixed point for Romans going east. Cicero names him with Diodotus, Philo and Antiochus as the men who formed him (On the Nature of the Gods 1.6), and Plutarch says that at Rhodes Cicero studied oratory with Apollonius Molon and philosophy with Posidonius (Cicero 4). The tie outlasted the student years: in 60 Cicero sent him a Greek memoir of his consulship to be worked up in style, and Posidonius replied that reading it had not encouraged him to write but frightened him off (Letters to Atticus 2.1.2).

Pompey came as a conqueror. Returning from the Mithridatic war in 62, he heard the Rhodian teachers and gave each a talent, and Posidonius wrote up the lecture he gave before him (Plutarch, Pompey 42). Pliny says Pompey, about to enter Posidonius' house, forbade his lictor to knock as custom required and lowered his fasces to the door of learning, the man to whom East and West had submitted (Natural History 7.112). Pompey himself told the story Cicero repeats: he found the philosopher crippled by gout, and Posidonius lectured from his bed that nothing is good but the honourable, telling the pain at each spasm that it would never make him admit it was an evil (Tusculan Disputations 2.61). Strabo calls him Pompey's friend and says he wrote Pompey's history (11.1.6).

Both sides gained: the general showed reverence for Greek wisdom before the Greek world he was reordering, and the philosopher gained a patron and a subject. Rhodes, a free ally on the road to Asia, offered what Athens after Sulla's siege could not.

Where sources disagree. Cicero's own account of his travels names at Rhodes only Molon, the rhetor he had already heard at Rome (Brutus 316); Plutarch adds Posidonius (Cicero 4). Cicero's list of his teachers (On the Nature of the Gods 1.6) supports Plutarch, though the Brutus, which names Philo, Diodotus and Antiochus, is silent. Whether Cicero followed a course or paid calls cannot be settled.

04The philosopher in the house

Most Greek philosophers entered Roman life through a household. Deep dive module 13.3 takes Philodemus with Piso and Antiochus with Lucullus. The type was well established: Marcus Pupius Piso kept the Peripatetic Staseas of Naples with him for years (On Ends 5.8), and Crassus' companion Alexander the Peripatetic was lent a cloak for each journey and asked for it back on return (Plutarch, Crassus 3). The philosopher taught the sons, followed his patron to provinces and camps, and lent the house a name for learning.

Diodotus

Cicero worked with the Stoic Diodotus, above all at dialectic, and says that he lived in his house and had lately died there (Brutus 309). Blind in old age, Diodotus went on studying, had books read to him day and night, played the lyre in the Pythagorean manner and, what seemed impossible without sight, taught geometry (Tusculan Disputations 5.113). He died in 59 and left Cicero his property (Letters to Atticus 2.20.6). A resident of more than twenty-five years who made his patron his heir was a companion, not a hired tutor.

The two Athenodori

Strabo, listing the philosophers of Tarsus, keeps two Stoics of the name apart (14.5.14). Athenodorus Cordylion lived with Marcus Cato and died in his house. Plutarch tells how Cato, a military tribune in Macedonia about 67, used his leave to sail to Pergamum and persuade the old man, who had refused the friendship of kings and governors, to join him, and came back prouder of this capture than Pompey and Lucullus of their kingdoms (Cato Minor 10). He is probably the Athenodorus who, as keeper of the library at Pergamum, cut the embarrassing passages from Zeno's Republic (Diogenes Laertius 7.34; Greek module 12.7). Deep dive module 13.4 follows him into Cato's Stoicism.

Athenodorus son of Sandon taught the young Octavian and was greatly honoured by him; in old age he went home to Tarsus and, with the authority Augustus gave him, drove out Boethus, who had risen by flattering Antony, and reformed the city's government (Strabo 14.5.14). A saying in Plutarch's collection shows the courtier: taking leave because of his age, he advised Augustus, when angry, to say or do nothing until he had recited the twenty-four letters of the alphabet, and Augustus took his hand and kept him another year (Sayings of Kings and Commanders 207c).

Arius

Arius of Alexandria was the court philosopher in the full sense: Suetonius says Augustus' learning came from living with Arius and his sons Dionysius and Nicanor (Augustus 89.1). Entering Alexandria in 30, Octavian walked hand in hand with Arius and told the citizens he spared them for Alexander's sake, for the city's beauty and as a favour to his friend (Plutarch, Antony 80; Dio 51.16). Plutarch adds that Arius helped seal Caesarion's death with a pun on Homer: too many Caesars are not a good thing (Antony 81). Seneca gives the consolation Arius offered Livia after Drusus died in 9 BC (Consolation to Marcia 4–5). That he is the Arius Didymus whose epitome of Stoic ethics Stobaeus keeps (Greek module 12.7) is usually assumed; Tryggve Göransson (Albinus, Alcinous, Arius Didymus, 1995) challenged the traditional picture of Arius as that doxographer.

05Athens and Rhodes: the finishing school

Greek module 7.3 states the pattern in a sentence: the senatorial boy finished his education at Athens or Rhodes. Cicero's Brutus gives the fullest case (306–316). At Rome he heard Philo of Larissa, head of the Academy, who had fled the Mithridatic war in 88 (Greek module 12.8 takes his doctrine), Molon on a visit, and Diodotus at home. In 79 he left for two years: six months at Athens with Antiochus, the rhetors of Asia, then Molon again at Rhodes, returning in 77, he says, almost changed. At Athens he and Atticus heard the Epicureans Phaedrus and Zeno (On Ends 1.16), and Atticus stayed in the city for some twenty years (Nepos, Atticus 2–4). Caesar was captured by pirates on his way to study under Molon at Rhodes (Suetonius, Julius 4).

The next generation followed. Horace says Athens added a little more art, so that he might wish to tell straight from crooked and seek the truth among the groves of Academus (Epistles 2.2.43–45), until civil war swept him away. In 44 Brutus at Athens attended the Academic Theomnestus and the Peripatetic Cratippus and drew the young Romans studying there into his army (Plutarch, Brutus 24); Cicero's son, Cratippus' pupil, was among them (deep dive module 13.3). The tour mixed rhetoric, philosophy and pilgrimage to the places of Plato and Demosthenes (On Ends 5.1–5); nobody expected a Roman to stay.

Where sources disagree. Cicero says he went abroad in 79 because his thin frame and strained voice needed rest and a gentler delivery (Brutus 313–314). Plutarch says he feared Sulla after defending Sextus Roscius and gave out his health as a pretext, and that he meant, if shut out of public life, to move to Athens and live as a philosopher (Cicero 3–4). Cicero's version is his own, but written in 46 to justify a career.

06Cicero's Latin philosophy, in outline

Module 5.6 lists the works of 45–44, gives Cicero's reason (with the state ruled by one man, teaching Romans philosophy was the service left to him) and his new Latin words. Two things matter here. First, he made Roman nobles the speakers: Cato expounds the Stoics, Torquatus the Epicureans, Lucullus Antiochus, Varro the Old Academy. He knew the objection and met it: some thought it unbecoming to show men of such rank discussing philosophy, others doubted they had the learning (Academica 2.5–7). The dialogues naturalise Greek argument by setting it in consulars' villas.

Second, his prefaces catalogue the Roman objections. Some disapprove of philosophising at all; some allow it if pursued lightly; some would rather read the Greeks; some think it beneath his rank (On Ends 1.1–3). His answers became the standard Roman defence. The lost Hortensius turned the nineteen-year-old Augustine to philosophy (Confessions 3.4.7).

07Why the Romans took the ethics

Greek module 12.7 hands on the question why Romans kept Stoic ethics and left the physics and logic that held it up; Greek module 7.2 outlines the systems they chose from. Four reasons can be read from the sources.

  1. The goal was action. Virtue, unlike an art, cannot be held without being used, and its greatest use is governing the state (On the Republic 1.2); all the praise of virtue lies in action, so to be drawn from affairs by study is against duty (On Duties 1.19). Knowledge that did not serve the community ranked below the duties of fellowship (1.153–158).
  2. Philosophy came through rhetoric. Romans met it in an orator's training, and Cicero's Crassus wants the orator to master philosophy's matter because speeches turn on justice, duty and the good (On the Orator 3). Logic became the art of argument.
  3. Religion was public business. Stoic theology and Epicurean indifference both bore on a cult run by magistrates, and Romans kept school and cult apart, as the three theologies of Scaevola and Varro and the pontiff Cotta's trust in the ancestors show (module 2.1).
  4. Latin lacked the terms. Lucretius lamented it and Cicero coined words (module 5.6), but ethics could be argued in the language of virtus, officium and honestum that Romans already used of their own conduct.

The teachers pointed the same way. The Stoicism Romans met in Scipio's house and at Rhodes was the revised school of Panaetius and Posidonius (Greek module 12.7), spoken to men of affairs. A century on, Seneca mocked the logic that proves a syllable gnaws cheese because a mouse is a syllable (Letters 48.6), as a waste of a short life.

Where sources disagree. The picture is partly a modern inheritance. Eduard Zeller's history of Greek philosophy classed the period as eclecticism, a mixing of doctrines for use, and the label coloured judgements of Cicero for a century. Elizabeth Rawson (Intellectual Life in the Late Roman Republic, 1985) and Katharina Volk (The Roman Republic of Letters, 2021) show senators arguing physics, astronomy, grammar and theology in earnest, and John Dillon and A. A. Long (eds.), The Question of "Eclecticism" (1988), took the label apart. Lucretius' six books of physics and Cicero's On Fate tell against the thesis; Cicero's own ranking of action above knowledge tells for it.

08What the suspicion was about

Roman objections to philosophy were rarely objections to doctrines. Cato wanted the envoys of 155 gone so that the youth would listen to laws and magistrates as before (module 4.5). Four fears recur. Time: the edict of 92 complains of young men idling whole days, and philosophy was otium, leisure a Roman had to justify against business. Authority: the ancestors decided what children learned, and a man who could argue both sides of justice, as Carneades did, claimed a court above custom. Politics: Tiberius Gracchus had a Stoic adviser, Blossius of Cumae, who said he would have burned the Capitol at Tiberius' bidding (module 5.1), and the imperial charge that Stoicism made men restless and meddling (Tacitus, Annals 14.57) is deep dive module 13.4's. Manners: philosophy was Greek, and Athenaeus' Epicureans were expelled for the pleasures they brought.

The Roman answer was not rejection but measure. Ennius' Neoptolemus said he must philosophise, but in few words, since to do it wholly did not please him (Cicero, Tusculan Disputations 2.1; On the Orator 2.156); Tacitus gives the classic case. Agricola used to say that in early youth, studying at Massilia, he drank in philosophy more keenly than was allowed to a Roman and a senator, ultra quam concessum Romano ac senatori, had not his mother's prudence restrained his burning spirit; reason and age tempered him, and he kept from wisdom what is hardest, a sense of measure (Agricola 4). Suetonius says Agrippina turned the young Nero from philosophy as unsuited to a future ruler (Nero 52). Two centuries after the decree of 161, governing families still treated philosophy as a drug to be dosed.

09Self-test

  1. What did the censors' edict of 92 BC object to, and what penalty did it impose?
    Answer

    New schools of Latin rhetors where young men idled whole days, against the ancestral rule on what children should learn. It declared them displeasing to teachers and pupils and expelled no one (Suetonius, On Grammarians and Rhetoricians 25; Gellius 15.11.2).

  2. Why is the date of the expulsion of Alcaeus and Philiscus uncertain?
    Answer

    Athenaeus (12.547a) dates it only by the consulship of a Lucius Postumius, and the likely consuls of that name held office in 173 and 154; no other source fixes the year.

  3. How did Crassus justify the edict, and what political reading has been offered?
    Answer

    In On the Orator 3.93–94 he says the Latin rhetors could teach only boldness, a school of impudence. Marx (1894) saw a blow at Latin oratory open to Marius' friends; Gelzer rejected the thesis, and von Ungern-Sternberg found nothing certain about Plotius' politics.

  4. Name two Roman pupils of Panaetius and say how each lived the teaching.
    Answer

    Rutilius Rufus refused to plead for pity at his trial in 92 and accepted conviction (On the Orator 1.229–230). Tubero's austere funeral feast for Scipio cost him the praetorship (Pro Murena 75–76).

  5. What did Pompey do at Posidonius' door, and what did it show?
    Answer

    He forbade his lictor to knock and lowered his fasces (Pliny, Natural History 7.112). The conqueror of the East deferred in public to Greek learning, which advertised his own standing as well as the philosopher's.

  6. Who was Diodotus, and what does his case show about house philosophers?
    Answer

    A Stoic, blind in old age, who lived in Cicero's house, trained him in dialectic and died there in 59, leaving him his property (Brutus 309; Tusculan Disputations 5.113; Letters to Atticus 2.20.6). The relation was lifelong companionship, not hired tuition.

  7. How does Strabo keep the two Athenodori apart?
    Answer

    Cordylion lived with Cato and died in his house; the son of Sandon taught Octavian, was honoured by him and in old age reformed the government of Tarsus (14.5.14).

  8. What roles does Arius play in the sources for Augustus?
    Answer

    Teacher and companion (Suetonius, Augustus 89.1); counsellor at Alexandria in 30, where the city was spared partly for his sake and Caesarion's death was sealed with his pun (Plutarch, Antony 80–81); and consoler of Livia (Seneca, Consolation to Marcia 4–5).

  9. Give two passages in which Cicero ranks action above knowledge.
    Answer

    Virtue exists only in use, and its greatest use is governing the state (On the Republic 1.2). All praise of virtue lies in action, so study that draws a man from affairs is against duty (On Duties 1.19).

  10. What does Tacitus, Agricola 4, show about Roman attitudes to philosophy in the first century AD?
    Answer

    That philosophy was respected but rationed: Agricola's mother curbed a zeal beyond what was allowed a Roman and a senator, and he kept from wisdom a sense of measure, the same limit Ennius' Neoptolemus set two centuries earlier.

10Carry forward

  1. Rome never banned philosophy as doctrine. It regulated teachers, the young and the use of time, and its measures asserted that the ancestors, not foreign masters, decided what a Roman learned, usually without force behind the words.
  2. Greek philosophy entered Rome through persons: house philosophers, teachers at Athens and Rhodes, and the patrons who kept them. Its Roman form was shaped by that client relation and by teachers who addressed men of affairs rather than sages.
  3. Romans took philosophy in measure and for use, ethics for conduct and office and the rest as equipment for argument. How much philosophy a Roman in public life may allow himself is the question every later Roman Stoic faces, from Cato to Marcus Aurelius.
