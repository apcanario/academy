01Writers who were also the state

Latin literature began as the work of outsiders: a freedman from Tarentum, a client from Rudiae, comic poets from Umbria and Carthage (module 4.5). The generation born between about 116 and 84 BC changed that. A consul (Cicero), a dictator (Caesar), an expelled senator who became a Caesarian governor (Sallust), a Pompeian legate (Varro) and two men of means from the Po valley who never held office (Catullus, Nepos) wrote in their own names, in Latin, about their own world. Lucretius, whose life is almost unknown, addressed his poem to a praetor.

They wrote while the institutions they took for granted failed, and much of it was made possible by the failure: the forum closed, and writing became the public service left. A senator's leisure (otium) needed a defence, and each supplied one.

02Lucretius: physics against fear

Jerome's Chronicle, under 94 BC, says Titus Lucretius Carus was driven mad by a love potion, wrote in lucid intervals, had his books corrected by Cicero and killed himself in his forty-fourth year. Donatus (Life of Virgil 6) has him die in 55 BC, on the day Virgil took the man's toga. The dates conflict, and the madness is usually dismissed as legend. The earliest notice is Cicero's remark to his brother in February 54 that the poems show many flashes of genius and much art (Letters to Quintus 2.10.3).

On the Nature of Things sets out Epicurus' physics in six books of hexameters, about 7,400 lines, for Gaius Memmius, praetor in 58. Greek module 7.2 gives the doctrine; what is Lucretius' own is its use. The poem opens with a hymn to Venus, mother of Aeneas' people, begging her to calm Mars, since the poet cannot work with an even mind patriai tempore iniquo, in his country's time of trouble, nor can Memmius fail the common safety (1.41–43). Book 3 traces greed and the blind desire for office to the fear of death, and makes civil bloodshed their fruit (3.59–86); Sisyphus is the candidate who seeks the fasces from the people and always loses (3.995–1002). Book 5 concludes that it is better to obey in quiet than to wish to rule (5.1127–1128). The crisis, for Lucretius, is a disease of belief, and the cure is physics, its bitterness disguised by the honey of verse (1.936–950).

Epicureanism had reached Rome earlier in Latin prose: Cicero sneers that Amafinius' books took over Italy because they were easy (Tusculan Disputations 4.6–7). Philodemus taught under the patronage of Piso, Caesar's father-in-law. Not every Roman Epicurean withdrew. Cassius, the tyrannicide, was one (Plutarch, Brutus 37), and Sallust gives Caesar an Epicurean argument in the Senate in 63: death is rest from troubles, not punishment, and beyond it there is no place for grief or joy (Catiline 51.20).

Where sources disagree. How Roman is the poem? David Sedley (Lucretius and the Transformation of Greek Wisdom, 1998) reads Lucretius as a fundamentalist working from Epicurus' own On Nature and indifferent to later debate. Benjamin Farrington (Science and Politics in the Ancient World, 1939) took its assault on religion as an attack on the state cult that Polybius praised as a means of control, and Don Fowler ("Lucretius and Politics", 1989) argued that its images of ambition and civil war answer the Roman crisis directly.

03Catullus and the new poets

Gaius Valerius Catullus came from a leading family of Verona; his father entertained Caesar, who forgave the son's verses on Mamurra and asked him to dinner the same day (Suetonius, Julius 73). The poems mention Caesar's Britain (11) and Pompey's second consulship (113), so Jerome's dates (87–57) are too early. He served under Memmius in Bithynia in 57–56 and complained that it made no one rich (10, 28).

The label is Cicero's. In 50 BC he quoted a spondaic hexameter built round a Greek place-name and told Atticus to sell it as his own to one of the neoteroi (Letters to Atticus 7.2.1); later he spoke of the poetae novi (Orator 161) and the "singers of Euphorion" (Tusculan Disputations 3.45). Catullus' friends include Calvus (50) and Cinna, whose miniature epic Zmyrna took nine years and will outlast the annals of Volusius, "shitty paper" (95, 36). Their creed is Callimachus' (Greek module 7.2): short, learned, polished poems against bulky epic. Poem 64, on the wedding of Peleus and Thetis with Ariadne's desertion set inside it as the picture on a coverlet, is the Alexandrian epyllion in Latin.

Lesbia, a name metrically equal to Clodia, was a Clodia according to Apuleius (Apology 10); whether the Clodia of Cicero's Pro Caelio is the problem of deep dive module 11.4. The poems run from kisses counted in thousands (5) to "I hate and I love" (85), and their novelty is vocabulary: the affair is measured in the words Cicero's letters use for political alliance, fides, foedus, amicitia, bene velle (72, 76, 87, 109). The code of obligation failing in the forum becomes the standard by which a private love fails.

The politics is open. Poem 29 asks who can bear to see Mamurra hold the wealth of Gaul and Britain, and ends on Caesar and Pompey: socer generque, perdidistis omnia, father-in-law and son-in-law, you have ruined everything (29.24). Poem 49 thanks Cicero as the best of advocates from the worst of poets, perhaps ironically. Poem 64 closes with its own theory of decline: since crime stained the earth and brothers killed brothers, the gods no longer visit men (64.397–408).

Where sources disagree. Was there a school? Cicero treats the new poets as a recognisable set, and R. O. A. M. Lyne ("The Neoteric Poets", 1978) read the line parodied at Letters to Atticus 7.2.1 as an exact caricature of a shared manner. The old picture of the grammarian Valerius Cato as the group's leader has been given up, and sceptics such as Edward Courtney (The Fragmentary Latin Poets, 1993) doubt that the category existed at all; many prefer a loose network of friends to a movement with a programme.

04Cicero: orator, philosopher, correspondent

Marcus Tullius Cicero (106–43) made Latin prose. Fifty-eight speeches survive whole or in part, each a political act revised for readers; the speeches against Catiline belong to module 5.3. The Pro Sestio (56) defined the optimates as all honest men of every order and their goal as cum dignitate otium, peace with standing (96–98). On the Orator (55), the Brutus and the Orator (both 46) made the ideal orator a statesman schooled in law, history and philosophy.

Pushed aside by the alliance of 60, he turned to political theory. On the Republic (54–51) adapts Polybius' mixed constitution (module 4.5) and imagines a guiding statesman, the moderator rei publicae, against whom Cicero later measured Pompey and found him wanting (Letters to Atticus 8.11.1–2). Its fifth book quoted Ennius on ancient customs and men, then gave the verdict: through our own vices, not by chance, we keep the republic in name and have long since lost it in fact (5.2, preserved by Augustine, City of God 2.21).

The great burst came in 45–44, with Caesar ruling and Cicero's daughter Tullia dead in February 45. In some eighteen months he wrote the Consolation and the Hortensius (both lost), the Academica, On Ends, the Tusculan Disputations, On the Nature of the Gods, On Divination, On Fate, On Old Age, On Friendship and, late in 44, On Duties for his son. His reason: since the state had to be governed by one man's counsel, teaching philosophy to Romans was the public service left to him (On the Nature of the Gods 1.7; On Divination 2.1–7). The form was the dialogue: Roman nobles argue the Stoic, Epicurean and Academic cases, and Cicero, an Academic sceptic, leaves the verdict to the reader.

The letters, some 900 including about 90 written to him, were not composed for publication. Nepos said that whoever read those to Atticus would hardly need a history of the times (Atticus 16.3). Deep dive module 11.5 reads them.

Where sources disagree. Cicero told Atticus the dialogues were copies made with little labour: he supplied only the words, of which he had plenty (Letters to Atticus 12.52.3). In On Ends (1.6) he claims instead not to act as a translator but to add his own judgement and arrangement. Nineteenth-century source critics took the first statement at its word and mined the dialogues for lost Hellenistic originals; the essays in J. G. F. Powell (ed.), Cicero the Philosopher (1995), read them as philosophy in their own right.

05Varro and Nepos: putting the past in order

Marcus Terentius Varro of Reate (116–27) was a Pompeian, legate against the pirates and in Spain in 49, and the satirist who called the alliance of 60 the Three-Headed Monster (Appian, Civil Wars 2.9). Caesar pardoned him and charged him with assembling Rome's first public library (Suetonius, Julius 44.2); Antony proscribed him in 43. By his seventy-eighth year he had written 490 books (Gellius 3.10.17), nearly all lost. The Antiquities Human and Divine (module 0.1) survives through Augustine, who reports Varro's fear that the gods would perish not by enemy attack but by the citizens' neglect, and his claim to rescue them as Aeneas saved the Penates from Troy (City of God 6.2). Six books On the Latin Language and On Agriculture, written at eighty (1.1.1), survive. Cicero put the tribute in a dialogue: Romans wandering like strangers in their own city were led home by Varro's books, so that they could recognise who and where they were (Academica 1.9).

Cornelius Nepos (c. 110–c. 25), from the Po valley (Pliny, Natural History 3.127), held no office and was a friend of Cicero and Atticus. Catullus praised his Chronica, which alone of Italians dared unfold all history in three learned volumes (1.5–7). His On Famous Men paired Roman lives with foreign ones; the surviving book On Foreign Generals runs from Miltiades to Hannibal. The Atticus, begun in its subject's lifetime and finished after his death in 32, portrays a man who survived every civil war by refusing office and helping each side in turn (6–11); Fergus Millar ("Cornelius Nepos, 'Atticus' and the Roman Revolution", 1988) read it as a guide to how a private man lived through the revolution. Varro, Nepos and Atticus, whose one-volume chronology prompted Cicero's Brutus (13–15), answered disorder by cataloguing dates, names, lives and words.

06Caesar: the general as stylist

Caesar wrote his wars as commentarii, notes nominally meant as material for a historian: seven books on Gaul and three on the civil war, in the third person. Cicero called them bare, upright and graceful, stripped of ornament like a body of its clothes (Brutus 262); Asinius Pollio thought them careless and not always true (Suetonius, Julius 56.4). The plain style was itself an argument, since a narrative without rhetoric reads as a report of fact. The Civil War makes Caesar the defender of the tribunes, of his own dignitas, dearer to him than life (1.9.2), and of a Roman people "oppressed by a faction of the few" (1.22.5). He also wrote On Analogy, dedicated to Cicero, and an Anticato (Suetonius, Julius 56.5), both lost. Cicero's Cato of 46 praised the dead man; Caesar's reply praised Cicero's eloquence and life, likening him to Pericles and Theramenes, while it blackened Cato (Plutarch, Cicero 39; Caesar 54). Module 5.4 treats Caesar as author and legend; deep dive module 11.6 takes the Commentaries apart.

07Sallust: decline as a thesis

Gaius Sallustius Crispus (86–35), a Sabine from Amiternum, was tribune in 52, expelled from the Senate by the censors in 50 (Dio 40.63.4), and governed Caesar's new province of Africa in 46–45, coming home rich and charged but not condemned (Dio 43.9.2–3). After the Ides he withdrew. His preface confesses youthful ambition and claims a mind now free of hope, fear and party (Catiline 3–4). He wrote the Catiline (about 42–41), the Jugurtha (about 41–40) and the Histories of 78–67, which survive in speeches, letters and fragments.

The monographs rest on a theory. Early Rome was held together by concord and fear of enemies; when Carthage, rival of Roman power, was destroyed root and branch, fortune began to rage, and first the desire for money grew, then the desire for power, the material of all evils (Catiline 10). The Jugurtha names the mechanism: metus hostilis, fear of the enemy, kept the city to good practices; once it was gone, the nobility turned its standing and the people their liberty into licence (41.2–5). Module 4.5 lists the rival ancient dates for the onset of decline; deep dive module 11.8 weighs the theory as an explanation.

As literature, the thesis organises everything. The Jugurthine War is chosen because then the arrogance of the nobility was first opposed (Jugurtha 5.1). In the Catiline, Cicero gives a speech "brilliant and useful to the state" (31.6) and little more; the climax belongs to Caesar and Cato, men of great virtue and different character (53–54). Sallust's Cato complains that Romans have lost the true names of things, calling the squandering of others' goods generosity and recklessness courage (52.11), the corruption of words Thucydides found in civil war (3.82; Greek module 6.3). In the Histories, written under the triumvirs, the thesis darkened: discord now ran back to the Republic's beginning, and the best morals lay only between the second and third Punic Wars (fragments 1.11–12 Maurenbrecher).

Where sources disagree. Can a corrupt man write moral history? The hostile tradition, Varro's story that Milo caught Sallust in adultery and had him flogged (Gellius 17.18), the forged Invective against Sallust under Cicero's name and Dio's account of Africa, makes him a hypocrite. D. C. Earl (The Political Thought of Sallust, 1961) and Ronald Syme (Sallust, 1964) took him seriously as a political thinker, and Syme rejected reading the monographs as pamphlets for Caesar's party.

Lucretius and Sallust both blame avarice and ambition, but Lucretius makes them symptoms of a false belief that physics can cure, Sallust the consequence of an event, the end of fear, that nothing can reverse. Cicero looks for a person, a statesman backed by the honest classes; Caesar's narrative supplies one and calls its opponents a faction, the word Augustus reused in the first sentence of the Res Gestae (module 0.1). Catullus and Nepos decline the question: one turns the language of obligation inward, the other shows a man surviving by staying out.

09What this generation did to Latin

Lucretius complained of the poverty of his native tongue (1.139, 1.832, 3.260). Cicero answered that Latin was richer than Greek (On Ends 1.10) and proved it by coining: qualitas for Greek poiotes (Academica 1.25), moralis "to enlarge the Latin language" (On Fate 1), beatitas and beatitudo, both harsh until use softened them (On the Nature of the Gods 1.95). Much of Europe's philosophical vocabulary passed through his Latin, and his rhythmic periodic sentence became the model of prose for Quintilian (10.1.112) and for the Renaissance. Caesar argued for purity: avoid an unheard-of word as you would a reef (Gellius 1.10.4, from On Analogy). Catullus brought fashionable Rome's diminutives and slang into lyric metres and made the elegiac couplet a vehicle for personal poetry, the form the elegists inherited (module 6.3); with Lucretius he made the hexameter supple enough for Virgil. Sallust built a counter-style, abrupt and archaic, with Thucydides as model (Quintilian 10.1.101) and Cato's old words as material, for which Asinius Pollio mocked him (Suetonius, On Grammarians 10) and Augustus mocked his imitators (Suetonius, Augustus 86); Tacitus followed it.

Where sources disagree. Calvus called Cicero loose and nerveless, Brutus "broken and loinless" (Tacitus, Dialogue on Orators 18.5), and men of his own day, Quintilian reports, called him swollen, Asian and redundant (12.10.12): these self-styled Atticists wanted Lysias' plainness. Cicero replied in the Brutus and the Orator that Demosthenes, master of every register, was the true Attic orator. Posterity sided with Cicero; Sallust's success shows the rival taste survived.

10Self-test

  1. What do Jerome, Donatus and Cicero each tell us about Lucretius, and why should the reader trust Cicero most?
    Answer

    Jerome (Chronicle, under 94 BC) gives madness from a love potion, Cicero's correction of the books and suicide at forty-three; Donatus (Life of Virgil 6) puts his death in 55, which Jerome's dates do not allow. Cicero's letter of February 54 (Letters to Quintus 2.10.3) is the earliest witness and the only contemporary verdict on the poem, and it says only that the poems combine genius and art.

  2. How does Lucretius connect political ambition with the fear of death?
    Answer

    Book 3 makes greed and the blind desire for office products of the fear of death, ending in civil bloodshed (3.59–86), and turns Sisyphus into the perpetual losing candidate (3.995–1002). Since Epicurean physics removes that fear, it removes the root of the political disease; quiet obedience beats ruling (5.1127–1128).

  3. Where do the terms for the "new poets" come from, and what aesthetic did Catullus and his friends share?
    Answer

    From Cicero's sneers: neoteroi (Letters to Atticus 7.2.1), poetae novi (Orator 161), "singers of Euphorion" (Tusculan Disputations 3.45). The shared aesthetic was Callimachus': short, learned, laboured poems, as in Cinna's nine-year Zmyrna (Catullus 95) and Catullus' poem 64, against long annalistic epic.

  4. What is new about the language of Catullus' Lesbia poems, and how does it connect them to the political crisis?
    Answer

    He describes love in the vocabulary of aristocratic alliance (fides, foedus, amicitia, bene velle: 72, 76, 87, 109). The code that was failing in public life becomes the measure of a private betrayal, while poems such as 29 attack Caesar and Pompey openly.

  5. Why did Cicero write most of his philosophy in 45–44 BC, and what form did he choose?
    Answer

    Caesar's rule left him no public role and Tullia died in February 45; he says that, with the state governed by one man, teaching Romans philosophy was his remaining service (On the Nature of the Gods 1.7; On Divination 2.1–7). He wrote dialogues in which Romans argue the Greek schools' positions, leaving the reader to judge in the Academic manner.

  6. What did Varro believe threatened Roman religion, and what was his answer?
    Answer

    Not enemies but the citizens' own neglect, which would let the gods be forgotten. He set out to rescue them in the Antiquities, as Aeneas saved the Penates from Troy (Augustine, City of God 6.2): record as preservation.

  7. What does Nepos' Atticus offer as a response to the civil wars?
    Answer

    A model of survival: Atticus refused office and helped each side in turn, from Brutus' friends to Fulvia and the proscribed (Atticus 6–11). Millar (1988) read it as a document of how a private man lived through the Roman Revolution.

  8. How does the plain style of Caesar's Commentaries serve his political case?
    Answer

    Stripped of rhetoric (Cicero, Brutus 262), the third-person narrative reads as a neutral report. Within it Caesar presents himself as defending his dignitas, the tribunes and a people "oppressed by a faction of the few" (Civil War 1.9.2, 1.22.5), a claim Pollio doubted (Suetonius, Julius 56.4).

  9. State Sallust's theory of moral decline, and say how the Histories changed it.
    Answer

    Fear of the enemy (metus hostilis) kept Rome united and virtuous; after Carthage fell in 146, avarice and then ambition grew, and nobles and people abused standing and liberty (Catiline 10; Jugurtha 41). The Histories push discord back to the Republic's beginning and confine the best morals to the interval between the second and third Punic Wars.

  10. How did Lucretius and Cicero disagree about the Latin language, and what did Cicero do about it?
    Answer

    Lucretius lamented the poverty of his native tongue (1.139, 1.832); Cicero held Latin richer than Greek (On Ends 1.10). He coined the words philosophy needed, such as qualitas (Academica 1.25), moralis (On Fate 1) and beatitudo (On the Nature of the Gods 1.95).

11Carry forward

  1. The writers of the late Republic were participants: consuls, generals, partisans and their friends. Even a turn away from politics, Lucretius' withdrawal or Sallust's and Cicero's otium, is a position in the crisis, and should be read as one.
  2. They disagreed about the disease: false belief, the end of fear after 146, the want of good men, forgetting, faction. The Augustan settlement borrowed from all of them, from Caesar's language of faction in the Res Gestae to Varro's restored gods and Sallust's decline in Livy's preface.
  3. This generation made Latin able to carry Greek philosophy, Alexandrian poetry and serious history: Cicero's vocabulary and periodic prose, Catullus' elegiacs and Lucretius' hexameter are the instruments that Virgil, Horace, Livy and Ovid took up (module 6.3).
