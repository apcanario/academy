The periods and why the boundaries fall where they do

None of these period names is Roman. "Early", "middle" and "late" Republic are modern; the contrast of Principate and Dominate was sharpened by nineteenth-century German constitutional history; "third-century crisis" is a twentieth-century label. Each boundary marks a change of regime, of evidence, or both.

PeriodConventional rangeBoundary markers

Third-century crisisAD 235–284Short reigns, usurpers, Persian and Gothic wars, breakaway Gaul and Palmyra
Late empire (Dominate)AD 284–476Diocletian to Romulus Augustulus in the West; the East continues

The line at 509 is Livy's own: Book 2 opens with a free people under annual magistrates (2.1.1). The line at 264 is Polybius', who began with the first Roman crossing to Sicily, where Timaeus had stopped (1.5.1); the evidence also improves there, since Fabius Pictor, Polybius' source for that war, wrote within living memory of it. The line at 133 follows Plutarch, for whom Tiberius Gracchus' death was the first civil strife since the kings to end in bloodshed (Tiberius Gracchus 20.1), and Appian, whose Civil Wars start there. In January 27 BC Octavian returned the provinces and became Augustus (Res Gestae 34); Tacitus says he took over a world worn out by civil war "under the name of princeps" (Annals 1.1). The murder of Severus Alexander in 235 ended the last stable dynasty; Diocletian's accession in 284 began the rebuilt state of module 8.2. "Dominate" comes from dominus, "lord", since late emperors were greeted by prostration; most historians now say "late empire", because the contrast with a constitutional Principate was overdrawn.

Where sources disagree. Karl-Joachim Hölkeskamp (Die Entstehung der Nobilität, 1987) dates the formation of the patricio-plebeian nobility, and so the classic Republic, to the decades between the Licinio-Sextian laws of 367 and the lex Hortensia of 287. Harriet Flower (Roman Republics, 2010) divides the span into several successive republics and ends the republic of the nobles with Sulla's march in 88. The three-part scheme is a teaching convenience, not a finding.

The span falls into five blocks of roughly a quarter-millennium: kings 753–509 (244 years), early Republic 509–264 (245), conquest and civil war 264–27 (237), Principate 27 BC–AD 235 (261), crisis and late empire 235–476 (241). The first block's length is an artefact of the tradition, but the rhythm is a usable peg.

Consuls and the Fasti

A Roman year had a name, not a number: "the consulship of" two men. To place it you needed the list, the fasti consulares, by which the pontiffs' annual records were organised (module 0.1) and along which Livy's narrative moves. The best copy, the Fasti Capitolini, was inscribed in the Forum under Augustus, probably about 18 BC, with a list of triumphs; its fragments, found in 1546, are in the Capitoline Museums. As Greek module 0.3 showed for archons and Olympiads, chronology means reconciling lists.

There are two traps. The consular year began on 1 January only from 153 BC; before that, usually 15 March, and earlier the day shifted several times, so a consular year straddles two of ours. And the pre-Julian calendar drifted, by about three months before Caesar's reform of 46 BC (module 2.3). Under the emperors the year still bore the names of the consules ordinarii of 1 January, though replacements followed within months. The last private citizen to name a year was Basilius in AD 541; later documents counted years "after the consulship of Basilius".

Where sources disagree. Tim Cornell (The Beginnings of Rome, 1995) defends the fifth-century list: it contains families that later vanished and plebeian names that contradict the later belief that plebeians were barred from the consulship before 367. Hypercritics from Ettore Pais onwards held that annalists padded or reconstructed it. The usual view accepts the names from about 500 BC and doubts the arithmetic.

Years from the founding: seven ancient answers

Counting ab urbe condita, "from the founding of the city", was a historian's device, not a civil one: Livy has the decemvirs take office "in the three hundred and second year" (3.33.1), and Orosius in the fifth century AD built a whole history on it. It needed a zero point the Romans did not have. The first historians, writing in Greek about 200 BC, five and a half centuries after the supposed event, had to convert a consular list and a king list into Olympiads. Dionysius of Halicarnassus, under Augustus, sets out the results (Roman Antiquities 1.74).

Set Timaeus and Ennius aside and the dates fall between 753 and 728, which suggests a common method: count the consular colleges back to the first consuls, then add a royal span of about 240 years agreed in advance. Timaeus' date pairs the two powers of the west; Ennius' belongs with the version in which Romulus was Aeneas' grandson (module 1.3). Varro's won by authority: Atticus used it in his Liber annalis of 47 BC, the Augustan Fasti a reckoning one year lower (752), and Censorinus in AD 238 called his year the 991st by Varro's count (De die natali 21). Modern books still convert with it.

Emperors' years and the indiction

Under the empire a second count ran beside the consuls. Egyptian papyri date by "year 15 of Tiberius Caesar Augustus", and Luke 3:1 dates the Baptist's preaching to "the fifteenth year" of Tiberius. Coins and inscriptions count annual renewals of tribunician power: Augustus' began in 23 BC and stood at thirty-seven when he finished the Res Gestae (4). With numbered consulships, these titles date most imperial inscriptions to the year.

The indiction grew out of Diocletian's tax assessments and became, under Constantine, a fifteen-year cycle counted from 1 September 312. Documents give the year within the cycle, never the cycle, so an indiction alone is ambiguous. Justinian made the full formula compulsory in 537: regnal year, consul, indiction, month and day (Novel 47). Constantinople kept the indiction to the end, beside an era of the world from 5509 BC. Our own era is a late arrival: Dionysius Exiguus devised Anno Domini for Easter tables in 525, and Bede's Ecclesiastical History (731) made it the historian's era in the West.

The big chronological problems

The foundation date

The archaeology recognises no foundation event. Graves in the Forum valley go back to about 1000 BC, the Palatine had huts in the eighth century, and the first Forum paving, implying one community managing the valley, belongs to the later seventh. Andrea Carandini dated a wall at the foot of the Palatine to the eighth century and read it as Romulus' circuit (La nascita di Roma, 1997); most archaeologists accept the wall but not the reading. The story is module 1.4; for chronology, 753 is a calculated date attached to a process.

Seven kings for 244 years

The traditional reigns, Romulus (753–716) to Tarquinius Superbus (534–509), average thirty-five years, in a line where one king vanished in a storm, one was struck by lightning, two were murdered and one was expelled. Long dynasties seldom average much above twenty. Dionysius saw part of the difficulty: he refused to make Superbus the son of Tarquinius Priscus, as Fabius had, because the dates would not allow it (Roman Antiquities 4.6–7). Most likely the span was fixed first, by setting a computed foundation against a Republic dated from the consular list, and the remembered kings were stretched to fill it (module 1.5). Livy and Dionysius wrote some five centuries after the last king.

The dictator years and the Varronian chronology

Count the consular pairs between the first consuls and about 300 BC and there are fewer than Varro's dates require. The Augustan Fasti fill the gap with four "dictator years", 333, 324, 309 and 301, in which a dictator and his master of horse appear with no consuls. Livy knows nothing of them, and modern scholars almost all regard them as insertions. A second elastic stretch is the "anarchy" of the 370s, when tribunes blocked curule elections: five years in Livy (6.35.10), one in Diodorus. The result is the Varronian chronology in which modern books give every date before about 300 BC: consistent, and probably about four years too early in the fourth century. "509" and "390" are positions in a system, not Julian years.

390 or 387: the Gallic sack

This is the one early Roman event Greeks recorded near the time: Aristotle had heard that Rome was taken by Celts and saved by a certain Lucius (Plutarch, Camillus 22.4). Polybius dates it by the King's Peace and the siege of Rhegium, 387/6 (1.6.1–2); Dionysius of Halicarnassus puts it in Pyrgion's archonship, 388/7 (1.74.4). Varro's 390 is higher because of the dictator years. The day is secure: 18 July, the unlucky dies Alliensis. Books use 390 in Roman narrative and 387/6 when synchronising with Greece.

The first consuls and the Capitoline temple

Three indications point to the end of the sixth century. Polybius dates the first treaty with Carthage to the consuls who dedicated the Capitoline temple, twenty-eight years before Xerxes crossed to Greece: 508/7 (3.22.1–2). Pliny records that Cn. Flavius as aedile in 304 BC dedicated a shrine of Concord 204 years after the Capitoline dedication (Natural History 33.19). Livy cites a law that the chief magistrate drive a nail into the temple wall each Ides of September, a count of years (7.3.5–7). The details conflict: Livy dedicates the temple in the Republic's first year (2.8.6–8), Tacitus in Horatius' second consulship (Histories 3.72), and Livy's first year has five consuls, as if several traditions had been stacked. Gjerstad's lowering of the end of the monarchy to about 450, and the suspicious closeness of 509 to Athens' 510, are weighed in module 1.6.

When did the Republic end?

Sallust (Catiline 10) and Velleius (2.1) put the moral turn at 146, when fear of Carthage was removed; Appian and Plutarch start the violence in 133. Augustus claimed no ending: in the Res Gestae he wrote that in 28–27 BC he had "transferred the res publica" to the Senate and people (34). Cassius Dio says monarchy strictly began in 27 (53.17); Tacitus asks how many were left in AD 14 "who had seen the Republic" (Annals 1.3). Modern candidates add 88, 60, 49, 43 and 23 BC. The convention of 31 or 27 marks the end of rival dynasts, not a day Romans thought the Republic gone.

Where sources disagree. Ronald Syme (The Roman Revolution, 1939) saw an oligarchy destroyed by revolution between 60 BC and AD 14. Erich Gruen (The Last Generation of the Roman Republic, 1974) argued that the institutions worked until 49 and that civil war caused the fall rather than resulting from it. Flower ends the traditional republic in 88. Module 11.8 takes it further.

Why 476 is a convention and 1453 is not

In 476 Odoacer, commanding the barbarian troops in Italy, killed the patrician Orestes and deposed his son, the boy emperor Romulus Augustulus, pensioning him off to Campania. He named no successor and sent the regalia to Zeno at Constantinople, with a senatorial message that one emperor sufficed (Malchus); Zeno replied that the West still had a lawful emperor, Julius Nepos, alive in Dalmatia until 480. Odoacer ruled as king, the Senate met, western consuls were named until 534, and Theoderic's Ostrogoths governed Italy on Roman lines from 493. Western contemporaries barely registered 476.

It became an ending fifty years later, in the East. Marcellinus Comes, writing at Constantinople, entered under 476 that "the western empire of the Roman people" perished with Augustulus (Chronicle, s.a. 476). Brian Croke ("A.D. 476: the manufacture of a turning point", Chiron, 1983) argued that the idea took shape in early sixth-century Constantinople and suited Justinian's claim to Italy as a lost province. It labels a transition running from the Vandal capture of Carthage (439) to the Lombard invasion (568).

Where sources disagree. Whether anything "fell" is the live question. Peter Brown (The World of Late Antiquity, 1971) and Walter Goffart (Barbarians and Romans, 1980) stressed transformation and accommodation; Peter Heather (The Fall of the Roman Empire, 2005) and Bryan Ward-Perkins (The Fall of Rome, 2005) restored violence and material collapse. Module 8.5 weighs them.

1453 is a different kind of end. On 29 May Mehmed II took Constantinople by assault and Constantine XI Palaiologos died at the walls: a capital, a siege, a last emperor and a date recognised everywhere at once. That state claimed an unbroken line from Augustus, interrupted only by the Latin occupation of 1204–1261; its people called themselves Rhomaioi, Romans, and "Byzantine" is a label popularised by Hieronymus Wolf in 1557. Fragments survived briefly (the Morea to 1460, Trebizond to 1461; module 8.6). The year 476 marks a western office that lapsed; 1453 marks the conquest of the Roman state itself.

Self-test

  1. Why does the Varronian date for the Gallic sack (390) differ from Polybius' (387/6)?
    Answer

    Polybius fixed it by Greek synchronisms, the King's Peace and the siege of Rhegium (1.6.1–2). The Varronian system, as on the Augustan Fasti, includes four inserted dictator years (333, 324, 309, 301), which push every earlier date back by about four years.

  2. What does Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Roman Antiquities 1.74, show about the foundation date?
    Answer

    That it was computed, not remembered: Timaeus 814/13, Fabius Pictor 748/7, Cincius 729/8, Cato 432 years after Troy, Polybius 751/0, each converting Roman lists into Olympiads by a different reckoning.

  3. Why is the regal period of 244 years suspect, and what is the likeliest explanation?
    Answer

    Seven reigns averaging thirty-five years is implausible for a line with three or four violent ends. The span was probably fixed first, by setting a computed foundation date against a Republic dated from the consular list, and the remembered kings were stretched to fill it (module 1.5).

  4. Give two independent ancient indications that the Capitoline temple was dedicated around 509–507 BC.
    Answer

    Any two of: Polybius 3.22.1–2, dating the first Carthage treaty to the consuls who dedicated the temple, 28 years before Xerxes; Pliny, Natural History 33.19, Flavius' shrine of 304 BC built 204 years after the dedication; the annual nail in the temple wall (Livy 7.3.5–7).

  5. What two traps make it hard to convert a Republican consular year into a modern date?
    Answer

    The consular year began on different days until 1 January became fixed in 153 BC, so each straddles two of ours; and the pre-Julian calendar drifted, by about three months before Caesar's reform of 46 BC.

  6. Why does the line between the early and middle Republic usually fall at 264 BC?
    Answer

    It is Rome's first war outside Italy and Polybius' chosen starting point, where Timaeus had stopped (1.5.1); it is also where the evidence improves, since Fabius Pictor wrote within living memory of it.

  7. What was the indiction, and why does an indiction date alone not fix a year?
    Answer

    A fifteen-year fiscal cycle, counted under Constantine from 1 September 312. Documents give only the year within the cycle, never which cycle, so the same number recurs every fifteen years; Justinian's Novel 47 (537) required it alongside regnal year and consul.

  8. Who first treated 476 as the end of the western empire, and why is that suspicious?
    Answer

    Marcellinus Comes at Constantinople, some fifty years later (Chronicle, s.a. 476). Croke (1983) argued the idea suited Justinian's case for reconquering Italy; western contemporaries barely noticed, and Julius Nepos was still the lawful emperor until 480.

  9. Give three different ancient answers to when the Republic declined or ended.
    Answer

    Sallust (Catiline 10) and Velleius (2.1): 146 BC, with the fall of Carthage. Plutarch and Appian: 133, the death of Tiberius Gracchus. Cassius Dio (53.17): monarchy from 27; Augustus himself claimed to have transferred the res publica to the Senate and people (Res Gestae 34).

  10. In what sense is 1453 a different kind of end from 476?
    Answer

    In 1453 a capital fell by assault, a last emperor died and a continuous Roman state ended, recognised at once by contemporaries. In 476 no state ended: a western office lapsed and the date became an ending only in retrospect.

Carry forward

  1. Every Roman date before about 300 BC is a Varronian label, produced by counting a list back to a computed foundation; treat 753, 509 and 390 as positions in a system, and always ask who computed them and how many centuries after the event.
  2. The Romans dated by names, not numbers: consuls, then emperors' years and indictions. Counting "from the founding" was a historian's tool, so Roman chronology, like Greek, is built by reconciling lists.
  3. Period boundaries mark changes of regime or of evidence and are mostly modern; 476 is a retrospective convention and 1453 an event, and the Roman state outlived Rome's western empire by nearly a thousand years.
