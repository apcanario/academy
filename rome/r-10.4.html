Rome without an emperor: popes, Franks and a forged gift

After 476 the city kept its name, its bishop and its legends, and the bishop inherited the idea. Leo I told the Romans that Peter and Paul had founded their city better than the brothers who built its walls, one of whom stained it with a brother's blood (Sermon 82). Hobbes put the result in a sentence: the papacy is "the ghost of the deceased Roman empire, sitting crowned upon the grave thereof" (Leviathan, chapter 47, 1651).

The empire returned as a western title, whose coronation of 800 and history to 1806 are module 8.6's; what it carried was a programme. Charlemagne's chancery spoke of renovatio, the renewal of the Roman empire. Otto III put renovatio imperii Romanorum on his seal in 998 and made his tutor Gerbert pope in 999 as Silvester II, so that emperor and pope replayed Constantine and Silvester I. The doctrine of translatio imperii made the western emperor Augustus' successor in law; Voltaire mocked the Holy Roman Empire as neither holy, nor Roman, nor an empire (Essai sur les mœurs, chapter 70).

The popes answered with a Constantine of their own. The Actus Silvestri, a Roman legend of the later fifth century, made him a persecutor struck with leprosy and healed by baptism at Silvester's hands; the Constitutum Constantini, the Donation, built on it a grant to the pope of the Lateran palace, the imperial insignia, and Rome with the provinces of Italy and the West. It is usually placed at the papal court in the later eighth century; Johannes Fried (Donation of Constantine and Constitutum Constantini, 2007) argued for a ninth-century Frankish origin. Otto III's chancery called it a fabrication in 1001, and Nicholas of Cusa doubted it in 1433. Lorenzo Valla, secretary to Alfonso of Aragon, then at war with Pope Eugenius IV, demolished it in 1440 on philology: no fourth-century emperor wrote eighth-century Latin or spoke of satraps, and no contemporary mentions so vast a gift.

Where sources disagree. Eusebius has Constantine baptised on his deathbed near Nicomedia in 337 (Life of Constantine 4.61–62), by Eusebius of Nicomedia according to Jerome's Chronicle (module 8.3). The Actus Silvestri, followed by the Liber Pontificalis in its life of Silvester, has him baptised at Rome by the pope. The West preferred the Roman version, which made the first Christian emperor a convert of the Roman see.

The city of marvels and Dante's providential empire

Medieval Rome read its ruins as wonders. Hildebert of Lavardin, about 1100, began an elegy on the city:

Par tibi, Roma, nihil, cum sis prope tota ruina

Nothing equals you, Rome, though you are almost wholly ruin. The Mirabilia urbis Romae, a guide of about 1140 usually credited to Benedict, a canon of St Peter's, attached legends to the monuments: the Sibyl showing Augustus the Virgin and Child on the Capitol, the Pantheon built for Cybele and given to Mary. The bronze horseman at the Lateran survived because it was taken for Constantine; it is Marcus Aurelius. The same centuries made Virgil a magician, a legend that deep dive module 12.8 follows with Dante's Virgil the guide. In 1143 the Roman commune revived the Senate: the idea could be republican too.

Dante built a theology of Rome. In the Monarchia the Roman people won its empire by right and under providence, which is why Christ chose to be born under Augustus (book 2), and in Paradiso 6 Justinian tells the eagle's flight from Aeneas to Charlemagne. Dante split the republican heroes accordingly. The Brutus who drove out Tarquin sits among the virtuous pagans in Limbo (Inferno 4.127); Brutus and Cassius hang from Lucifer's side mouths with Judas between them, traitors to the empire beside the traitor to God (34.61–67). The Donation, he lamented, was the dowry that made the first rich pope (19.115–117).

Petrarch, Cola di Rienzo and the measured ruins

Petrarch made the idea a programme of recovery. Crowned poet on the Capitol on 8 April 1341, he wrote a Latin epic on Scipio, the Africa, and letters to Cicero after finding the letters to Atticus at Verona in 1345 (Familiares 24.3–4). What else, he asked, is all history but the praise of Rome (Invectiva contra eum qui maledixit Italie, 1373)? He set the light of antiquity against the darkness after it, the origin, as Theodor E. Mommsen showed ("Petrarch's Conception of the 'Dark Ages'", Speculum, 1942), of the idea of a middle age. Greek module 9.3 follows his Homer.

Cola di Rienzo, a notary who could read the inscriptions, acted the programme out. On 20 May 1347 an assembly on the Capitol gave him power and he took the title of tribune. He displayed the bronze law of Vespasian's powers as proof of the Roman people's sovereignty (module 7.2), bathed in the font of Constantine's legendary baptism before his knighting, and summoned the rival emperors to submit their claims to Rome. Petrarch hailed a new Brutus. Cola fell in December, returned as senator in 1354 and was killed by a crowd that October; Wagner's Rienzi (1842) made him a romantic hero.

The Renaissance then measured the ruins. Flavio Biondo's Roma instaurata (1444–46) rebuilt the ancient topography from texts and remains; Poggio Bracciolini opened De varietate fortunae (1448) on the Capitol, surveying what fortune had left; and a letter to Leo X written with Raphael about 1519 condemned the popes who had burned ancient marble for lime.

Machiavelli and the republican tradition

Florence had already claimed the Republic. Coluccio Salutati's De tyranno (1400) still defended Caesar and Dante's verdict on his killers; Leonardo Bruni's Laudatio Florentinae urbis (about 1403–04) answered that Florence was founded by Sulla's veterans while Rome was free, so its liberty was Roman by descent.

Machiavelli wrote the Discourses on the First Ten Books of Livy about 1513–19 (printed 1531), the years in which, by his letter to Vettori of 10 December 1513, he put on court dress each evening to question the ancients. The mixed constitution, after Polybius book 6, gave consuls, Senate and people each a share (1.2). The quarrels of plebs and Senate, which others blamed, made Rome free and powerful, because good laws came from them (1.4). A dictatorship with a term saved the Republic (1.34), prolonged commands enslaved it (3.24), and a republic must return often to its first principles (3.1). Modules 1.6 and 7.3 give his Brutus (3.2) and his good emperors (1.10). Through Harrington's Oceana (1656) and Trenchard and Gordon's Cato's Letters (1720–23) the argument crossed the Atlantic, as J. G. A. Pocock traced (The Machiavellian Moment, 1975), and Quentin Skinner (Liberty before Liberalism, 1998) recovered its Roman core: to be free is not to live under another's will, as the jurists distinguished the free man from the slave.

Where sources disagree. Hans Baron (The Crisis of the Early Italian Renaissance, 1955) argued that Florence's war with Giangaleazzo Visconti of Milan, who died in 1402, turned Bruni and his circle into champions of a republican "civic humanism" modelled on Rome. Jerrold Seigel ("'Civic Humanism' or Ciceronian Rhetoric?", Past and Present, 1966) answered that Bruni's praise of liberty was rhetoric for an occasion. That Florence read its liberty as Roman is not in question; how sincerely, and from when, is.

Shakespeare's Roman plays

Shakespeare read Rome in Thomas North's English Plutarch (1579), made from Jacques Amyot's French (1559), and his three Plutarch plays follow it closely. Julius Caesar, seen at the Globe by the Swiss traveller Thomas Platter on 21 September 1599, ends by calling Brutus "the noblest Roman of them all" (5.5.68); deep dive module 11.7 sets it against the ancient sources. Antony and Cleopatra (about 1606) versifies North: Enobarbus' barge on the Cydnus (2.2) is Plutarch's description (Antony 26) in blank verse. Coriolanus (about 1608) opens with a corn riot and Menenius' fable of the belly (Livy 2.32; Plutarch, Coriolanus 6), written after the Midland Rising of 1607; its mother Volumnia and wife Virgilia follow Plutarch, where Livy has Veturia and Volumnia (module 3.2), and Volumnia's plea (5.3) keeps North's sentences. Behind these stand The Rape of Lucrece (module 1.6), Titus Andronicus, a late-imperial nightmare built from Ovid and Seneca, whose afterlives modules 10.1 and 10.2 follow, and Cymbeline, set in Augustan Britain. Shakespeare's Rome stays a mirror: Orson Welles staged Julius Caesar in Fascist uniforms in New York in 1937.

Where sources disagree. M. W. MacCallum (Shakespeare's Roman Plays and Their Background, 1910) defined the group as the three Plutarch tragedies. Robert Miola (Shakespeare's Rome, 1983) widened it to Lucrece, Titus and Cymbeline, one Rome of honour, piety and constancy tested across the career, and Coppélia Kahn (Roman Shakespeare, 1997) read the whole set through virtus as a code of masculinity.

1776: Gibbon's fall and the founders' republic

The first volume of Gibbon's Decline and Fall appeared in February 1776. Module 8.5 gives his explanation of the fall; its reception made "decline and fall" a pattern for every later power. Gibbon dated the idea to 15 October 1764, "musing amidst the ruins of the Capitol" (Memoirs), and the work ends where it began, with Petrarch and Rienzi (chapter 70) and Poggio's view from the Capitol (chapter 71). His chapters on the spread of Christianity (15–16) drew clerical attacks, answered in his Vindication (1779), and Thomas Bowdler's family edition (1826) cut them. Montesquieu's Considerations on the Causes of the Greatness of the Romans and their Decline (1734) had set the pattern; successors multiplied the causes until Alexander Demandt (Der Fall Roms, 1984) counted 210. Thomas Cole painted the cycle for New Yorkers as The Course of Empire (1833–36), from wilderness through consummation to desolation, and Americans still ask whether they are Rome (Cullen Murphy, Are We Rome?, 2007).

The founders' Rome was the Republic, read in Plutarch, Livy, Sallust and Cicero and seen in Addison's Cato (1713), performed at Valley Forge in May 1778 before Washington, by an officer's letter. Surrendering his commission in December 1783 made Washington Cincinnatus. Madison, Hamilton and Jay signed The Federalist (1787–88) as Publius, after Publicola (module 1.6); their opponents wrote as Brutus and Cato. The republic took a Senate, an eagle and a Capitol, Jefferson modelled Virginia's capitol on the Maison Carrée at Nîmes, and the Great Seal's novus ordo seclorum adapts Virgil (Eclogue 4.5).

Where sources disagree. Bernard Bailyn (The Ideological Origins of the American Revolution, 1967) found the classics everywhere in revolutionary pamphlets but judged them "illustrative, not determinative" of thought, which came from English opposition writers and the common law. Carl J. Richard (The Founders and the Classics, 1994) argued that classical models shaped the founders' ideas of virtue, mixed government and tyranny, not only their pen names.

Brutus in Paris, eagles at the Tuileries

The French Revolution took the Republic's sternest myths. Jacques-Louis David's Oath of the Horatii (1784–85), painted for the crown, became a revolutionary icon, and his Lictors Bring to Brutus the Bodies of His Sons hung at the Salon of 1789, weeks after the Bastille fell (Livy 2.5; module 1.6). Voltaire's Brutus (1730) was revived in November 1790 to uproar, and Babeuf signed himself Gracchus. Marx caught the theatre of it: the men of 1789 to 1814 did the work of their time "in Roman costume and with Roman phrases", the Revolution draped as the Republic and Napoleon as the empire (The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte, 1852).

Napoleon passed through the Roman titles in five years: First Consul in 1799, consul for life by plebiscite in 1802, emperor in 1804, when he gave his regiments eagles. Paris gained the Arc du Carrousel (1806–08), modelled on the arches of Severus and Constantine and crowned with the horses taken from St Mark's, and the Vendôme column (1806–10), Trajan's spiral cast in the bronze of guns taken at Austerlitz. Rome, annexed in 1809, became the empire's second city, and his son was born King of Rome in 1811. Napoleon III wrote a History of Julius Caesar (1865–66), excavated Alesia and set up a colossal Vercingetorix there: France claimed both the conqueror and the conquered.

Spades, scholars and the Duce

The nineteenth century made Rome an object of science and a national capital. Herculaneum (from 1738) and Pompeii (from 1748) were dug for the Bourbon kings of Naples; from 1863 Giuseppe Fiorelli poured plaster into the hollows left by the dead. Macaulay's Lays of Ancient Rome (1842) wrote the lost ballads Niebuhr had imagined (module 0.1). Mommsen, met throughout this course, shaped the century's Rome three ways: the Römische Geschichte (1854–56), whose Caesar is Rome's one creative genius, and which won him the Nobel Prize in 1902; the Corpus Inscriptionum Latinarum, first volume 1863; and the Römisches Staatsrecht (1871–88), the Republic as a system of public law. After 1870 Rome was Italy's capital, Mazzini's "Third Rome" after the Rome of the Caesars and of the popes; Rodolfo Lanciani mapped the ancient city as the new one buried it (Forma Urbis Romae, 1893–1901).

Mussolini made romanità a state cult. The lictors' fasces became the regime's emblem, and 21 April, the city's birthday, replaced May Day as the labour holiday. For the Via dell'Impero, opened on 28 October 1932, the tenth anniversary of the March on Rome, his planners demolished the quarter over the imperial fora and cut away the Velia; marble maps on the Basilica of Maxentius showed Rome's growth, with a fifth for the new Fascist empire, whose reappearance on the fated hills of Rome he proclaimed on 9 May 1936. The Augustan Exhibition, the Mostra Augustea della Romanità, opened in September 1937 for the bimillenary of Augustus' birth, directed by Giulio Quirino Giglioli, with casts of monuments from across the empire and Italo Gismondi's model of the ancient city. The Mausoleum of Augustus was isolated in a new square and the Ara Pacis reassembled beside it (module 6.2), in a pavilion whose wall carried the Res Gestae, as it still does.

Rome on screen and on the page

Film inherited the Victorian Christian novel. Lew Wallace's Ben-Hur (1880) and Henryk Sienkiewicz's Quo Vadis (1896) set pious heroes against a decadent empire. Enrico Guazzoni's Quo Vadis (1913) and Giovanni Pastrone's Cabiria (1914), a Punic War epic with intertitles by D'Annunzio, came from an Italy fresh from conquering Libya. Hollywood turned the formula against dictators: Mervyn LeRoy's Quo Vadis (1951) made Nero a modern tyrant, William Wyler's Ben-Hur (1959) won eleven Oscars, and Stanley Kubrick's Spartacus (1960), from Howard Fast's novel, gave the blacklisted Dalton Trumbo screen credit under his own name. Ridley Scott's Gladiator (2000) reworked Anthony Mann's The Fall of the Roman Empire (1964) and killed Commodus in the arena rather than in his bath (module 7.3). Maria Wyke (Projecting the Past, 1997) showed each cycle using Rome to talk about its own present; Greek modules 9.3 and 10.12 take Greek myth and Troy on screen.

The BBC's I, Claudius (1976) adapted Robert Graves's two Claudius novels of 1934, which turned Tacitus' insinuations (Annals 1.5) and Dio's poisoned figs (56.30) into Livia the serial poisoner. Rome (HBO and BBC, 2005–07) followed two centurions from Caesar's Gallic War (5.44), Vorenus and Pullo, through the civil wars. Goscinny and Uderzo's Asterix, launched in Pilote in 1959, set one Gaulish village against Caesar's legions in 50 BC and put Rome's occupation of Gaul at the service of French self-mockery. The novel returned with Marguerite Yourcenar's Memoirs of Hadrian (1951), Lindsey Davis's Falco (from 1989), Colleen McCullough's Masters of Rome (1990–2007) and Robert Harris's Pompeii (2003) and Cicero trilogy (2006–15), narrated by Cicero's secretary Tiro.

Who owns Rome?

Every heir has claimed Rome against another: popes against emperors, Florence against Milan, Philadelphia against London, Mussolini against everyone. Italy keeps Fascist Rome in stone, from the obelisk inscribed MUSSOLINI DUX at the Foro Italico to the maps on what has been the Via dei Fori Imperiali since the war; the European Economic Community was founded by a treaty signed on the Capitol in 1957; far-right groups have taken up SPQR and the fasces. Charles Martindale (Redeeming the Text, 1993) argued that meaning is realised at the point of reception, so that there is no Rome outside its readings; Mary Beard's SPQR (2015) told Rome's history as a widening citizenship.

Where sources disagree. Dan-el Padilla Peralta, profiled in the New York Times Magazine in February 2021, argued that classics had served slavery, race science, colonialism and fascism since it entered the modern university, and deserved to survive only if remade from the ground. When Howard University moved that spring to dissolve its classics department, Cornel West and Jeremy Tate called the decision "a spiritual catastrophe" (Washington Post, 19 April 2021), arguing that Black students should not be cut off from those books. Both accept that Rome has belonged to whoever used it; they disagree whether that history disqualifies the subject or obliges its students.

Self-test

  1. What did the Silvester legend and the Donation of Constantine each claim, and how did Valla show the Donation was forged?
    Answer

    The Actus Silvestri made Constantine a leper healed by baptism at Pope Silvester's hands in Rome, against Eusebius' deathbed baptism near Nicomedia; the Donation added a grant of the Lateran, the insignia, and Rome with Italy and the West. Valla (1440) showed that its Latin and terms such as "satraps" were not fourth-century and that no contemporary mentions the gift.

  2. How does Dante's placing of the two Brutuses express his idea of Rome?
    Answer

    Lucius Brutus, who expelled Tarquin, is among the virtuous pagans in Limbo (Inferno 4.127), while Marcus Brutus and Cassius are chewed by Lucifer beside Judas (34.61–67). For the Monarchia the empire was providential, so its betrayers rank with the betrayer of Christ.

  3. Which pieces of antiquity did Cola di Rienzo use to legitimise his regime in 1347?
    Answer

    The title of tribune; the bronze law conferring Vespasian's powers, displayed as proof of the Roman people's sovereignty (module 7.2); the font of Constantine's legendary baptism, in which he bathed before his knighting; and the Capitol as the seat of his assembly, with Petrarch hailing him as a new Brutus.

  4. Why was Machiavelli's claim in Discourses 1.4 provocative, and what other Roman lessons did he draw?
    Answer

    He argued that the quarrels of plebs and Senate, usually blamed as disorder, made Rome free and powerful because good laws came from them. He also praised the mixed constitution (1.2) and the time-limited dictatorship (1.34), warned against prolonged commands (3.24), and urged a return to first principles (3.1).

  5. Give two examples of Shakespeare following North's Plutarch closely.
    Answer

    Enobarbus' description of Cleopatra's barge (Antony and Cleopatra 2.2) versifies Plutarch's Antony 26 in North's prose, and Volumnia's plea in Coriolanus 5.3 keeps North's sentences; even the mother Volumnia and wife Virgilia follow Plutarch, where Livy's pair are Veturia and Volumnia.

  6. How did Gibbon's work become a model beyond the history of Rome?
    Answer

    Following Montesquieu (1734), it made "decline and fall" a pattern to be applied to any great power. Later writers multiplied causes (Demandt counted 210 in 1984), Thomas Cole painted the cycle of empire for Americans (1833–36), and the question whether America is Rome is still asked.

  7. What do Bailyn and Richard disagree about?
    Answer

    Bailyn (1967) held that the classics in revolutionary pamphlets were "illustrative, not determinative", the ideas coming from English opposition writers and the common law. Richard (1994) argued that classical models of virtue, mixed government and tyranny actually shaped the founders' thinking.

  8. What did Marx mean by the Roman costume of the French Revolution, and how did Napoleon fit his description?
    Answer

    That the revolutionaries performed a modern task in borrowed Roman dress, first the Republic's (David's Brutus, Voltaire's revived Brutus, the name Gracchus), then the empire's. Napoleon moved from First Consul to emperor, gave his army eagles, built the Arc du Carrousel and the Vendôme column, and made his son King of Rome.

  9. What did the Via dell'Impero and the Augustan Exhibition of 1937 do with the ancient city?
    Answer

    The road, opened on 28 October 1932, demolished the quarter over the imperial fora and cut away the Velia to make a broad avenue lined with maps of Roman expansion. The exhibition gathered casts from the whole empire for Augustus' bimillenary, while the Mausoleum was isolated and the Ara Pacis reassembled beside it (module 6.2).

  10. On what do Padilla Peralta and West and Tate disagree, and how does Martindale's position bear on the quarrel?
    Answer

    Padilla Peralta argued that classics has served slavery, race science, colonialism and fascism and must be remade from the ground; West and Tate called Howard's move to dissolve its department "a spiritual catastrophe". Martindale's claim that meaning is made at the point of reception implies that both are arguing over readings of Rome, not over a fixed Rome.

Carry forward

  1. After 476 Rome was an idea open to any claimant, and each claimant chose a different Rome: the papacy a Christian Constantine, emperors a renewed empire, communes and republics the Senate and Brutus, nation-states the legions and the arches. Ask of any use of Rome which Rome it chooses, and against whom.
  2. Reception works through intermediaries and inventions: Plutarch reached Shakespeare through North, Livy reached the Atlantic world through Machiavelli, the papacy's Constantine through a legend and a forgery, and Gibbon's fall through Montesquieu. Forgery and the criticism that exposes it, from the Donation to Valla, belong to the same tradition.
  3. Republic and empire have alternated as models: the Republic for revolutions (Florence, Philadelphia, Paris in 1789), the empire for consolidations (Charlemagne, Napoleon, Mussolini). The present quarrel over who owns Rome repeats the old pattern of claiming it to legitimise.
