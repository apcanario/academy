Tarquinius Superbus: the tyrant's portrait

Every narrative of these events is late. Livy wrote Books 1–2 around 27–25 BC and Dionysius of Halicarnassus published his Roman Antiquities from 7 BC, both nearly five centuries after 509 and both dependent on annalists from Fabius Pictor (about 200 BC) onward (module 0.1). What they offer is a finished portrait, and it should be read as one.

Lucius Tarquinius, who took the throne by murdering Servius Tullius (module 1.5), is a tyrant by checklist. Livy 1.49 has him deny his predecessor burial, keep a bodyguard, kill leading senators without replacing them, judge capital cases alone, and make war and treaties without senate or people; hence Superbus, "the Proud". He drove the plebs to forced labour on the Capitoline temple and the Cloaca Maxima (1.56.1–3). He was also an effective general who led the Latin league and took Suessa Pometia from the Volsci (1.50–53).

Gabii fell to a trick (Livy 1.53.4–54.10; Dionysius 4.53–58). Sextus Tarquinius fled there complaining of his father's cruelty (in Dionysius after a public scourging staged for the purpose, 4.55), won command of the Gabine forces, and sent to ask what next. Tarquin said nothing to the messenger and walked in his garden striking off the tallest poppy heads (Livy 1.54.6); Sextus killed or exiled the leading men. Both halves are Herodotean. The self-maimed deserter is Zopyrus at Babylon (Herodotus 3.153–158); the wordless advice is Thrasybulus lopping the tallest ears of corn for Periander (5.92). Yet Dionysius saw the resulting treaty, written on an ox-hide stretched over a wooden shield in the temple of Dius Fidius (4.58.4).

Livy omits the Sibylline Books. In Dionysius (4.62) and Gellius (Attic Nights 1.19) an old woman offers Tarquin nine books of oracles, burns three and then three more when he refuses her price, and sells him the last three for the original sum. They were kept beneath the Capitoline temple and consulted only on the senate's order. Varro, quoted by Lactantius (Divine Institutes 1.6), made the buyer Priscus: the story followed whichever Tarquin was credited with the Capitol.

Delphi, Lucretia and the oath on the dagger

A snake glided from a wooden column in the palace, and Tarquin sent his sons Titus and Arruns to Delphi with their cousin Lucius Junius, who had survived the king's purge by playing the fool; hence Brutus, "dullard" (Livy 1.56.4–8). His gift to Apollo was a hollow cornel-wood staff filled with gold, an image of his own mind. The oracle promised supreme power to whoever first kissed his mother. Brutus stumbled on purpose and kissed the earth, the common mother of all mortals (1.56.9–12).

At the siege of Ardea the princes and their kinsman Tarquinius Collatinus wagered on their wives and rode out to look. The royal wives were feasting; Lucretia, at Collatia, sat spinning among her maids late at night (1.57). Days later Sextus returned alone, was received as a kinsman, and at night entered her room armed. The threat of death did not move her. The threat to kill a slave beside her and leave her seeming an adulteress did, and she submitted (1.58.1–5).

She summoned her father, Spurius Lucretius, and her husband, each with one friend: Publius Valerius and Lucius Brutus. She told them, made them swear vengeance, and refused their answer that the mind sins, not the body:

No unchaste woman shall live by Lucretia's example.

Then she stabbed herself (1.58.6–12). Brutus drew out the knife and swore by her blood to pursue the king, his wife and his children with fire and sword and to suffer no king at Rome; Collatinus, Lucretius and Valerius swore after him (1.59.1–2). As tribune of the Celeres, Brutus had the people revoke the king's power and exile the family. Tarquin found the gates shut and went to Caere, Sextus was killed at Gabii, and the centuriate assembly elected Brutus and Collatinus consuls (1.59.7–60.4).

Where sources disagree. Dionysius moves the climax to Rome: Lucretia drives to the city in mourning and dies in her father's house before kin and leading men (4.66–67). His Delphic embassy concerns a plague (4.69), and his Tarquin flees to Gabii (4.85). Ovid's Regifugium (Fasti 2.685–852) follows Livy but adds her care, even dying, to fall modestly. Guest, threat, testimony, suicide and oath never vary.

The first year: Brutus' sons, Collatinus and Publicola

Livy opens Book 2 by defining the new liberty: annual office, not any reduction of royal power (2.1.7). A rex sacrorum, barred from other office, took over the king's rites (2.2.1–2). Collatinus fell first. The people could not bear the name Tarquinius in power, so he resigned and retired to Lavinium, and the whole gens was exiled (2.2.3–11); Valerius replaced him. When royal envoys came to reclaim Tarquin's property, young nobles, the Vitellii, the Aquilii and Brutus' sons Titus and Tiberius among them, plotted a restoration, and the slave Vindicius informed (2.3–4). Brutus watched his sons flogged and beheaded, his father's feeling visible through the public office (2.5.8).

Veii and Tarquinii marched for the king. At the Silva Arsia, Brutus and Arruns Tarquinius charged each other and fell together; at night a voice from the wood, thought to be Silvanus', declared that the Etruscans had lost one man more (2.6–7.2). The matrons mourned Brutus for a year. Valerius, suspected of aiming at kingship because of his house on the Velia, pulled it down, lowered the fasces before the assembly and carried laws granting appeal to the people (provocatio) and outlawing would-be kings, earning the name Publicola (2.7.5–8.2). The year's fifth consul, Marcus Horatius Pulvillus, dedicated the Capitoline temple (2.8.4–8).

Where sources disagree. Dionysius (5.9–12) and Plutarch (Publicola 7) put the conspiracy first and have Collatinus deposed for pleading for the Aquilii, his sister's sons; Livy's Collatinus is exiled for his name alone. Dionysius says Brutus left no children (5.18), but Posidonius, cited by Plutarch (Brutus 1), had a third son survive to found the Junii Bruti, a claim that served Caesar's assassin. The appeal law recurs in 449 and 300 (Livy 10.9.3–6), and its 509 version is generally treated as a retrojection.

Porsenna of Clusium: heroes and a surrender

Tarquin then turned to Lars Porsenna, king of Clusium (Livy 2.9). The Etruscans took the Janiculum. Horatius Cocles held the far end of the Pons Sublicius, first with Spurius Lartius and Titus Herminius, then alone, until the bridge fell behind him, and swam back in armour (2.10). Gaius Mucius entered Porsenna's camp to kill him, stabbed the king's secretary by mistake, and when taken held his right hand in an altar fire, warning that three hundred young men had sworn the same oath; Porsenna released him, and the left-handed hero became Scaevola (2.12). Peace brought hostages and the return of land taken from Veii. The hostage Cloelia led the girls back across the Tiber, and Porsenna, after demanding her return, let her free half the hostages; she chose the boys (2.13).

Where sources disagree. Polybius, writing some 350 years after the event, has Horatius drown by choice (6.55); Livy has him swim to safety, Dionysius survive lamed (5.24–25). Each hero had a monument, and Ogilvie (A Commentary on Livy Books 1–5, 1965) and others read the tales as their aetiologies: Horatius' statue was moved after a lightning strike to the precinct of Vulcan, the lame god (Gellius 4.5), and the "Cloelia" on the Sacred Way was claimed for Valerius' daughter (Plutarch, Publicola 19). Dumézil (Mitra-Varuna, 1940) instead saw one-eyed Cocles and one-handed Scaevola as an inherited pair, like Odin and Tyr.

A rival tradition has Porsenna win. Tacitus, six centuries later, says that neither Porsenna, "when the city was surrendered", nor the Gauls had violated the Capitoline temple (Histories 3.72). Pliny had read Porsenna's treaty, which forbade the Romans to use iron except in farming (Natural History 34.139), a conqueror's disarmament clause. Dionysius has the senate send Porsenna an ivory throne, sceptre, gold crown and triumphal robe (5.35), and the old auction formula "the goods of King Porsenna are sold" (Livy 2.14.1) sounds like booty. Porsenna's son Arruns then attacked Aricia: the Etruscan objective was Latium, not Tarquin's restoration.

Where sources disagree. Alföldi (Early Rome and the Latins, 1965) took the capture as fact and made Porsenna, not Brutus, the man who ended the Tarquin monarchy. Cornell (The Beginnings of Rome, 1995) and most recent historians accept the occupation but leave open whether Porsenna came for Tarquin or for himself. Livy and Dionysius keep the honourable peace; the surrender is the version no Roman had reason to invent.

Aristodemus of Cumae, Aricia and Lake Regillus

Dionysius' digression on Aristodemus of Cumae (7.3–12) derives, most scholars think, from a Cumaean Greek chronicle independent of the Roman annalists. Aristodemus, nicknamed Malakos, "the Soft", won fame when Cumae beat an Etruscan and Umbrian army in 524 (7.3–4). In the twentieth year after, about 504, he led the Cumaeans to relieve Aricia and killed the Etruscan commander himself (7.5–6), whom Dionysius elsewhere names as Arruns (5.36; compare Livy 2.14.5–9). Back home he massacred the aristocracy and ruled as tyrant. Tarquin died at his court in 495 (Livy 2.21.5), and Aristodemus, as his heir, later held Roman grain ships (2.34.4). The Greek dates corroborate the Roman structure: an Etruscan army in Latium after Tarquin's fall, broken by Latins and Greeks.

The last throw came through Tarquin's son-in-law Octavius Mamilius of Tusculum, who led the Latins at Lake Regillus against the dictator Aulus Postumius; Mamilius fell and Tarquin was wounded (Livy 2.19–20). Postumius vowed a temple to Castor, dedicated in 484 (2.20.12, 2.42.5). Dionysius (6.13) adds that two horsemen of superhuman stature led the Roman charge and that evening watered their horses at the spring of Juturna in the Forum, announced the victory and vanished. A bronze plaque from Lavinium of about 500, dedicated to Castor and Pollux as kouroi (module 1.1), shows the cult was already in Latium.

Where sources disagree. Livy places the battle in 499 but notes that others put it in 496, Dionysius' year (Livy 2.21.3–4; Dionysius 6.2). Livy omits the epiphany, which Cicero (On the Nature of the Gods 2.6) and Plutarch (Coriolanus 3) know. The twins had already ridden for Locri at the river Sagra, the news of victory reaching Greece the same day (Justin 20.3): the Roman story fits a Greek template.

The documentary evidence

The first treaty with Carthage

Polybius (3.22) quotes a treaty dated to Lucius Junius Brutus and Marcus Horatius, "the first consuls after the expulsion of the kings", twenty-eight years before Xerxes crossed to Greece: 508/7. Writing some 350 years later, he worked from the bronze kept beside the Capitoline temple (3.26.1), in Latin so archaic that experts could barely construe it. Romans are not to sail beyond the Fair Promontory unless forced; trade in Libya and Sardinia requires a herald; Carthage must not harm Ardea, Antium, Laurentum, Circeii, Tarracina or other subject Latins, nor build a fort in Latium. The Republic had inherited the Tarquins' coastal hegemony intact.

Where sources disagree. Diodorus (16.69) calls the treaty of 348, also in Livy (7.27.2), the first between Rome and Carthage, and Mommsen (Römische Chronologie, 1859) identified Polybius' text with it. Beaumont ("The Date of the First Treaty between Rome and Carthage", JRS 1939) and Cornell keep 509/8: Polybius saw the bronze, its language was archaic, and Antium and Tarracina as Roman dependants fit the sixth century, not the fourth. The early date is the majority view.

The Satricum stone

In 1977 Dutch excavators at Satricum in southern Latium found a stone reused in the foundations of the second temple of Mater Matuta, inscribed about 500 BC: the suodales of Poplios Valesios set it up to Mars (Mamartei) (Stibbe, Colonna, De Simone and Versnel, Lapis Satricanus, 1980). That he is Publicola is possible, not provable. The key word is suodales: companions bound to a leader, not a city. With the clan army of the Fabii at the Cremera in 477 (Livy 2.48–50) and the Vibenna brothers (module 1.5), it points to a world of warlords, in which 509 may have been one aristocratic following displacing another.

The Pyrgi tablets

The Pyrgi tablets (module 1.2) record about 500 BC that Thefarie Velianas dedicated a holy place to Uni, equated with Astarte, in the third year of his rule. The Phoenician calls him king over Caere; the Etruscan uses zilac, a magistrate's title. Superbus' neighbour and contemporary thus ruled in a form between king and magistrate, with Carthaginian ties public enough to inscribe, as Caere's alliance with Carthage at Alalia (Herodotus 1.166–167) would predict.

The consular lists

The magistrate lists in Livy, Dionysius, Diodorus and the Augustan Fasti Capitolini all begin with Brutus and Collatinus. Two items anchor them: Polybius' treaty, and the shrine of Concord dedicated by Cn. Flavius in 304, inscribed as built 204 years after the Capitoline dedication, again 508/7 (Pliny, Natural History 33.19). Livy (7.3.5–8) knew an archaic law making the praetor maximus drive a nail into the temple each September. Cornell argues the early lists are substantially authentic, since they contain families later plebeian that no patrician forger would have inserted; Beloch (Römische Geschichte, 1926) held them heavily interpolated. Whether the first magistrates were paired consuls or a praetor maximus with junior colleagues is uncertain.

Superbus also matches the Greek theory of the tyrant. Otanes' monarch violates women and kills men untried (Herodotus 3.80.5); Aristotle lists tyrannies overthrown for outrages against subjects, the Peisistratids included (Politics 1311a), and great building works as a device for keeping subjects poor and busy (1313b). Livy's king fulfils every item.

Where sources disagree. Gjerstad lowered the end of the monarchy to about 450 (module 1.5), and Alföldi argued that Fabius Pictor, writing in Greek for Greeks, shaped early Rome to Greek patterns. Cornell answers that the Greek colouring is literary but the date is anchored by the treaty and Flavius' inscription, and that the coincidence reflects a real wave of tyrannies across Greece and Etruria around 500. The best reading is a real event of 509–504 told in borrowed forms.

Lucretia as political myth

Livy's preface states the method: history sets every kind of exemplum on a monument, from which "you may choose for yourself and your state what to imitate" (praef. 10). Lucretia's last words name herself as one. The story makes a citizen wife's chastity the boundary of Roman liberty, and Livy repeats it for 449, when Verginius kills his daughter to save her from the decemvir Appius Claudius and the decemvirate falls (3.44–58; unit 3). Sandra Joshel ("The Body Female and the Body Politic", 1992) reads both as stories in which a woman must die so that men can act.

Augustine, writing after the sack of 410 and for Christian women raped in it, attacks the exemplum (City of God 1.19). He cites a declaimer, "there were two, and only one committed adultery", and sets the dilemma:

If adulterous, why praised? If chaste, why killed?

Chastity lives in the will (1.18); Lucretia, he argues, killed an innocent woman out of the Roman love of praise and fear of being thought complicit. Machiavelli returned the story to politics: Brutus was wise to feign folly (Discourses 3.2), and Tarquin lost his kingdom by breaking the laws, not through his son's crime (3.5).

Later artists choose between the private story and the public one. Shakespeare's The Rape of Lucrece (1594), 1,855 lines of rhyme royal dedicated to the Earl of Southampton, confines the politics to its prose Argument and last stanza, where the Romans consent "to Tarquin's everlasting banishment"; the poem itself is Tarquin's self-debate, Lucrece's complaints against Night, Opportunity and Time, and her long gaze at a painting of Troy. Titian's Tarquin and Lucretia (1571, Fitzwilliam Museum, Cambridge), painted for Philip II, isolates the assault without any civic frame. Botticelli's panel of about 1500 (Isabella Stewart Gardner Museum, Boston) had placed her body in a forum with Brutus calling Rome to arms, a republican reading for Florence after the Medici fell in 1494. Britten's chamber opera The Rape of Lucretia (Glyndebourne, 1946; libretto by Ronald Duncan after André Obey) sets the story between a Male and a Female Chorus who comment as Christians and close by answering her death with Christ's: Augustine's frame, inside the opera.

Self-test

  1. Which elements of the Gabii story come from Herodotus, and what keeps the episode from being pure invention?
    Answer

    Sextus' false desertion copies Zopyrus at Babylon (Herodotus 3.153–158), and the poppy heads copy Thrasybulus' ears of corn (5.92). But Dionysius saw a treaty with Gabii on an ox-hide shield in the temple of Dius Fidius (4.58.4), so a real relationship lies behind the literary dress.

  2. How do Livy and Dionysius differ over where Lucretia died, and why does it matter?
    Answer

    Livy has her die at Collatia, so the revolt begins there before Brutus carries it to Rome (1.58–59). Dionysius has her drive to Rome and die in her father's house before leading citizens (4.66–67), making the revolution an urban, civic act from the start.

  3. What is Livy's definition of the new liberty, and how does the first year test it?
    Answer

    Liberty begins with annual consular power, not with any reduction of royal power (2.1.7). The year tests it through a consul exiled for his name, a father executing his sons, and Valerius demolishing his house and lowering the fasces to dispel suspicion of kingship.

  4. Give the two ancient explanations for Collatinus leaving office.
    Answer

    Livy 2.2: the people could not tolerate a Tarquinius in power, so he went for his name alone. Dionysius 5.9–12 and Plutarch, Publicola 7: he pleaded for his nephews the Aquilii after the conspiracy and was deposed.

  5. State three versions of Horatius' fate and one modern explanation of the legend.
    Answer

    Polybius 6.55: he drowns by choice; Livy 2.10: he swims to safety; Dionysius 5.24–25: he survives lamed. Either an aetiology of a statue later set in Vulcan's precinct (Gellius 4.5), or, for Dumézil, an inherited Indo-European one-eyed hero.

  6. What evidence suggests Porsenna actually took Rome?
    Answer

    Tacitus, Histories 3.72, speaks of the city surrendered to him; Pliny, Natural History 34.139, cites a treaty forbidding iron except in farming; Dionysius 5.35 has regalia sent to him; the formula for selling his goods (Livy 2.14.1) suggests booty.

  7. Why does Dionysius' account of Aristodemus matter for Roman history?
    Answer

    It comes from a Greek source independent of the annalists and dates the Etruscan defeat at Aricia to about 504, twenty years after Cumae's victory of 524 (7.5). It confirms an Etruscan campaign in Latium after Tarquin's fall and explains Tarquin's last refuge at Cumae.

  8. What are the arguments for dating Polybius' first treaty to 509/8 rather than 348?
    Answer

    Polybius dates it to Brutus and Horatius and worked from the bronze; its Latin was archaic; it treats Antium and Tarracina as Roman dependants, which fits before the fifth-century Volscian advance. Mommsen followed Diodorus 16.69 in preferring 348.

  9. Name three parallels between the fall of the Peisistratids and of the Tarquins, and the main argument against wholesale borrowing.
    Answer

    A sexual outrage as trigger, a Delphic role, and foreign attempts to restore the tyrant, who dies in exile under a foreign patron. Against borrowing: the date is anchored independently by Polybius' treaty and Flavius' inscription (Pliny, Natural History 33.19), and tyrannies were really falling across the region around 500 (Cornell).

  10. State Augustine's dilemma about Lucretia and the situation that prompted it.
    Answer

    If adulterous, why praised; if chaste, why killed (City of God 1.19)? He wrote after the sack of 410 for Christian women raped in it, arguing that chastity resides in the will and that Lucretia's suicide sprang from the Roman love of glory.

Carry forward

  1. The narrative of 509 is a literary construction, written about five centuries later and shaped by Greek models (Herodotus, the tyrant topos, the Dioscuri), yet a few documents (Polybius' treaty, Flavius' inscription, the Satricum stone, the Pyrgi tablets) anchor a real change around 509–504. Separate the frame from the anchors.
  2. The fall of the monarchy was a regional event: Porsenna, Aristodemus, the Latins and Carthage all had a hand in it, and the early Republic emerges in a Tyrrhenian world of tyrants and warlords with private followings, which the Fabii and Coriolanus of unit 3 continue.
  3. Lucretia and Brutus define Roman libertas as annual office, an inviolable citizen household and the state placed above family. The pattern of an outraged woman toppling a tyranny returns with Verginia in 449, and the myth was reused by Caesar's assassins, Augustine, Machiavelli and the Renaissance.
