01Why drop "Silver Latin"

Latin literature after Augustus has long been filed as a Silver Age, second to a Golden Age from Cicero to Ovid. The metals come from the myth of declining races (Hesiod, Works and Days 109–201; Ovid, Metamorphoses 1.89–150), applied to Latin by early modern philologists; for English readers J. Wight Duff fixed the scheme in A Literary History of Rome in the Silver Age (1927), from Tiberius to Hadrian. The label turns a period into a verdict, so that point and paradox, which Ovid used freely, become symptoms. It misreads poets who wrote with the Aeneid open, answering it rather than failing to equal it. And it stops in the second century, leaving Apuleius and Augustine outside literature. Scholars now speak of Neronian, Flavian and Trajanic writing, by reign, the work of men for whom praise, silence and indirection were the conditions of writing under a princeps. The story of decline is also ancient: first-century Romans told it about eloquence.

Where sources disagree. Seneca made style a symptom of morals: as men live, so they speak (Letters 114.1). Petronius' Encolpius blames the declamation schools (Satyricon 1–2), and Quintilian wrote a lost book on the causes of corrupted eloquence. In Tacitus' Dialogue on Orators Messalla blames upbringing and the schools (28–35); Maternus answers that great oratory fed on the disorder of the free Republic, which peace under one man has ended (36–41).

02Seneca's tragedies

Seneca's life and philosophy are deep dive module 13.5, his satire on Claudius module 7.1; here he is the one Roman tragedian whose plays survive whole. Eight of the ten under his name are accepted: Hercules Furens, Trojan Women, Phoenician Women (unfinished, without a chorus), Medea, Phaedra, Oedipus, Agamemnon and Thyestes; Hercules Oetaeus is doubted. Octavia, a historical play on Nero's divorce and killing of his wife, puts Seneca on stage and foretells Nero's end, so it postdates 68; the eleventh-century Etruscus manuscript omits it. None of the plays is securely dated.

The plots are Greek (the Theban saga is Greek module 3.5), the treatment Roman. Seneca compressed his models and moved the interest from action to passion: each play studies furor, anger, lust or grief swelling past every limit in self-exhorting monologues. Medea talks herself into her own name, "Medea nunc sum" (Medea 910), and kills her children in the audience's sight; in Thyestes a Fury drives Tantalus' ghost to infect his house, and Atreus feeds Thyestes his sons. Ghosts, messengers' anatomical horror, five acts and the pointed sententia are what the Renaissance took. Thomas Newton's collection, Seneca His Tenne Tragedies (1581), put all ten into English, and Kyd's Spanish Tragedy and Shakespeare's Hamlet owe their ghosts and revenge to Seneca more than to any Greek play.

Where sources disagree. Otto Zwierlein (Die Rezitationsdramen Senecas, 1966) argued that the plays were written for recitation, since scenes such as the reading of entrails in Oedipus are hard to stage. Dana Sutton (Seneca on the Stage, 1986) answered that every scene can be performed. No ancient source records a performance.

03Lucan: the anti-epic of civil war

Marcus Annaeus Lucanus, Seneca's nephew, born at Corduba in 39, was dead at twenty-five. His ten books on the war of Caesar and Pompey, transmitted as Civil War, are often called Pharsalia from his boast that "our Pharsalia" will live (9.985–986); the poem breaks off with Caesar besieged at Alexandria (10.546).

It is written against the Aeneid. The first line promises wars "more than civil" (1.1). No Olympian intervenes; Fortune and Fate drive events. Virgil's hero founds; Lucan's leaders destroy or fail. Pompey, "the shadow of a great name", is an old oak standing by its own weight, Caesar the lightning that rejoices to make its way by ruin (1.135–157); Cato, the third, belongs to deep dive module 13.4. The winning cause pleased the gods, the losing one Cato (1.128). Book 6 inverts Aeneid 6: instead of a Sibyl showing Rome's future heroes, the witch Erictho reanimates a corpse to prophesy ruin to Pompey's son (6.507–830). Quintilian thought Lucan fiery, famous for his sententiae, and "more to be imitated by orators than by poets" (10.1.90). Nero forbade him to recite (Tacitus, Annals 15.49.3); a Pisonian conspirator, ordered to die, he recited his own lines on a soldier dying the same death (15.70.1; module 7.1). Statius wrote a birthday poem for him at his widow's request (Silvae 2.7).

Where sources disagree. The proem calls the civil wars worth their cost if they led to Nero, and asks him not to tilt heaven by his weight when he takes his place among the stars (1.33–66). Frederick Ahl (Lucan: An Introduction, 1976) read the praise as double-edged; Michael Dewar ("Laying It On with a Trowel", Classical Quarterly 1994) set it among conventional panegyrics and denied that its excess was meant to amuse.

04Petronius' Satyricon and Trimalchio's dinner

The Satyricon survives in fragments, probably of books 14 to 16, in prose with verse inset, the mixed form of Menippean satire. The narrator Encolpius drifts through southern Italy with his rival Ascyltos, the boy Giton and later the poet Eumolpus, pursued, he says, by Priapus' wrath as Ulysses was by Neptune's (139.2). Epic and Greek romance are parodied, the romance's faithful couple becoming a fickle threesome. So is criticism: the text opens on the decline of eloquence, and Eumolpus' Civil War (119–124), 295 hexameters restoring the gods Lucan removed, is usually read as an answer to Lucan. Eumolpus also tells the Widow of Ephesus (111–112), seduced in her husband's tomb by a soldier guarding crucified men.

Trimalchio's dinner (26.7–78) survives in a single manuscript found at Trogir in Dalmatia about 1650. Module 9.3 reads the host as a social type; as literature the dinner parodies the philosophical banquet, a symposium whose talk is money, funerals and the weather. Every course is theatre: a zodiac dish (35), a boar that releases live thrushes (40), a pig cut open to spill sausages (49). The freedmen's talk (41–46) is the fullest record of colloquial Latin. Trimalchio ends by reading his will and rehearsing his funeral until the watch, thinking the house on fire, breaks in (71–78). F. Scott Fitzgerald considered calling The Great Gatsby "Trimalchio in West Egg".

Where sources disagree. The manuscripts name the author Petronius Arbiter, and Tacitus describes Petronius, Nero's arbiter elegantiae, who died in 66 after sending Nero a sealed list of his debauches (Annals 16.18–19). K. F. C. Rose (The Date and Author of the Satyricon, 1971) argued from names, prices and the quarrels with Lucan and Seneca for a Neronian date and that identification; Enzo Marmorale (La questione petroniana, 1948) had argued from the language for a much later date. Most accept the courtier, though Tacitus mentions no novel.

05Satire and epigram: Persius, Juvenal, Martial

Quintilian claimed satire as wholly Roman (10.1.93). Persius (34–62), a rich young man from Volaterrae taught by the Stoic Cornutus, left six short satires, published after his death: compressed to obscurity, full of Horace turned inside out, Stoic in doctrine. The first mocks fashionable recitations ("who will read this?", 1.2); the fifth argues that only the philosopher is free.

Juvenal is almost unknown outside his sixteen satires, written from about 110 into the late 120s: Satire 1 alludes to Marius Priscus' conviction in 100 (1.49–50), Satire 15 to the consulship of Iuncus, 127 (15.27). It is hard not to write satire (1.30), and where talent fails, indignation makes the verse (1.79); yet he attacks only the dead (1.170–171), Domitian's Rome assailed under Hadrian. Satire 3 drives Umbricius from a city of Greeks and fires (module 9.4); Satire 6 rails against marriage; Satire 10, on the vanity of prayer, ends with a sound mind in a sound body (10.356) and became Johnson's The Vanity of Human Wishes (1749). The last books are cooler.

Where sources disagree. Gilbert Highet (Juvenal the Satirist, 1954) read the satires as autobiography, a life of poverty and disappointment. William Anderson's essays of the 1960s and Susanna Braund (Beyond Anger, 1988) treat the angry speaker as a persona the poet constructs and changes, which explains why rage gives way to irony. The persona reading prevails, and it leaves Juvenal's own views unknown.

Martial (about 40 to about 104), from Bilbilis in Spain, wrote On the Spectacles for Titus' amphitheatre in 80, Saturnalia gift-tags (books 13–14) and twelve books of Epigrams from 86 to about 101; his flattery of Domitian is module 7.2's. His programme is anti-mythological: readers of Oedipus, Thyestes and Medea read only monsters, while "my page tastes of man" (10.4.10). That page is the city, its patrons, clients, legacy-hunters and dinners, its obscenity defended as a playful page from a decent life (1.4.8). He fixed the closing sting that Lessing analysed in 1771 as expectation and resolution. Pliny, who paid his fare home, doubted the poems would last (Letters 3.21).

06The Flavian epic: Valerius, Statius, Silius

Module 7.2 places these poets at court; as poems, all three answer Virgil with myth or the distant past. Valerius Flaccus' unfinished Argonautica retells Apollonius (Greek module 3.4) and Romanises him: in book 1 Jupiter makes the Argo the start of a passing of power from Asia to Greece and onward, and Medea's love is modelled on Dido's.

Statius of Naples (about 45 to about 96) spent twelve years, he says, on the Thebaid (12.811–812), the Seven against Thebes (Greek module 3.5): civil war displaced into myth, with brothers' hatred, Furies who start the action, helpless gods, and atrocity at its height when the dying Tydeus gnaws his killer's skull and Minerva, bringing him immortality, turns away (book 8). Theseus comes from Athens, city of an altar to Clemency, to enforce burial of the dead (book 12). The unfinished Achilleid hides Achilles on Scyros (Greek module 10.1). The thirty-two Silvae, occasional poems on villas, roads, a dead father and Sleep (5.4), show another Statius. Dante made him a secret Christian converted by Virgil's fourth Eclogue (Purgatorio 21–22).

Silius Italicus' Punica, seventeen books on the Hannibalic war, takes events from Livy and machinery from Virgil: Juno drives Hannibal to avenge Dido, and young Scipio chooses Virtue over Pleasure (book 15), the Choice of Hercules made Roman. Pliny judged that Silius wrote with more care than talent (Letters 3.7.5).

07The Plinys and Quintilian

The elder Pliny (23/24–79) dedicated the Natural History to Titus in 77: thirty-seven books from the cosmos through geography, man, animals, plants and their medicines to metals and stones, where the history of Greek art survives as a digression (books 33–37). He claims 20,000 facts from about 2,000 volumes by a hundred authors (preface 17), and book 1 lists his sources. His nephew describes notes taken while he ate, bathed and travelled, 160 notebooks, and his saying that no book is so bad that no part of it is useful (Letters 3.5); his death is module 7.2's. Nature provides, and luxury, digging into her body for gold, abuses her (33.1–3). The Middle Ages used the work as their encyclopaedia.

The younger Pliny (about 61 to about 112) published nine books of letters, each letter on one subject, arranged, he says, without regard to date (1.1). Cicero's were written to be sent (module 5.6); Pliny's were written to be read, a self-portrait of the good senator: his villa (2.17), his wife Calpurnia (4.19), a ghost story (7.27). Book 10 is module 7.5's.

Quintilian of Calagurris was the first rhetorician paid from the treasury: Suetonius gives Vespasian's salaries as 100,000 sesterces (Vespasian 18), and Jerome's Chronicle names Quintilian first. His Institutio oratoria (mid-90s) trains the orator from the cradle and defines him, after Cato, as a good man skilled in speaking (12.1.1). Book 10 gives a reading list with verdicts (10.1.46–131), the first canon of Latin literature, Cicero its model (10.1.112). Poggio's discovery of a complete manuscript at St Gall in 1416 made him the humanists' schoolmaster.

08Tacitus and Suetonius as writers

Module 0.1 introduces both as sources, and modules 7.1 and 7.2 use them. Tacitus (about 56 to about 120), praetor in 88 (Annals 11.11.1) and consul in 97, wrote the Agricola and Germania (98), the Dialogue on Orators, the Histories (69–96) and the Annals (14–68), thirty books for the two histories by Jerome's count (Commentary on Zechariah 3.14). What survives of the two histories rests on two manuscripts, one for Annals 1–6 and one from Monte Cassino for the rest.

His style is built against Cicero's: brevity, asymmetry, archaic and poetic words, Sallust the model (module 5.6). He promises to write without anger or partiality (1.1.3) and judges by arrangement. At Augustus' funeral the prudent men give two verdicts (1.9–10): the favourable one short, the hostile one longer and last. Rumour is reported and disowned, motives are offered as alternatives, and an epigram closes the paragraph. The Ciceronian Dialogue shows the manner was chosen. Nerva, he wrote, had joined principate and liberty, things once irreconcilable (Agricola 3.1); the Annals show senators learning servility.

Suetonius (about 70 to after 122), Pliny's protégé (Letters 10.94), ran the palace libraries and correspondence, as an inscription from Hippo Regius shows, and the late Historia Augusta has him dismissed under Hadrian (Hadrian 11.3). The Lives of the Caesars, Julius to Domitian, treat each life by categories rather than time (Augustus 9): family, career, public acts, private habits, appearance, death. He quotes documents, above all Augustus' letters, and lets the sorting of virtues and vices do the judging. Nepos had written Latin lives (module 5.6), Plutarch moral portraits in Greek; Suetonius set the form of imperial biography, continued by Marius Maximus, imitated by the Historia Augusta (module 0.1) and followed in Einhard's Life of Charlemagne.

09Apuleius: the one complete Latin novel

Apuleius of Madauros, Platonist and orator, answered a charge of magic in the Apology (module 2.4). Augustine already called his eleven-book Metamorphoses the Golden Ass (City of God 18.18). The prologue promises Milesian tales to please the ear (1.1). Lucius, curious about magic, lodges at Hypata in Thessaly, gets the wrong ointment through the maid Photis and becomes an ass with a man's mind (3.24–25). Owned by robbers, begging priests of the Syrian goddess, a miller and a soldier, he sees the hidden life of the provinces and its adulteries (book 9), until he flees the arena at Corinth and is restored by Isis (book 11; module 2.5 has the dispute over its sincerity).

At the centre an old woman tells a captive bride the tale of Cupid and Psyche (4.28–6.24), a fifth of the whole. Psyche looks by lamplight at her forbidden husband, loses him, and wins him back through Venus' labours; her curiosity mirrors Lucius', and a goddess saves both. A Greek Ass among Lucian's works tells Lucius' story without Psyche or Isis, and Photius judged that it and a Greek Metamorphoses by Lucius of Patrae copied one from the other (Bibliotheca codex 129): Apuleius added the inset tales, the tone and the ending. Boccaccio took the lover in the tub (9.5–7) for the Decameron (7.2), Raphael's workshop painted Psyche in the Villa Farnesina, and C. S. Lewis retold her story in Till We Have Faces (1956).

Where sources disagree. Fulgentius (Mythologies 3.6) read the tale as allegory, Psyche the soul and Cupid desire, and Platonist readings still have defenders. Jan-Öjvind Swahn (The Tale of Cupid and Psyche, 1955) treated it as the oldest written version of an international folktale of the search for a lost husband. Detlev Fehling (Amor und Psyche, 1977) reversed the direction: Apuleius built the story from literary motifs, and the folktales descend from his book.

10Coda: late Latin and the bridge to the Middle Ages

Pagan Latin literature thins in the third century and returns changed. Ausonius of Bordeaux (about 310 to about 395), Gratian's tutor and consul in 379, wrote the Mosella, a poem on the river, and a wedding cento of Virgil's half-lines rearranged into obscenity. Claudian, an Alexandrian Greek writing Latin hexameters, turned Stilicho's politics into panegyric and invective (module 8.5) and left an unfinished Rape of Proserpina. Ammianus Marcellinus, module 8.4's main source, began his history in 96, where Tacitus' Histories ended; his digressions on the Roman nobles and plebs (14.6, 28.4) are satire inside history.

The decisive works were Christian. Jerome (about 347–420), asked by Pope Damasus to revise the Latin Gospels in 383, went on to translate the Old Testament from the Hebrew; his version, the Vulgate, became the Bible of the Latin Middle Ages. He dreamt that he was flogged as "a Ciceronian, not a Christian" (Letters 22.30), and went on writing like one. Augustine's Confessions (about 397–400), addressed to God, make a life into an argument: Cicero's Hortensius turned him to philosophy (3.4.7), his tears for Dido (1.13; deep dive module 12.4) became a charge against his schooling, and a child's "take up and read" sent him to Paul (8.12.29). Virgil and Cicero survived inside the Christian books that renounced them.

11Self-test

  1. Why is "Silver Latin" a misleading label, and what shows that the idea of decline is also ancient?
    Answer

    It borrows the myth of declining metals to turn a period into a verdict, reads deliberate answers to Virgil as failures, and stops before Apuleius and late Latin. Romans themselves argued about the decline of eloquence: Seneca blamed morals (Letters 114.1), Tacitus' Messalla the schools and Maternus the end of free politics (Dialogue 28–41).

  2. What makes Seneca's tragedies Roman rather than Greek, and why is the Octavia not his?
    Answer

    He compresses Greek plots and shifts the interest to the passion, furor, voiced in self-exhorting monologues, with ghosts, horror in anatomical detail and sententiae; Medea kills her children in sight. The Octavia puts Seneca on stage and foretells Nero's end, so it postdates 68.

  3. In what ways is Lucan's Civil War an anti-Aeneid?
    Answer

    It removes the gods as actors in favour of Fortune and Fate, tells civil war instead of a founding, gives it leaders who destroy or fail (Caesar the lightning, Pompey the shadow of a name, 1.135–157), and inverts the underworld book with Erictho's necromancy (6.507–830). The gods backed the winning cause, Cato the losing one (1.128).

  4. Which literary targets does the Satyricon mock, and what kind of scene is Trimalchio's dinner?
    Answer

    Epic (Encolpius pursued by Priapus as Ulysses by Neptune, 139.2), Greek romance, the declamation schools (1–2) and Lucan, through Eumolpus' Civil War with its restored gods (119–124). The dinner (26.7–78) parodies the philosophical banquet: staged courses, talk of money and funerals, and a rehearsed funeral at the end.

  5. What is Juvenal's stated programme, and how have Highet and Braund read the speaker?
    Answer

    Indignation makes his verse (1.79), but he attacks only the dead (1.170–171). Highet (1954) read the satires as autobiography; Braund (1988), after Anderson, as a constructed persona that moves from anger to irony across the books.

  6. How does Martial define his poetry against mythological verse?
    Answer

    Readers of Oedipus, Thyestes and Medea read only monsters, while his page "tastes of man" (10.4.10): the city's patrons, clients, legacy-hunters and dinners, ending on a sting. He defended his obscenity as a playful page from a decent life (1.4.8).

  7. How does each Flavian epic poet answer Virgil?
    Answer

    Valerius makes the Argo the start of a passage of empire that leads towards Rome and models Medea's love on Dido's. Statius displaces civil war into the Theban myth, with Furies and helpless gods, and ends with Theseus enforcing burial (books 8 and 12). Silius puts Virgil's divine machinery into Livy's war: Juno avenges Dido through Hannibal.

  8. How do Tacitus and Suetonius pass judgement differently?
    Answer

    Tacitus judges by arrangement and innuendo, giving the hostile verdict on Augustus more length and the last word (Annals 1.9–10) and closing on an epigram. Suetonius sorts each life by categories rather than time (Augustus 9) and lets the classification of virtues and vices, backed by quoted documents, carry the verdict.

  9. How is Cupid and Psyche connected to the rest of the Golden Ass, and what do Swahn and Fehling disagree about?
    Answer

    Psyche's forbidden curiosity mirrors Lucius', and a goddess saves both; the tale, absent from the Greek Ass, is one of Apuleius' additions. Swahn (1955) took it as the earliest written version of an international folktale; Fehling (1977) argued that Apuleius made it up and the folktales derive from him.

  10. How did Jerome and Augustine carry classical Latin into the Middle Ages?
    Answer

    Jerome's Gospel revision and Old Testament translation from the Hebrew became the Vulgate, the work of a man who dreamt he was condemned as a Ciceronian (Letters 22.30). Augustine's Confessions show a convert formed by Cicero's Hortensius and Virgil's Dido, so the classical schoolroom survived inside the books that renounced it.

12Carry forward

  1. Imperial literature is a conversation with the Augustan classics, not a decline from them: Lucan, Valerius, Statius and Silius answer the Aeneid, the satirists rework Lucilius and Horace, and Petronius parodies epic and the critics of his own day.
  2. Under a princeps writing works by indirection: praise that may be irony, attacks kept for the dead, civil war displaced into myth, and history that judges by arrangement. Ask of each text what its author could safely say, and how he said the rest.
  3. The forms this period made, the comic novel, the epigram with a sting, biography by categories, the encyclopaedia and Quintilian's canon, together with the Christian Latin of Jerome and Augustine, are what the Middle Ages and the Renaissance inherited alongside Virgil.

The prompt this answers

Give me an in-depth report on Latin literature under the emperors, AD 14 to c. 200, with a coda on late antiquity. Cover: the 'Silver Latin' label and why to drop it; Seneca's tragedies (deep dive module 13.5 takes his life and philosophy); Lucan's anti-epic on the civil war; Petronius' Satyricon and Trimalchio's dinner; Persius and Juvenal; Martial; the Flavian epic poets (Statius' Thebaid and Silvae, Silius Italicus' Punica, Valerius Flaccus' Argonautica), whom module 7.2 places at court; the elder and younger Pliny; Quintilian on the orator; Tacitus as historian and stylist; Suetonius and the biographical tradition; Apuleius' Golden Ass as the one complete Latin novel; and a short coda on late Latin (Ausonius, Claudian, Ammianus, Jerome's Vulgate, Augustine's Confessions) as the bridge to the Middle Ages. Format: about 2500 words, headed sections, name sources, flag disagreements, end with a 10-question self-test with answers and the three ideas I must carry forward.
