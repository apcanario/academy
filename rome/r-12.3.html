01The Iliadic half and the second proem

Books 7–12 answer the Iliad: a war over a promised bride, an alliance of peoples, a young friend killed, a hero's new armour made by a god at his mother's asking, and a final duel in sight of the walls. Module 12.1 sets out Donatus' plan of a poem matching both of Homer's (Life 21) and G. N. Knauer's scene-by-scene map of the correspondences (Die Aeneis und Homer, 1964); module 12.2 takes books 1–6; module 6.3 places the whole poem among the Augustan writers. The Greek side is Greek module 4.2 for the Iliad as a whole and Greek module 10.6 for its seven key books, read closely; the passages are not retold here.

The roles do not hold still. In book 6 the Sibyl foretells a second Achilles born in Latium, alius Latio iam partus Achilles (6.89), and a foreign bride as the cause of a second war (6.93–94). Turnus takes the part on himself: inside the Trojan camp he tells an enemy he is about to kill to report to Priam that here too an Achilles has been found (9.742). By the end Aeneas is the one who kills a man begging for his body and his life, and Turnus is the defender of his people who runs before him. The half is also not purely Iliadic. A hero lands in the country he is to rule, finds allies in a humble host and fights for a bride claimed by a rival, which is the shape of Odyssey 13–24; Virgil has fused the two plots, so that Ithaca becomes a war.

The book opens with a new invocation, to Erato (7.37), the Muse Apollonius had called on at the start of his own third book, the book of Medea's love. Virgil announces wars, kings driven by their passions to death, Etruscan troops and all Italy under arms:

maior rerum mihi nascitur ordo, / maius opus moveo (7.44–45)

A greater order of things is born for me; I set in motion a greater work. The Iliadic half is the greater because war was the higher theme in the ancient ranking of genres, and because this war is the one that makes Rome: the Latins and Trojans who fight it are the two halves of one future people. Homer has one proem, at the head of the poem; Virgil's second marks a structural hinge that his readers were meant to feel.

02Book 7: Latium, Allecto and the catalogue

Latinus and Lavinia

The fleet slips past Circe's headland at night (7.10–24), an Odyssean danger left unvisited, and enters the Tiber at dawn (7.25–36). Latinus rules a peaceful country. Virgil gives him the descent from Saturn through Picus and Faunus (7.45–49), which module 1.3 sets out with its rivals; here what matters is that Latium is Saturn's old land of the golden age, and that the king's palace is a museum of that past, with the statues of ancestors in its hall and Picus holding the augur's staff (7.170–191). Latinus has one child, Lavinia, already sought by many suitors, Turnus first among them, favoured by the queen Amata (7.52–58). Two portents stand against the match. Bees swarm on the sacred laurel at the top of the palace, and when Lavinia lights the altar fire her hair seems to catch and burn without harm (7.64–80). The oracle of Faunus at Albunea forbids a Latin marriage: sons-in-law will come from abroad, externi venient generi (7.98), whose blood will raise Latin names to the stars.

On the shore the Trojans eat the wheat cakes they have used as plates, and Iulus jokes that they are eating their tables (7.107–119). Aeneas hails the land of destiny and credits the prophecy to Anchises (7.120–129), though in book 3 it was the Harpy Celaeno's: one of the seams of the unrevised poem that module 12.1 describes. The embassy of Ilioneus asks only for land and water (7.212–248). Latinus recognises the foreign son-in-law of the oracle and offers his daughter (7.249–285). The war that follows is therefore not over an abducted wife, as Homer's was, but over a betrothal: the Sibyl's foreign bride (6.93–94) has replaced Helen.

Allecto

Juno sees the Trojans settling and resolves that, if she cannot bend the gods above, she will move the underworld (7.286–322); why she hates is module 12.6's. She summons Allecto, a Fury from below (7.323–340), who works in three stages. She throws one of her snakes into Amata's breast, and the queen, first pleading, then raving, carries Lavinia off to the woods in a counterfeit Bacchic rite (7.341–405). She visits Turnus asleep at Ardea in the shape of Calybe, an old priestess of Juno (7.406–474). He mocks the old woman for meddling in war; Allecto shows herself and hurls a torch into his breast, and he wakes sweating, crying for arms, his anger boiling like water in a cauldron over a fire (7.458–466). Last, she sets Ascanius' hounds on the scent of a tame stag kept by Silvia, daughter of the royal herdsman; Ascanius wounds it, the stag runs home bleeding, and the farmers take up arms. The first dead are Almo, Tyrrhus' eldest son, and old Galaesus, the justest man in Ausonia, killed as he tried to make peace (7.475–539). Latinus refuses to open the gates of war, the custom Virgil describes as still practised by the consul of his own day; Juno comes down and pushes them open herself (7.601–622).

The Homeric model for the night visit is the Dream that Zeus sends to Agamemnon, taking Nestor's shape to stand by the sleeping king's head with a false promise of victory (Iliad 2.1–40). Virgil keeps the scene and changes its source and its effect. Homer's Dream deceives a king into battle he would have fought anyway; Allecto, sent by Juno from the underworld, plants madness in a man who first answers sensibly. As in Homer, the divine cause doubles a human one: Turnus already has reason to resent losing Lavinia, and the torch inflames it. The deer is pastoral, not Homeric. A war that will kill thousands begins with a pet wounded by a boy out hunting, and its first victim is a peacemaker.

The catalogue of Italians

The Muses are invoked again, for a list of the kings and peoples who filled the plains (7.641–646), in words that recall Homer's appeal before the Catalogue of Ships (Iliad 2.484–493). The catalogue (7.641–817) names the leaders and their contingents. Mezentius of Caere, the Etruscan tyrant, comes first, contemptor divum, scorner of the gods (7.648), with his son Lausus, worthy of a better father (7.649–654). Then Aventinus, son of Hercules, wearing his father's lion-skin (7.655–669); Caeculus, founder of Praeneste; Messapus, Neptune's son, tamer of horses; Clausus of the Sabines, from whom the Claudian tribe and family descend (7.706–722); Umbro, the Marsian priest who charms snakes; and Virbius, son of Hippolytus, brought back to life by Diana and hidden in Aricia (7.761–782). Turnus comes near the end, a head taller than the rest, with a Chimaera breathing fire on his helmet and Io, already turned into a heifer, on his shield (7.783–802). The last is a woman: Camilla of the Volsci, so swift she could run over standing corn without bruising the ears, while the young men and mothers crowd to stare at her (7.803–817).

What Virgil changes is the side the list belongs to. Homer catalogues the Greek host first and at length, then the Trojans briefly (Iliad 2.816–877). Virgil gives his catalogue to the enemy, and the enemy is Italy: the Sabines, Praeneste, Aricia, the Claudii, all of whom Roman readers knew as their own. The heroic gallery of the future Rome stands against the Trojans who will found it.

Where sources disagree. Macrobius, through his speaker Eustathius, faults Virgil's catalogue for keeping no order of regions, leaping from place to place where Homer's moves round Greece in a continuous route (Saturnalia 5.15.4). R. D. Williams ("The Function and Structure of Virgil's Catalogue in Aeneid 7", Classical Quarterly, 1961) argued that the order is by persons, not places, building from the impious Mezentius to Turnus and Camilla as its climax. Read that way, the list frames the war to come: Camilla dies at the end of book 11 and Turnus at the end of book 12.

03Book 8: Evander's Palatine and the shield

To Pallanteum

The river god Tiberinus appears to Aeneas in a dream and tells him to seek alliance with the Arcadians, and to look for the white sow with thirty piglets (8.31–65); the sow is found on the bank and sacrificed to Juno (8.81–85). The Trojans row upstream through quiet woods to a small town on a hill. By chance, forte, it is the day of Evander's annual sacrifice to Hercules (8.102–104). Evander's son Pallas challenges the strangers, and Aeneas offers the olive branch (8.112–125). Evander welcomes him as the son of Anchises, whom he met long ago (8.152–174), and explains the rite.

Hercules and Cacus

Module 1.3 gives the story in its versions and the Ara Maxima with its cult; Virgil's version (8.185–275) is the fullest and the most violent. Evander points to a shattered cliff. There Cacus lived, half a man, semihomo (8.194), a son of Vulcan who breathed his father's black fire, with heads of men nailed at the door of his cave. When Hercules came with Geryon's cattle, Cacus dragged four bulls and four heifers backwards by the tail into the cave; the lowing of the herd as it left, and the answer of one heifer from inside, betrayed him (8.205–218). Hercules is seized by fury: hic vero Alcidae furiis exarserat atro / felle dolor, then indeed Alcides' grief blazed up in fury with black bile (8.219–220). Cacus blocks the cave with a boulder; Hercules three times circles the mountain, then tears off the ridge above, and the light falls into the cave as if the earth gaped and the underworld were laid bare (8.230–246). He strangles Cacus in the smoke and drags the body out by the feet (8.247–267). The Salii then sing a hymn of Hercules' labours (8.285–305).

What Virgil does with a story module 1.3 already tells is to make it a model for his own plot. Livy's Cacus is a shepherd (1.7.5); Virgil's is a monster from the underworld, fire-breathing, a son of the god who will make Aeneas' armour. Hercules wins by rage, and the words for it, furiae and burning, are the words the poem uses for Allecto's victims in book 7 and for Aeneas in its last lines (12.946–947). The aetiology is also a calendar. Evander's feast falls on the day the Roman calendars gave to Hercules at the Ara Maxima, 12 August; Octavian's triple triumph of 29 BC began on the next day, 13 August. Denis Feeney (Caesar's Calendar, 2007) drew attention to the way the day joins the arrival of Aeneas, the victory of Hercules and the return of Caesar.

Where sources disagree. G. Karl Galinsky ("The Hercules-Cacus Episode in Aeneid VIII", American Journal of Philology, 1966) read Hercules as the saviour hero whose victory over a monster prefigures Aeneas' over Turnus and Augustus' at Actium, and the rage as righteous. Michael Putnam (The Poetry of the Aeneid, 1965) stressed the same rage as the link to the poem's ending, where Aeneas too kills in fury, and saw in it the violence that civilisation needs and cannot cleanse. Both readings start from 8.219–220; what the furor means at the end of the poem is module 12.7's debate.

The site of Rome

Evander walks his guest through his town (8.306–369), and Virgil lays the future city over it: the Carmental gate, the Asylum, the Lupercal, the Tarpeian rock and the Capitol, aurea nunc, olim silvestribus horrida dumis, golden now, once bristling with woodland thickets (8.348); cattle lowing in what will be the Forum and the smart Carinae (8.360–361). At his humble house Evander tells Aeneas that Hercules stooped to enter it: aude, hospes, contemnere opes, dare, guest, to despise wealth (8.364–365). Book 8 is the poem's one sustained picture of Rome, and it shows the place before the city.

The Homeric model is Nestor, the old king of Pylos who remembers his youth. Evander's prayer as he sends Pallas away, o mihi praeteritos referat si Iuppiter annos, if only Jupiter would give me back the years gone by (8.560), is Nestor's wish to be young again (Iliad 7.132–157, 11.670–761). Evander gives his son and two hundred horsemen, and Pallas adds two hundred of his own (8.518–519); he points Aeneas to the Etruscans, who have driven out Mezentius for tortures such as binding the living to the dead, face to face, until they rotted (8.481–495). Achilles sends Patroclus; Evander sends a son into another man's war, and the reader knows from the parallel what the son's fate will be.

The shield

Venus asks Vulcan for arms for her son (8.370–406), and the Cyclopes forge them in the caves under Vulcan's island, off Sicily by Lipari (8.416–453); she brings them herself (8.608–625). The model is Iliad 18: Thetis goes to Hephaestus, the god forges, and the poem describes the shield (18.369–617; Greek module 10.6 gives its rings). Virgil's seduction of Vulcan also borrows from Hera's seduction of Zeus (Iliad 14.153–351). Module 6.3 reads the shield's Roman scenes from the wolf and twins to Catiline and Cato, and module 5.5 its Actium at the centre (8.675–713); they are not read again here. What belongs to this book is what Virgil changes in Homer's object.

Achilles' shield shows the world in general: a cosmos, two cities, a year of farming, a dance, Ocean round the rim, nobody named. Aeneas' shows history in sequence, Romans by name from Ascanius' line to the triple triumph (8.626–728), and all of it future. Achilles receives his after Patroclus is dead and knows he is arming for his own death; Aeneas receives his before Pallas has died and before he has fought. Achilles, seeing the arms, feels his anger grow and his eyes flash (Iliad 19.15–17). Aeneas marvels at the images and does not understand them:

rerumque ignarus imagine gaudet / attollens umero famamque et fata nepotum (8.730–731)

Ignorant of the events, he rejoices in the image, lifting onto his shoulder the fame and fates of his descendants. The gesture repeats the end of book 2, where he lifted his father onto the same shoulders: the past he carried out of Troy has become the future he carries into Italy.

Where sources disagree. Lessing (Laokoon, 1766) praised Homer for showing the shield being made, so that description becomes action, and judged Virgil's finished shield, described scene by scene, the weaker piece. Philip Hardie (Virgil's Aeneid: Cosmos and Imperium, 1986) argued that Virgil read Homer's shield as ancient allegorists did, as an image of the cosmos, and gave Aeneas' shield, with Rome's history on it, the same cosmic meaning, so that empire becomes the order of the world; on that reading the change of subject is the point, not a failure of technique.

04Book 9: the camp and the night raid

With Aeneas away, Iris tells Turnus to attack (9.1–24). The Trojans keep to their walls as ordered; Turnus tries to burn the ships, and Cybele, whose pines built them, turns them into sea nymphs (9.77–122). Turnus reads the portent as a loss for the Trojans and boasts that he needs no arms of Vulcan and no thousand ships against them (9.128–158).

Nisus and Euryalus

The friends of the foot race in book 5, which module 12.2 reads, are on watch at a gate. Nisus asks whether the gods put this burning in men's minds or each man's fierce desire becomes his god, dine hunc ardorem mentibus addunt, / Euryale, an sua cuique deus fit dira cupido? (9.184–185), and proposes to cross the enemy lines and bring Aeneas back. Euryalus insists on coming. Ascanius promises them gifts, and Euryalus asks only that his mother, who followed him from Troy, be cared for (9.176–313). They pass through the sleeping Rutulian camp and kill as they go: the augur Rhamnes, who could not foresee his own death, and many others, until Nisus checks his friend (9.314–356). Euryalus takes Rhamnes' ornaments and Messapus' plumed helmet (9.359–366). On the road, the helmet glints in the moonlight and betrays him to Volcens' cavalry (9.367–374). Nisus escapes, turns back, throws spears from cover, and when Volcens draws his sword on the boy, cries me, me, adsum qui feci, me, me, here I am who did it (9.427). Euryalus falls like a purple flower cut by the plough, or a poppy bowing its head under the rain (9.435–437); Nisus kills Volcens and dies on his friend's body. The poet intervenes in his own voice:

Fortunati ambo! si quid mea carmina possunt (9.446)

Fortunate both: if my songs have any power, no day will ever take you from the memory of time, as long as the house of Aeneas lives by the Capitol's immovable rock and the Roman father holds empire (9.446–449). The Rutulians fix the two heads on spears, and Euryalus' mother's lament (9.481–497) is cut short because it unmans the army.

The model is the Doloneia, Iliad 10, in which Odysseus and Diomedes go out by night, kill the spy Dolon, then the Thracian king Rhesus and his men in their sleep, and come home with his horses. Homer's raid succeeds and its heroes return. Virgil's fails, and fails because of plunder: the glinting helmet kills Euryalus. The poppy is Homer's simile for Gorgythion's drooping head (Iliad 8.306–308), crossed with Catullus' flower at the edge of a meadow, cut by the passing plough (11.22–24). The apostrophe has no Homeric precedent: Homer does not promise his heroes fame in his own voice. Statius answered the episode with his own pair of night raiders in the Thebaid (module 10.2 sets out the poem).

Ascanius, the gate and Turnus inside

Numanus Remulus taunts the Trojans from below the wall as twice-captured Phrygians in saffron and sleeves, against Italians who harden their babies in rivers (9.598–620), and Ascanius kills him with his first arrow; Apollo praises him and orders him out of the fighting (9.621–658), a scene module 12.6 reads. The giant brothers Pandarus and Bitias open a gate to fight the Rutulians in it, as Polypoetes and Leonteus hold the gate of the Greek wall (Iliad 12.127–194). Turnus breaks in, kills Bitias, and when Pandarus shuts the gate he shuts Turnus inside with the Trojans; Turnus kills him with the boast that he will tell Priam an Achilles has been found here too (9.672–755). Cornered by the whole garrison, he gives ground step by step under a hail of weapons until he leaps into the Tiber, which carries him back to his men (9.789–818). Lines 9.806–814 closely follow the passage in which Ajax, pressed at the ships, can no longer hold (Iliad 16.102–111). Turnus has claimed Achilles and is drawn as Ajax.

05Book 10: Pallas and Mezentius

The council of the gods that opens the book (10.1–117) belongs to module 12.6, with Jupiter's refusal to take sides. Aeneas returns by sea with the Etruscans, whose ships Virgil catalogues a second time (10.163–214), and the battle on the shore is a long sequence of single kills in the Iliadic manner.

The death of Pallas

Pallas rallies his Arcadians and has his own aristeia until Turnus, seeing him, orders the others aside: he alone will fight Pallas, and wishes Pallas' father were there to watch (10.441–443). Pallas prays to Hercules, who weeps in heaven and is answered by Jupiter (10.460–473; module 12.6). Turnus' spear goes through shield and breastplate; Pallas falls, and Turnus, standing over him, tells the Arcadians to take Evander the son he deserved, then strips the dead boy's sword-belt, engraved with the Danaids' crime, fifty bridegrooms killed on their wedding night (10.479–500). The narrator speaks:

nescia mens hominum fati sortisque futurae (10.501)

The mind of man, ignorant of fate and what is to come: a time will come when Turnus would pay a great price to have left Pallas untouched, and will hate the spoils and the day (10.501–505).

The model is Hector's killing of Patroclus and the stripping of Achilles' armour (Iliad 16–17; Greek module 10.6), with Sarpedon's death in the same book behind Hercules' tears. Virgil changes the victim and the relation. Patroclus is a grown warrior who disobeys his friend; Pallas is a boy entrusted to Aeneas by a father. Hector puts on the armour he strips; Turnus wears the belt, and the narrator has already told the reader that it will kill him.

Where sources disagree. What does the picture on the belt mean? Gian Biagio Conte ("Il balteo di Pallante", 1970; in English in The Rhetoric of Imitation, 1986) read the murdered bridegrooms as an emblem of young men who die before marriage, like Pallas. Putnam ("Virgil's Danaid Ekphrasis", Illinois Classical Studies, 1994) read it as an emblem of the poem's violence, with Virgil's word foede, foully, giving the killings their moral weight, and argued that when the belt reappears in book 12 it casts Aeneas, avenging a father's quarrel, in the Danaids' part.

Aeneas' rampage

The news turns Aeneas into Achilles after Patroclus. He takes eight young men alive to sacrifice at Pallas' pyre (10.517–520), as Achilles takes twelve Trojans from the river to kill at Patroclus' (Iliad 21.26–32, 23.175–176). Magus clasps his knees and offers ransom; Aeneas answers that Turnus ended such trading when he killed Pallas, and kills him (10.521–536), as Achilles kills the suppliant Lycaon (Iliad 21.34–135). He is compared to Aegaeon, the hundred-handed giant who fought Jupiter's thunderbolts (10.565–570). The poem keeps calling him pius through all of it. Juno, with Jupiter's leave, draws Turnus away by a phantom of Aeneas that flees to a ship, and casts the ship off with Turnus aboard (10.633–688): the device of the phantom Aeneas that Apollo makes in Iliad 5.449–453, used here to save the enemy.

Mezentius and Lausus

Mezentius takes Turnus' place and kills like a boar at bay (10.689–768). Aeneas wounds him; Lausus steps between and Aeneas kills him, then, seeing the boy's face, pities him: miserande puer, pitiable boy, what can pious Aeneas give you worthy of such a nature (10.825–826). He lifts Lausus from the ground himself. Mezentius, told of his son's death, mounts his horse Rhaebus and speaks to it as a companion of long service (10.858–866), as Achilles speaks to his horses before his last battle (Iliad 19.400–403). He dies asking only that his body be buried with his son's, protected from his people's hatred (10.900–906). The scorner of the gods dies as a father. Virgil keeps the impious king of Cato's version, who claimed the first fruits owed to the gods (module 1.3), and turns his end into a lament.

06Book 11: funerals, council and Camilla

Aeneas sets up Mezentius' arms on an oak as a trophy to Mars (11.5–11) and sends Pallas home on a bier of oak and arbutus, with captured horses, the prisoners for the pyre and Pallas' war-horse Aethon weeping (11.29–99). Evander meets the body and laments: he would rather have died, and he lives only to bring news of vengeance on Turnus to his son among the dead (11.139–181). Aeneas grants the Latins leave to bury their dead, and both sides agree a twelve-day truce (11.106–138), and both sides burn their dead (11.182–224). The models are the funeral of Patroclus (Iliad 23) and the truce for burial in Iliad 7; Evander's grief takes Priam's place, but no one comes to ransom anything.

The Latin envoys return from Diomedes in Apulia with a refusal (11.225–295). Diomedes says that he faced Aeneas at Troy and knows his strength; if Ida had borne two more such men, the Trojans would have come to Greece; Aeneas and Hector were equals, ambo animis, ambo insignes praestantibus armis, / hic pietate prior, both great in spirit and arms, Aeneas the first in piety (11.291–292). The enemy who wounded Aeneas in Iliad 5 becomes the witness to his greatness. In the council, Drances, rich, eloquent and cowardly, tells Turnus to fight alone or give up (11.336–375); Turnus answers with scorn and offers to meet Aeneas (11.376–444). The pair recall Polydamas and Hector (Iliad 18.249–313), but Virgil reverses the moral weight: Polydamas is wise and Hector wrong; Drances is right and contemptible.

Camilla

Diana tells her attendant Opis Camilla's story (11.532–596). Her father Metabus, driven out of Privernum, came to the flooded Amasenus with his infant daughter, bound her to his spear, vowed her to Diana and threw her across; he raised her in the wilds on mare's milk, a huntress dressed in a tiger-skin. In battle Camilla rides with one breast bare, an Amazon like those who fight around Hippolyta or Penthesilea by the Thermodon (11.648–663), and kills a long list of men (11.664–724). Arruns stalks her. She becomes fixed on Chloreus, a priest of Cybele in gold and purple, and pursues him with a woman's love of plunder and spoils, femineo praedae et spoliorum ardebat amore (11.782). Arruns prays to Apollo of Soracte to let him kill her and come home without glory (11.785–793); Apollo grants the first half and lets the winds scatter the second (11.794–798). She dies in Acca's arms, and Opis shoots Arruns for Diana (11.818–867). Her last line is the line on which the poem will end:

vitaque cum gemitu fugit indignata sub umbras (11.831)

And life with a groan flees, resentful, to the shades. The models are two. Penthesilea, the Amazon queen whom Achilles kills in the Aethiopis, as Proclus summarises it (Greek module 4.3), gives the warrior woman. Zeus granting half of Achilles' prayer for Patroclus (Iliad 16.249–252) gives the half-granted prayer. Virgil's change is that Camilla is Italian and has no Trojan or Greek counterpart: her death, like Pallas', is the death of a young life the war consumes, and the repeated line joins it to Turnus'. Like Pallas, she is undone by spoils.

07Book 12: the treaty, the duel and the death of Turnus

The broken treaty

Turnus opens the book like a Punic lion wounded by hunters, roused to fight (12.4–9), Achilles' lion going out against the spearmen (Iliad 20.164–175). He will meet Aeneas alone. Latinus urges him to give up Lavinia; Amata begs him not to fight. Lavinia hears her mother, and her cheeks flood with a blush like Indian ivory stained with crimson dye, or lilies mixed with roses (12.64–69). The ivory is Homer's simile for the blood of Menelaus' wound spreading on his thigh (Iliad 4.141–147). Lavinia never speaks in the poem; the blush is her one moment of inner life, and R. O. A. M. Lyne ("Lavinia's Blush", Greece & Rome, 1983) read it as a sign of love for Turnus; the text does not say whom it is for. Her later life is in module 12.8's reception.

A treaty is sworn: Aeneas swears that if Turnus wins the Trojans will withdraw, and if he wins the Latins will keep their laws, the Trojans will build the walls and Lavinia will give the city her name (12.161–215). The model is Iliad 3, the oaths before the duel of Paris and Menelaus, and Iliad 4, in which Athena prompts Pandarus to break the truce with an arrow. Virgil keeps the sequence and puts the breach in the hands of Turnus' side. Juno urges Juturna, Turnus' sister and a nymph, to save her brother (12.134–160); disguised as Camers she stirs the Rutulians, sends a portent of an eagle forced to drop a swan, and the augur Tolumnius throws the first spear (12.222–276). Aeneas, bareheaded and unarmed, tries to hold his men to the oath (12.311–317), and an arrow from no one knows whose hand wounds him (12.318–323). Venus heals the wound through the physician Iapyx (12.383–440), and Aeneas arms and speaks his farewell to Ascanius (12.435–440), whose Sophoclean model module 12.1 gives.

Juturna takes the place of her brother's charioteer Metiscus and drives Turnus round the field, away from Aeneas (12.468–499). Aeneas turns on the city itself; Amata, believing Turnus dead, hangs herself (12.593–611). Turnus learns of it and sends his sister away: usque adeone mori miserum est?, is it so wretched a thing to die? (12.646). He returns to fight.

The duel

Jupiter weighs the two men's fates in his scales (12.725–727; module 12.6). Turnus' sword shatters on Vulcan's armour, for in his haste he had taken Metiscus' blade (12.728–741). He runs, and Aeneas, slowed by his wound, pursues like an Umbrian hound after a stag hemmed in by a river (12.749–757): five circuits forward and five back (12.763). Homer's chase is the dog after a fawn (Iliad 22.189–193), three times round the walls of Troy. Juturna brings back the sword; Venus frees Aeneas' spear from a tree. In heaven Jupiter and Juno are reconciled (12.791–842): she withdraws from the war on condition that the Latins keep their name and language, and Jupiter grants it. What the terms mean for the gods and for fate is module 12.6's; in the action they leave Turnus alone. Jupiter sends one of the Dirae, which flies at Turnus' face in the form of a small owl, and Juturna, recognising it, tears her hair, laments her immortality and plunges into the river (12.843–886). Homer's Apollo leaves Hector when the scales sink (Iliad 22.213); Virgil gives the abandoning god a sister's grief.

Turnus lifts a boundary stone that twelve men of today could hardly raise (12.896–902), as Aeneas lifts a stone against Achilles that two men of now could not carry (Iliad 20.285–287), but his knees fail and the stone falls short (12.903–907). He is like a man in a dream who tries to run and cannot, whose voice will not come (12.908–914), Homer's dream in which pursuer and pursued cannot close (Iliad 22.199–201). Aeneas' spear goes through his thigh, and he falls.

The death of Turnus

Turnus, on his knees, holds out his hand. He does not ask for mercy: use your fortune; if any care for a parent can touch you, pity Daunus, and give me or my body back to my people. You have won; the Ausonians have seen me stretch out my hands; Lavinia is yours; go no further in hatred (12.930–938). Aeneas stands, rolling his eyes, and checks his hand; the words begin to bend him (12.938–941). Then he sees on Turnus' shoulder the belt of Pallas (12.941–944):

furiis accensus et ira / terribilis (12.946–947)

Set on fire by furies and terrible in his anger, he says that it is Pallas who strikes, Pallas who sacrifices him and takes the penalty from his criminal blood (12.948–949), and buries the sword in his chest, fervidus, burning (12.950–951). Turnus' limbs go slack with cold, and his life with a groan flees, resentful, to the shades (12.951–952). The poem ends there.

The model is Iliad 22: Hector, dying, asks Achilles to take ransom and return his body (22.338–343), and Achilles refuses, wishing he could eat him raw (22.346–348; Greek module 10.6). Virgil changes three things. Turnus is alive and asks for life or body, and concedes the bride and the war; Hector asks only for burial. Aeneas hesitates, which Achilles never does, and it is a sight, not a word, that decides him: the belt Turnus took in book 10. And the Iliad goes on for two more books, to the funeral of Patroclus and the ransom of Hector, in which the killer gives the body back and weeps with the father. The Aeneid has no book 24. Its last line repeats Camilla's (11.831), and the slack limbs repeat Aeneas' own first appearance in the poem, his limbs loosed with cold in the storm (1.92). Whether the killing is justice, vengeance or a failure of the virtue the poem praises is module 12.7's debate, with what the ancient commentators made of it.

09Self-test

  1. What does the second proem (7.37–45) announce, and why is the war half called the "greater work"?
    Answer

    It invokes Erato and announces wars, kings driven to death and all Italy in arms: a greater order of things and a greater work (7.44–45). War ranked as the higher epic theme, and this war is the one that makes Rome, fought between the two halves of one future people.

  2. How does Allecto's visit to Turnus rework the Dream of Iliad 2?
    Answer

    Zeus's Dream, in Nestor's shape, deceives the sleeping Agamemnon into battle (Iliad 2.1–40). Allecto, sent by Juno from the underworld and disguised as the priestess Calybe, is mocked by a Turnus who first answers sensibly, then throws a torch into his breast (7.406–474): madness from below, added to a grievance he already had.

  3. Whose side does Virgil's catalogue list, and how is it ordered?
    Answer

    The enemy's, the Italians, many of them ancestors of Romans (the Claudii, Praeneste, Aricia) (7.641–817). It opens with Mezentius and closes on Turnus and Camilla; Macrobius faulted its lack of geographical order (Saturnalia 5.15.4); Williams (1961) read the order as one of persons, building to Turnus and Camilla.

  4. What does Virgil change in the story of Hercules and Cacus, and why does it matter for the end of the poem?
    Answer

    His Cacus is a fire-breathing half-man and son of Vulcan, not Livy's shepherd, and his Hercules wins in fury and burning grief (8.219–220). The same vocabulary of furor returns when Aeneas kills Turnus (12.946–947), which is why Galinsky and Putnam read the episode in opposite ways.

  5. Give three ways the shield of Aeneas differs from the shield of Achilles.
    Answer

    Achilles' shows the world in general with no names; Aeneas' shows named Roman history in sequence, all of it future. Achilles receives his after Patroclus' death, Aeneas his before Pallas'. Achilles' anger grows at the sight; Aeneas rejoices in images he does not understand (8.730).

  6. How does the night raid of Nisus and Euryalus differ from the Doloneia?
    Answer

    In Iliad 10 Odysseus and Diomedes kill Dolon and Rhesus and return with his horses. Virgil's pair die: Euryalus' plundered helmet glints in the moonlight and betrays him (9.373–374), and Nisus dies on his body. The poet then promises them fame in his own voice (9.446–449), which Homer never does.

  7. In what respects is the death of Pallas modelled on the death of Patroclus, and what is changed?
    Answer

    As Hector kills Patroclus and strips his armour (Iliad 16–17), Turnus kills Pallas and strips his belt (10.479–500). But Pallas is a boy entrusted to Aeneas by his father, and the narrator says at once that Turnus will one day wish he had left him untouched (10.501–505).

  8. Which Iliadic acts does Aeneas repeat after Pallas dies, and how does the Mezentius episode answer them?
    Answer

    He takes eight youths alive for Pallas' pyre, as Achilles takes twelve Trojans (10.517–520; Iliad 21.26–32), and kills the suppliant Magus as Achilles kills Lycaon (10.521–536). Then he pities Lausus, miserande puer (10.825), and Mezentius, the scorner of the gods, dies asking to be buried with his son (10.900–906).

  9. How is Camilla's death linked both to the Iliad and to the end of the poem?
    Answer

    Apollo grants only half of Arruns' prayer (11.794–798), as Zeus grants half of Achilles' for Patroclus (Iliad 16.249–252); her Amazon figure recalls Penthesilea in the Aethiopis. Her last line, life fleeing resentful to the shades (11.831), is repeated as the last line of the poem for Turnus (12.952).

  10. What three things does Virgil change in the final duel's Homeric model?
    Answer

    Turnus asks for his life or his body and concedes Lavinia, where Hector asks only for burial (Iliad 22.338–343); Aeneas hesitates and is decided by the sight of Pallas' belt (12.938–944), where Achilles refuses at once; and the poem ends on the death, with nothing to answer Iliad 23–24's funeral and ransom.

10Carry forward

  1. The Homeric roles shift across the second half: Turnus claims Achilles and is drawn as Ajax and Hector, Aeneas becomes Achilles after Pallas' death. Read each Iliadic echo by asking who is now in whose place and what that does to sympathy.
  2. The war is fought between the two peoples who will make Rome, so its catalogue, its dead and its last victim are Italian ancestors. The poem's cost falls on the future Romans themselves, which is why the Iliadic half is both the "greater work" and the sadder one.
  3. Spoils and fury link the second half from book 8 to its last line: Hercules' rage, Euryalus' helmet, Pallas' belt, Camilla's love of plunder and Aeneas' burning anger. The Aeneid ends where the Iliad refused to end, on a killing with no ransom after it, and every reading of the poem starts from that difference.
