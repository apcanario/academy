01Empire without end, empire within limits

In the first book of the Aeneid Jupiter consoles Venus with the future of her son's line:

his ego nec metas rerum nec tempora pono: imperium sine fine dedi.

"For these I set no bounds in space or time: I have given empire without end" (1.278–279). The coming Caesar's empire is bounded only by Ocean (1.287). Module 6.3 reads the prophecy as the poem's programme; Tacitus supplies the counter-claim. After the funeral in AD 14 Tiberius had Augustus' handwritten summary of the state's resources read in the Senate: soldiers, fleets, kingdoms, provinces and taxes.

addideratque consilium coercendi intra terminos imperii, incertum metu an per invidiam.

He had added advice to keep the empire within its limits, "whether from fear or from jealousy is uncertain" (Annals 1.11.4). Suetonius describes the summary without the advice (Augustus 101.4). Dio has it, and claims that Augustus always followed it in deed as well as word, having declined much barbarian land (56.33.5–6). The reign refutes Dio. Augustus annexed more territory than any Roman before him, and the advice came after the Pannonian revolt of AD 6–9 and the loss of three legions in Germany.

Where sources disagree. P. A. Brunt ("Augustan Imperialism", Journal of Roman Studies 1963) took the poets' universal empire as Augustus' real aim, abandoned only when the disasters of AD 6–9 exhausted his manpower. Erich Gruen ("The Imperial Policy of Augustus", 1990) set an ideology of world rule, aimed at opinion at home, against a practice of piecemeal, opportunistic expansion.

02The standing army

Octavian came out of Actium with more legions than the state could pay. The Res Gestae puts some 500,000 citizens under his oath in his lifetime, more than 300,000 of them settled in colonies or sent home with land or money (3.3), at a cost of about 860 million sesterces for land in 30 and 14 BC and 400 million in cash between 7 and 2 BC (16). The rest became a permanent army, paid by the state, enlisted for fixed terms, stationed in the provinces and sworn to one man; its background is module 5.2 and deep dive module 11.2. Augustus stopped addressing soldiers as commilitones, fellow-soldiers, and called them milites (Suetonius, Augustus 25.1). Pay stayed at Caesar's 225 denarii a year, the ten asses a day that the mutineers of AD 14 set against the guard's two denarii, complaining too that discharged men were kept on under the banner (Tacitus, Annals 1.17). Glory was centralised. Wars were fought under his auspices, and after Cornelius Balbus' triumph of 19 BC no one outside the family triumphed; generals received triumphal ornaments instead, given, Suetonius says, to rather more men than the thirty-odd full triumphs Augustus had voted (Augustus 38.1).

Where sources disagree. Dio, under AD 5, gives twenty-three or, as others say, twenty-five legions (55.23.2); Tacitus counts twenty-five in AD 23 (Annals 4.5). The familiar twenty-eight is a modern sum, Tacitus' total plus Varus' three legions, whose numbers were never reused, and it holds only if no legion was raised in the emergencies of AD 6–9, which Dio's lower figure leaves open.

The military treasury

In AD 6 Augustus founded the aerarium militare to pay bounties after twenty years' service, putting in 170 million sesterces from his patrimony (Res Gestae 17.2). Dio adds gifts in his and Tiberius' names, contributions from kings and cities but none from private citizens, and three ex-praetors chosen by lot to run the fund (55.25.2–3). Its income came from a five per cent tax on inheritances, sparing near kin and the poor (Dio 55.25.5), and a one per cent tax on sales that Tiberius refused to abolish in AD 15 because the treasury depended on it (Annals 1.78). Italy, free of direct tax since 167 BC (module 4.4), now paid for the army.

03Spain, the Alps and the Danube

Suetonius says Augustus fought only two foreign wars in person, the Dalmatian as a young man and the Cantabrian (Augustus 20). In 26 BC he campaigned against the Cantabrians and Asturians of the north-west, fell ill and withdrew to Tarraco (Dio 53.25). Janus was closed in 25 (53.26.5), but the mountain peoples rose again and again until Agrippa ended the war in 19, after enslaved prisoners had killed their masters and gone home and his own troops had wavered (Dio 54.11). Velleius calls Spain pacified after two hundred years of war (2.90); three legions still stood there in AD 23 (Annals 4.5).

In the Alps Varro Murena crushed the Salassi of the Aosta valley in 25 BC, selling 36,000 by Strabo's figure (4.6.7). In 15 BC Drusus from Italy and Tiberius from Gaul took Raetia and Vindelicia in a single summer (Dio 54.22; Velleius 2.95), and Horace celebrated each (Odes 4.4, 4.14). Cottius was kept on as prefect of his Alpine peoples, as his arch at Susa records, and the trophy at La Turbie, dedicated in 7/6 BC, listed the conquered tribes (Pliny, Natural History 3.136–137). The Alps secured the routes between Italy and the northern armies.

On the Danube Marcus Crassus beat the Bastarnae in 29–28 and killed their king with his own hand, but was refused the spolia opima because he had not fought under his own auspices (Dio 51.24). Tiberius conquered Pannonia in 12–9 BC (Velleius 2.96). In AD 6, as he moved against the Marcomannic king Maroboduus in Bohemia, the Pannonians and Dalmatians rose. Velleius, who served there, gives the rebels 200,000 infantry and 9,000 cavalry (2.110.3) and has Augustus warn the Senate that they could be in sight of Rome within ten days (2.111.1); Suetonius calls it the gravest foreign war since the Punic Wars (Tiberius 16.1). The Res Gestae claims Illyricum carried to the Danube (30.1).

04Egypt, the emperor's province

Module 5.5 tells how Egypt was taken in 30 BC. Augustus says he added it to the empire of the Roman people (Res Gestae 27.1), but it was his. An equestrian prefect, holding in Strabo's phrase the rank of the king, governed it and, uniquely for a knight, commanded legions: three in Strabo's day (17.1.12), two by Tacitus' count in AD 23 (Annals 4.5). Senators and leading knights needed leave to enter, one of the secrets of domination, lest anyone seize the province and starve Italy (Annals 2.59). A late epitome credits Augustan Egypt with twenty million modii of grain a year for Rome (Epitome de Caesaribus 1.6). The first prefect, the poet Cornelius Gallus, boasted on a trilingual stele at Philae in 29 BC of leading an army beyond the cataract; by 26 he had been renounced by Augustus and had killed himself (Dio 53.23). In the temples, as at Dendur, Augustus appeared as pharaoh. From Egypt Aelius Gallus marched into Arabia and Gaius Petronius against Kush as far as Napata (Res Gestae 26.5; Strabo 17.1.54). A bronze head of Augustus that the Kushites carried off, buried beneath a temple stair at Meroe, is now in the British Museum.

05The standards from Parthia

Three Roman armies had lost standards to Parthia: Crassus' at Carrhae in 53 (module 5.4), Decidius Saxa's in 40 and Antony's in 36. In 20 BC, with Augustus in the East and Tiberius leading an army into Armenia, Phraates IV returned the standards and prisoners. Armenia, which Augustus says he could have made a province, went by ancestral example to a king, Tigranes (Res Gestae 27.2). No battle was fought; the presentation made it a conquest. Coins showed a kneeling Parthian offering a standard, Horace has Phraates accept Caesar's rule on his knees (Epistles 1.12.27–28), and the Res Gestae says Augustus compelled the Parthians to return the standards of three armies and to seek friendship as suppliants, and laid them in the temple of Mars Ultor (29.2; module 6.2).

The Prima Porta statue carries the scene on its cuirass. At the centre a bearded, trousered Parthian hands an eagle standard to a Roman in armour. Above, Caelus spreads the sky and the Sun drives his chariot; at the sides sit two mourning subject peoples, often identified as Gaul and Spain; below are Apollo on a griffin, Diana on a stag and Earth with a cornucopia. A negotiated exchange sits at the centre of an ordered cosmos. Whether the Roman is Tiberius, Mars Ultor or a figure for the army is uncertain; module 6.2 reads the statue as a whole.

Where sources disagree. The Res Gestae says Phraates later sent his sons and grandsons to Italy not because he was beaten in war but to seek Roman friendship through pledges (32.2), and Suetonius has the Parthians return the standards on demand and offer hostages besides (Augustus 21.3). Strabo says Phraates gave up the princes to rid himself of rivals around whom revolt could gather (16.1.28), and Tacitus that he acted less from fear of Rome than from distrust of his own people (Annals 2.1).

06Germany: Drusus, Tiberius and Varus

Suetonius counts only two serious defeats in the reign, both in Germany: Lollius' loss of an eagle in 16 BC (Velleius 2.97.1), more disgraceful than damaging, and Varus', almost fatal (Augustus 23.1). From 12 BC Drusus campaigned beyond the Rhine and in 9 BC reached the Elbe, dying on the return, after a fall from his horse in Livy's epitome (Periochae 142), of disease in Dio (55.1.4). Tiberius, in Velleius' words, reduced the country almost to a tributary province (2.97.4). At Waldgirmes in the Lahn valley a Roman town begun about 4 BC, with a forum and a gilded equestrian statue of Augustus, was abandoned unfinished; Dio describes cities being founded and Germans learning to hold markets (56.18.2).

In AD 7 Publius Quinctilius Varus took command; Dio says he exacted money as from subjects (56.18.3). Arminius, a Cheruscan noble with Roman citizenship and equestrian rank (Velleius 2.118.2), led the conspiracy. In autumn AD 9 Varus, marching from summer camp to deal with a reported rising, was caught in forest and bog; he killed himself, and his head reached Augustus by way of Maroboduus (2.119.5). Velleius counts three legions, three alae and six cohorts lost (2.117.1); Suetonius, three legions with the commander, his legates and all the auxiliaries (Augustus 23.1). Reckoning from paper strengths, Michael McNally (Teutoburg Forest AD 9, 2011) puts Varus' force at about 17,000 soldiers and 3,800 servants. The legions were the Seventeenth, Eighteenth and Nineteenth; the cenotaph of Marcus Caelius at Xanten commemorates a centurion of the Eighteenth who fell in the Varian war (CIL XIII 8648).

Augustus, Suetonius says, left hair and beard uncut for months and struck his head against a door, crying Quintili Vare, legiones redde, "Quintilius Varus, give back the legions" (Augustus 23.2). Dio adds conscription by lot, the enrolment of veterans and freedmen, and the German bodyguard sent away to islands (56.23.2–4); Tiberius was sent back to Germany (Velleius 2.120.1). An empire that had not stopped at the shore of Ocean, Florus wrote, stopped at the bank of the Rhine (2.30). Germanicus' campaigns of AD 14–16 are module 7.1.

Where sources disagree. Velleius has the army trapped by forests, marshes and ambush (2.119.2), and Dio a running fight of several days through rain and broken country (56.19–22). Florus instead has the Germans fall on an unsuspecting camp while Varus summoned litigants to his tribunal (2.30), which suits his theme of a governor playing judge. Tacitus' account of the field, a first camp built by three legions and then a half-ruined rampart where the remnant stood (Annals 1.61), supports a battle on the move.

In 1987 Tony Clunn, a British officer with a metal detector, found Roman coins at Kalkriese, north of Osnabrück, where Mommsen had placed the battle in 1885. Excavation in the narrow passage between the Kalkrieser Berg and the Great Bog has produced a turf wall, a silvered cavalry face mask, an almost complete segmented cuirass, pits of human and mule bones buried some years after death, and coins that end in the first years AD, some countermarked VAR.

Where sources disagree. Most specialists accept Kalkriese as part of the Varian battlefield. Reinhard Wolters argued that the coins cannot exclude a later date, since bronze circulating on the lower Rhine was old stock, and proposed Caecina's fight at the pontes longi in AD 15 (Tacitus, Annals 1.63–68); David Wigg-Wolf replied that the profile of the coin finds separates a Varian horizon from a Germanicus horizon, and Kalkriese belongs to the first. The turf wall, long read as a German ambush rampart, has been re-examined since 2016 as possibly a Roman emergency camp.

07Client kings and the provinces of the Senate and of Caesar

Suetonius says Augustus restored kingdoms or gave them to other foreigners, and treated all the kings as membra partisque imperii, limbs and parts of the empire (Augustus 48). Tacitus speaks of an old Roman habit of using even kings as instruments of servitude (Agricola 14). Herod, made king of Judaea by the Senate in 40 BC, was confirmed by Octavian at Rhodes in 30 (Josephus, Jewish Antiquities 15.187–201), built Caesarea with a temple of Rome and Augustus, and executed three sons; Augustus reportedly said it was better to be Herod's pig than his son (Macrobius, Saturnalia 2.4.11). After his death in 4 BC the kingdom was divided, and in AD 6 Judaea became a province under an equestrian prefect (Jewish Antiquities 18.1–2). Galatia was annexed when its king Amyntas died in 25 BC (Dio 53.26.3). Kings cost no legions and were provinces in waiting.

Module 6.1 treats the division of January 27 BC as a constitutional act; this is how it worked. The people's provinces, Africa, Asia, Achaea, Macedonia, Sicily, Baetica and others (Dio 53.12), went by lot to former magistrates for a year, ex-consuls to Africa and Asia and ex-praetors elsewhere, all styled proconsul and, once Illyricum passed to Caesar in 11 BC (Dio 54.34.4), without legions outside Africa. Caesar's provinces, the rest of Spain, the Gauls, Syria and later conquests, went to senators of his choice, legati Augusti pro praetore, who served at his pleasure and commanded the legions, while equestrian procurators handled his revenues. Suetonius' reason is practical: Augustus kept the provinces that annual governors could not safely hold (Augustus 47). The line was no wall. The Cyrene edicts of 7/6–4 BC show Augustus instructing the proconsul of a public province on juries and extortion, and Fergus Millar ("The Emperor, the Senate and the Provinces", 1966) argued that communities in both kinds of province dealt directly with the emperor. The Republican governor is module 4.4; later government, module 7.5.

08The Res Gestae's map of the world

Chapters 26–33 of the Res Gestae, copied in Latin and Greek at Ancyra in annexed Galatia, are a tour of the world. They open with the claim that Augustus extended the boundaries of every province bordering peoples not subject to Rome (26.1), then go west and north: Gaul, Spain and Germany to the mouth of the Elbe pacified, the Alps pacified without unjust war, a fleet sent to the Cimbri (26.2–4). Then south to Ethiopia and Arabia (26.5), east to Egypt, Armenia and the recovered provinces (27), and on to colonies (28), standards (29) and the Danube (30). The outer ring comes last: Indian embassies never before seen with a Roman general (31), suppliant kings, two of them British (32), and kings given to the Parthians and Medes (33).

The map has no frontier. Power shades outward from provinces to kings to peoples seeking friendship, each grade an acknowledgement of the princeps. It is also selective: Germany pacified to the Elbe stands in a text finished five years after Varus, and Aelius Gallus' march, which his friend Strabo admits failed (16.4.22–24), appears as an advance. Claude Nicolet (Space, Geography, and Politics in the Early Roman Empire, 1991) set the text beside the census and Agrippa's map of the world in a Roman portico (Pliny, Natural History 3.17), and argued that the regime made geographical knowledge an instrument of rule.

Where sources disagree. Module 0.2 sets out the general quarrel over Roman frontiers. For Augustus it turns on intent. Edward Luttwak (The Grand Strategy of the Roman Empire, 1976) read the Julio-Claudian empire as a design, client kings absorbing lesser threats while concentrated legions served as a striking force; Benjamin Isaac and C. R. Whittaker, whose books module 0.2 cites, denied any such doctrine. The Res Gestae, in which boundaries exist to be extended (26.1, 30.1), and a halt that followed Varus rather than a plan, suit the critics better.

09Self-test

  1. What does Jupiter promise at Aeneid 1.278–279, and how does Tacitus' report of Augustus' last advice qualify it?
    Answer

    Empire without bounds of space or time. Tacitus says Augustus' summary of the empire ended with advice to keep it within its limits, adding that it was uncertain whether from fear or jealousy (Annals 1.11.4); Dio's claim that he always practised this (56.33.5–6) is contradicted by the conquests of the reign.

  2. How did Augustus turn the armies of the civil wars into a standing army, and what does Suetonius' detail about commilitones show?
    Answer

    He discharged more than 300,000 of some 500,000 men who served under his oath, paying for land and cash bounties (Res Gestae 3.3, 16), and kept the rest as a permanent, paid, fixed-term force in the provinces. Dropping "fellow-soldiers" for "soldiers" (Augustus 25.1) marks the shift from a general's partisans to an emperor's servants.

  3. Where does the figure of twenty-eight legions come from, and why is it not certain?
    Answer

    Tacitus counts twenty-five in AD 23 (Annals 4.5); adding the three lost with Varus, whose numbers were never reused, gives twenty-eight. Dio's figure for AD 5, twenty-three or twenty-five (55.23.2), leaves open whether legions were raised in the crises of AD 6–9.

  4. What did a legionary serve and receive after AD 5, and how was his bounty funded?
    Answer

    Twenty years, pay of 225 denarii a year (the ten asses a day of Annals 1.17) and 12,000 sesterces on discharge (Dio 55.23.1). The military treasury of AD 6 began with 170 million sesterces from Augustus (Res Gestae 17.2) and lived on a five per cent inheritance tax (Dio 55.25.5) and a one per cent sales tax (Annals 1.78).

  5. Why was Egypt governed by an equestrian prefect and closed to senators?
    Answer

    Whoever held Egypt, with its legions and grain, could starve Italy (Tacitus, Annals 2.59), so it went to a knight who depended on Augustus and could not aspire to rule. Even so, Cornelius Gallus claimed too much glory and was destroyed in 26 BC (Dio 53.23).

  6. How were the Parthian standards recovered in 20 BC, and how was the event represented?
    Answer

    By negotiation with Phraates IV, backed by Augustus' presence in the East and Tiberius' army in Armenia, without a battle. Coins with a kneeling Parthian, Horace's king on his knees (Epistles 1.12.27–28), the Res Gestae's "compelled" (29.2) and the Prima Porta breastplate turned it into a submission within a cosmic order.

  7. What did Rome lose with Varus according to Velleius and Suetonius, and how did Augustus respond?
    Answer

    Velleius: three legions, three alae and six cohorts (2.117.1); Suetonius: three legions with the commander, legates and all the auxiliaries (Augustus 23.1). Augustus mourned publicly, conscripted by lot, enrolled veterans and freedmen, sent away his German guard (Dio 56.23) and sent Tiberius to the Rhine.

  8. What links Kalkriese to AD 9, and what was Wolters' objection?
    Answer

    Military equipment, bone pits of men and mules, and coins that end in the first years AD, some countermarked VAR. Wolters argued that old bronze stock could not exclude AD 15 and proposed Caecina's battle at the pontes longi (Annals 1.63–68); Wigg-Wolf answered that the coin profile is Varian.

  9. How did the provinces of the people and of Caesar differ in administration, and where did client kings fit?
    Answer

    Proconsuls chosen by lot for a year, mostly without legions, against legates chosen by Augustus who served at his pleasure and commanded the legions, with equestrian procurators and prefects (Suetonius, Augustus 47). Kings such as Herod governed at no cost to Rome and were replaced by provinces when convenient, as in Galatia in 25 BC and Judaea in AD 6.

  10. What is the structure of the map in Res Gestae 26–33, and what does it leave out?
    Answer

    It moves from provinces whose boundaries were extended, through conquests west, north, south and east, to colonies and standards, then to an outer ring of embassies, suppliant kings and kings given to Parthia and Media: graded acknowledgement with no frontier. It claims Germany pacified to the Elbe after Varus and presents Aelius Gallus' failed Arabian march as an advance.

10Carry forward

  1. Augustan ideology promised empire without end while the paid army and its treasury set a finite scale; the advice of AD 14 records the moment the two met, and every later emperor who annexed or withdrew was choosing between them.
  2. Augustus made the army permanent, salaried and bound to the princeps, with its pensions paid from taxes on citizens; from now on whoever paid and commanded the legions and the guard held the state, which is the question the Julio-Claudians and their successors keep answering.
  3. Roman power was graded, not bounded: provinces of the people and of Caesar, client kingdoms waiting to be annexed, and peoples who sent embassies; the Rhine after Varus was where expansion stopped, not a line anyone had drawn in advance.
