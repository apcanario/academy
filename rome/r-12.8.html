01A school text and its commentary

Greek module 10.12 follows the Roman Troy at large, from Naevius and Ennius to Nero's Sack of Ilium and the medieval Troy of Dares and Benoît; module 10.4 follows the idea of Rome after 476. This module keeps to Virgil's poem: who read it, how, and what they made of it.

In the schoolroom

The Aeneid entered the schools almost as soon as it was published. Suetonius says that Quintus Caecilius Epirota, a freedman of Atticus, was the first to read Virgil and the other new poets with his pupils (On Grammarians 16). A century later Quintilian laid it down that reading should begin with Homer and Virgil (1.8.5), and Juvenal pitied the grammar-master who was paid for a year what a charioteer won in one race (7.242–243), after a year in a room where every Horace was discoloured and soot clung to the blackened Virgil (7.226–227). The walls show the same thing from the pupils' side. The first line of the poem is scratched at least fifteen times at Pompeii, and on the house of Fabius Ululitremulus someone turned it into a joke about the fullers' guild: fullones ululam e[go] cano, non arma virumq(ue), "I sing of fullers and the owl, not arms and the man" (CIL IV 9131). At Vindolanda, on the northern frontier, a hand of about 97–105 wrote out Aeneid 9.473 on the back of a draft letter, and a second hand added letters that the editors read, tentatively, as seg(niter), "sloppy", a teacher's mark (Tab. Vindol. 118). A papyrus found at Masada among the Roman garrison's documents carries Aeneid 4.9 (Masada II, no. 721); who wrote it, and when, is uncertain.

The school made the poem a book of Latin. Lines were learned by heart, parsed, scanned and imitated; a boy who could not place a quotation from it was uneducated. Augustine, who learned to weep for Dido in such a school (deep dive module 12.4), gives the reason in the City of God: the boys read Virgil so that the greatest and best of poets, drunk in at a tender age, may not easily be forgotten (1.3). Christian schooling kept the pagan poet because no other text did his work.

Servius

The commentaries grew out of the classroom. The fourth-century grammarian Aelius Donatus wrote one whose preface and life survive (module 12.1); the rest is lost. Tiberius Claudius Donatus, about the same time, wrote Interpretationes Vergilianae, reading the poem as rhetoric, a speech in praise of Aeneas. The commentary that lasted was Servius', written at Rome about 400 on all three works. Macrobius makes the young Servius a guest at his Saturnalia; deep dive module 12.7 takes Servius and Macrobius as readers of the poem's politics, and this module takes the commentary as a school book.

Servius goes line by line. He explains grammar, metre and rare words, names the figures of speech, records variant readings, supplies the myth or the Roman custom behind an allusion, and notes where Virgil follows Homer or Ennius. Much of the antiquarian lore of Rome survives only in his notes: the annals of the pontiffs (module 0.1), Ennius' account of Juno's reconciliation (deep dive module 12.6). He takes the poet to know everything: totus quidem Vergilius scientia plenus est, all Virgil is full of learning, and book 6 holds first place in it (preface to book 6). In 1600 Pierre Daniel printed a fuller version from other manuscripts, the "Servius auctus" or "Servius Danielis", whose added notes are widely thought to come from Aelius Donatus' lost commentary. Medieval copies of Virgil carried Servius in the margins, and the reader met the poem through him. Simone Martini's frontispiece to Petrarch's Virgil, now in the Ambrosiana at Milan, paints the relation: Servius draws back a curtain to show Virgil to Aeneas, a farmer and a shepherd, the three works.

The allegorists went further. Fulgentius, in the late fifth or early sixth century, has Virgil's ghost appear to him and explain the Aeneid as the course of a human life: the shipwreck of book 1 is birth, Dido's love in book 4 the passions of youth, the descent of book 6 the search for wisdom (Exposition of the Content of Virgil). A twelfth-century commentary on the first six books, attributed to Bernard Silvestris, read them the same way. The poem had become a map of the soul.

02The Christian Virgil: the fourth Eclogue and Proba

Module 12.1 sets out the poem of the child and the ancient candidates for him. The Christian reading starts in the early fourth century. Lactantius quotes the fourth Eclogue's golden age, the earth bearing everything unploughed, as a pagan glimpse of the reign of the saints (Divine Institutes 7.24). The Oration to the Assembly of the Saints, ascribed to Constantine and transmitted with Eusebius' Life of Constantine, goes through the poem line by line as a prophecy of Christ, the Virgin and the new progeny from heaven, adding that Virgil veiled the truth to escape persecution (chapters 19–21). Augustine accepted the lines on the removal of sin's traces (4.13–14) as words of the Cumaean Sibyl about Christ, which Virgil passed on (City of God 10.27). Jerome would not: in a letter to Paulinus of Nola he mocks those who read iam redit et Virgo (4.6) as the Virgin Mary, as if Maro could be called a Christian without Christ (Letters 53.7). The reading survived his scorn. Dante's Statius says that the fourth Eclogue made him a Christian (module 10.2), and the poem kept its place among Christmas readings of the Sibyl and the prophets.

Proba took the poem apart. Her Cento, 694 hexameters built entirely of lines and half-lines from Virgil, tells the Creation, the Fall and the Flood, then the life of Christ to the Ascension. Her proem renounces her earlier poem on civil war and declares that she will tell how Virgil sang the holy gifts of Christ (Vergilium cecinisse loquar pia munera Christi, 23). The cento was an old game; Ausonius made an obscene wedding poem in it (module 10.2). Proba made it devotional, and her serpent in Eden moves in the coils of Laocoön's snakes. Jerome's letter aims at centos of this kind without naming her; the sixth-century list of approved and rejected books called the Decretum Gelasianum rejects a cento on Christ made of Virgil's verses; Isidore names Proba, wife of Adelphius, as its author (Etymologies 1.39.26). It was copied, printed and taught through the Renaissance.

Where sources disagree. Which Proba? Schenkl's edition (1888) and most scholars since give the Cento to Faltonia Betitia Proba, wife of Clodius Celsinus Adelphius, prefect of Rome in 351, writing about the 350s or 360s, which fits Isidore. Danuta Shanzer (1986, 1994) argued for her granddaughter Anicia Faltonia Proba, a correspondent of Augustine, which would move the poem to the end of the century. The Christian Virgil is not in question; only whether it came a generation earlier or later.

03Oracle and magician

The sortes Vergilianae

A poet who knew everything could be asked anything. The sortes Vergilianae opened the poem at random and read the first line the eye fell on as an answer. The examples begin in the Historia Augusta, a late fourth-century collection whose stories are often invented (module 0.1). Hadrian, anxious about Trajan's intentions, drew the lines on the king who would give Rome laws (6.808–812), Numa in Virgil, taken as a promise of empire (Hadrian 2.8); Severus Alexander was seen in the lines on the arts left to others and the charge to rule the peoples (6.847–853; Severus Alexander 14.5). The practice outlived its pagan setting. Rabelais' Pantagruel and Panurge consult the sortes on whether Panurge should marry (Tiers Livre, 1546). John Aubrey recorded the most famous case: in a library at Oxford during the Civil War, Lord Falkland invited Charles I to try the sortes, and the king opened at Dido's curse on Aeneas, that he be harried by war, torn from his people and fall before his time (4.615–620); Falkland, trying to lighten the moment, drew Evander's lament for Pallas (book 11) and was killed at Newbury in 1643 (Remaines of Gentilisme and Judaisme, written 1686–87). Charles was beheaded in 1649. Whatever happened in the library, the story shows what the poem had become: a book whose lines would fit any fate because they had been made to fit so many.

Virgil the magician

In Naples, where Virgil lived and was buried, the learned poet became a wonder-worker. The first witnesses are foreign clerics of the twelfth century. John of Salisbury tells of a bronze fly Virgil made that kept the flies out of Naples (Policraticus 1.4). Conrad of Querfurt, Henry VI's chancellor, wrote home in 1194 that Naples was protected by a palladium Virgil had made, a model of the city in a narrow-necked bottle, and that the poet's bones, kept in a castle by the sea, raised a storm when exposed to the air. Gervase of Tilbury, in the Otia imperialia (about 1211), adds a meat market where meat never rotted, and Alexander Neckam gives others. Later the stories multiplied: the egg in the foundations of the Castel dell'Ovo on which the castle's survival hung, the brazen head that answered questions, the tunnel through Posillipo cut by a spell in a night, and a cycle in which a Roman lady leaves the magician hanging in a basket halfway up her tower. Domenico Comparetti (Virgilio nel medio evo, 1872) set the two Virgils apart, the learned Virgil of the schools and the popular Virgil of the Neapolitan legends, and argued that the magician grew from local memory around the tomb and the monuments.

Where sources disagree. Conrad of Querfurt and Gervase of Tilbury report the magic works as things seen or vouched for in Naples. Petrarch, in his Itinerarium (1358), tells how King Robert of Naples asked him whether the Posillipo tunnel was Virgil's magic; he answered that he had never read that Virgil was a magician, and that the walls showed the marks of iron. The humanist's Virgil was the poet of the text, though the magician lived on in print, in the French, Dutch and English chapbooks of the early sixteenth century.

04The Roman d'Eneas

The first retelling in a vernacular is the anonymous Old French Roman d'Eneas of about 1160, a little over 10,000 octosyllabic lines, one of the three romances of antiquity written in the Plantagenet orbit that Greek module 10.12 places with the Roman de Troie. It keeps Virgil's story and moves its weight. The gods are reduced, the underworld kept as spectacle, and the second half becomes a love story Virgil never wrote. In the Aeneid Lavinia blushes once (12.64–69) and never speaks; in the Eneas Lavine sees the hero from a tower, falls in love at first sight, debates love with her mother in long Ovidian dialogues, and sends him a letter wrapped round an arrow. Dido's love is recast as lust that Lavine's lawful love surpasses, and at the end Eneas knows Turnus by Pallas' ring, not the sword-belt. The romance made the poem courtly: Heinrich von Veldeke's German Eneas follows it, and through it the founding epic of Rome entered the literature of love. The founding of Rome barely matters; the founding of a dynasty by marriage does, which suited a court that traced its kings, as the British chroniclers did, through Brutus to Aeneas (Greek module 10.12).

05Dante's guide

Module 10.4 has Dante's Rome, the providential empire of the Monarchia; deep dive module 12.4 has his Dido among the lustful, and deep dive module 12.5, by its prompt, follows book 6 into the Inferno. What belongs here is the choice of Virgil himself. When the shade appears in the dark wood, he names himself by his date, born sub Iulio and living at Rome under good Augustus "in the time of the false and lying gods" (Inferno 1.70–72), and Dante answers: tu se' lo mio maestro e 'l mio autore, "you are my master and my author", from whom alone he took the fine style that has done him honour (1.85–87). Virgil calls the Aeneid "my high tragedy" (20.113), and the Commedia is a reader's poem, full of translated lines.

The reasons for the choice are layered. Virgil was the poet of the empire Dante believed providential, the author of the one classical descent into the underworld that Dante's readers knew in Latin, and, through the fourth Eclogue, a prophet. He was also the school's master of style, read with Servius. Dante uses all of it and then marks its limit. Virgil leads through Hell and up the mountain, but he belongs to Limbo, lost not for any sin but for lack of faith (Inferno 4.39–42). Statius, meeting him, says that the Aeneid was his mother and his nurse in poetry (Purgatorio 21.97–98), and that Virgil was like a man who walks at night carrying a lamp behind him, which does himself no good but lights those who follow: per te poeta fui, per te cristiano (22.67–73), with the fourth Eclogue rendered into Italian between. When Beatrice comes, Dante turns to Virgil with Dido's words, conosco i segni de l'antica fiamma, "I know the signs of the old flame" (Purgatorio 30.48, from Aeneid 4.23), and finds him gone (30.49–51); the angels have just scattered lilies with the Latin of the Marcellus lament, manibus, oh, date lilia plenis (30.21, from 6.883). The guide's last appearance is as a quotation. Erich Auerbach ("Figura", 1938) argued that this Virgil is no allegory of reason but the historical poet himself, fulfilled and judged.

Where sources disagree. Could Virgil be saved? A sequence sung at Mantua for the feast of St Paul imagined the apostle weeping at Virgil's tomb: what would I have made of you, had I found you alive, greatest of poets. Dante's answer is no, and he sharpens it: in Paradiso 20.67–69 he puts in heaven Ripheus, a Trojan who has two lines in the Aeneid as the most just of men (2.426–427), while the poet who wrote those lines stays in Limbo.

06The Aeneid in English: Douglas, Surrey, Dryden

The first British translation of the whole poem is Scots. Gavin Douglas, later bishop of Dunkeld, finished his Eneados in 1513, the year of Flodden; it was printed in London in 1553. He calls his tongue "Scottis", adds the thirteenth book that Maffeo Vegio wrote in 1428 to give the poem a wedding and an apotheosis, and puts an original prologue before each book. The first prologue attacks William Caxton's Eneydos of 1490, a version of a French prose romance, as nothing like Virgil, and scolds Chaucer for calling Aeneas false to Dido. Ezra Pound thought Douglas better than the original, because Douglas had heard the sea ("How to Read", 1929).

Henry Howard, Earl of Surrey, executed in January 1547, translated books 2 and 4 into unrhymed iambic pentameter. Book 4 was printed in 1554 as "drawne into a straunge metre", and both books in 1557; the strange metre was English blank verse, here for the first time, the line of Marlowe, Shakespeare and Milton. Florence Ridley's edition (1963) shows how often Surrey borrowed Douglas's words. Set beside Dryden, the opening of book 2 shows what each century wanted. The Latin is conticuere omnes intentique ora tenebant (2.1). Surrey:

They whisted all, with fixed face attent

Dryden, a century and a half later: "All were attentive to the godlike man, / When from his lofty couch he thus began." Surrey keeps Virgil's silence and the fixed faces; Dryden supplies the "godlike" and the rhyme.

Dryden's Works of Virgil (1697) is the one module 12.1 hands on as reception. He made it in the years after 1688, a Catholic convert who had refused the new oaths and lost the laureateship, and he paid for it by subscription through his publisher Jacob Tonson. Tonson reused the 101 plates engraved for John Ogilby's Virgil of 1654, each sold to a subscriber whose arms were cut into it, and had Aeneas' face recut for the 1697 printing, apparently to resemble William III. Dryden dedicated the Aeneis not to William but to the Earl of Mulgrave, and stated his method there: to make Virgil speak such English as he would himself have spoken if he had been born in England, and in this present age. The result is an Augustan poem of Dryden's own Augustan age, and for a century it was the English Aeneid.

Where sources disagree. Was the Aeneis a Jacobite poem? Paul Hammond ("Dryden's Virgilian Kings", 2014) finds Dryden using "usurp" eight times where Virgil gives no prompt, a deliberate mistranslation in book 6 aimed at Queen Mary, and wide borrowing from the manuscript translation of the Jacobite Earl of Lauderdale, though he calls such moments comparatively rare. The book as published told another story: Tonson's Aeneas seemed to wear William's face, and John Barnard (2000) found its subscribers drawn from diverse political groupings, a national monument rather than a partisan one.

07Purcell and Berlioz

Deep dive module 12.4 tells what Nahum Tate and Purcell did to Dido in Dido and Aeneas and what Berlioz did to her in Les Troyens, and the dispute over the opera's date is told there. For the poem as a whole the two works show which Aeneid Europe kept on its stage. Tate's libretto takes book 4 alone and ends with the queen: the founding of Rome has dropped out, as it had for Augustine. Berlioz took more. He had learned Latin on Virgil from his father and broke down as a boy over the death of Dido, by his own account (Memoirs, chapter 2); four decades later he wrote both words and music of an opera in two parts, the fall of Troy from book 2, with Cassandra as its centre, and Carthage from books 1 and 4. The Trojans' cry of "Italie!" runs through the Carthage acts as Mercury's command does through book 4, and Didon dies foreseeing Rome. It is the fullest setting the poem has had, and it too is the Odyssean half only: no Latium, no Turnus, no Lavinia.

08Eliot, Broch, Le Guin: the classic and its voices

In 1944, with Europe at war, T. S. Eliot gave the first presidential address to the Virgil Society, "What is a Classic?", delivered on 16 October and printed in 1945. A classic, he argued, needs maturity of mind, of manners and of language, and can only be recognised in hindsight; English has great poets but no classic, and Latin had one. The essay ends by naming Virgil "the classic of all Europe". Virgil's Rome stands for a common European culture, and his sense of destiny and order for something that led towards a Christian civilisation he could not know. Theodor Haecker's Vergil, Vater des Abendlandes ("Virgil, Father of the West", 1931) had already read Virgil, from a Catholic standpoint, as the father of Western Christendom. The essay is the high point of the imperial Virgil in English, and it warns against "provincialism", the habit of judging the past only by the present.

Where sources disagree. Frank Kermode (The Classic, 1975) took Eliot's essay as his starting point and turned it: Eliot's classic is the imperial classic, a text whose authority mirrors the authority of Rome and the Church; a classic in the modern sense survives because it supports readings its first age could not have made. Eliot's Virgil is the source of a single tradition; Kermode's is a text that keeps changing meaning.

Hermann Broch began The Death of Virgil in 1936 and worked on it during three weeks in prison after the Anschluss of 1938; it was published in New York in 1945, in German and in Jean Starr Untermeyer's English at once. It spans the last eighteen hours of Virgil's life at Brundisium in four parts, Water, Fire, Earth and Ether, mostly inside the dying poet's mind, in sentences that run for pages. Its subject is Donatus' story of the order to burn the poem (module 12.1). Virgil comes to think art worthless beside the suffering of the crowds below his window and wants the Aeneid destroyed; Augustus argues for it as the state's possession, and Virgil gives it up in exchange for the freedom of his slaves. Written by an exile in the years of the Nazi state, it asks whether a poem that serves an empire is worth keeping, and leaves the answer to the poem's survival.

Ursula K. Le Guin's Lavinia (2008) gives a voice to the woman Virgil left silent. Lavinia narrates her own life in Latium, and in the woods near her father's house she meets the dying poet's shade, who regrets how little he gave her in his poem. She knows that she may exist only in his poem, and lives past its last line into a marriage, a widowhood and a son. Le Guin's afterword calls Aeneas' link to Rome "pure legend", much of it Virgil's own invention. The novel won the Locus Award in 2009. Its method answers the Roman d'Eneas, which gave Lavinia love and letters; Le Guin gives her the poem's knowledge of itself.

09Virgil in Fascist Italy and after

Module 10.4 has Mussolini's romanità, the Via dell'Impero and the Augustan Exhibition of 1937; Virgil had his own cult inside it. At Mantua the monument to the poet inaugurated in 1927 carries Anchises' charge to Rome, to rule the peoples, spare the conquered and war down the proud (6.851–853), with lines from the fifth Eclogue and the Georgics' praise of Italy. The bimillenary of Virgil's birth in 1930 was a state occasion of monuments, plaques, conferences and editions, and Mussolini called him the poet of empire and country; Tommaso Ricchieri (Studi storici, 2016) shows the regime reading the Georgics as a sanction for its return to the land and the Aeneid as Rome's mandate for Italian primacy. The conquest of Ethiopia in 1936 made the mandate literal.

After the war the Fascist reading was the one to answer. The pessimistic reading of the poem, its private voice of loss against the public voice of empire, took shape in the 1960s, and deep dive module 12.7 runs that debate, with Richard Thomas's study of how the Augustan reading was itself a reception. The lines kept their public use. Since 2014 the National September 11 Memorial Museum in New York has carried Aeneid 9.447 on its wall in letters of steel from the towers, "No day shall erase you from the memory of time"; Caroline Alexander had objected in the New York Times in 2011 that the line is Virgil's epitaph for Nisus and Euryalus, lovers killed after a night raid in which they butchered sleeping men, and the museum's director, Alice Greenwald, defended the choice. Seamus Heaney's translation of book 6, published after his death in 2016, closes the list of famous versions so far.

10Self-test

  1. What evidence shows that the Aeneid was a school text within a century of Virgil's death, and as far as the frontiers?
    Answer

    Suetonius has Caecilius Epirota teaching Virgil (On Grammarians 16); Quintilian makes Homer and Virgil the first reading (1.8.5); Juvenal's soot-blackened Virgil (7.226–227); at least fifteen Pompeian graffiti of the first line; Aeneid 9.473 as a writing exercise at Vindolanda (about 97–105) and 4.9 on a papyrus at Masada.

  2. What kind of commentary did Servius write, and what does the "Servius Danielis" add?
    Answer

    A line-by-line school commentary of about 400 on all three works: grammar, metre, rhetoric, variants, myth, Roman antiquities and Homeric models, treating Virgil as full of learning. Pierre Daniel's fuller version (1600) adds notes widely thought to come from Aelius Donatus' lost commentary.

  3. How did Lactantius, the Oration to the Assembly of the Saints, Augustine and Jerome differ on the fourth Eclogue?
    Answer

    Lactantius quoted its golden age as a pagan glimpse of the saints' reign (Divine Institutes 7.24); the Oration read it line by line as a prophecy of Christ (chapters 19–21); Augustine took 4.13–14 as the Sibyl's words about Christ, passed on by Virgil (City of God 10.27); Jerome mocked the reading (Letters 53.7).

  4. What is Proba's Cento, and what is disputed about its author?
    Answer

    A poem of 694 hexameters made entirely of Virgil's lines and half-lines, telling Genesis and the life of Christ. Most follow Schenkl in giving it to Faltonia Betitia Proba, wife of the prefect of 351; Shanzer argued for her granddaughter Anicia Faltonia Proba, later in the century.

  5. Why is the story of Charles I and the sortes Vergilianae evidence of what the poem had become, whatever its truth?
    Answer

    Aubrey's tale has the king open at Dido's curse (4.615–620) and Falkland at Evander's lament for Pallas, both read after the fact as foretelling their deaths in 1649 and 1643. It shows the Aeneid treated as an oracle whose lines could fit any fate, a use that runs from the Historia Augusta to Rabelais.

  6. Who first records Virgil as a magician, and how did Petrarch answer the legend?
    Answer

    Foreign clerics of the twelfth century: John of Salisbury's bronze fly (Policraticus 1.4), Conrad of Querfurt's palladium and bones (1194), Gervase of Tilbury's meat market that never rotted. Petrarch told King Robert that he had never read that Virgil was a magician and that the Posillipo tunnel showed marks of iron (Itinerarium).

  7. How does the Roman d'Eneas change the weight of Virgil's second half?
    Answer

    It turns it into a love story: Lavine, who never speaks in the Aeneid, falls in love at first sight, debates love with her mother in Ovidian dialogues and sends Eneas a letter on an arrow, while Dido's love becomes the lust her lawful love surpasses. A dynastic marriage replaces the founding of Rome as the point.

  8. Give three reasons Dante chose Virgil as his guide, and say how the Commedia marks the limit of that guidance.
    Answer

    He was the poet of the providential empire, the author of the Latin descent to the dead, the supposed prophet of the fourth Eclogue and Dante's master of style (Inferno 1.85–87). But he belongs to Limbo for lack of faith (4.39–42), lights others like a lamp carried behind (Purgatorio 22.67–73), vanishes when Beatrice comes (30.49–51), and stays in Limbo while the just Ripheus is saved (Paradiso 20.67–69).

  9. What did Surrey give English poetry, and what did Dryden say he was trying to do?
    Answer

    Surrey's books 2 and 4 (printed 1554 and 1557) were the first English blank verse, the "straunge metre" later used by Marlowe, Shakespeare and Milton. Dryden said he tried to make Virgil speak the English he would have spoken had he been born in England in Dryden's own age (Dedication of the Aeneis, 1697).

  10. How did Eliot and the Fascist bimillenary each use Virgil's empire, and how did Kermode answer Eliot?
    Answer

    Eliot made Virgil "the classic of all Europe", the source of a common culture leading towards Christianity (1944); the regime in 1930 made him the poet of empire and country, the Aeneid a mandate for Italian primacy and the Georgics for the return to the land. Kermode (1975) called Eliot's the imperial classic and argued that a classic lasts by bearing new readings.

11Carry forward

  1. Most readers of the Aeneid met it in pieces: a line to parse, a half-line for a cento, a page opened at random, a book set to music, a sentence cut into a wall. Ask of any reception which part of the poem it took and what it left out.
  2. The poem has been read as grammar, prophecy, oracle, magic, guide and national charter, and each use rested on the same premise, Servius' claim that Virgil knew everything; the humanists' Virgil of the text was one reading among these, not the end of them.
  3. The question deep dive II has pressed, whether the Aeneid serves its empire or mourns its cost, was asked by its readers long before its critics: Dryden under a king he would not serve, Broch in exile from the Nazi state, Le Guin for the bride with no lines. The poem lasts because it keeps that question open, and each age answers for itself.
