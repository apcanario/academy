Familia and paterfamilias

The Latin familia is not the English family. Ulpian lists its senses (Digest 50.16.195): a man's property; his slaves; everyone in one man's power; and all agnates, kin through males. The people under one roof, kin and slaves together, were the domus. Richard Saller ("Pater familias, mater familias, and the gendered semantics of the Roman household", Classical Philology 1999) showed that paterfamilias usually meant a property owner, and Ulpian counts a childless man, even a boy, as one (50.16.195.2). Module 2.4 gives his household priesthood; this module gives his law.

On paper it was extreme. Gaius calls paternal power, patria potestas, peculiar to Romans (Institutes 1.55). It lasted until the father died, whatever the son's age or office. A son in power owned nothing beyond a peculium held at his father's pleasure, as a slave's was; the father made and unmade his marriage and held the power of life and death. The Twelve Tables, as Gaius reports them (1.132), freed a son sold three times, and lawyers turned the rule into emancipation by fictitious sale.

The limits were real. Fathers who killed grown sons were remembered because they were rare, and the famous cases mix paternal with public authority: Brutus and Manlius Torquatus acted as consuls (Livy 2.5, 8.7), while the senator Fulvius, who had his son killed for setting out to join Catiline, acted as father (Sallust, Catiline 39.5). Custom expected a council of kin: Tarius, finding his son plotting against him, called one that included Augustus, and the son was exiled (Seneca, On Mercy 1.15). Hadrian deported a man who killed his son on a hunt, since paternal power should rest on devotion, not cruelty (Marcian, Digest 48.9.5). Death limited it most: most men had lost their fathers before they married.

Where sources disagree. Dionysius (Roman Antiquities 2.26–27) has Romulus give fathers lifelong power harsher than any Greek law. Saller ("Patria potestas and the stereotype of the Roman family", Continuity and Change 1986; Patriarchy, Property and Death in the Roman Family, 1994) called this a stereotype: Romans saw pietas as owed by fathers too, the power of life and death was almost never used, and his simulations leave few adult sons with living fathers.

Marriage, manus, dowry and divorce

Marriage needed no priest or document: it was the cohabitation of two people with conubium, the capacity to marry each other, who meant to be married. Consent, not intercourse, makes a marriage (Ulpian, Digest 50.17.30). A bride had to be twelve (Digest 23.2.4); slave unions had no legal effect.

What mattered was manus. Module 2.4 gives its three forms (Gaius, Institutes 1.110–113). A wife in manus passed into her husband's power "in the place of a daughter" (1.111), and her property became his. A wife without manus stayed in her father's power or, after his death, was sui iuris under a guardian; her property stayed hers, and custom voided gifts between spouses (Digest 24.1.1). By the late Republic marriage without manus was normal. The paradox: a wife belonged to her father's family, not her husband's, and at her father's death became an owner of property in her own right.

The husband managed the dowry (dos) but owed it back when the marriage ended, and could not sell Italian dotal land without his wife's consent (Gaius 2.63); if a divorce was her fault he kept a sixth per child, up to half (Ulpian, Rules 6.10). Divorce needed only one party's will, or in the Republic her father's. It ended no careers: Sulla and Pompey each married five times. Yet epitaphs praised the univira, married once. The Augustan marriage laws are module 6.2; Constantine's curb on unilateral divorce is module 8.3.

Where sources disagree. Gellius, citing the jurist Servius Sulpicius, makes Spurius Carvilius Ruga's divorce of a barren wife, about 230 BC, Rome's first and the reason dowry contracts began (4.3.1–2); Dionysius (2.25.7) and Valerius Maximus (2.1.4) agree. Yet Valerius records a senator expelled by the censors of 307 for divorcing without consulting his friends (2.9.2). Carvilius' may have been the first divorce without fault by the wife.

Age at marriage and the shape of the family

Keith Hopkins ("The Age of Roman Girls at Marriage", Population Studies 1965), from literary cases and epitaphs giving a marriage's length, concluded that girls commonly married in their early teens; for senatorial daughters that holds. Brent Shaw ("The Age of Roman Girls at Marriage: Some Reconsiderations", Journal of Roman Studies 1987) and Saller ("Men's Age at Marriage and Its Consequences in the Roman Family", Classical Philology 1987) traced instead the age at which the commemorator on thousands of epitaphs changes from parent to spouse: the late teens for ordinary women in the western provinces, the late twenties or about thirty for men. Roger Bagnall and Bruce Frier found a similar gap in the census returns of Roman Egypt (The Demography of Roman Egypt, 1994).

Husbands were often ten years older than their wives and died first. Frier's model of the imperial population (Cambridge Ancient History XI, 2000) uses a life expectancy at birth of about twenty-five, pulled down by infant deaths. Many children lost a parent young; widows, stepmothers and half-siblings were common. The three-generation household under an aged patriarch was for most Romans brief or imaginary.

Where sources disagree. Hopkins' young brides rest largely on elite cases and on the few epitaphs that state a marriage's length; Shaw and Saller's later ages assume that a spouse took over commemoration from parents, and their stones come from families able to pay for them, mostly urban. How far ordinary practice differed from the elite's is still argued.

Women: law, practice and the Laudatio Turiae

A woman could not vote, hold office or sit on a jury, and after Gaia Afrania wearied the praetors the edict barred women from pleading for others (Valerius Maximus 8.3.2; Digest 3.1.1.5). A woman sui iuris needed a guardian's authority to make a will or sell land. The Voconian law of 169 BC, which the aged Cato spoke for (Cicero, On Old Age 14), stopped the richest citizens making a woman their heir (Gaius 2.274). Augustus freed freeborn mothers of three from guardianship (module 6.2); Claudius abolished agnates' guardianship of women (Gaius 1.157).

Practice ran ahead of law. Cicero mocks jurists for inventing guardians in women's power (Pro Murena 27), and Gaius found the usual ground, women's light-mindedness, more specious than true (1.190). Women married without manus held fortunes: at Pompeii Eumachia built a hall on the forum (CIL X 810), and Julia Felix advertised her baths and shops for rent (CIL IV 1136). In 195 BC women thronged the Forum until the Oppian law, a wartime limit on their gold and dress, was repealed against Cato's opposition (Livy 34.1–8). Cornelia (module 5.1) and Hortensia's speech of 42 BC (module 5.5) were remembered as exceptional; deep dive module 11.4 takes up the women of the late Republic.

The empire gave women a new power, nearness to the emperor. Livia (module 6.1) was adopted by Augustus' will as Julia Augusta (Tacitus, Annals 1.8); Agrippina the Younger listened behind a curtain when the Senate met in the palace (13.5; module 7.1); Julia Domna was mother of the camps (module 7.4). None held office; each was accused of ruling through men.

The so-called Laudatio Turiae (CIL VI 41062; ILS 8393) is a husband's speech for his dead wife, cut in marble at Rome late in the first century BC. She brought her murdered parents' killers to punishment and beat relatives who tried to break her father's will and take her and the estate into their guardianship. In the proscriptions she gave her husband her jewellery, hid him, and threw herself at Lepidus' feet to have his recall honoured, and was dragged away bruised. Childless, she offered to divorce him so that he could have children by another wife; he says the offer nearly drove him out of his mind. The marriage lasted some forty years, ended by death, not divorce, which he calls rare. She is praised for wool-work in the speech that shows her handling law, money and armed men.

Where sources disagree. The surviving text names neither partner. Valerius Maximus (6.7.2) tells how Turia hid her husband Quintus Lucretius Vespillo in the proscriptions, a story Appian tells without her name (4.44), and Mommsen made the identification standard. Nicholas Horsfall (1983) judged the speaker too remote from politics to be the consul of 19 BC, and Josiah Osgood (Turia: A Roman Woman's Civil War, 2014) treats the couple as unknown equestrians.

Childhood, nurses and schools

The naming day, bulla and toga of manhood are in module 2.4. Before them came the father's decision to rear. Exposure was lawful: Hilarion, working in Alexandria in 1 BC, tells his pregnant wife to keep a boy and expose a girl (P.Oxy. 4.744), and Pliny asked Trajan about the status of foundlings reared as slaves (Letters 10.65–66).

Where sources disagree. John Boswell (The Kindness of Strangers, 1988) argued that most exposed infants were taken up by others, so that exposure redistributed children more than it killed them. W. V. Harris ("Child-Exposure in the Roman Empire", Journal of Roman Studies 1994) held that it was common, that many foundlings died, and that survivors fed the slave supply.

Propertied children were suckled by slaves. Tacitus' Messalla complains that a newborn is handed to some Greek slave girl (Dialogue on Orators 29.1); Favorinus urges a mother to nurse her own child (Gellius 12.1). The bond could last: Pliny gave his nurse a farm worth 100,000 sesterces (Letters 6.3), and nurslings set up nurses' epitaphs. Keith Bradley ("Wet-nursing at Rome", 1986) saw childhoods spent among many carers.

Schooling was private and paid: letters from about seven with a ludi magister, a slave paedagogus as escort, then poetry and grammar with a grammaticus and, for the elite, declamation with a rhetor. Horace remembered his flogging master Orbilius (Epistles 2.1.70–71); Quintilian opposed beating (Institutio oratoria 1.3.14). Harris (Ancient Literacy, 1989) argued that most people never learned to read.

Slavery: numbers, sources and work

Moses Finley (Ancient Slavery and Modern Ideology, 1980) counted Roman Italy among the few genuine slave societies, with classical Athens, the Caribbean, Brazil and the American South. Numbers are guesswork: P. A. Brunt (Italian Manpower, 1971) put Augustan Italy's slaves at about three million of no more than seven and a half million people; Walter Scheidel ("Human Mobility in Roman Italy, II: The Slave Population", Journal of Roman Studies 2005) at no more than one and a half million, built by two to four million imports over the last two centuries BC.

War came first: the second-century captives are in module 4.4, and Plutarch has Caesar take a million prisoners in Gaul (Caesar 15). Children of slave mothers (vernae) were slaves; foundlings, pirates' victims, self-sellers and convicts added more; trade linked them all.

Where sources disagree. Scheidel ("Quantifying the Sources of Slaves in the Early Roman Empire", Journal of Roman Studies 1997) argued that under the empire most new slaves were born to slave mothers. Harris ("Demography, Geography and the Sources of Roman Slaves", Journal of Roman Studies 1999) doubted that slaves reproduced on that scale and gave more weight to exposed infants and imports across the frontiers.

Cato's slave estate is module 4.4. Varro classes slaves as speaking farm equipment, beside half-speaking oxen and mute carts (On Agriculture 1.17.1); Columella keeps chained gangs in the ergastulum, lit by windows too high to reach (1.6). Great houses kept hundreds: the burial chamber of Livia's household lists slaves and freedmen by job, from hairdressers to wardrobe keepers (Susan Treggiari, "Jobs in the Household of Livia", 1975). Slaves were also craftsmen, managers, secretaries and doctors, and imperial slaves kept the emperor's accounts (module 7.5). They were flogged, used sexually, sold apart from their children and tortured as witnesses. When a slave killed the city prefect Pedanius Secundus in AD 61, the Senate sent his whole household to execution, four hundred in the figure Tacitus gives the senator Cassius, over the plebs' protests (Annals 14.42–45). Seneca's plea that slaves are men and humble friends (Letters 47.1) asks for gentler mastery, not abolition.

The great revolts fell within seventy years. Eunus' Sicilian war from about 135 is in module 4.4. The second began in 104, when the Senate ruled that no free-born ally be a slave in a province: the governor freed some eight hundred, then stopped under the owners' pressure, and slaves under Salvius and Athenion fought until Manius Aquillius crushed them (Diodorus 36.3–10). Spartacus' war of 73–71 is module 5.3. Each drew on newly enslaved adults, often sharing a language and a past as fighters, working as armed herdsmen or trained as gladiators. Keith Bradley (Slavery and Rebellion in the Roman World, 1989) tied the revolts to these conditions. Nothing on that scale followed: home-born slaves became a larger share, and manumission, which Bradley elsewhere read as an instrument of control (Slaves and Masters in the Roman Empire, 1984), offered the able a way out.

Manumission and the freedmen

A slave formally freed by a Roman citizen became a citizen. Philip V of Macedon, urging Larisa in 214 BC to enlarge its citizen body, cited the Romans, who enrolled freed slaves and admitted them to office (Syll.³ 543). The forms of manumission, the Junian Latins and Augustus' limits on manumission are module 9.1. Most of the freed were urban household slaves.

A freedman took his patron's first and family names: Cicero's secretary became Marcus Tullius Tiro. He owed respect (obsequium) and often agreed days of work (operae); he could not hold office at Rome, and Augustus forbade senators to marry freedwomen. His freeborn son was a full citizen: Horace, a freedman's son, became a military tribune and Maecenas' friend (Satires 1.6.45–48). In the towns rich freedmen joined the seviri Augustales, honour without office. They dominate the city's epitaphs, partly because, as Lily Ross Taylor showed ("Freedmen and Freeborn in the Epitaphs of Imperial Rome", 1961), they commemorated themselves more eagerly than the freeborn. Their reliefs show a couple's busts, the man togate, hands joined in the marriage slaves could not make; on the relief of the butcher Aurelius Hermia and his fellow freedwoman and wife Aurelia Philematium, she says he took her into his lap when she was seven (CIL VI 9499).

Petronius' Trimalchio is the type drawn by a probably senatorial pen. Brought from Asia as a boy and his master's favourite for fourteen years, he inherited, lost thirty million sesterces in shipwrecks in a day, won ten million in one voyage, then bought land and lent money (Satyricon 75–76); his planned epitaph boasts that he was a sevir, left thirty million and never heard a philosopher (71). Module 10.2 reads the novel as literature. Paul Veyne ("Vie de Trimalcion", 1961) and Finley (The Ancient Economy, 1973) took the career as typical: trade to make money, land to make it respectable, and no way into the elite.

Where sources disagree. John D'Arms (Commerce and Social Standing in Ancient Rome, 1981) answered Veyne and Finley that commerce carried less stigma, and senatorial money ran deeper in it through freedman agents, than the Trimalchio model allows. Whether Petronius drew a specimen or a snob's caricature cannot be settled from the novel.

How the study of the Roman family changed

For a century the Roman family was legal history, read from Gaius and the Digest. From the 1980s social historians turned to epitaphs, demography and letters; Beryl Rawson's The Family in Ancient Rome (1986) marked the shift. Susan Treggiari's Roman Marriage (1991) followed the institution from Cicero to Ulpian, law and sentiment together. Suzanne Dixon (The Roman Mother, 1988; The Roman Family, 1992) set ideals against practice. Bradley (Slaves and Masters in the Roman Empire, 1984; Discovering the Roman Family, 1991) put slaves, nurses and step-kin inside the household.

Where sources disagree. Saller and Shaw ("Tombstones and Roman Family Relations in the Principate", Journal of Roman Studies 1984) found that most epitaphs in the western provinces were set up within the nuclear family of parents, children and spouses, and concluded that it was the primary unit of obligation. Dale Martin ("The Construction of the Ancient Family", Journal of Roman Studies 1996) argued from the inscriptions of Asia Minor that their counting method inflated the nuclear share, and that the family is better seen as a continuum running out to wider kin, freedmen and slaves.

Against the Athenian household of Greek module 8.5, the Roman father's power outlasted his son's coming of age; a Roman woman without manus owned property under a guardianship grown formal, where the Athenian woman never left her kyrios; and the freed slave who at Athens became a metic became at Rome a citizen.

Self-test

  1. Why is paterfamilias not simply the Roman word for "father"?
    Answer

    It denotes a man in no one else's power, the owner of the estate and holder of power over its members; Ulpian counts a childless man, even a boy, as one (Digest 50.16.195.2), and Saller showed it usually meant an owner of property.

  2. What did patria potestas give a father in law, and what limited it in practice?
    Answer

    Lifelong power: a son in power owned nothing beyond a revocable peculium, and the father controlled his marriage and held power of life and death. Custom expected a council of kin (Seneca, On Mercy 1.15), emperors punished abuse (Hadrian, in Marcian, Digest 48.9.5), and mortality meant most men had lost their fathers by the time they married.

  3. How did marriage without manus differ from marriage with it, for the wife's property and family?
    Answer

    In manus she passed into her husband's power as if a daughter (Gaius 1.111) and her property became his. Without manus she stayed in her father's power or became sui iuris under a guardian, kept her property, and could not receive gifts from her husband (Digest 24.1.1).

  4. What happened to a dowry when a marriage ended, and why did it matter?
    Answer

    The husband had to return it, keeping a sixth for each child up to half if the divorce was the wife's fault (Ulpian, Rules 6.10); Augustus forbade him to sell Italian dotal land without her consent (Gaius 2.63). Return gave the wife's family leverage and made divorce costly.

  5. How did Shaw and Saller estimate age at marriage, and how did their results differ from Hopkins'?
    Answer

    They noted the age at which the commemorator on epitaphs changes from parent to spouse: late teens for women, late twenties or about thirty for men. Hopkins, from elite cases and epitaphs giving a marriage's length, had argued for brides in their early teens, which remains true of the senatorial class.

  6. Why does late male marriage matter for understanding paternal power?
    Answer

    With men marrying near thirty and life expectancy at birth around twenty-five in Frier's model, most men were free of paternal power by the time they married, so the lifelong patriarch of the law books governed few grown men; widows, stepmothers and half-siblings were common instead.

  7. What does the Laudatio Turiae show about women in law and practice?
    Answer

    A wife defends her inheritance in court against relatives claiming guardianship, protects and supplies her proscribed husband, pleads before Lepidus, and offers divorce so he can have children; she acts in law, property and politics while being praised for wool-work and modesty.

  8. What is the evidence for exposure of children, and what do Boswell and Harris disagree about?
    Answer

    Hilarion's letter telling his wife to expose a girl (P.Oxy. 4.744) and Pliny's query about foundlings raised as slaves (Letters 10.65–66). Boswell thought most exposed infants were taken up by others; Harris that exposure was common, many died, and survivors fed the slave supply.

  9. Why did the great slave revolts fall between about 135 and 71 BC and not recur?
    Answer

    They drew on masses of newly enslaved adults with shared language and fighting experience, working as armed herdsmen or gladiators. Later the slave population was more home-born and manumission offered the able a way out, as Bradley stressed.

  10. What made Roman manumission different from Greek, and what kind of freedman does Trimalchio represent?
    Answer

    A slave formally freed by a citizen became a citizen, as Philip V noted (Syll.³ 543), and his sons were freeborn. Trimalchio is the rich freedman who made money in trade, moved into land and lending, and held the Augustal priesthood, but could not enter the elite; D'Arms warned against taking the satire as typical.

Carry forward

  1. Roman family law describes powers, not lives: patria potestas, manus and guardianship were extreme on paper and limited by custom, by emperors and above all by death, so every legal rule must be read against who was alive to exercise it.
  2. Marriage without manus, easy divorce and the returnable dowry gave women of property a practical independence the law never declared, while office stayed closed to them; women's power ran through fathers, husbands and sons, and under the empire through nearness to the emperor.
  3. Slavery was a structure of the Roman household and economy, and manumission its outlet: it made freed slaves citizens and their sons freeborn, which kept the system stable after the war captives of the late Republic and made freedmen and their descendants a large, visible part of the urban population.
