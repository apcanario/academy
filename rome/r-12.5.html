01Cumae, the Sibyl and the golden bough

Book 6 is the hinge of the poem. It closes the Odyssean wanderings of books 1–5, which deep dive module 12.2 takes book by book and leaves this book in outline, and it shows Aeneas what the wanderings were for, so that he comes back up ready for the Iliadic war of books 7–12. The fleet beaches at Cumae, the oldest Greek city in Italy, Strabo says (5.4.4; module 0.2 gives the site), near the crater lake of Avernus, which Greeks had long made the setting of Homer's visit to the dead and the seat of an oracle of the dead (Strabo 5.4.5). Aeneas climbs to Apollo's temple. Its doors, the poem says, were carved by Daedalus, who landed here after his flight from Crete: the death of Androgeos, the Athenian children chosen by lot, Pasiphae and the Minotaur, the labyrinth undone by Ariadne's thread. Icarus is missing: twice the father tried to carve his son's fall, twice his hands dropped (6.14–33). The Sibyl breaks off the looking (6.37).

The Sibyl, Deiphobe daughter of Glaucus (6.36), prophesies in a cave cut into the rock, with a hundred mouths (6.42–44). As Apollo takes her she changes colour and grows larger: deus, ecce deus (6.46). The god rides her like an unbroken horse (6.77–80), and what she says is books 7–12 in a sentence: wars, the Tiber foaming with blood, a second Achilles already born in Latium, a foreign bride once more the cause, and help from a Greek city (6.83–97). Aeneas asks one thing, to go down to his father, as Orpheus, Pollux, Theseus and Hercules went down (6.103–123). Her answer is the book's most quoted sentence: the descent to Avernus is easy, the door of dark Dis stands open night and day, but to retrace the step and come out to the upper air, hoc opus, hic labor est (6.126–129). Aeneas' vow of a temple to Phoebus and the Sibyl as Roman religion are deep dive module 12.6's; the Sibylline Books and their sale to Tarquin are module 1.6's.

Two conditions follow. A golden bough hides in a dark tree, sacred to the Juno of the underworld; Proserpina has ordained it as her gift; pluck one and another grows; if fate calls a man it comes away easily, and otherwise no force will tear it nor iron cut it (6.136–148). And an unburied friend pollutes the fleet (6.149–152): Misenus, Hector's trumpeter, drowned by Triton for challenging the gods on his conch (6.162–174). The burial comes first, as Elpenor's burial is the first thing asked of Odysseus among the dead (Odyssey 11.51–80; Greek module 10.7). Two doves of Venus lead Aeneas to the tree, where the gold glints through the branches like mistletoe in winter on a tree not its own (6.190–209). He breaks it off greedily, and it hesitates, cunctantem (6.211). Misenus is burned and gives his name to the cape (6.212–235); black victims go to Hecate, Night, Earth and Proserpina (6.243–254); the ground groans at dawn, the Sibyl cries procul, o procul este, profani, away, away, you uninitiated (6.258), and plunges into the cave with Aeneas behind her, sword drawn.

Where sources disagree. Why does the bough hesitate, when the Sibyl promised that a man called by fate would find it come away easily? Servius argues against reading the word as a sign that Aeneas is not chosen, which, as Richard Thomas (Virgil and the Augustan Reception, 2001) observed, shows the reading was already current; he also connects the bough with the grove of Diana at Aricia, where a challenger broke a branch before fighting the priest, the note from which James Frazer built The Golden Bough (1890). R. A. Brooks ("Discolor aura: Reflections on the Golden Bough", 1953) and Charles Segal, in articles of 1965–68, read the hesitation as deliberate: a lifeless gold bound to death, the first doubt the book casts on its own promise.

02The descent: the threshold, Charon and the unburied

At the turn into the dark the poet stops to pray to the gods who rule the souls: sit mihi fas audita loqui, may it be right for me to tell what I have heard (6.264–267). Then a line famous for its displaced adjectives, ibant obscuri sola sub nocte per umbram (6.268): they went dark beneath the lonely night, where sense wants lonely figures under dark night. The approach is a series of thresholds. In the forecourt live Grief and avenging Cares, Diseases, Old Age, Fear, Hunger, Want, Death, Toil, Sleep the kinsman of Death, the mind's evil joys, War, the iron chambers of the Furies and mad Discord with bloody ribbons in her snaky hair (6.273–281). An elm in the middle holds false dreams under every leaf (6.282–284), an image the book will close on. Then the monsters: Centaurs, Scyllas, hundred-armed Briareus, the Hydra, the Chimaera, Gorgons, Harpies and three-bodied Geryon (6.285–289). Aeneas draws on them, and would have struck at empty air had the Sibyl not warned him that they are thin lives without body (6.290–294).

Beyond lies the river, Acheron pouring into Cocytus, and its ferryman (6.295–304): Charon, filthy, white-bearded, with eyes of standing flame (stant lumina flamma, 6.300). Charon is not Homeric; Pausanias says that Polygnotus painted him in his picture of the underworld at Delphi following the lost epic Minyas (10.28.2). He belongs to the later Greek underworld that module 2.4 had in mind when it called Virgil's map Greek in plan. The dead crowd the bank, as many as the leaves that fall in the first frost of autumn or the birds that flock to land when the cold drives them over the sea to sunny countries (6.305–312), and they stand begging to cross first, tendebantque manus ripae ulterioris amore, stretching out their hands in longing for the farther shore (6.314). The leaves descend from Glaucus' generations of men (Iliad 6.146–149), and Bacchylides had already set them beside the Cocytus, where his Heracles saw the shades like leaves shaken on Ida (Bacchylides 5). The Sibyl explains the sorting: these are the unburied, who wander a hundred years before Charon takes them (6.325–330).

Among them is Palinurus (6.337–383). How he fell is book 5's, and the clash between his two accounts is deep dive module 12.1's. Here he asks what Elpenor asked, burial or passage, and is refused in a line that states the book's theology: desine fata deum flecti sperare precando, stop hoping that the gods' decrees can be bent by prayer (6.376). The Sibyl consoles him with a cult: neighbouring peoples, driven by portents, will build his tomb, and the place will keep his name for ever (6.378–381), as Capo Palinuro still does. Charon challenges the living man, recalling that he regretted ferrying Hercules, who dragged Cerberus in chains from the king's throne, and Theseus and Pirithous, who came to carry off Proserpina (6.388–397); the Sibyl shows the bough, unseen for a long time (6.405–409), and the stitched boat groans under Aeneas' weight and takes in water through its seams (6.413–414). On the farther bank Cerberus swallows a drugged cake of honey and grain and sprawls asleep across his cave (6.417–425).

03The fields of mourning and Deiphobus

Across the river Virgil groups the dead who died too soon or wrongly, an arrangement with no Homeric precedent. First the wailing of infants torn from the breast (6.426–429); then those condemned to death on false charges, whose cases Minos, presiding with his urn, hears again (6.430–433); then the suicides, who would gladly bear poverty and hard labour in the upper air now (6.434–437), the bargain Achilles would have struck in preference to kingship over the dead (Odyssey 11.489–491). The Styx winds nine times round them (6.439). Then the fields of mourning, lugentes campi, where those consumed by cruel love walk hidden paths in a myrtle wood (6.440–444): Phaedra, Procris, Eriphyle, Evadne, Pasiphae, Laodamia, and Caeneus, once a woman, turned into a man, and a woman again (6.445–449). Three of them stand already in Homer's catalogue of heroines (Odyssey 11.225–332); Virgil rewrites the catalogue as one of unhappy love, and it prepares the last of them, Dido, whose meeting at 6.450–476 is deep dive module 12.4's.

The last field holds those famous in war (6.477–493): Tydeus, Parthenopaeus and Adrastus of the Theban wars, then the Trojan dead, who crowd round Aeneas and want to walk with him, while the Greek ghosts, seeing his armour, run as they once ran to their ships or raise a thin cry that dies in their open mouths (6.489–493). Deiphobus, Priam's son, stands there mutilated, ears, nose and hands cut away (6.494–499). In the Cyclic Little Iliad, as Proclus summarises it, he married Helen after Paris' death, and in the Odyssey he walks beside her round the wooden horse (4.274–289). In Virgil he tells how she betrayed him on the last night: leading the Trojan women in a Bacchic dance with a torch to signal the Greeks, removing his sword from under his pillow and opening the door to Menelaus (6.509–530). The account sits badly with Aeneas' sight of Helen hiding at Vesta's shrine in book 2, one reason for doubting those lines (deep dive module 12.1). The Sibyl cuts the meeting short, since the night is passing in tears (6.539), and Deiphobus leaves with a blessing, i, decus, i, nostrum; melioribus utere fatis, go, our glory, go, and enjoy a better fate (6.546). The Trojan past ends here, with a man destroyed by the night Aeneas survived.

04Tartarus and its sinners

At a fork the Sibyl gives the road: right, under the walls of Dis, to Elysium; left, to Tartarus, where the wicked are punished (6.540–543). Aeneas looks back to the left and sees a fortress with a triple wall and the burning river Phlegethon round it, a gate on columns of adamant, an iron tower, Tisiphone on watch, and from within, groans, the crack of the lash and dragging chains (6.548–561). He does not go in. No pure soul may set foot on the threshold of crime, the Sibyl says (6.563), and she tells him what Hecate showed her when she was set over the groves of Avernus (6.564–565).

A judge, Rhadamanthus, forces out confessions of crimes concealed in life and put off until death; Tisiphone and her sisters lash the guilty; a Hydra with fifty mouths sits within; and Tartarus drops twice as far as heaven rises to Olympus (6.566–579), outdoing Homer's measure, as far below Hades as heaven is above earth (Iliad 8.16). The mythical rebels come first: the Titans, the Aloadae who tried to storm heaven, and Salmoneus, who aped Jupiter's thunder with a chariot over bronze (6.580–594); then Tityos with the vulture at his liver (6.595–600), and the Lapiths, Ixion and Pirithous under a rock always about to fall, before a feast a Fury forbids them to touch (6.601–607). Then, without a break, Roman crimes (6.608–614): hating a brother while he lived, striking a parent, cheating a client, sitting alone on found wealth without sharing it with one's own (the largest crowd), being killed for adultery, following impious arms and betraying a master's trust. A second list adds the man who sold his country for gold and set a tyrant over it, who made and unmade laws for a price, who forced his way into his daughter's bed (6.621–623). Theseus sits and will sit for ever (6.617–618); Phlegyas cries the lesson through the shadows, discite iustitiam moniti et non temnere divos, be warned: learn justice and do not despise the gods (6.620). A hundred tongues and a voice of iron would not list the rest (6.625–627).

The catalogue is a Roman code of offences in Greek dress. The fraud against a client (fraus innexa clienti, 6.609) breaks the fides of patronage, and Servius' note on the line preserves the Twelve Tables' curse on it: patronus si clienti fraudem fecerit, sacer esto. Following impious arms and selling one's country point to the civil wars just ended; the shield of book 8 hangs Catiline from a rock in Tartarus (8.666–669; module 6.3). Homer's Odysseus saw three sinners, Tityos, Tantalus and Sisyphus, in a passage that ancient critics suspected because a man at a trench could not see into Erebus (Greek module 10.7). Virgil keeps the visitor out and gives the sight to a guide who has been inside. Module 2.4 notes that Rome had no map of the underworld of its own; here the Roman dead get Greek sites and Roman crimes.

Where sources disagree. Did Theseus come back? Aeneas cites him among those who went down (6.122), and Charon remembers ferrying him (6.393); the Sibyl seats him in Tartarus for ever (6.617–618). Apollodorus (Library 2.5.12) and Euripides' Heracles have Heracles bring Theseus up and leave Pirithous below. Virgil sets the versions side by side in one book and chooses the harshest for his Tartarus.

05Elysium, Musaeus and Anchises' doctrine

The groves of the blessed

Aeneas fixes the bough on the threshold of Dis' palace as Proserpina's gift (6.635–636), and they come to the glad places, the green lawns of the fortunate groves (6.638–639). The air is wider there and clothes the fields in brilliant light, and the place knows its own sun and its own stars (6.640–641). The blessed wrestle on the grass, dance and sing; Orpheus in his long robe plays the seven notes (6.642–647); the founders of Troy, Ilus, Assaracus and Dardanus, keep their chariots and arms, and others feast and sing a paean in a laurel grove where the Eridanus rises (6.648–659). Then the poem says who is here, and the list is a Roman roll of honour: those wounded fighting for their country, priests who were chaste while they lived, faithful poets who spoke what was worthy of Phoebus, those who improved life by the arts they discovered, and those whose deserving made others remember them (6.660–664). There is no Achilles. In Homer the Elysian plain is promised to Menelaus because he is Zeus' son-in-law (Odyssey 4.561–569); here it is earned by service. Cicero's Dream of Scipio had already promised a fixed place in the sky to all who preserved, helped or enlarged their country (On the Republic 6.13).

The Sibyl asks the way of Musaeus, who stands head and shoulders above the crowd (6.666–668). He is the mythical singer whom the Greeks paired with Orpheus and credited with oracles and initiation poems, and whom Plato's Adeimantus mocks, with his son, for promising the just an eternal drinking party below (Republic 363c–d). Virgil gives the founder of the Greek poetry of the afterlife a guide's part and a line that makes Elysium a landscape, not a city: nulli certa domus, none of us has a fixed home; we live in shady groves and on soft river banks (6.673–675). He leads them to a ridge above a green valley where Anchises is reviewing the souls of his descendants (6.676–683).

Father and son

Anchises greets him, venisti tandem, you have come at last (6.687). Three times Aeneas tries to put his arms round his father's neck, three times the image escapes his hands, like light winds or a winged dream (6.700–702): the lines of his attempt on Creusa (2.792–794), both taken from Odysseus' attempt on his mother Anticleia (Odyssey 11.204–208; Greek module 10.7). In a secluded valley by the river Lethe he sees souls in countless swarms, like bees in a summer meadow settling on bright lilies (6.703–709): these, Anchises says, are souls owed second bodies, who drink at Lethe the waters of forgetting (6.713–715). Aeneas asks the question the doctrine answers. Can souls really go back to sluggish bodies? quae lucis miseris tam dira cupido?, what is this dreadful longing of the wretched for the light? (6.719–721). Augustine quotes the line to argue that the desire for a body arises in the soul itself (City of God 14.5).

The world-soul and the wheel of rebirth

Anchises answers in 28 lines (6.724–751) whose Latin is clear and whose meaning is not. In the beginning a spirit within nourishes heaven, earth, the sea, the moon and the stars, and mind, poured through the limbs, moves the whole mass and mingles with the great body: mens agitat molem (6.724–727). From it come men, beasts, birds and the monsters of the sea; their seeds have a fiery force and a heavenly origin, so far as harmful bodies do not slow them and earthly, dying limbs do not dull them (6.728–732). Hence they fear and desire, grieve and rejoice, and shut in the darkness of a blind prison do not look up at the sky (6.733–734). Even at death the evils of the body do not wholly leave them, so they are punished: some hung out to the empty winds, some washed of their stain under a vast whirlpool, some burned clean in fire (6.735–742). Quisque suos patimur manis, each of us suffers his own ghost (6.743). Then we are sent through wide Elysium, and a few of us hold the glad fields until long time, the cycle completed, has removed the ingrained stain and left pure ethereal sense and the fire of simple air (6.743–747). All the others, when they have turned the wheel for a thousand years, the god calls in a great throng to Lethe, so that, forgetting, they may see the vault of heaven again and begin to wish to return to bodies (6.748–751).

Each element has a Greek owner, and the Greek modules have them. One world animated by soul and mind is Plato's Timaeus (30b, 35a–36d; Greek module 12.3). The body as a prison through which the soul looks out is the Phaedo's (82e), and so are souls still weighted with body after death (81c–d; Greek module 8.4). The thousand-year journey, the choice of a new life and the river of forgetting are Er's in Republic 10 (614b–621d), and the long purification by which a few leave the cycle answers the Phaedrus' ten thousand years and the philosopher's three thousand (248e–249a; Greek module 12.3). The eternal Tartarus can stand beside the wheel because it stands beside it in Plato, whose incurable souls are thrown into Tartarus and never come out (Phaedo 113e; Gorgias 525c). Rebirth itself is Pythagorean (Greek modules 5.6 and 12.1), and Ovid gave Pythagoras a long speech on it (Metamorphoses 15; module 10.1). The vocabulary is Stoic: spiritus and mens render pneuma and mind, the soul's substance is fire, and fear, desire, grief and joy are the four passions of the Stoic classification (Cicero, Tusculan Disputations 4; Greek module 12.7). And the whole is an answer to Lucretius, whose third book had made the punishments of Acheron a picture of the living mind (Greek module 8.4): Anchises' principio opens as a Lucretian lesson opens and teaches the opposite, an immortal soul, as deep dive module 12.1 notes in handing the passage here. Eduard Norden's commentary (1903) traced the system to a single Hellenistic source, which he took to be Posidonius; most readers since see Virgil combining his sources himself. Augustine read the purgations as the Platonists' doctrine of purifying punishment and quoted them in arguing about it (City of God 21.13).

Where sources disagree. Does every soul return? John Conington called the passage one of the hardest in Virgil. One reading, given in R. D. Williams' commentary (1972) and R. G. Austin's (1977), takes 6.743–747 to describe a few pure souls who stay in Elysium until they become simple fire, while the rest are purged and sent to Lethe; T. E. Page held that Elysium is only a resting place and that all souls are reborn. The clause pauci laeta arva tenemus (6.744), set between the punishments and the thousand years, allows both.

06The parade of heroes and Anchises' charge

Anchises climbs a mound with Aeneas and the Sibyl so that the souls pass before them face by face (6.752–755), and the doctrine becomes history: these are the souls who will drink at Lethe and be born Romans. Module 6.3 reads the parade's politics, Augustus' place in it straight after Romulus, the unhappy Brutus, Caesar and Pompey, and Marcellus; deep dive module 12.7 takes Marcellus as a test case in the debate over the poem. The rest is set out here.

The procession opens with the Alban kings (6.756–776). First, leaning on a headless spear, comes Silvius, Aeneas' posthumous son by Lavinia, brought up in the woods (6.760–766): Virgil's Silvius is the one Dionysius gives, not Livy's son of Ascanius (module 1.3). Then Procas, Capys, Numitor and Silvius Aeneas, and the towns they will found, Nomentum, Gabii, Fidenae, Collatia and others, lands still nameless in Aeneas' day (6.767–776). Romulus wears the double crest of Mars, and under his auspices Rome will ring seven hills with one wall, happy in her sons like the Berecyntian mother riding crowned with towers (6.777–787). Then Augustus (6.788–807), and after him, back in sequence, Numa the lawgiver from little Cures (6.808–812), warlike Tullus (6.812–815), boastful Ancus (6.815–816), the Tarquins and Brutus (6.817–823). The Republic follows in a press of names: the Decii and Drusi, Torquatus with his axe, Camillus bringing back the standards (6.824–825); Caesar and Pompey (6.826–835); one who will drive his chariot to the Capitol in triumph over Corinth (6.836–837), Mummius in 146 (module 4.4); and one who will overthrow Argos, Mycenae and the Aeacid himself, of Achilles' line, avenging his Trojan ancestors and Minerva's violated temple (6.838–840). The Aeacid is usually taken as Perseus of Macedon, beaten by Aemilius Paullus at Pydna in 168, as module 3.5 notes in handing the line here. Propertius calls Achilles Perseus' ancestor (4.11.39), as Pyrrhus had claimed Achilles' blood, and the Ennian oracle to the Aeacid that module 3.5 reads stands behind the word: the conquest of Greece becomes the sack of Troy repaid. Then Cato, Cossus, the Gracchi, the two Scipios, thunderbolts of war and the ruin of Libya, Fabricius, Serranus sowing his field, and the Fabii, ending on Fabius Maximus, who alone restores the state by delaying, Ennius' line turned to the second person (6.841–846; deep dive module 12.1).

Then the charge (6.847–853). Others, Anchises says (he believes it), will beat out breathing bronze more softly and draw living faces from marble, plead causes better, trace the movements of the sky with the rod and name the rising stars:

tu regere imperio populos, Romane, memento (6.851)

Remember, Roman, to rule the peoples with your power; these will be your arts: to set the custom of law upon peace, to spare the conquered and war down the proud, parcere subiectis et debellare superbos (6.852–853). Some manuscripts read pacis for paci, the custom of peace. The others are Greeks, and the arts conceded to them are those Rome took from them: sculpture, oratory, astronomy. The concession is strange in a Latin poem built on Homer, and stranger after Cicero, but it states a Roman self-image older than Virgil. Cicero had laid down that victory should spare those who had not been cruel or savage in war (On Duties 1.35); Horace's Carmen Saeculare, sung in 17, prays that Augustus be first in war and gentle to the fallen enemy (51–52; module 6.3). The precept is also a standard by which the poem measures its own hero. When Aeneas stands over the beaten Turnus at the end of book 12 and kills him, readers ask whether he spared the conquered or warred down the proud: deep dive module 12.7's question. After the charge comes Marcus Claudius Marcellus, winner of the spolia opima in 222, and beside him the young Marcellus, Augustus' nephew, whom the parade mourns (6.854–886; module 6.3); Octavia's fainting at the reading is told in deep dive module 12.1.

07The gates of horn and ivory

Anchises leads his son through the whole, tells him of the wars to come, the Laurentine peoples and Latinus' city, and how to face or flee each labour (6.887–892). Then the book ends in six lines:

sunt geminae Somni portae (6.893)

There are twin gates of Sleep: one, it is said, of horn, by which an easy exit is given to true shades; the other gleaming, made of white ivory, but through it the Manes send false dreams to the upper world. There Anchises, still talking, escorts his son and the Sibyl and sends them out by the ivory gate (6.893–898). Aeneas makes his way to the ships and sails along the coast to Caieta (6.899–901). The gates are Penelope's, who tells the dreams that come through horn and are fulfilled from those that come through ivory and deceive, on a pun Latin cannot keep (Odyssey 19.560–567; Greek module 10.7). Plato's Socrates had already used the pair, inviting a hearer to judge whether his dream came through horn or through ivory (Charmides 173a). Virgil changes two things: what passes through horn is true shades, not true dreams, and his hero leaves by the false gate. The elm of false dreams at the entrance (6.282–284) and the ivory gate at the exit close the book in a ring.

Where sources disagree. Servius takes the poetic sense to be plain, that Virgil wants everything he has said understood as false, and glosses horn as the eyes, which see truly, and ivory as the mouth, from the teeth, which can lie. William Everett (1900) argued that false dreams came before midnight and true ones after, so that the gate gives the hour of return, and Norden followed him in the 1926 edition of his commentary. R. G. Austin's commentary (1977) took the plain sense of veris umbris: Aeneas and the Sibyl are living, not true shades, so the horn gate is not theirs. Brooks Otis (1959) read the exit as marking the vision as a dream to be interpreted; A. J. Boyle (1986) as a sign that Anchises' promises were lies; Shadi Bartsch ("Ars and the Man", Classical Philology, 1998) as a shadow over the poem's own art. Deep dive module 12.7 sets the exit beside Marcellus in the debate over the poem's politics.

The book had imitators within a century. Ovid's little Aeneid lets the Sibyl tell her own story on the way back up (Metamorphoses 14; module 10.1); Lucan answered with Erictho's necromancy, a corpse prophesying ruin instead of a father showing glory, and Silius Italicus sent Scipio to the dead at Cumae (Punica 13; module 10.2 reads both poets).

08Book 6 and Odyssey 11

Homer gave the frame, and Greek modules 4.4 and 10.7 give Homer's book: Odysseus' visit to the dead, prescribed by Circe, with Elpenor, Tiresias' prophecy, Anticleia, the heroines, Agamemnon, Achilles, silent Ajax and the sinners. The correspondences are close: the unburied companion who asks for burial, the mother or father whose shade slips through the embrace, the catalogue of women, and Ajax's silent turning away (Odyssey 11.563–564), which becomes Dido's silence (deep dive module 12.4).

Deep dive module 12.2 gives the difference at the root in a sentence; it runs through the whole book. Odysseus sits by a trench of blood and the dead come up to drink; Aeneas goes down in his body, with a guide, a passport and a route. Odysseus' dead have no moral geography except in the suspect passage of the sinners; Virgil's are sorted by how they died and how they lived. Tiresias tells one man how he will get home and how he will die (11.121–137); Anchises tells a people its history and its mission (6.756–886). Achilles in Hades says he would rather labour for a poor man alive than rule the dead, and Homer's book is about what is lost at death; Virgil's is about what waits before birth. And the Homeric visit is a story within a story, told by Odysseus to the Phaeacians, whose truth Greek module 10.7 asks how far to trust; Virgil's is told by the poet, who prays for leave to tell it, and is closed by a gate that raises the same doubt in another form.

Where sources disagree. Was Odyssey 11 the main model? G. N. Knauer (Die Aeneis und Homer, 1964) mapped the Aeneid onto Homer scene by scene, book 6 onto the Nekyia among them. Norden proposed that the descent was modelled on a lost poem of Heracles' journey down for Cerberus, a living hero past a ferryman and a hound; Hugh Lloyd-Jones argued for an early epic of that descent, and R. J. Clark ("How Vergil Expanded the Underworld in Aeneid 6", 2001) built on the suggestion. The poem names its precedents: Aeneas cites Orpheus, Pollux, Theseus and Hercules (6.119–123), and Charon remembers Hercules chaining Cerberus (6.392–396).

09Book 6 in Dante's Inferno

Dante read book 6 as the map of the place he meant to describe, and the Inferno repays it line by line. Dante's choice of Virgil as guide, the medieval Virgil and the poem's wider afterlife are deep dive module 12.8's, and Dante's providential Rome is module 10.4's; what follows is the debt to book 6.

The precedent. In Inferno 2 Dante doubts his fitness for the journey by recalling who went before him: the father of Silvius went, still corruptible, to the immortal world, and in the body (2.13–15), because he was chosen to father Rome and its empire, the seat of Peter's successor (2.20–24). Book 6 and Paul's rapture are the two authorities for a living man among the dead:

Io non Enëa, io non Paulo sono (Inferno 2.32)

The threshold and the river. Charon is Virgil's, eyes of glowing coal for eyes of standing flame (Caron dimonio, con occhi di bragia, Inferno 3.109; Aeneid 6.300). He refuses the living man and tells him a lighter boat must carry him (3.91–93), where Virgil's boat groans and ships water under Aeneas' weight. The souls on the bank leave it like autumn leaves falling one after another, and like a bird answering its call (3.112–117): Virgil's two similes for the same crowd (6.305–312) joined in a tercet. And they are eager to cross (3.124–126), as Virgil's stretch their hands to the farther shore.

The officials. Virgil's underworld supplies Dante's staff, each moved to a new post. Minos, who in book 6 retries the falsely condemned, becomes the judge at the entrance to the second circle, sentencing with the coils of his tail (Inferno 5.4–12). Cerberus is quietened not with a honey cake but with fistfuls of earth thrown into his gullets (Inferno 6.25–27). Phlegyas, who preaches justice in Virgil's Tartarus, ferries Dante across the Styx (8.13–24). The Furies on the walls of Dis are named Megaera, Alecto and Tisiphone (9.37–48). The Centaurs (canto 12), the Harpies nesting in the wood of the suicides (13.10), Geryon (canto 17) and Briareus among the giants (31.98) are the monsters of Virgil's forecourt given work.

The plan. Virgil's suicides and his unhappy lovers become Dante's wood of suicides and the circle of the lustful, where Dido is named as the woman who killed herself for love and broke faith with Sychaeus' ashes (Inferno 5.61–62; deep dive module 12.4). Virgil's Elysium, with its own light, its green lawns and its poets and founders, lends Limbo its noble castle and meadow of fresh green (Inferno 4.106–111), and Aeneas is there with Hector (4.122). The difference is the doctrine. Virgil's Tartarus and Elysium are stations in a cycle of purgation and return; Dante's Hell is eternal and admits no return, and Anchises' cleansing by wind, water and fire is nearer to Dante's Purgatory, up whose mountain Virgil leads Dante in deep dive module 12.8.

10Self-test

  1. What two conditions does the Sibyl set before the descent, and what does each signify?
    Answer

    Aeneas must find the golden bough, Proserpina's gift, which comes away easily only for a man fate calls (6.136–148), and he must bury Misenus, whose corpse pollutes the fleet (6.149–152). The bough is the sign of election and the passport shown to Charon (6.405–409); the burial repeats Odysseus' duty to Elpenor (Odyssey 11.51–80).

  2. Why has the single word cunctantem (6.211) generated an argument, and who stands on each side?
    Answer

    The Sibyl promised that the bough would yield easily to the chosen, yet it hesitates. Servius argued against taking this as a sign that Aeneas is not chosen; Brooks (1953) and Segal read it as deliberate, a deathly gold that casts doubt on the book's promise.

  3. How does book 6 change the situation of Odyssey 11 at the root?
    Answer

    Odysseus calls the dead up to a trench of blood; Aeneas descends in his body with a guide along a mapped route. Homer's dead have little moral order, Virgil's are sorted by their deaths and lives; Tiresias foretells one man's homecoming, Anchises a people's history.

  4. Which groups of the dead does Aeneas pass across the river before the fork, and what Homeric catalogue does Virgil rewrite?
    Answer

    Infants, the falsely condemned whom Minos retries, suicides, the lovers in the fields of mourning and the famous warriors (6.426–493). The lovers' list rewrites the catalogue of heroines in Odyssey 11.225–332, three of whose women, Phaedra, Procris and Eriphyle, reappear.

  5. In what ways is Virgil's Tartarus Roman, and how does he avoid the viewpoint problem of Homer's sinners?
    Answer

    Beside the mythical rebels it punishes Roman crimes: cheating a client, for which Servius preserves the Twelve Tables' curse, following impious arms, selling one's country and laws for a price (6.608–623). Aeneas never enters; the Sibyl tells what Hecate showed her, where Odysseus implausibly sees Erebus from his trench.

  6. Summarise Anchises' doctrine and name a Platonic, a Pythagorean and a Stoic element in it.
    Answer

    A spirit and mind pervade the cosmos; souls are fiery seeds dulled by bodies, purged after death, and after a thousand years sent to Lethe to be reborn (6.724–751). Platonic: the world-soul of the Timaeus and Er's thousand years and river of forgetting; Pythagorean: rebirth; Stoic: pneuma, fire and the four passions (6.733).

  7. What is disputed about the "few" who hold the glad fields (6.743–747)?
    Answer

    Whether they stay in Elysium until they become pure fire, while the rest are reborn, as Williams and Austin take it, or whether Elysium is only a resting place and every soul returns, as Page held. The grammar allows both.

  8. Which arts does Anchises leave to others, and which does he give Rome?
    Answer

    Sculpture in bronze and marble, oratory and astronomy go to the Greeks; Rome's arts are to rule the peoples, set the custom of law on peace, spare the conquered and war down the proud (6.847–853). Cicero (On Duties 1.35) and Horace (Carmen Saeculare 51–52) state the same ideal of clemency.

  9. What are Virgil's models for the two gates, and what explanations of the exit by ivory have been offered?
    Answer

    Penelope's gates of dreams (Odyssey 19.560–567), already used by Plato (Charmides 173a). Explanations: Servius, everything told is false; Everett, followed by Norden, the hour, since false dreams come before midnight; Austin, the living are not true shades; and Otis, Boyle and Bartsch read the exit as a sign about the vision's truth.

  10. Give three things the Inferno takes from book 6.
    Answer

    Any three of: Aeneas as the precedent for a living visitor (Inferno 2.13–32); Charon with eyes of fire and the souls like falling leaves (3.109, 3.112–117); Minos as judge (5.4–12); Cerberus quietened by something thrown into his jaws (6.25–27); Phlegyas, the Furies by name, and the monsters of the forecourt (cantos 8, 9, 12, 13, 17, 31).

11Carry forward

  1. Book 6 turns Homer's visit to the dead into a moral and historical map: the dead are sorted by how they lived and died, Roman crimes and Roman virtues are judged on Greek sites, and Plato, the Pythagoreans and the Stoics supply the theory of the soul that makes a future visible before birth.
  2. The book frames its own authority with doubt: the hesitating bough, the elm of false dreams, the "few" of 6.744 and the ivory gate. Every reading of the parade's promise, Augustan or pessimistic, has to account for that frame.
  3. Anchises' charge defines Rome by rule, law on peace, clemency and war on the proud, and the rest of the poem tests its hero against it; later poets, from Lucan and Silius to Dante, took book 6 as the pattern of a living man's journey among the dead.
