01How to sit this exam

Closed book. Write every answer in full before opening its model answer; an answer you can produce only after reading the model is an answer you do not have. Allow about four and a quarter hours: two minutes a question in Part A, five in Parts B and C, twenty-five for each essay. The parts may be sat on different days, but sit each part in one sitting, in order. The model answers cite the reports by number, so that every weak answer leads back to the module that repairs it.

Scoring. Part A: two marks a question, one for the fact and one for the source or reason, 30 marks. Part B: five marks, awarded only when the answer names the mechanism linking the two items rather than describing them side by side, 50 marks. Part C: five marks, one for naming the source and its date, two for the interest it serves, two for the independent check that would confirm or undermine it, 50 marks. Part D: fourteen marks an essay, 70 marks. Total 200. Above 150 means the course has settled; between 100 and 150 means the connections are in place but the sources are not; below 100 means go back to modules 0.1 and 0.3 first, because a reader who does not date the source before the event will misread every later module.

Where sources disagree. Several model answers take a position on open points: whether the tophet's infants were sacrificed (Smith, Stager, Xella, Quinn) or died naturally (Moscati, Bénichou-Safar, Schwartz); whether the army became proletarian, as Gabba held, or never did, as Cadiou argues; whether Constantine's sign began as the solar halo of 310 that Weiss and Barnes propose or as the dream Lactantius sets on the eve of battle. Give yourself full marks for an answer that names the dispute and the evidence on each side even where it lands differently from the model. Give yourself nothing for an answer that states one side as settled fact.

02Part A: short answer (15)

  1. Varro's date for the foundation of Rome is 21 April 753 BC. Explain how such a date was produced, and give one piece of archaeological evidence that it marks no event.
    Model answer

    The day is the Parilia, a shepherds' festival that became Rome's birthday, and Tarutius cast Romulus' horoscope for Varro (Plutarch, Romulus 12). The year was computed. Dionysius (1.74) lists rival results, from Timaeus' 814/13 to Cincius' 729/8, because most writers, Timaeus and Ennius aside, seem to have counted the consular colleges back to the first consuls and added a royal span of about 240 years fixed in advance (module 0.3). The ground knows no foundation: graves in the Forum valley go back to about 1000 BC (module 1.1). Carandini's eighth-century Palatine wall matches the date, in Wiseman's verdict, by accident (module 1.4).

  2. What is the inscribed cippus beneath the Lapis Niger, what does it prove, and why did the late Republic pave the spot rather than clear it?
    Model answer

    A tufa pillar of about 570–550 BC, found by Giacomo Boni in 1899, inscribed boustrophedon with a sacred law (CIL I² 1): sakros esed, "let him be accursed"; recei, "to the king"; kalatorem, "the herald" (modules 0.1, 1.5). It proves that a rex with a herald acted in the Comitium before 509, in contemporary Latin, though it names no king of the tradition and recei may mean the rex sacrorum. The black paving marked a place of ill omen Romans could no longer explain; Festus records guesses at Romulus' tomb, Faustulus or Hostus Hostilius (module 1.4). Rome kept the spot because it could not read it (module 9.5).

  3. Why is the rex sacrorum the strongest evidence for an early Roman priest-king, and what does the office say about the Republic that kept it?
    Model answer

    Livy says the Republic created a priest to perform the kings' rites, subordinate to the pontifex maximus so that the name should not threaten liberty (2.2.1–2; modules 1.5, 1.6). He ranked first in Festus' order of priests, proclaimed each month's festivals on the Nones and fled the Comitium after his sacrifice (Plutarch, Roman Questions 63; module 2.3). So awkward an office is unlikely to be invented: the king's sacral duties were too important to drop. The Republic kept the title and stripped the power; in 180 BC Dolabella preferred a fine to taking the post (Livy 40.42). Liberty was defined against the name of king.

  4. Which Greek writer first brought Aeneas to Rome, and give two reasons a Trojan ancestor served Rome better than a Greek founder.
    Model answer

    Hellanicus of Lesbos, late in the fifth century, as Dionysius reports him (1.72.2): Aeneas came from the Molossians with Odysseus and named the city after Rhome, a Trojan woman; the ascription is disputed (module 1.3). Homer had saved Aeneas to rule the Trojans (Iliad 20.307–308). A Trojan put Rome inside the Homeric world, as old as any Greek city, without making it a colony owing a Greek mother city deference; and a son carrying father and gods matched pietas. Segesta in 263 and Flamininus, leader of the Aeneadae, used the kinship (Greek module 10.12 follows the Roman Troy). The cost was descent from the defeated.

  5. Livy reports an embassy to Athens in 454 BC to copy Solon's laws before the Twelve Tables were written. What is doubted, and what Greek influence on the Tables is better attested?
    Model answer

    The embassy (Livy 3.31.8, written some four centuries later) is widely doubted, and the Tables survive only in later quotation (module 3.1). Particular borrowings are better attested. Cicero says the funeral rules, no more than ten flute-players and no tearing of cheeks, were taken almost word for word from Solon (On the Laws 2.58–59, 2.64; module 2.4); Pomponius (Digest 1.2.2.4) and Pliny (Natural History 34.21) name Hermodorus of Ephesus as adviser; and Gaius matched a boundary rule to Solon's (Digest 10.1.13). Most think the models came through southern Italy. Greek module 5.5 gives Solon's reforms, Greek module 8.4 his funeral law.

  6. What three statuses did the settlement of 338 BC create, and which later events ended the system it began?
    Model answer

    Full citizenship in self-governing municipia (Lanuvium, Tusculum); citizenship without the vote, with legions and tax but no vote or office (Capua, Fundi); and Latin allies under treaty (Tibur, Praeneste), each tied to Rome alone, the other Latins forbidden intermarriage, trade and councils with one another (Livy 8.14; module 3.3). From Cales in 334 the Latin right came loose from Latium as a legal status (module 3.4). The Social War ended the ladder in Italy, through the lex Julia of 90 and the lex Plautia Papiria of 89 (module 5.2). In the provinces the Latin right survived as a step to citizenship (module 7.5) until the edict of 212 (module 7.4).

  7. What happened at the lectisternium of 217 BC, and what does the pairing of the gods reveal?
    Model answer

    After Trasimene the Sibylline Books prescribed a banquet for twelve gods on six couches (Livy 22.10.9): Jupiter and Juno, Neptune and Minerva, Mars and Venus, Apollo and Diana, Vulcan and Vesta, Mercury and Ceres (module 2.2). The pairs follow Greek myth, not Roman cult: spouses, the rivals for Attica, the lovers of Odyssey 8, the divine twins, the two fires; only Mercury and Ceres, trade and grain, look Roman. Consulting books of Greek oracles, the state seated its gods as Greek couples: the equation of Roman and Greek gods was a dated process, not a fact of nature, and here the state made it official. Greek module 2.2 gives the Olympians themselves.

  8. What does the bronze from Tiriolo show about the senate's aim in 186 BC that Livy's narrative of the Bacchanalia hides, and where does the same logic reappear?
    Model answer

    The consuls' letter relaying the decree (CIL I² 581) names no crime. Livy's sexual crimes, forgeries, poisonings and conspiracy (39.8–19), written some 170 years later as melodrama, are absent; the decree regulates structure: no male priest, no master, no common fund, no oaths, no secret rites, no more than five worshippers, all by licence of the urban praetor and the senate (module 2.5). As North argued, the target was an organisation outside the state's control, not Bacchus. Pliny in Bithynia executed Christians for obstinacy and released those who sacrificed to the gods and Trajan's image (Letters 10.96): allegiance tested by a rite, not belief examined.

  9. According to Polybius (6.53–54), what did the aristocratic funeral do for Rome, and what did the same institution do to the historical record?
    Model answer

    The dead noble was carried to the Rostra and a son praised his deeds, while men of matching build wore the wax masks of ancestors who had held office, in the robes of each one's highest office. No finer sight, Polybius says, could meet a young man who loves glory (modules 2.4, 4.5). The same eulogies corrupted the record: Cicero complains of false triumphs, too many consulships and false genealogies (Brutus 62), and Livy of a record corrupted by funeral praises (8.40.4–5; module 0.1). The 306 Fabii and the Valerii who carry the appeal law three times are family stories (modules 3.1, 3.2). The school of ambition was also a workshop of fiction.

  10. What was Bibulus doing when he "watched the sky" in 59 BC, and why did it matter to Caesar's position ten years later?
    Model answer

    He announced by edict that he was observing the heavens on every assembly day (Suetonius, Julius 20.1), a form of obnuntiatio, by which a magistrate's report of adverse signs halted public business (modules 2.1, 5.4). Caesar legislated regardless, so every law of 59, Clodius' adoption included, was open to the charge of invalidity, which Cicero pressed (De domo sua 39–40). With Cato sworn to prosecute him once he gave up his army (Julius 30.3), Caesar could not safely become a private citizen: he needed to pass straight from his command to a second consulship, and the civil war began over whether he could be made to stand down first.

  11. Which clause of the peace of 201 BC led to the destruction of Carthage in 146, and how?
    Model answer

    Carthage was forbidden to make war outside Africa, or inside it without Rome's consent (Polybius 15.18; Livy 30.37; module 4.3). For fifty years Masinissa of Numidia exploited the clause while Roman arbitrators ruled for him or not at all, and in 150 Carthage fought him without leave and lost (module 4.4). Cato closed every speech with the opinion that Carthage must not exist (Plutarch, Cato Maior 26–27). In 149 Carthage surrendered by deditio; the consuls demanded hostages, then all arms, then removal of the city about fifteen kilometres from the sea (Appian, Punica 76–81). It refused, and fell to Scipio Aemilianus in 146.

  12. What is the lex Irnitana, and how did it turn the leading men of a small Spanish town into Romans?
    Model answer

    Six of the ten bronze tablets of the charter of the municipium Flavium Irnitanum, found near El Saucejo in 1981: a copy of a standard Flavian municipal statute, issued after Vespasian gave the Latin right to all Spain (Pliny, Natural History 3.30; module 7.5). It sets out council, elections, magistrates and finances, and makes the town's courts follow the governor's edict (ch. 85), itself modelled on the praetor's (module 9.1). Chapter 21 gives Roman citizenship to those who have held a magistracy, with their parents, wives and children. The Latin right, born as a status for Latium's towns in 338 (module 3.3), had become a ladder: local office made Roman citizens.

  13. Why is the Verona palimpsest of Gaius so important, and why must Justinian's Digest be read with care as evidence for the classical jurists?
    Model answer

    Niebuhr found it at Verona in 1816, a manuscript of Jerome's letters written over an erased legal text, and Savigny identified Gaius' Institutes of about 161 (module 9.1). It is the one classical textbook to survive nearly whole and unrevised, and its plan of persons, things and actions (1.8) passed through Justinian's Institutes into the French Civil Code of 1804. The Digest of 533 holds about 9,000 extracts from thirty-nine jurists, but Deo auctore licensed Tribonian to cut and alter (modules 8.6, 9.1). Gradenwitz and Beseler found interpolations everywhere; since Wieacker and Kaser most judge them fewer and lighter. Every "Roman" rule has passed through a compiler's hands.

  14. What did formal manumission give a Roman slave and his son, and why did Philip V hold the practice up to Larisa?
    Model answer

    A slave formally freed by a Roman citizen became a citizen, took his patron's first and family names and owed him respect and often agreed days of work; he could not hold office at Rome, but his freeborn son was a full citizen, as Horace, a freedman's son, became a military tribune (Satires 1.6.45–48; module 9.3). Urging Larisa in 214 to enlarge its citizen body, Philip V cited the Romans, who enrolled freed slaves and had sent colonies to nearly seventy places (Syll.³ 543; modules 3.4, 9.3); Athens had confined citizenship to children of two Athenians in 451/0 (Greek module 6.2). Manumission was also a means of control, as Bradley read it, and after the revolts of about 135 to 71 it offered the able a way out (module 9.3). Rome grew its citizen body from its slaves as well as its allies.

  15. What did the legend of Silvester and the Donation of Constantine claim, and how did Lorenzo Valla expose the Donation?
    Model answer

    The Actus Silvestri, a Roman legend of the later fifth century, made Constantine a persecutor struck with leprosy and healed by baptism at Pope Silvester's hands, against Eusebius' account of a deathbed baptism near Nicomedia in 337 (Life of Constantine 4.61–62; modules 8.3, 10.4). The Donation, usually placed in the later eighth century, built on it a grant to the pope of the Lateran, the imperial insignia, and Rome with Italy and the West. In 1440 Valla, secretary to Alfonso of Aragon, then at war with Pope Eugenius IV, showed that no fourth-century emperor wrote eighth-century Latin or spoke of satraps. The critic had an interest; his philology was still right.

03Part B: connections (10)

  1. Explain the connection between the Gracchan crisis of 133–121 BC and Livy's account of the Struggle of the Orders.
    Model answer

    Livy wrote books 2 to 6 in the 20s BC, naming Fabius Pictor rarely and chiefly annalists who wrote after 133: Calpurnius Piso amid the Gracchan crisis, Valerius Antias, Claudius Quadrigarius, Licinius Macer and Aelius Tubero after Sulla had curbed the tribunate (module 3.1). Their early Republic looks like their own. Spurius Cassius' land bill of 486 includes Latins and Hernici, like Gaius Gracchus' programme for the allies; the 500-iugera limit of 367 is Tiberius' figure; in Piso's version Servilius Ahala kills Maelius as a private man at the senate's bidding, a precedent for Scipio Nasica in Piso's own consulship of 133 (Dionysius 12.4.2–5; module 3.2); Camillus' temple of Concord echoes Opimius' after 121. Macer, a tribune of 73 fighting to restore the tribunate, gave Licinii the first plebeian honours. Yet the traffic runs both ways. Dionysius found the private killing in Cincius too, if he is the annalist of about 200 (module 3.2). The tribunate the Gracchi used was a real fifth-century creation, sacrosanct by the plebs' oath, and Cato in 167 already knew a 500-iugera penalty (Gellius 6.3.37). The skeleton is old; the flesh is Gracchan.

  2. Explain the connection between the legend of Lucretia and Brutus and the assassination of Julius Caesar.
    Model answer

    Livy's 509, written some five centuries later, defines liberty as annual office rather than any reduction of royal power (2.1.7), an inviolable citizen household and the state above family: Brutus swears on Lucretia's blood to suffer no king at Rome, then watches his own sons beheaded (1.59, 2.5; module 1.6). By 44 the story was political property. Marcus Brutus claimed descent from the liberator, though Dionysius gives the first Brutus no surviving children, and in 54 put the first Brutus and Ahala on a denarius (module 3.2). Caesar's honours read as the tyrant's: a statue among the kings, a diadem refused at the Lupercalia, "Caesar, not Rex" (Suetonius, Julius 76–79; module 5.4). The conspirators acted out the legend, and later readers kept choosing sides: David painted Brutus' executed sons in 1789, Dante put Lucius Brutus in Limbo and Marcus beside Judas (module 10.4). The myth told Rome what a tyrant looked like, and Caesar was cast in the part.

  3. Explain the connection between the plebeian tribunate created in 494 BC and the tribunician power of Augustus.
    Model answer

    The tribunate was a revolutionary creation: the seceding plebs made its own officers sacrosanct by a collective oath and armed them with help against the consuls, then the veto and the plebiscite, binding on all citizens from 287 (module 3.1). Mostly an instrument of the nobility thereafter, though tribunes jailed the consuls of 151 over the levy (deep dive module 11.1), it became a weapon again when Tiberius Gracchus legislated through the plebeian council over the Senate's head in 133 and had a vetoing colleague deposed (module 5.1). Sulla left it the power to help without the power to harm (Cicero, On the Laws 3.22; module 5.2); restored in 70, it reopened the road to power through the assemblies, as Gabinius' law for Pompey showed in 67 (module 5.3). In 23 BC Augustus resigned the consulship and took tribunician power for life without the office, numbering his years by it (Res Gestae 4.4); Tacitus calls it the title for supreme power devised to avoid the names of king and dictator (Annals 3.56; module 6.1). The office invented to protect the plebs from magistrates became the monarch's republican mask.

  4. Explain the connection between Rome's alliance system in Italy and the failure of Hannibal's invasion.
    Model answer

    Rome turned defeated enemies into allies who owed soldiers, not tribute, registered on the formula togatorum (module 3.4). Polybius' lists for 225, probably from Fabius Pictor, give more than 700,000 foot and about 70,000 horse able to bear arms, three-fifths of them allied, the Samnites alone 77,000 (2.24). Hannibal reached Italy with 20,000 infantry and at most 6,000 horse by his own tablet (Polybius 3.56.4); he could destroy armies but not the lists, so he released Italian prisoners and claimed to fight for Italy's freedom (3.77, 3.85; module 4.3). After Cannae much of the south and Capua went over, but the Latin colonies held: in 209 twelve pleaded exhaustion, none defected, and eighteen declared men ready according to the formula (Livy 27.9–10). Rome kept the rule Appius Claudius had laid down against Pyrrhus, no negotiation while an invader stood in Italy (module 3.5). The system's fault line, allies without citizenship, opened in 91 (module 5.2): what beat Hannibal was what the Social War broke.

  5. Explain the connection between Polybius' analysis of the Roman constitution in book 6 and the Greek political theory he brought to it.
    Model answer

    Polybius, an Achaean statesman deported in 167 and the friend of Scipio Aemilianus, asked a Greek question: how Rome conquered almost the whole inhabited world in under fifty-three years (1.1.5; module 4.5). His answer was Greek theory. Kingship, aristocracy and democracy decay by nature into tyranny, oligarchy and mob rule, a cycle only a mixture halts; Lycurgus designed Sparta's by reasoning, the Romans reached theirs through struggle and disaster (6.3–10). So consuls look like kings, the Senate like an aristocracy and the people like a democracy, each checking the others (6.11–18). The frame fits badly: Polybius predicts Rome's own slide (6.57) and lists powers while omitting the nobility, patronage and an assembly weighted by wealth, the broadened aristocracy module 3.1 shows emerging from the Struggle of the Orders. Yet the modern argument still starts from book 6: Millar took its democratic element at its word, Hölkeskamp and Mouritsen answered that assemblies voted only on what magistrates proposed. Greek module 5.4 gives the Sparta he measured Rome against.

  6. Explain the connection between Hellenistic ruler cult and the worship of Augustus.
    Model answer

    Greek cities had long given kings cult for benefactions, as Athens received Antigonus and Demetrius as Saviour Gods in 307 (Greek module 7.1), and Smyrna gave the goddess Roma a temple in 195 BC (Tacitus, Annals 4.56). Augustus borrowed the idiom with care: Alexander's head on his seal (Suetonius, Augustus 50), and a tale that Apollo as a serpent fathered him, answering the serpent of Olympias (Greek module 11.4). What he added was gradation (module 6.2). In the East, temples with Roma at Pergamum and Nicomedia (Dio 51.20.6–7); at Rome no temple, but the household cult of module 2.4 enlarged, freedmen magistri honouring the Lares Augusti and his Genius at the crossroads; and divinity only at death, a senator swearing he saw him ascend as Proculus had seen Romulus. The line leaked, as the temple at Pola shows, and emperors greeted as lord and god in Rome itself, Domitian or, in Aurelius Victor's account, Diocletian (module 8.2), dropped the gradation Augustus had kept.

  7. Explain the connection between the Etruscan disciplina and the Roman state from the Republic to AD 408.
    Model answer

    The Etruscans had a revealed religion: Tages, risen from a furrow at Tarquinia, chanted the discipline, and its books of entrails, lightning and rites were read by trained haruspices (Cicero, On Divination 1.72, 2.50; module 1.2). Rome imported the experts, not the theology. The Senate summoned haruspices to read prodigies, part of the expiation procedure of module 2.1. But Roman augural law decided: in 163 the consul Gracchus called them Etruscan barbarians, then found the fault in his own augural books (module 2.3). The experts outlived the Etruscan language, whose inscriptions end in the early Empire, by four centuries. A haruspex declared the comet of 44 the end of the ninth saeculum; Claudius founded a college in AD 47 lest the oldest discipline of Italy lapse (Tacitus, Annals 11.15); Constantine banned private haruspicy but kept the consultation when lightning struck the palace (Theodosian Code 9.16.1, 16.10.1; module 8.3); and in 408 the prefect Pompeianus let Tuscan diviners offer to save Rome from Alaric (Zosimus 5.41; module 8.5).

  8. Explain the connection between the fathers who execute their sons in Roman legend and the demography of the Roman family.
    Model answer

    The legends make paternal power absolute: Brutus watches his sons beheaded in 509 (Livy 2.5), Torquatus executes his victorious son in 340 (8.7), and one version has Spurius Cassius scourged to death by his father (2.41; modules 1.6, 3.2). The law agrees on paper: patria potestas lasted until the father died, and a son in power owned nothing beyond a revocable peculium (Gaius, Institutes 1.55; module 9.3). But the cases are famous because rare, and Brutus and Torquatus acted as consuls as well as fathers (module 9.3). Custom expected a council of kin (Seneca, On Mercy 1.15), and Hadrian deported a father who killed his son on a hunt (Marcian, Digest 48.9.5). Demography did most. With men marrying near thirty and women in their late teens (Shaw, Saller) and life expectancy at birth about twenty-five in Frier's model, Saller's simulations leave few adult sons with living fathers: most men were free of power before they married. The exemplum preserved a severity few households ever had the chance to exercise.

  9. Explain the connection between army pay and the late Roman tax system.
    Model answer

    Augustus made the army permanent and salaried, its discharge bounties paid from taxes on citizens through the military treasury of AD 6 (module 6.4). Pay stood at 225 denarii, then 300 under Domitian; Severus and Caracalla raised it again, and Macrinus put the cost of Caracalla's rise at 280 million sesterces a year (Dio 79(78).36.3; module 7.4). Each rise drove new taxes and debasement: Caracalla's antoninianus of 215 held about half silver and only a few per cent by the late 260s (module 8.1). The state turned to feeding its soldiers by requisition, and Diocletian made requisition a regular tax in kind, the annona, assessed on units of land and heads of people with published rates (P.Cair.Isid. 1, of 297; module 8.2). Its reassessments became the indiction, by which Constantinople dated documents to the end (module 0.3). The question Augustus disguised, who pays the soldiers, produced the fiscal machinery of the late empire and even its calendar.

  10. Explain the connection between Virgil's prophecies of Rome and the Forum of Augustus.
    Model answer

    The Aeneid's characteristic device is prophecy: Roman history seen forward from the mythic past as fate, every line ending at Augustus. Jupiter promises empire without end and a Trojan Caesar, Julius or Augustus, and the gates of war shut (1.257–296); Anchises shows Augustus beside Romulus as refounder of the golden age (6.791–805); the shield ends in the triple triumph of 29 (8.714–728; module 6.3). The Forum of Augustus, dedicated in 2 BC, set the same history in stone (module 6.2). Aeneas carrying Anchises, with the Alban kings and the Julii, faced Romulus with the spolia opima and the Republic's great men, each above an inscribed summary of deeds; Augustus' edict made them the standard for himself and his successors (Suetonius, Augustus 31.5), and his own chariot stood in the square. The legends of modules 1.3 and 1.4, Trojan descent and the founder's spoils, became the regime's argument. The poets made the Augustan myth in words; the regime quoted it back in marble.

04Part C: sources and their bias (10)

  1. "Rome was a Greek city, founded by successive waves of Greeks; even the Trojans descended from the Arcadian Dardanus." Who says this and why should you doubt it?
    Model answer

    Dionysius of Halicarnassus, who came to Rome in 30 BC and published his Roman Antiquities from 7 BC (1.5.1, 1.61–62, 1.89–90; modules 0.1, 1.3). Doubt it because the thesis had a purpose: to reconcile Greek readers to Roman rule by making their masters kin. The same purpose shaped his other verdicts. He rejected the Lydian origin of the Etruscans on sound linguistic grounds, but native Etruscan neighbours also made a Greek Rome easier to argue (1.28–30; module 1.2). He stands in the long tradition of Greek genealogy for foreign peoples, from Hellanicus to Cato's Achaean Aborigines (1.11.1), and his extra detail beside Livy is usually extra rhetoric. The check is the ground and the language: Latial culture develops in place from about 1000 BC, Latin is an Italic language, and Rome's culture was hybrid, Latin, Etruscan and Greek, from the start (module 1.1). Evander's Palatine is an etymology, Pallantion made Palatium (Livy 1.5.1).

  2. "Camillus arrived with an army as the Romans were weighing out the gold, declared that Rome would be recovered with iron, not gold, and routed the Gauls in the city." Who says this and why should you doubt it?
    Model answer

    Livy (5.49.1–6), writing some 360 years after the sack; Plutarch's Camillus follows the same tradition (module 3.3). Doubt it because the competing versions are sparer, and the oldest name no Camillus. Polybius has the Gauls make a treaty and leave after seven months because the Veneti threatened their land (2.18.3, 2.22.5); Diodorus has the gold paid and recovered later at Veascium (14.116–117); Strabo credits the Caeretans, and Suetonius a Livius Drusus who brought the gold back from Gaul. The Greeks who heard of the sack within a generation or two, Heraclides, Aristotle and Theopompus, name no Camillus, and Aristotle's saviour is a Lucius (Plutarch, Camillus 22; Pliny, Natural History 3.57). Doubt it too because of when Livy wrote: his Camillus is hailed as a Romulus, father of his country and second founder (5.49.7) in the years when some proposed calling Octavian Romulus (Suetonius, Augustus 7.2), and the pious, exiled, temple-restoring hero has an Augustan profile. Mommsen judged the rescue patriotic growth. The check is the barest version, and in it the gold is paid.

  3. "The Senate never sanctioned the war of 264; the people, worn out by wars, voted to help Messana because the commanders promised them plunder." Who says this and why should you doubt it?
    Model answer

    Polybius (1.11.1–3), writing after 167, a century later, and probably following Fabius Pictor, the senator who wrote in Greek about 200 and whom Polybius himself calls a partisan who saw wisdom and courage only in Romans (1.14; modules 4.2, 0.1). Doubt it because the version clears the Senate, Fabius' own order, of starting Rome's first war overseas. Harris read it as an alibi for an aristocracy that lived on military glory; Hoyos argued that Rome voted to help the Mamertines against Hiero, not for war with Carthage. The other side's partisan, Philinus of Acragas, claimed that Rome broke a treaty barring it from Sicily, which Polybius denies (3.26). The check is the next war vote known in detail. In 200 the centuriate assembly almost unanimously refused war on Philip until the consul, on the Senate's instruction, told it the choice was Macedonia or Italy (Livy 31.6–8; module 4.4): there the people had to be persuaded and the Senate wanted the war. Rome's aims in 264 then grew with success, from Messana to Sicily to Africa.

  4. "In 310, with Agathocles in Africa, the Carthaginians sacrificed two hundred children of their noblest families to Kronos, rolling them from the hands of a bronze statue into the fire." Who says this and why should you doubt it?
    Model answer

    Diodorus (20.14.4–6), writing nearly three centuries later from a Greek tradition hostile to Carthage; Plutarch adds the mother standing by without tears while flutes and drums drowned the cries (On Superstition 13; module 4.1). Doubt it because Carthage is known almost entirely through its enemies, whose stereotype of Punic faith made every Carthaginian vice credible, and because the statue and the round numbers belong to a story. But here the check partly vindicates the hostile source. The tophet at Salammbô held thousands of urns of burnt infants and young animals under stelae to Baal Hammon and Tanit recording vows the god heard, and the offering is called mlk, a sacrificial term. Moscati, Fantar and Bénichou-Safar read a cemetery for infants who died naturally, and Schwartz found many prenatal or newborn; Smith, Stager and colleagues found ages clustered at one to two months, and Xella, Quinn and others set the vow formulae beside the texts. Opinion now leans to sacrifice, its scale disputed: doubt Diodorus' scene, not the practice.

  5. "The rich took the public land, added their poor neighbours' plots by purchase or force and worked it with slaves, while the free population of Italy dwindled; crossing Etruria, Tiberius Gracchus found it almost empty of free men." Who says this and why should you doubt it?
    Model answer

    Appian (Civil Wars 1.7, about AD 160) and Plutarch (Tiberius Gracchus 8, early second century), whose Etruria comes from a pamphlet by Gaius Gracchus (8.7; module 5.1). Doubt it because it is Gracchan advocacy relayed by imperial Greek writers two and a half centuries and more later. Rosenstein argued that the levy fell on late-marrying young men whose families could spare them, so service did not itself ruin farms, and that the crisis came from population growth and partible inheritance; de Ligt likewise traced rural misery to population growth rather than slaves displacing citizens. The checks are the census and the ground. The totals in Livy's epitomes barely move, 317,933 in 136/135 and 318,823 in 131/130, then jump to 394,736 in 125/124, which may reflect allottees or a corrected count of evaders; and field surveys find small rural sites persisting through the second century, though Rathbone asks how visible the poor are to survey. The poverty was real; its cause is open.

  6. "Catiline was a monster plotting the murder of the consul and the ruin of the city, and the consul alone, by his vigilance, saved Rome." Who says this and why should you doubt it?
    Model answer

    Cicero, in the four speeches against Catiline of November and December 63, which he published in 60 among his consular orations, when the executions had become a liability; how far he revised them is uncertain (Letters to Atticus 2.1.3; module 5.3). Doubt it because it is the consul's own case, made by the one interested witness who shaped the whole tradition of 63, and because executing citizens without trial, under the final decree and against Gaius Gracchus' law, needed a monster to justify it: Cicero argued that a public enemy is no citizen (Against Catiline 4.10). The check is Sallust's Catiline, written about 42–41: it gives Catiline a voice, treats the plot as a symptom of a society corrupted by wealth after 146 and gives the First Catilinarian one sentence (31.6; module 5.6). Sallust had his own thesis and his own past as a Caesarian governor, so he is a second witness, not a neutral one; where the two agree, on outline and dates, the story stands.

  7. "In 28 and 27 BC I transferred the res publica from my own power to the judgement of the Senate and people; after that I had no more power than my colleagues in each magistracy." Who says this and why should you doubt it?
    Model answer

    Augustus, in the Res Gestae (34.1, 34.3), finished in his seventy-sixth year and set up in bronze before his Mausoleum (module 6.1). Doubt it because the Res Gestae lies by arrangement and silence rather than by statement, and the quotation leaves out what Augustus did claim: after 27 he excelled all in auctoritas, influence, though no more in potestas (34.3). True of each office, the claim is false of the whole: no colleague held at once a consulship, the provinces where most of the legions stood, and later tribunician power and greater imperium. Dio saw that under pretence of taking the burden he left the senators unarmed and kept the soldiers (53.12.3), and Tacitus that he drew to himself the functions of Senate, magistrates and laws while the boldest were dead (Annals 1.2). Judge argued that res publica meant the commonwealth, so that what was restored was order and legality, not a constitution, a reading the aureus of 28, published in 1999, fits. The check is who paid and commanded the legions: his own legates in his provinces, bounties from his military treasury (module 6.4), and no triumph outside his family after 19 BC.

  8. "Nature gives whatever son it pleases; adoption lets the ruler choose the best man. Rome had been one family's inheritance; that we have begun to be chosen will stand in place of liberty." Who says this and why should you doubt it?
    Model answer

    Dio gives the first thought to Hadrian adopting Antoninus (69.20) and Tacitus the second to Galba in 69 (Histories 1.16.1); Pliny's Panegyricus of 100 made it the regime's doctrine, that he who is to rule all should be chosen from all (7.4–6; module 7.3), and Machiavelli made the five adopted emperors the good ones (Discourses 1.10). Doubt it because adoption was the expedient of childless men dressed as a principle. Nerva adopted Trajan after the praetorians had besieged his palace, borrowing an army (Dio 68.3); Hadrian was the son of Trajan's cousin and husband of his great-niece, and Dio, citing his father, governor of Cilicia, says Plotina hid Trajan's death until an adoption could be announced (69.1); every choice followed blood or marriage. Pliny's speech praises Trajan by blackening Domitian, whom Pliny had served to the end (module 7.2). The check is the family tree: Marcus, the first emperor of the line with a surviving son, made Commodus his colleague, and the doctrine ended at its first test.

  9. "At midday, on the march, Constantine and his whole army saw a cross of light above the sun with the words 'by this conquer'." Who says this and why should you doubt it?
    Model answer

    Eusebius, Life of Constantine 1.27–32, left unfinished at his death in 339, from an account the emperor gave him under oath long afterwards (1.28.1; module 8.3). Doubt it because the accounts change with their dates. The panegyrist of 310 has Constantine see Apollo with Victory offering wreaths at a Gallic temple (Pan. Lat. VI(7).21.3–5); Lactantius, On the Deaths of the Persecutors, about 315, has a dream on the night before the Milvian Bridge and a monogram painted on shields (44.5–6); Eusebius' own Church History, written nearer the event, mentions a statue holding the saving sign but no vision. The later the account, the more Christian and the more shaped it is. Weiss argued that all three go back to a solar halo seen in Gaul in 310, read first as Apollo's and retold after 312, and Barnes adopted the thesis. The check is the contemporary material: the Arch of 315 credits an unnamed divinity (CIL VI 1139), and Sol stayed the emperor's companion on the coins into the 320s.

  10. "The people that once gave out commands, fasces and legions now longs anxiously for two things only, bread and circuses." Who says this and why should you doubt it?
    Model answer

    Juvenal (10.77–81), writing between about 110 and the late 120s, in a satire on vain wishes set beside the fall of Sejanus; Fronto made it a maxim of government (modules 9.4, 10.2). Doubt it because it is the view from the front rows, by a satirist who attacks only the dead and whose angry speaker most now read as a persona (Braund). The dole was grain, not bread: five modii a month for a closed list of about 200,000 male citizens, a privilege of citizens, not poor relief. The elite watched the shows too, seated by rank, and the crowd spoke, demanding cheaper grain and the fall of ministers, as with Cleander in 190 (module 7.3), while Cicero counted the games among the places the people's judgement was most clearly expressed (Pro Sestio 106). The check is a town without a dole: Pompeii, where local magnates paid for shows and baths and the electoral notices of fullers and fruit-sellers show the politics Juvenal thought Rome had lost.

05Part D: essays (5)

Where sources disagree. None of the essays has a single right answer. On the fourth, Heather (The Fall of the Roman Empire, 2005) makes the Huns the shock that started the chain, while Halsall (Barbarian Migrations and the Roman West, 2007) has the West's own civil wars draw the barbarian armies in; on the fifth, de Ste. Croix held that Christians died for the name and the refusal of the gods it stood for, Sherwin-White for defying a governor's order. An essay that argues one side and shows it knows the other earns the marks; an essay that balances without deciding earns half.
  1. "Rome had gods without stories and borrowed its myths from Greece." Discuss, with reference to at least three Roman legends and the periods that wrote them down.
    Model answer

    The claim is ancient before it is modern. Dionysius praised Romulus for rejecting the Greek myths of castrated and warring gods (2.18–19), and Rose and Latte made it a thesis of cult without narrative (modules 0.1, 2.1). Varro explains the impression: he filed myth under the poets, so anyone seeking it in civil cult found none (Augustine, City of God 6.5). Much was borrowed. Greeks wrote Rome's prehistory before any Roman did, and the gods acquired families and stories through their Greek doubles, sealed when the state seated twelve of them as Greek couples in 217 (modules 1.3, 2.2).

    Yet the legends show a people that told its myths as history. A Greek writer named Rhomos and Rhomylos about 300 BC (module 1.3); Romulus and Remus reached Roman prose about 200 BC, five and a half centuries after Varro's date, and never settled: Ennius puts Romulus on the Aventine, Promathion makes the twins sons of a slave girl and a phallus from the hearth, and Wiseman counts sixty-one versions (module 1.4). Lucretia, written five centuries after 509 in the shape of Herodotus' tyrant, defines liberty, and Verginia repeats her for 449 (modules 1.6, 3.1). Cassius, Maelius and Manlius resemble the Gracchi, and Torquatus was cited in 63 (module 3.2). And myth was fixed to places: the Lacus Curtius has three founders, and the Lupercal, the hut of Romulus and Juturna's spring were kept to prove their stories (modules 0.1, 0.2).

    The thesis therefore needs inverting. Rome had myths and told them as history, dated by consuls, fixed to a cognomen, a rite, a place or a family, and judged as models or warnings. Dumézil thought the gods' stories had moved into the city's past, Wiseman that they were staged at the games and lost with the drama, Feeney that the question assumes a Greek definition (module 2.1). The consequence is the course's first rule of method: each legend is evidence first for the age that told it, the lone Fabian survivor for Fabius Pictor's family, Camillus for Augustan Rome, Spurius Cassius for the Gracchan age. The gods without Greek doubles, Janus, Terminus, Fides, guard procedures rather than plots; the stories went to the people.

  2. "From Marius to Odoacer, whoever paid the soldiers held the state." Discuss.
    Model answer

    In the middle Republic the army was the citizen body under arms, levied each year by the census, sworn to a consul for a campaign and paid little more than its keep (Polybius 6.19–21, 6.39; module 9.2). It became political by degrees. Long wars in Spain and a falling property minimum lengthened service, though Rich and Cadiou doubt that Marius made the army proletarian (module 5.2). The decisive moves were political: a plebiscite gave Marius the Numidian command in 107, a tribune's law transferred Sulla's to Marius in 88, and Sulla told his legions at Nola that the eastern plunder would go to others; they marched. Pompey's commands and Octavian's private army, recruited at 500 denarii a man, followed the precedent (modules 5.3, 5.5).

    Augustus made the army permanent, salaried and sworn to himself, paid its bounties from taxes on citizens through the military treasury, and kept the legions in provinces under his legates (modules 6.1, 6.4). The Senate voted the powers; the soldiers chose the man. The guard made Claudius in its camp for a donative (module 7.1); in 69 emperors were made by armies elsewhere than at Rome (module 7.2). Severus told his sons to enrich the soldiers and scorn all other men, and the pay rises drove taxes and debasement (module 7.4). From 235 the emperor was whoever an army could make and keep (module 8.1).

    In the West the thesis holds to the end. As provinces fell, above all Africa in 439, the revenue that paid the army shrank, and the government depended on barbarian forces and on generals, Stilicho and Aetius who governed for emperors and Ricimer who made and unmade them; in 476 the troops in Italy demanded a third of its land, Orestes refused, and they made Odoacer their ruler (Procopius, Wars 5.1.4–8; module 8.5). Two qualifications. Paying was not enough: armies were loyal to dynasties, which broke the Tetrarchy within eighteen months of the abdication (module 8.2), and legitimacy still ran through the Senate's vote, the family and, from Constantine, the Christian God (module 8.3). And the East shows the alternative: a court that kept its tax base, killed Aspar and exported Theoderic's Goths to Italy kept the state (module 8.6). The soldiers held the state wherever the civil power could no longer pay them or command their generals.

  3. "Captive Greece took her savage conqueror captive" (Horace, Epistles 2.1.156). How far was Roman culture Greek? Answer with reference to religion, literature and art, and to the Greek course.
    Model answer

    Horace's line is narrower than its fame: in context it argues about literary taste against admirers of the old Latin poets, with a vague chronology (module 4.5). The borrowing began long before the conquest of Greece. Euboean cups reached Veii and Rome's river harbour in the eighth century, Latin took its letters through Etruria with signs of direct Greek contact (module 1.1), and Greeks wrote Rome's prehistory first (module 1.3). Religion shows two layers, an Italic cult of priest, day and place under a Greek identity of image, family and myth (module 2.2). Apollo came unrenamed, and in 217 the state seated its twelve as Greek couples; yet Janus, Terminus and Fides had no doubles, and Rome put the auspices in its magistrates' hands where Greek priests belonged to single sanctuaries (Greek module 8.1).

    Literature began as deliberate translation. In 240 Livius Andronicus staged a play and turned the Odyssey's Muse into a Camena; Ennius took Homer's hexameter and claimed to be Homer reborn; Feeney stresses that no other ancient people set out to translate Greek literature (module 4.5). Cicero coined the Latin for Greek philosophy (module 5.6), Virgil answered Homer from the Trojan side, and Ovid taught Europe Greek myth under Latin names, changing frames and voices more than plots (module 10.1; Greek module 3.7). Art spoke in borrowed parts recombined: an Etruscan podium under Greek columns at the temple of Portunus, Greek bodies with Roman heads, Greek types chosen for a room (module 10.3).

    What was Roman lay in function and institution. Historical relief told what Romans had done where Greek public sculpture told myths; concrete made interior space the subject of architecture (module 10.3); the exemplum did the work of myth in historical dress (module 3.2); law grew from Rome's own pontiffs and praetors, Solon's influence confined to particulars (modules 3.1, 9.1). Above all Rome extended citizenship by grades and made freed slaves citizens, where Athens closed its citizen body in 451/0 (module 3.4; Greek module 6.2). Romans defined themselves against Greece with Greek tools, as Gruen reads Cato's invective. In the end the capture ran both ways. The Greek East became Roman while staying Greek (module 7.5), and for a thousand years after 476 the Roman name was carried by Greek speakers who called themselves Rhomaioi (module 8.6).

  4. The western empire fell and the eastern did not. Explain the difference, and say what kind of event the fall of the West was.
    Model answer

    First, what fell. In 476 Odoacer deposed a boy emperor and sent the regalia to Constantinople; western contemporaries barely noticed, and the date became an ending some fifty years later in Marcellinus Comes, writing at Constantinople, a view Croke tied to Justinian's claim on Italy (module 0.3). The eastern state kept Roman law, the Roman name and an unbroken succession to 1453 (module 8.6), and its survival is the control case. A. H. M. Jones argued that the East had the same institutions, taxes, Church and army, so internal weakness, Gibbon's immoderate greatness or his clergy preaching patience and pusillanimity (module 8.5), cannot alone explain the West.

    The difference was money, geography and the control of generals. Each lost western province shrank the taxes that paid the army. Africa, taken by Gaiseric in 439, fed Rome and paid much of the budget; the armada of 468, which Procopius says cost 130,000 pounds of gold, burned off Cape Bon (module 8.5). The West had always governed through cities and local elites with little machinery behind them (module 7.5), and its armies became barbarian peoples settled by treaty, often drawn in by Roman civil wars, while generals from Stilicho to Ricimer ruled in the emperors' names, and Ricimer made and unmade them. The East had the richer provinces, the straits and the Theodosian Walls, a Persian neighbour that kept treaties, and a court that massacred Gainas' men, killed Aspar and sent Theoderic to Italy (module 8.6).

    What kind of event? Brown and Goffart stressed transformation; Heather and Ward-Perkins restored violence and material collapse, the vanishing of fine tableware, tiles, coin and everyday literacy; Wickham traced the simplification to the end of the tax system, and Koepke and Baten found heights rising in the fifth and sixth centuries (module 8.5). The camps answer different questions. The western state fell, as a process, not on a date; the economy it sustained fell unevenly, worst in Britain; law, Latin, the Church and Roman identity passed to the kingdoms and the East. The East's later history confirms the analysis: each time it lost its tax base, in the seventh century, after Manzikert, after 1204, it shrank to what its capital could defend (module 8.6). Follow the money and the army, not the battles.

  5. "Rome tolerated every god and punished only disloyalty." Discuss, with reference to the treatment of new and foreign cults from the Bacchanalia to Theodosius.
    Model answer

    For the Republic the claim is nearly right. Roman polytheism was absorptive: gods were called out of enemy cities, as Juno Regina from Veii in 396, invited through the Sibylline Books, as Aesculapius in 293 and the Magna Mater in 204, or tolerated as a people's ancestral worship, as Judaism was through grants to particular communities (module 2.5). Newness was never the offence, and the senate decided. What it punished was organisation outside its control: the Tiriolo decree of 186 names no crime but forbids male priests, masters, common funds and oaths; Pliny executed Christians for obstinacy and released those who sacrificed (Letters 10.96–97). De Ste. Croix held that the name itself stood for a refusal of the gods that endangered the pax deorum; Sherwin-White, that governors punished defiance of their orders.

    "Disloyalty", however, was always partly religious, because the community's relationship with the gods depended on exact performance on its behalf (module 2.1). The third century made that explicit. Decius ordered every inhabitant to sacrifice before commissioners in 250, and the certificates from Egypt suggest to Rives a general religious act that treated the empire as one religious community (module 8.1). Diocletian tied the gods' favour to the piety of all his subjects and called the undoing of what the ancients had settled the greatest crime (Collatio 6.4, 15.3); the Great Persecution of 303–311 followed, uneven by the map of the Tetrarchy, until Galerius conceded its failure (module 8.2).

    Then the same logic changed gods. Constantine began with patronage and arbitration but coerced the Donatists (module 8.3); Constantius II ordered the madness of sacrifice abolished (Theodosian Code 16.10.2); Theodosius' laws of 391–392 made sacrifice treason, and the Serapeum fell (module 8.4). Symmachus' plea that one cannot reach so great a mystery by one road (Relatio 3.10) restated the old polytheist tolerance, and Ambrose rejected it. The thesis holds for the Republic and the early empire only if disloyalty includes independent organisation and refusal of the common cult; from Decius on, emperors sought one religion as the test of loyalty, first the gods and then Christ (module 8.1). Toleration had always been the senate's licence, never a right, and law could end public sacrifice far faster than society ended paganism (module 8.4).

06Where the gaps will still be

A fifty-four module course is a strong map with blank areas. These are the ten most likely blanks. The first three are filled by the optional deep dives, which the notes name; the other seven need a prompt of their own.

Where sources disagree. Several of these are gaps because the field itself is unsettled. Whether the Roman economy was shaped chiefly by status and land, as in Finley's picture (The Ancient Economy, 1973; module 9.3), or grew as taxation drove trade and monetisation, as Hopkins argued ("Taxes and Trade in the Roman Empire", 1980; module 7.5), is a live controversy; for Persia the sources themselves collide, Shapur's inscription claiming Valerian taken in battle and Zosimus at a parley (module 8.1). The prompts ask for the dispute, not a verdict.
  • How the Republic actually worked. Module 3.1 listed the offices and assemblies and module 4.5 gave Polybius' theory and Millar's challenge; elections, the Senate's procedure, patronage and the crowd at the contio were sketched, not studied. Deep dive modules 11.1 and, once written, 11.3 fill the gap. 
    After deep dive modules 11.1 and 11.3, write a short report on the consular elections of 64 BC, which made Cicero consul for 63: the candidates and their backers, the voting order of the centuriate assembly, bribery and the laws against it, the contiones, Asconius' commentary on Cicero's In toga candida, and what Quintus Cicero's handbook on electioneering says a new man had to do.
  • The Aeneid as a poem. Module 6.3 read its three prophetic passages and module 4.1 traced Dido's sources; the poem itself was never read through. 
    After deep dive unit 12, write a short essay comparing the endings of the Aeneid and of Statius' Thebaid: Turnus killed in anger at Aeneid 12.938–952 against Anchises' charge to spare the conquered (6.851–853), and Theseus marching from Athens and its altar of Clemency to force the burial of the Argive dead in Thebaid 12, asking how far Statius' ending answers Virgil's and what each says about clemency under an emperor.
  • Roman philosophy. Lucretius, Cicero's dialogues and Seneca appeared as writers and politicians (modules 5.6, 7.1, 10.2); Stoic doctrine, the rival schools and Marcus Aurelius as a thinker did not. Deep dive unit 13 is the follow-up. 
    After deep dive unit 13, write a short report on how Latin Christianity took over Roman philosophy: Lactantius' Divine Institutes against the philosophers, Ambrose's On Duties as a Christian rewriting of Cicero's, Augustine's debts to Cicero's Hortensius, Varro and the Platonists in the Confessions and the City of God, and Boethius' Consolation of Philosophy, written in prison under Theoderic, asking what the Latin West kept of the Stoa and the Academy.
  • The Roman economy. Cato's estate (module 4.4), Hopkins's taxes and trade (module 7.5), the collapse of the coinage (module 8.1) and Trimalchio (module 9.3) were glimpses; the economy as a system was never examined. 
    Write a module on the Roman economy from 200 BC to AD 250: agriculture and the villa, the grain trade and the annona, amphorae, shipwrecks and Monte Testaccio as evidence, coinage, banking and credit, the Greenland lead record, Finley's model against the case for growth, and how far the empire's economy was a market at all.
  • The Church before Constantine. Christians appeared as the object of persecution (modules 2.5, 8.1, 8.2) and as Constantine's bishops (module 8.3); the movement itself, its growth, writings and quarrels, was never studied. 
    Write a module on Christianity in the Roman world from Paul to 312: house churches and the Dura-Europos building, bishops and the rise of the Roman see, the apologists Justin and Tertullian, martyr acts such as Perpetua's, Gnostics and Marcion, Origen, the formation of the canon, and the modern estimates of Christian numbers.
  • Judaea and the Jews under Rome. The Jews appeared in the expulsions (module 2.5), at Alexandria under Gaius (module 7.1) and in the great revolts (modules 7.2, 7.3); their history from their own side, from Herod to the rabbis, was not told. 
    Write a module on the Jews under Rome from Pompey's capture of the Temple in 63 BC to the Mishnah: Herod's kingdom, the prefects and the census of AD 6, the Temple and its economy, Josephus as historian, the revolts of 66, 115–117 and 132–135 from the Jewish side, the diaspora communities, and the rise of rabbinic Judaism after the Temple fell.
  • Parthia and Persia from their own side. Rome's eastern rival appears only as the enemy at Carrhae, in the standards of 20 BC, at Ctesiphon, in Ardashir's rise, Valerian's capture and Julian's death (modules 5.4, 6.4, 7.3, 7.4, 8.1, 8.4); the course looked from the Iranian plateau only through Shapur's inscription in module 8.1, never as Greek module 11.3 did for the Achaemenids. 
    Write a module on the Arsacid and Sasanian empires as states: Arsacid kingship and the great noble houses, Surena and Carrhae, Ardashir's revolution, Shapur I's inscription at Naqsh-e Rustam read in full, Zoroastrianism and Mani, and how Roman and Iranian sources describe the same wars.
  • Science, medicine and engineering. Galen appeared for the plague (module 7.3), Pliny, Frontinus and Vitruvius as sources, and aqueducts and concrete as buildings (modules 9.4, 10.2, 10.3); how Romans studied nature, and why their technology went no further, was never a subject. 
    Write a module on Roman science and technology: Greek doctors at Rome from Cato's hostility to Galen, Galen's anatomy and his career at court, Ptolemy's astronomy and geography, Vitruvius, Pliny's Natural History as a system of knowledge, water-mills and mines, and the old question why the Roman world produced no industrial revolution.
  • How Latin literature survived. The course met the manuscripts one at a time: Petrarch's letters to Atticus (module 10.4), Gaius' palimpsest (module 9.1), Trimalchio at Trogir, Quintilian at St Gall and Tacitus in two manuscripts (module 10.2). It never asked how the texts were transmitted. 
    Write a module on the transmission of Latin texts: late antique editions and the codex, Cassiodorus and the monasteries, the Carolingian revival and minuscule, palimpsests and their recovery, the humanist discoveries of Petrarch and Poggio, the first printed editions, and what was lost, such as 107 of Livy's 142 books.
  • The successor kingdoms and the end of Roman Italy. Module 8.5 ended in 476 and module 8.6 moved to Constantinople; the Roman West under Gothic, Frankish and Visigothic kings, and the Latin writers who worked for them, fell between the two. 
    Write a module on the Roman West from 476 to 604: Odoacer and Theoderic's Ostrogothic Italy, Boethius and Cassiodorus, Justinian's Gothic War seen from Italy, the Lombards, Gregory the Great's Rome, Clovis and the Franks, Visigothic Spain and Isidore, and how far the kingdoms ran on Roman law, taxes and personnel.

07Carry forward

  1. Date the source before the event. Every Roman story reached the page through a chain of tellers, Greek genealogists, Fabius Pictor, the annalists, Livy and Dionysius, Byzantine epitomators, and its final shape is evidence for the age that shaped it: the Gracchan Struggle of the Orders, the Augustan Camillus, the Christian Constantine. The ten cases of Part C are the template for every future one.
  2. Roman institutions lasted by changing their purpose under an old form: the tribunate became the emperor's power, the Latin right a provincial ladder to citizenship, the haruspices consultants to a Christian emperor when lightning struck his palace, the pontiffs' law the jurists' science and then Europe's codes. Ask of any institution what it was made for and what later holders made of it.
  3. "Who is Roman?" is the thread of this course, as "what is the polis?" was of the Greek one. The answer widened from a Latin town to Italy in 89, to the empire in 212 and to Greek-speaking Rhomaioi until 1453, and every later claimant, pope, emperor, republic or dictator, has chosen which Rome to inherit.

The prompt this answers

I have completed a 54-module course on Roman history and mythology covering sources, geography and chronology; the peoples of Iron Age Italy and the Etruscans; the founding legends (Evander, Hercules and Aeneas, Romulus and Remus, the seven kings, Lucretia and Brutus); Roman religion (its nature, the gods and their Greek doubles, priests and calendar, household and the dead, the foreign cults); the early Republic and the conquest of Italy; Carthage and the Punic Wars; the conquest of the Mediterranean and its cultural price; the fall of the Republic from the Gracchi to Actium; Augustus and the Augustan writers; the Principate from Tiberius to the Severans; the third-century crisis, Diocletian, Constantine and the Christian empire; the fall of the West and the eastern empire to 1453; law, the army, family and slavery, the city and the games; Ovid, imperial literature, Roman art and reception. Give me a 40-question final examination that tests integration across modules rather than isolated facts: questions that require connecting a legend to the period that wrote it down, a source to its bias, an institution to its long consequences, and Rome to the Greek course that came before it. Mix formats: 15 short-answer, 10 "explain the connection between X and Y", 10 "which source says this and why should we doubt it", 5 essay questions. Provide full model answers after the questions. Then list the ten topics where a reader of this course is most likely still to have gaps, with one follow-up prompt each.
