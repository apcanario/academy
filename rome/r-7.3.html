01Sources, and Nerva, 96–98

The second century has no Tacitus. Cassius Dio's books 68–73 survive only in the eleventh-century epitome of John Xiphilinus and in Byzantine excerpts, and Xiphilinus' copies already lacked the reign of Antoninus Pius (Dio 70.1.1). Dio sat in the Senate under Commodus and writes those years as an eyewitness, and is cited here by the Loeb book numbers, with the traditional book number, printed in the Loeb's margin, in brackets where they differ. Herodian, a Greek of the next generation, begins at Marcus' death. The Historia Augusta has a life of every emperor from Hadrian on; module 0.1 sets out the problem of its single late author. Its lives of Hadrian, Antoninus and Marcus rest on good early material and are mostly sober; the Verus is fair; the lives of Aelius and Avidius Cassius are padded with invention and forged letters; the Commodus is hostile but sound in outline. Pliny, Fronto, the late epitomes, coins, inscriptions and two sculpted columns supply the rest.

On 18 September 96, the day Domitian was killed (module 7.2), the Senate made Marcus Cocceius Nerva emperor: a childless senator in his sixties, consul with Vespasian in 71 and with Domitian in 90, a safe man. He recalled exiles, stopped the treason trials and executed slaves who had informed on their masters (Dio 68.1.2). Tacitus, writing in 98, credits him with combining two things once irreconcilable, principate and liberty (Agricola 3.1). The army had not been consulted. In 97 the praetorians under Casperius Aelianus besieged the palace and forced Nerva to surrender the men Aelianus demanded (Dio 68.3.3), Domitian's killers Petronius Secundus and Parthenius (Epitome de Caesaribus 12.8). In October, when a laurelled dispatch arrived from Pannonia, Nerva went up to the Capitol and announced that he adopted Marcus Ulpius Traianus, governor of Upper Germany (Pliny, Panegyricus 8; Dio 68.3.4). He died in January 98. The first "adoptive" succession was a frightened old man borrowing an army.

02Adoption as ideology and as accident

The regime made a doctrine of the event. Pliny, thanking Trajan as consul in 100 in a speech later expanded for publication (Letters 3.18), says Trajan was adopted not to please a wife, nor by a stepfather, but by a princeps (Panegyricus 7.4), and that he who is to rule all should be chosen from all (7.6). Tacitus gives Galba the same argument in 69: Rome had been one family's inheritance, and "that we have begun to be chosen will stand in place of liberty" (Histories 1.16.1). Dio's Hadrian, adopting Antoninus, says nature gives whatever son it pleases while adoption lets a man choose (69.20). Machiavelli made it a law of history: emperors who inherited were bad, Titus excepted, and the five adopted "from Nerva to Marcus" good (Discourses on Livy 1.10).

The facts tell another story. Nerva, Trajan and Hadrian had no sons; Antoninus' sons died young. Every choice followed blood or marriage. Hadrian was the son of Trajan's cousin and the husband of his great-niece Sabina. Hadrian's first choice, Aelius Caesar, died on 1 January 138; Hadrian then adopted Antoninus on condition that he adopt Marcus, his wife's nephew, and Aelius' son Lucius (Historia Augusta, Hadrian 24.1). The first emperor of the line with a surviving son made that son his colleague. Adoption was the expedient of childless men and the language of a Senate that wished to believe it had a voice.

Where sources disagree. Hadrian's own adoption is doubtful. Dio, citing his father Apronianus, who governed Cilicia where Trajan died, says Trajan never adopted him: Plotina hid the death for several days until the adoption was announced, and signed the letters to the Senate herself (69.1). The Historia Augusta reports that some said a man was hidden to speak in the dying emperor's voice, others that Trajan meant to name Neratius Priscus or no one (Hadrian 4.8–10). Hadrian kept 9 August, when he learnt of his adoption at Antioch, as its anniversary (4.6).

03Trajan, 98–117

Optimus princeps

Trajan, born at Italica in Baetica to a family of Italian settlers, was the first emperor from a province. He reached Rome only in 99 and entered on foot among the senators (Pliny, Panegyricus 22). The Panegyricus is the regime's manifesto, built on antithesis to Domitian: a princeps who behaves as a citizen, submits to the laws and lets senators speak. It praises the support of Italian children (26–28), the scheme whose loans to landowners are recorded on the bronze tablet of Veleia. Optimus, "best", already in Pliny's mouth, became an official title in 114. Pliny's letters from Bithynia belong to module 7.5, and his exchange with Trajan on the Christians (Letters 10.96–97) to module 2.5. In the late fourth century the Senate still hailed new emperors as "luckier than Augustus, better than Trajan" (Eutropius 8.5.3).

The Dacian Wars and the Column

Domitian had ended his Dacian war in 89 by paying Decebalus subsidies (Dio 67.7). Trajan reversed the settlement. The first war, 101–102, imposed terms that Decebalus soon broke; Apollodorus of Damascus then bridged the Danube at Drobeta (Dio 68.13 describes the bridge; Procopius, Buildings 4.6.12–13 names the architect). In the second, 105–106, the capital Sarmizegetusa fell and Decebalus killed himself; the tombstone of Tiberius Claudius Maximus, found near Philippi, records the cavalryman who carried his head to Trajan. Dacia became a province, settled with colonists from "the whole Roman world" (Eutropius 8.6.2). Its loot is a figure with a pedigree: John Lydus, citing Trajan's doctor Crito, gives five million pounds of gold and ten million of silver (On the Magistracies), which Jérôme Carcopino cut to a tenth. By Dio's count the victory games lasted 123 days, with 11,000 beasts killed and 10,000 gladiators fighting (68.15.1).

The Column, dedicated in 113 in Trajan's forum, is the main visual source for the wars. Its inscription says it shows the height of the hillside cut away for the forum, and its base later received Trajan's ashes. The spiral frieze tells both wars in sequence, but as evidence it is double-edged: soldiers build, march and listen to the emperor far more often than they fight, and whether it follows Trajan's lost war commentaries or arranges stock scenes is argued. Module 10.3 reads it as art.

Parthia and the greatest extent

Arabia was annexed in 106 (Dio 68.14.5). In 114 Trajan deposed Parthia's candidate in Armenia, Parthamasiris, and made the kingdom a province (68.19–20). In 115–116 he took Mesopotamia and the Parthian capital Ctesiphon and reached the Persian Gulf, where, Dio says, he watched a ship leave for India and wished he were young like Alexander (68.29.1). Eutropius names three new provinces, Armenia, Assyria and Mesopotamia (8.3.2), though whether Assyria was ever organised is doubted. This greatest extent lasted months. In 116 the conquered cities rose and a Jewish revolt, which Eusebius dates from Trajan's eighteenth year, 115 (Church History 4.2), spread through Cyrene, Egypt and Cyprus, for which Dio claims 220,000 dead in Cyrene and 240,000 in Cyprus (68.32). Trajan failed before Hatra (68.31), fell ill and died at Selinus in Cilicia in August 117.

04Hadrian, 117–138

Giving up the East

Hadrian gave up Armenia, Mesopotamia and Assyria at once. The Historia Augusta says he followed Cato, who declared the Macedonians free because they could not be protected (Hadrian 5.3); Eutropius blames envy of Trajan's glory and adds that friends dissuaded him from abandoning Dacia, lest its settlers be handed to the barbarians (8.6.2). The retreat applied Augustus' advice to keep the empire within its limits (Tacitus, Annals 1.11.4; Dio 56.33), discussed in module 6.4; the modern quarrel over frontier strategy is in module 0.2. In 118 four consulars, among them Trajan's marshals Palma and Lusius Quietus, were executed for conspiracy; Hadrian disowned the deed and swore never to punish a senator without the Senate's verdict (Hadrian 7; Dio 69.2), but the Senate did not forget.

The travelling emperor

Hadrian spent most of 121–132 away from Rome, in Gaul, Germany, Britain, Spain, Africa, Greece, Asia, Syria and Egypt. He inspected camps and tightened discipline (Dio 69.9); his speeches to the army of Africa at Lambaesis in 128 survive on stone, and his coins honoured the provinces one by one. Tertullian called him an explorer of every curiosity (Apology 5.7). In Britain, from about 122, he built the Wall; module 7.5 treats it with the frontier army in peacetime. The travel was government: the emperor went to the provinces instead of waiting for them to come to him.

Antinous

Antinous, a youth from Claudiopolis in Bithynia and the emperor's favourite, drowned in the Nile in October 130. Hadrian wept for him "like a woman" (Hadrian 14.5), founded Antinoopolis at the place and declared him a god; his cult and idealised portraits spread across the Greek East.

Where sources disagree. Hadrian wrote that Antinous fell into the Nile; Dio says the truth was that he was sacrificed, Hadrian being given to divination and magic (69.11.2–3). The Historia Augusta mentions a story that he gave his life for Hadrian (Hadrian 14.6), and Aurelius Victor makes it a voluntary death to lengthen the emperor's life (Caesares 14.7–9). The evidence cannot decide between accident, suicide and sacrifice.

The Bar Kokhba war

Jerusalem had lain in ruins since 70 (module 7.2); after Hadrian's visit in 130 it was to be refounded as a Roman colony, Aelia Capitolina. In 132 Judaea rose under Simon ben Kosiba, known from his own letters, found in caves of the Judaean desert, and from coins overstruck on Roman silver with "Year one of the redemption of Israel". Rabbi Akiva, a later tradition says, hailed him with "a star shall come out of Jacob" (Numbers 24:17), whence Bar Kokhba, "son of the star" (Jerusalem Talmud, Ta'anit 4.5). Rome needed three years and Julius Severus, brought from Britain (Dio 69.13.2). Dio counts fifty fortresses and 985 villages destroyed and 580,000 killed in the fighting, and notes that Hadrian omitted from his letter to the Senate the customary assurance that he and the army were well (69.14.1–3). Betar fell in 135; Jews were barred from Jerusalem (Eusebius, Church History 4.6), and the province became Syria Palaestina.

Where sources disagree. Dio makes the foundation of Aelia Capitolina, with a temple of Jupiter where the Temple had stood, the cause of the war (69.12.1–2); the Historia Augusta blames a ban on circumcision (Hadrian 14.2); Eusebius has the colony founded after the war (Church History 4.6). Werner Eck ("The Bar Kokhba Revolt: The Roman Point of View", 1999) argued from the transfer of Severus and the decorations awarded that Rome treated it as a grave crisis, against those, G. W. Bowersock (1980) among them, who held the threat grossly exaggerated.

Builder and philhellene

Hadrian rebuilt the Pantheon under the original builder's name (Hadrian 19.9–10), so that the porch still says Marcus Agrippa made it; Lise Hetland ("Dating the Pantheon", 2007) argued from brick stamps that work began under Trajan, about 114–116, and module 10.3 takes the building. Dio tells that Apollodorus, who had mocked the emperor's architectural drawings, was banished and later killed (69.4), perhaps senatorial malice. At Tibur Hadrian built a villa the size of a town and named its parts the Lyceum, the Academy, the Prytaneum, Canopus, the Painted Stoa and Tempe (Hadrian 26.5). He was the first emperor portrayed with a full beard and as a young man was nicknamed Graeculus, "the little Greek" (1.5). His Athenian archonship, the Olympieion, the arch with its two inscriptions, the Panhellenion and Herodes Atticus belong to Greek module 7.4.

05Antoninus Pius and the quiet reign, 138–161

Hadrian died at Baiae on 10 July 138, addressing his soul in verse, animula vagula blandula (Hadrian 25.9). The Senate, remembering the executions, was reluctant to deify him; Antoninus insisted, one of several explanations the Historia Augusta gives for his name Pius (Antoninus 2.3–7). He was a rich, mild consular of fifty-one from a family of Nemausus (Nîmes).

Of the next twenty-two years almost nothing is told. Antoninus never left Italy, saying that a prince's retinue burdened the provinces (Antoninus 7.11). His legate Lollius Urbicus built a second, turf wall in Scotland (5.4); his deified wife Faustina, who died in 140, gave her name to a foundation for poor girls and, with him, to the temple that still stands in the Forum; Aelius Aristides praised the empire as one well-ordered city (Greek module 7.4). He died in March 161, giving the guard the watchword aequanimitas (12.6). The quiet is partly an artefact. Dio's account was lost before Xiphilinus, the Historia Augusta's life is short and admiring, and even it records wars with Britons, Moors, Germans and Dacians and risings among the Jews and in Achaea and Egypt, all handled by legates (5.4–5).

06Marcus Aurelius and Lucius Verus, 161–180

Marcus at once made his adoptive brother Lucius his equal as Augustus, the first time Rome had two emperors (Historia Augusta, Marcus 7.5–6). His life and inner history are deep dive module 13.7; this is the reign in outline, and it was almost continuous war.

Parthia and the plague. Vologaeses IV took Armenia and destroyed a Roman army at Elegeia (Dio 71.2). Lucius went east and, in the Historia Augusta's portrait, enjoyed Antioch while his generals fought: Avidius Cassius sacked Seleucia on the Tigris and burned the palace at Ctesiphon in 165–166. Lucius died of a stroke early in 169. The army brought home an epidemic; Roman tradition said soldiers at Seleucia had opened a golden casket in a temple of Apollo and let it out (Verus 8.1–2; Ammianus 23.6.24). Galen describes fever, diarrhoea and a dark pustular rash, usually taken for smallpox. It returned for a generation: under Commodus, Dio says, 2,000 people often died at Rome in a single day (73(72).14.3).

Where sources disagree. No ancient source gives a total. J. F. Gilliam ("The Plague under Marcus Aurelius", 1961) thought the literary picture overdrawn; R. J. and M. L. Littman (1973), reading Galen's symptoms as smallpox, estimated 7–10 per cent of the population dead, 13–15 per cent in cities and armies. Richard Duncan-Jones ("The impact of the Antonine plague", 1996) read sudden breaks in building, inscriptions, coinage and Egyptian papyri as signs of catastrophe; each series can be explained otherwise, and estimates still range widely.

The northern wars. About 170 the Marcomanni and Quadi broke into Italy, besieged Aquileia and destroyed Opitergium (Ammianus 29.6.1). Rather than raise taxes, Marcus auctioned the palace furnishings in Trajan's Forum (Marcus 17.4–5), and he enlisted slaves, gladiators and brigands (21.6–7). He spent most of the 170s on the Danube, at Carnuntum and Sirmium, against the Marcomanni, the Quadi and the Iazyges. The rain miracle, Avidius Cassius' revolt of 175 and Commodus' elevation in 177 belong to deep dive module 13.7. A second expedition was under way when Marcus died on 17 March 180 (Dio 72(71).33), at Vindobona according to Aurelius Victor (Caesares 16.14), at Sirmium according to Tertullian (Apology 25). The Historia Augusta says he meant to make provinces of Marcomannia and Sarmatia (Marcus 24.5).

The column. The column of Marcus in the Piazza Colonna was finished by 193, when an inscription records that its custodian Adrastus might build a lodge beside it. Its frieze borrows Trajan's form and changes the tone: villages burned, prisoners beheaded, women dragged away. Module 10.3 reads it as art. In the same camps Marcus kept a Greek notebook whose manuscripts place one book "among the Quadi on the Granua" and another "at Carnuntum": the Meditations, which deep dive module 13.8 takes up.

07Commodus, 180–192

Commodus, Augustus since 177, was eighteen when his father died. He made peace with the Marcomanni and Quadi on terms that included grain, recruits and a cleared strip along the river (Dio 73(72).2), and went home to Rome. In 182, at his sister Lucilla's instigation, Claudius Pompeianus Quintianus drew a dagger on him, crying that the Senate had sent it, and was seized before he could strike (73(72).4.4). Government passed to favourites: the prefect Perennis, surrendered in 185 to 1,500 javelin-men sent by the army of Britain (73(72).9–10), and the freedman Cleander, who sold offices and made twenty-five consuls in one year (73(72).12.4), until a crowd angry at a grain shortage forced Commodus to have him killed in 190 (73(72).13).

In his last years Commodus presented himself as Hercules. He renamed Rome the Colonia Commodiana and the twelve months after his own titles (Dio 73(72).15.2–3), and the bust from the Esquiline, now in the Capitoline Museums, shows him in the lion skin with the club and the apples of the Hesperides. He fought in the arena as a secutor, charging the gladiatorial fund a million sesterces a day (73(72).19.3). Dio sat among the senators obliged to watch; when Commodus cut off an ostrich's head and waved it at them, Dio chewed laurel leaves from his garland to keep from laughing, which would have been fatal (73(72).21.1–2).

He meant to enter 193 as consul from the gladiators' barracks (73(72).22.2). On the night of 31 December 192 his mistress Marcia, his chamberlain Eclectus and the prefect Laetus gave him poisoned beef; when he vomited it, the athlete Narcissus strangled him in his bath (73(72).22.4–5). Herodian adds that Marcia had found her own name on his list of those to die (1.17). The conspirators already had a successor, the city prefect Pertinax, whose reign and the auction that followed open module 7.4.

Where sources disagree. Dio, who watched as a senator, and Herodian, writing a generation later, give the same portrait of a frivolous tyrant, and Dio dates the decline from the accession, when history descends "from a kingdom of gold to one of iron and rust" (72(71).36.4). Olivier Hekster (Commodus: An Emperor at the Crossroads, 2002) looked for method in the madness: the Herculean imagery arrived suddenly after Cleander's fall, and the arena won acclamation from people and soldiers over the Senate's head. The senators who wrote the history were the audience it bypassed.

08Gibbon's verdict and what it leaves out

Near the end of his third chapter Gibbon wrote the sentence that named the age: "If a man were called to fix the period in the history of the world, during which the condition of the human race was most happy and prosperous, he would, without hesitation, name that which elapsed from the death of Domitian to the accession of Commodus." The empire, he went on, "was governed by absolute power, under the guidance of virtue and wisdom" (Decline and Fall, chapter 3). His period ends in 180, not 192. The verdict has ancient parents: Tacitus on the rare happiness of a time when one might think what one liked and say what one thought (Histories 1.1.4), Aristides' world as one city, Dio's kingdom of gold. All are the view of senators and of the cities' governing class, and Gibbon himself saw that a happiness depending on one man's character was unstable. What the sentence leaves out:

  • The provinces that paid. Dacia conquered for its gold; the diaspora revolt of 115–117 ending in massacre; Judaea losing, on Dio's count, 580,000 men and 985 villages.
  • Plague and invasion. Both fall inside Gibbon's dates. Joseph McConnell and colleagues (2018) found that lead pollution in Greenland ice, a proxy for Roman silver mining, fell sharply with the plague and stayed low.
  • Slaves, captives and the persecuted. The 10,000 gladiators of the Dacian victory games, the prisoners of Dacia and Parthia, the slaves on the estates behind the senators' villas, and the Christians executed under Trajan's rescript (module 2.5) and at Lyon in 177 (deep dive module 13.7).
  • The succession. The happiness rested on four childless emperors in a row; the first with a son gave the empire to him.

What Gibbon saw was real for the class that left the evidence: no civil war between 69 and 193 beyond two failed usurpations, cities building in marble from Britain to the Euphrates, emperors who answered petitions. His sentence describes that class's experience and calls it the condition of the human race.

09Self-test

  1. How did Nerva come to adopt Trajan, and why do the circumstances matter for reading adoption as a principle?
    Answer

    The praetorians under Casperius Aelianus besieged the palace and forced Nerva to surrender Domitian's killers (Dio 68.3.3); he then announced on the Capitol the adoption of the governor of Upper Germany (Pliny, Panegyricus 8). A threatened emperor acquired an army; the doctrine of choosing the best came afterwards.

  2. What did Pliny, Tacitus' Galba and Dio's Hadrian claim for adoption, and what facts undercut the claim?
    Answer

    That choosing the best man from all replaces inheritance and even stands in place of liberty (Panegyricus 7.4–6; Histories 1.16.1; Dio 69.20). But Nerva, Trajan and Hadrian had no sons and Antoninus' died, each choice followed kinship or marriage, and the first emperor with a surviving son, Marcus, made him his heir.

  3. Why is Hadrian's adoption doubtful, and where does the doubt come from?
    Answer

    Dio, citing his father Apronianus, governor of Cilicia, says Plotina concealed Trajan's death so that an adoption could be announced first and signed the letters to the Senate herself (69.1). The Historia Augusta adds the story of a man impersonating the dying emperor's voice (Hadrian 4.10).

  4. Why is Trajan's Column double-edged evidence for the Dacian wars?
    Answer

    It is a contemporary narrative of both wars set up in 113, but a commissioned monument: it shows building, marching and imperial speeches far more than fighting, and its sequence may follow Trajan's lost commentaries or simply arrange stock scenes. It records what the regime wished remembered.

  5. What was the empire's greatest extent, why did it not last, and how do the ancient sources explain Hadrian's withdrawal?
    Answer

    By 116 Armenia, Mesopotamia and Assyria had been declared provinces (Eutropius 8.3.2) and Trajan reached the Gulf. Revolts in the conquered cities and the diaspora, failure at Hatra and his death undid it. The Historia Augusta cites Cato's precedent with Macedonia (Hadrian 5.3), Eutropius envy of Trajan (8.6.2); either way it fits Augustus' advice to keep within limits (module 6.4).

  6. What three ancient explanations of the Bar Kokhba war are there, and what suggests its seriousness for Rome?
    Answer

    Dio blames the foundation of Aelia Capitolina (69.12.1–2), the Historia Augusta a ban on circumcision (Hadrian 14.2), and Eusebius makes the colony a consequence of the war (Church History 4.6). Julius Severus was brought from Britain, and Hadrian dropped the formula that he and the army were well (Dio 69.13–14); Eck reads Rome's response as that to a grave crisis.

  7. In what sense is Antoninus' reign "quiet", and how far is that an artefact of the sources?
    Answer

    He never left Italy (Antoninus 7.11), fought no war in person and faced no succession crisis. But Dio's account was lost before Xiphilinus (70.1.1) and the Historia Augusta is brief, and even it records wars with Britons, Moors, Germans and Dacians and risings among the Jews and in Achaea and Egypt (5.4–5).

  8. Name three crises of Marcus' reign and two measures he took to pay for the northern wars.
    Answer

    Any three of: the Parthian war and the defeat at Elegeia; the plague from 165–166; the invasion of Italy and siege of Aquileia about 170; the revolt of Avidius Cassius in 175. He auctioned the palace furnishings in Trajan's Forum (Marcus 17.4–5) and enlisted slaves, gladiators and brigands (21.6–7).

  9. How do Dio and Hekster read Commodus' Hercules and his arena appearances differently?
    Answer

    Dio, an eyewitness, sees degradation, from a kingdom of gold to one of iron and rust (72(71).36.4), and records the ostrich scene and his laurel leaves (73(72).21). Hekster (2002) sees a deliberate programme after 190 that sought acclamation from people and soldiers and bypassed the Senate, whose members wrote the history.

  10. What period does Gibbon actually name, and what does his verdict leave out?
    Answer

    From the death of Domitian to the accession of Commodus, 96–180 (Decline and Fall, chapter 3). It leaves out the cost at the edges (Dacia, the diaspora revolt, Judaea), the plague and invasion inside its own dates, slaves, captives and Christians, and the dependence of the whole arrangement on childless emperors; it generalises the experience of the governing class.

10Carry forward

  1. The "adoptive" succession was the expedient of childless emperors dressed as a principle of choosing the best; power still passed through family, marriage and the army's acceptance, and the doctrine ended the moment an emperor had a son. Later dynasts will still borrow the Antonine name to claim legitimacy.
  2. The second-century peace was made at the edges: conquest in Dacia, withdrawal from Mesopotamia, devastation in Judaea, and from 165 plague and invasion. The empire's greatest extent on the map (116) and its high point in reputation (Gibbon's 96–180) are different measures.
  3. Our picture of the century is senatorial and damaged: Dio in epitome, a Historia Augusta of uneven worth, a panegyric and Herodian. Emperors are judged by how they treated the Senate, and a silence such as Antoninus' may be a loss of evidence rather than an absence of events.

The prompt this answers

Give me an in-depth report on the emperors from Nerva to Commodus, AD 96 to 192. Cover: adoption as ideology and as accident; Trajan (the Dacian Wars and the Column, the Parthian war and the empire's greatest extent, Pliny's Panegyricus); Hadrian (the abandonment of the eastern conquests, the travels, the Wall, Antinous, the Bar Kokhba war, the Pantheon and the villa at Tivoli, his philhellenism; point back to Greek module 7.4); Antoninus Pius and the quiet reign; Marcus Aurelius' reign in outline (the Antonine plague, the Marcomannic wars, the column, Lucius Verus; deep dive modules 13.7 and 13.8 take his life and the Meditations further); Commodus as gladiator and Hercules, and his murder; and Gibbon's verdict that this was the period in which 'the condition of the human race was most happy and prosperous', with what it leaves out. Format: about 3000 words, headed sections, name sources, flag disagreements, end with a 10-question self-test with answers and the three ideas I must carry forward.
