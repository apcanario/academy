01Coriolanus and his mother

Every story here reaches us through Livy's books 2–8, written in the 20s BC, and Dionysius of Halicarnassus' Roman Antiquities, published from 7 BC: three to five centuries after the events, and for the fifth century nearly three after Fabius Pictor, the first Roman historian (module 0.1).

Gnaeus Marcius took the Volscian town of Corioli in 493 and was named for it. Livy concedes that the consul in command would be forgotten had the bronze column of the Latin treaty not recorded his absence at the war (2.33.5–9): memory had already gathered round the hero. In the famine of 491 Marcius urged that Sicilian grain be sold cheap only if the plebs gave up their tribunes; summoned to trial, he went into exile with Attius Tullius of the Volsci (2.34–35). In 488 he led a Volscian army to the Cluilian trench, five miles from Rome, and sent back embassies of senators and priests (2.39).

Then the matrons gathered at the house of his mother Veturia and his wife Volumnia (2.40.1), and the women went to the camp with his sons. Veturia asks whether she has come to an enemy or a son, as captive or as mother; had she borne no son, Rome would not be besieged (2.40.5–8). He withdrew. The men of Rome, Livy says, did not begrudge the women their praise, and a temple of Fortuna Muliebris, Women's Fortune, was dedicated (2.40.11–12) at the fourth milestone of the Via Latina (Valerius Maximus 1.8.4).

Dionysius gives most of book 8 to the episode: Valeria, Publicola's sister, proposes the embassy (8.39), Veturia's pleading fills most of chapters 46–53, and the temple's statue speaks to approve the matrons' dedication (8.56.2). Plutarch's mother is Volumnia and his wife Vergilia (Coriolanus 33–36), names Shakespeare took from him (module 10.4).

Where sources disagree. Livy found in Fabius Pictor, "by far the oldest authority", that Marcius lived into old age in exile (2.40.10–11). Dionysius (8.57–59) and Plutarch (Coriolanus 39) have Tullus' faction kill him; Cicero gave him a suicide, and Atticus mocked him for it (Brutus 42). Even the praenomen varies: Gnaeus in Livy, Gaius in Dionysius and Plutarch.

02Cincinnatus and the Fabii

In 458 the Aequi trapped the consul Minucius on Mount Algidus, and the senate named Lucius Quinctius Cincinnatus dictator. Livy offers the scene to those who despise everything human beside wealth (3.26.7). The envoys found him on his four iugera across the Tiber, digging or ploughing, Livy cannot say which; his wife Racilia fetched his toga from their hut (3.26.8–10). He surrounded the Aequi by night, sent them under the yoke, triumphed and laid down a six-month office on the sixteenth day (3.29.7). Livy explains the poverty: the sureties forfeited when his son Kaeso fled trial were exacted from him (3.13.8–10). At over eighty he was dictator again, against Maelius (4.13–14). The Algidus wars are real; the plough, the toga and the sixteen days are what an age of great estates wished its ancestors had been.

The Fabii make a clan epic. A Fabius was consul every year from 485 to 479. In 479 the family offered to fight Veii at its own cost, and 306 Fabii marched out through the right-hand arch of the Porta Carmentalis, thereafter an unlucky gate (Livy 2.48–49; Ovid, Fasti 2.201–204), with some 4,000 clients and friends (Dionysius 9.15.3). From a fort on the Cremera they raided for two years until, in 477, an ambush killed them all (Livy 2.50). One boy, too young to march, survived at Rome to carry on a line that would one day save the state (2.50.11): the line of Fabius Maximus.

Where sources disagree. Ovid sets the disaster on the Ides of February (Fasti 2.193–196), Livy on 18 July, the day of the Allia (6.1.11). Dionysius rejects the lone survivor: Roman law made men marry and rear children, so 306 grown Fabii must have left sons, and the tale resembles the fictions of myth and the stage (9.22.1–3). Livy undercuts himself by making the survivor consul in 467 (3.1.1).

03Three men who aimed at kingship

Cicero names the set: Spurius Cassius, Spurius Maelius and Marcus Manlius, killed on suspicion of seeking kingship (Philippics 2.114).

Cassius, consul in 486, proposed the first agrarian law, sharing public land with the plebs and the Latins (Livy 2.41.1–3). Either his father tried him at home, had him scourged to death and consecrated his property to Ceres, or the quaestors prosecuted him for treason before the people and his house was razed (2.41.10–12). The site lay before the temple of Tellus (Cicero, De domo sua 101), where the censors of 158 melted down his statue (Pliny, Natural History 34.30).

Maelius, a rich equestrian, gave away grain bought in Etruria during the famine of 440. Accused of arming his house, he was cut down by Gaius Servilius Ahala, master of horse to the dictator Cincinnatus; his levelled site became the Aequimaelium, and the informer Minucius received a gilded ox outside the Porta Trigemina (Livy 4.13–16).

Manlius Capitolinus, saviour of the Capitol in 390 (module 3.3), is Livy's first patrician turned popular leader (6.11.7): he paid debtors' debts and accused the senate of hiding the Gallic gold (6.14). Tried in 384, he pointed to the Capitol, and the assembly had to move out of its sight to condemn him. Thrown from the Tarpeian Rock, he made one place a monument of outstanding glory and of final punishment (6.20.10–12); his house was razed, and the Manlii resolved that none of them should again be called Marcus (6.20.13–14).

Mommsen (1871) first grouped the three; Lintott (1970) read them as post-Gracchan. Land for the poor and the Latins and cheap grain are the programmes of the Gracchi, debt relief that of Catiline, and the kingship charge was the smear used against Tiberius Gracchus (Plutarch, Tiberius Gracchus 19). What survives is topographical, and orators used it: Cicero set his own demolished house beside these razed houses in 57 (De domo sua 101) and cited Ahala against Catiline (Against Catiline 1.3).

Where sources disagree. Dionysius reports that Cincius and Calpurnius Piso knew no dictator in 439: the senate chose the young Servilius, who stabbed Maelius in the street and reported that he had killed the tyrant at the senate's command (12.4.2–5). Piso was consul in 133, when the private citizen Scipio Nasica led the killing of Tiberius Gracchus, and the version reads like a precedent for him. Yet if this Cincius is the annalist of about 200, as Dionysius' editors assume, the private killing predates the Gracchi.

04Torquatus, the Decii and Curtius

Titus Manlius took the cognomen Torquatus from the torque of a Gaul he killed in single combat in 361 (Livy 7.9–10; Claudius Quadrigarius in Gellius 9.13). As consul in 340, against Latins alike in arms and language, he forbade fighting outside the line. His son accepted a Tusculan's challenge, killed him and brought the spoils; the father had him beheaded before the army (8.7). Livy is divided: the "Manlian orders" horrified at the time and left a grim example (8.7.22), yet made the army obedient (8.8.1). The exemplum absorbed rivals: Livy rejects a like tale of Postumius Tubertus because the orders are called Manlian, not Postumian (4.29.5–6).

In the same campaign both consuls dreamt that the general of one side and the army of the other were owed to the gods below and Earth (8.6.9–10). When his wing gave way at Veseris, Publius Decius Mus had a pontifex dictate the formula: Janus, Jupiter, Mars, Quirinus, Bellona, the Lares and other gods down to the Di Manes are asked for victory, and the enemy's legions are devoted with Decius to the gods below (8.9.6–8). Veiled, in the purple-bordered toga girt in the Gabine manner, standing on a spear, he rode into the Latins and died. Livy records the words because ancestral rite is forgotten (8.11.1) and adds the rules: a commander may devote any legionary instead; if the devoted man survives, a seven-foot image is buried (8.10.11–12). The son did the same at Sentinum in 295 (10.28; module 3.4), and Cicero adds a grandson at Asculum in 279 (Tusculan Disputations 1.89; module 3.5). Virgil sets the Decii beside Torquatus with his savage axe (Aeneid 6.824–825).

In 362 a chasm opened in the Forum that would close, the seers said, only when Rome offered what its strength lay in. Marcus Curtius, answering that this was arms and courage, rode armed into it (Livy 7.6.1–5). Livy keeps this explanation of the Lacus Curtius against its rivals (module 0.1) only because antiquity has removed certainty (7.6.6). It is a devotio without a battle, and the place kept its charge: under Augustus every order threw coins into it yearly for his health (Suetonius, Augustus 57.1).

Where sources disagree. The grandson's devotio is doubtful (module 3.5 sets out the evidence). Skutsch placed Ennius' devotio prayer (Annals 191–194) at Asculum, which would put the story into Latin verse within about a century; Cornell (1987) disagreed. Many critics suspect the father's devotio of 340 of copying the son's in 295.

05The women in the stories

Women act in these legends as mothers and wives standing between houses, or die as daughters whose bodies mark the limit of power. Veturia replays the Sabine women who ran between their fathers and their husbands (Livy 1.13; module 1.4). In both, women loyal to the house of birth and the house of marriage end a war the men cannot end, and both close in women's institutions: the Matronalia (Ovid, Fasti 3.167–258) and Fortuna Muliebris.

Verginia is the other type. In 449 a client of the decemvir Appius Claudius claimed her as his slave, and her father, the centurion Verginius, killed her in the Forum with a butcher's knife, the only way, he said, to make her free (Livy 3.44–48; Dionysius 11.28–37). Module 3.1 takes the politics, module 1.6 the pairing with Lucretia. A second Verginia, patrician wife of a plebeian consul, was barred from the shrine of Patrician Chastity in 296 and founded an altar of Plebeian Chastity in her own house (Livy 10.23.1–10): the struggle of the orders told through a matron's honour.

Three of these stories are anchored in women's cults (Fortuna Muliebris, the Matronalia, the chastity shrines) rather than in family eulogy, which points to a channel of transmission beside the funeral: the yearly rite at which a cult's story was retold.

06The exemplum as Roman myth

Livy's preface sets out the use: history displays every kind of example on a conspicuous monument, to imitate, or to avoid as foedum inceptu, foedum exitu, foul in beginning and in outcome (Preface 10; module 1.6). These legends supply both kinds, often in one man: Manlius saved the Capitol and died from its rock; Torquatus' severity was hateful and effective.

This is the work Greek myth did: charter, model, warning. Roman exempla do it in historical dress, dated by consuls and fixed to cognomina (Coriolanus, Capitolinus, Torquatus), to places (the Lacus Curtius, the Aequimaelium, the unlucky gate), to rites and to families. Quintilian puts it plainly: as much as the Greeks are strong in precepts, the Romans are in examples, which is greater (12.2.30). Matthew Roller (Models from the Past in Roman Culture, 2018) analyses the exemplum as deed, judging audience, monument and imitation; Jane Chaplin (Livy's Exemplary History, 2000) shows Livy's characters citing exempla themselves, as the younger Decius cites his father.

The late Republic used the stock as argument. Sallust's Cato cited Torquatus in 63 to justify executing the Catilinarians, calling him Aulus and placing the case in a Gallic war (Catiline 52.30); Cicero's Epicurean Torquatus explains his ancestor by utility, and Cicero answers with the Decii (On Ends 1.23–24, 2.60–61). Augustus' Forum, lined with statues of the men who had made Rome great (Suetonius, Augustus 31.5), each above an inscribed summary of his deeds, made the stock a canon.

07Families, funerals and the stage

Module 0.1 gave the ancient complaints that funeral praises corrupted the record, and module 2.4 describes the funeral. Polybius, about 150 BC, already counts these types among its lessons: men who fought single combats to decide battles, and magistrates who put their own sons to death against all law and custom (6.54.4–5).

The families worked their stories. The Fabii had the first historian, a survivor whose line produced Fabius Maximus, and descent from Hercules (Ovid, Fasti 2.237; Plutarch, Fabius Maximus 1.2). The Torquati re-enacted theirs: the consul of 165 condemned his own son for extortion in 140 and stayed away from his funeral (Valerius Maximus 5.8.3), and later moneyers of the family set a torque round their coins. The Decii made devotio hereditary. Marcus Brutus put his ancestors, the first Brutus and Ahala, on a denarius in 54 BC. An exemplum kept a family in office.

Wiseman (Roman Drama and Roman History, 1998; The Myths of Rome, 2004) adds the stage. Stage games began in 364 (Livy 7.2), and stories built as scenes, with a crowd, a confrontation, a mother's plea and a reversal, look like performances before they were history. Ancient writers sensed it: Dionysius likens the Fabian survivor to stage fiction (9.22.3), and Livy calls a tale from Veii's siege ad ostentationem scaenae gaudentis miraculis aptiora quam ad fidem, fitter for a stage that delights in marvels than for belief (5.21.9). Plays on Roman subjects (fabulae praetextae) are attested: Naevius' Romulus, Ennius' Sabinae, and Accius' Aeneadae or Decius on Sentinum.

Where sources disagree. Harriet Flower ("Fabulae praetextae in context", 1995) argues that such plays were few, occasional pieces for triumphs, funerals and dedications, mostly on recent events; Cornell (2003) counts ballad, epic and play among the possible pre-literary carriers of the Coriolanus story. Wiseman replies that most performances were never written down, so lists of titles cannot measure them.

08Greek models

Cicero drew the Coriolanus parallel himself: Marcius and Themistocles, contemporaries, were driven out by ungrateful peoples, joined the enemy and ended their anger by death (Brutus 41–42). Atticus' reply exposes the method: rhetoricians may lie in histories, as Clitarchus gave Themistocles bull's blood where Thucydides had illness (Brutus 42–43; Thucydides 1.138.4). Plutarch's Marcius sits veiled at Tullus' hearth (Coriolanus 23), as Themistocles sat at the Molossian king's (Thucydides 1.136–137). The annalists synchronised with Greek history, sometimes badly: Dionysius faults Licinius Macer and Gellius for sending the famine grain from Dionysius I of Syracuse instead of Gelon (7.1.4–6).

The 306 Fabii fall three years after Thermopylae, a few against a host with a lone survivor, like Leonidas' 300 (Herodotus 7.202, 7.229–231): a fit model for a family that claimed Hercules, as Leonidas did. Pairing became a genre. The Pseudo-Plutarchan Parallela minora matches Curtius with Anchurus, who rode into a chasm in Phrygia (5), and Decius with Codrus, the Athenian king who sought death because an oracle promised victory to the side whose king fell (18). The consuls' dream before Veseris has Codrus' logic.

Where sources disagree. Mommsen (1870) treated Coriolanus as a late fiction on Greek patterns. Cornell ("Coriolanus: myth, history and performance", 2003) argues for a core: an exile leading Volscians into Latium in the 490s fits the Volscian advance known on other grounds, and the Greek parallels are costume, not source.

09Valerius Maximus: the stock as a handbook

Valerius Maximus completed the transformation. His Memorable Deeds and Sayings, nine books dedicated to Tiberius and written after Sejanus' fall in 31 (9.11.ext.4), files about a thousand exempla under rubrics of religion, institutions, virtues and vices, Roman cases before foreign. The preface promises a selection from famous authors that spares those seeking examples long research. Cut from its narrative, each story serves one virtue: Coriolanus piety towards parents (5.4.1), not treason; Cincinnatus poverty (4.4.7); Curtius and the Decii love of country (5.6.2, 5.6.5–6); Torquatus military discipline (2.7.6). Spurius Cassius appears under the severity of fathers, in the domestic version, the one that fits (5.8.2). Epitomised in late antiquity by Julius Paris and Januarius Nepotianus, the book was widely read through the Middle Ages and the Renaissance and carried the stock to later Europe.

Where sources disagree. Bloomer (Valerius Maximus and the Rhetoric of the New Nobility, 1992) reads a declaimer's sourcebook that gave men without ancestral masks a shared stock of ancestors; Skidmore (Practical Ethics for Roman Gentlemen, 1996), a moral guide for general readers. Langlands (Exemplary Ethics in Ancient Rome, 2018) sees clashing cases meant to be thought through, not simply obeyed.

10Self-test

  1. What does Livy's citation of Fabius Pictor on Coriolanus' death suggest about the later versions?
    Answer

    The oldest authority had Marcius live to old age in exile (Livy 2.40.10–11). The murder by Tullus' faction (Dionysius 8.57–59; Plutarch, Coriolanus 39) and Cicero's suicide (Brutus 42) look like later elaboration for drama or for the Themistocles parallel.

  2. How does Livy frame the Cincinnatus story, and how does his own narrative explain the hero's poverty?
    Answer

    He offers it to those who value wealth above everything (3.26.7): a lesson in virtue without riches. The poverty comes from the forfeited sureties for his son Kaeso, which were exacted from him (3.13.8–10).

  3. Give two reasons to doubt the lone surviving Fabius, and say what the motif does for the family.
    Answer

    Dionysius notes that Roman law obliged men to marry and rear children, so 306 Fabii must have left sons (9.22.1–3); Livy makes the survivor consul in 467, ten years later (3.1.1). The motif guarantees the continuity of the gens that produced Fabius Maximus.

  4. What links Cassius, Maelius and Manlius, and why do many scholars treat them as retrojections?
    Answer

    Each championed the poor with land, grain or debt relief, was killed on suspicion of seeking kingship (Cicero, Philippics 2.114) and had his house razed. Their programmes and the charge of kingship match late-Republican politics from the Gracchi to Catiline (Lintott 1970).

  5. Why does the Cincius and Piso version of Maelius' death matter?
    Answer

    In it there is no dictator, and Servilius kills Maelius as a private citizen at the senate's bidding (Dionysius 12.4.2–5), a precedent for Scipio Nasica in Piso's consulship of 133. If Cincius is the annalist of about 200, the version predates the Gracchi, which complicates the retrojection theory.

  6. Describe the devotio as Livy records it.
    Answer

    Prompted by a pontifex, the commander prays to a list of gods from Janus to the Di Manes and devotes the enemy's legions with himself to the gods below (8.9.6–8); then, veiled, in the purple-bordered toga girt in the Gabine manner and standing on a spear, he rides into the enemy. If a devoted man survives, a seven-foot image is buried (8.10.11–12).

  7. In what sense is Torquatus' execution of his son both a model and a warning, and how was it used in 63 BC?
    Answer

    Livy calls the Manlian orders horrifying at the time and a grim example for later ages (8.7.22), yet credits them with restoring discipline (8.8.1). Sallust's Cato cited the case to justify executing the Catilinarians, calling him Aulus Manlius (Catiline 52.30).

  8. How does Veturia's embassy rework the Sabine women, and what commemorated it?
    Answer

    In both, women standing between the house of birth and the house of marriage stop a war that men cannot stop (Livy 1.13; 2.40). The matrons' temple of Fortuna Muliebris on the Via Latina commemorated it (2.40.12; Dionysius 8.55–56).

  9. State Wiseman's theory, two ancient items that fit it, and the main objection.
    Answer

    Many early legends were first performed as drama at the games before historians wrote them down. Dionysius likens the Fabian survivor to stage fiction (9.22.3), and Accius' Aeneadae or Decius staged the devotio at Sentinum. Flower (1995) replies that historical plays were few, occasional and mostly about recent events.

  10. How did Valerius Maximus change the stories by arranging them?
    Answer

    He cut them from their narratives and filed each under a single virtue, choosing the version that fitted: Coriolanus under piety towards parents (5.4.1), Spurius Cassius under paternal severity in the domestic version (5.8.2). His preface offers the collection as a saving of research for those who want examples.

11Carry forward

  1. The exemplum is the Roman form of myth: a deed dated by consuls, fixed to a place, a cognomen, a rite or a family, and judged as a model or a warning. Read every early-Republican legend as an argument about conduct, and expect one exemplum to justify opposite acts.
  2. These legends travelled through identifiable channels: family display (funerals, masks, coins, a Fabius as first historian), cult and topography (Fortuna Muliebris, the Lacus Curtius, razed houses), probably the stage, and Greek historiography, which supplied the shapes. Ask of each story which channel made it.
  3. Much of the early-Republican narrative is the late Republic arguing with itself: the would-be kings resemble the Gracchi, and Ahala, Torquatus and the Decii were cited in 63 and after. Valerius Maximus fixed the stock as a handbook of virtues, and that is the form in which later Europe received it.

The prompt this answers

Give me an in-depth report on the heroic legends of the early Republic as Livy books 2 to 8 and Dionysius tell them, and on how Romans used them. Cover: Coriolanus and his mother; Cincinnatus called from the plough; the 306 Fabii destroyed at the Cremera; Spurius Cassius, Spurius Maelius and Manlius Capitolinus as men killed for aiming at kingship; Manlius Torquatus executing his son; the self-sacrifice (devotio) of the Decii; Marcus Curtius leaping into the chasm in the Forum; and the women in these stories (Coriolanus' mother and wife, Verginia, the Sabine mothers). Horatius, Mucius Scaevola and Cloelia belong to module 1.6, and the geese of the Capitol to 3.3. Then the analysis: the exemplum as the Roman form of myth and Livy's preface on examples to follow and avoid; family traditions and funeral orations as the source of many stories; Greek models (Coriolanus and Themistocles, the Fabii and the three hundred Spartans); Wiseman's theory of historical drama on the Roman stage; and how Valerius Maximus turned the whole stock into a handbook. Format: about 2500 words, headed sections, name sources, flag disagreements, end with a 10-question self-test with answers and the three ideas I must carry forward.
