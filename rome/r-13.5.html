01Corduba, the elder Seneca and Egypt

Lucius Annaeus Seneca was born at Corduba, the chief city of Baetica in southern Spain, some time between about 4 and 1 BC; no ancient source gives the year, and modern estimates are inferences from what he says of his age and his schooling. His family was equestrian and provincial, rich enough to move to Rome while he was a small child: he was carried there, he tells his mother, in his aunt's arms (Consolation to Helvia 19.2).

His father, Lucius Annaeus Seneca the elder (born about 50 BC), is known from the work of his old age, the Controversiae and Suasoriae, a collection of the sayings of the declaimers he had heard over a long life, written down from memory for his three sons. He could have heard Cicero, he says, had the fury of the civil wars not kept him inside his colony (Controversiae 1 preface 11). The sons took three paths. The eldest, Novatus, was adopted by the orator Junius Gallio, took his name, and as proconsul of Achaea dismissed the case brought against Paul at Corinth (Acts 18.12–17). The youngest, Mela, stayed an equestrian, made money, and fathered the poet Lucan (Tacitus, Annals 16.17; module 10.2). The middle son went into the Senate and philosophy.

The father distrusted the second choice. His old-fashioned strictness, Seneca says, allowed his mother only a taste of philosophy (Consolation to Helvia 17.3–4). As a young man Seneca heard the Stoic Attalus (Letters 108), the Pythagorean Sotion and Papirius Fabianus of the Sextian school (module 13.3 has the Sextii). Sotion's arguments made him a vegetarian; after a year the habit had become pleasant, but in the first years of Tiberius, when foreign rites were being expelled and abstaining from certain meats was read as a sign of them, his father, who did not fear slander but hated philosophy, asked him to eat properly again (Letters 108.17–22).

Illness, Egypt and the start of a career

Seneca was ill for much of his youth. In Letters 78 he recalls a catarrh that wasted him to nothing and drove him to think of suicide, from which only the thought of his kind father's old age held him back (78.1–2). In Letters 54 he describes attacks of breathlessness, which doctors called a rehearsal for death (54.1–2). Modern biographers diagnose asthma or tuberculosis; the texts give symptoms, not a disease.

The same aunt who had carried him to Rome nursed him through a long illness and used her influence when he stood for the quaestorship (Consolation to Helvia 19.2). Her husband was prefect of Egypt; the identification with Gaius Galerius, prefect from about 16 to 31, is modern, since Seneca does not name him. She lost him on the voyage home and brought his body back through a storm (19.4–7). Modern biographers place Seneca in Egypt with them and date his return to that voyage, about 31; Seneca says neither outright. The quaestorship followed in the 30s; its year is unknown. It made him a senator.

He was also making a name as an orator, and that nearly killed him. Dio says that Gaius ordered his death because he had pleaded a case well in the Senate in the emperor's presence, and let him off only because a woman of the court said he was so far gone in consumption that he would soon die anyway (Dio 59.19.7–8, under 39). Suetonius preserves the emperor's literary verdict: Seneca's compositions were mere showpieces, sand without lime (Caligula 53.2).

02Corsica, 41–49

The new reign brought a new danger. Within months of Claudius' accession in 41 Messalina had Claudius exile his niece Julia Livilla on a charge of adultery, and Seneca was exiled with her as the adulterer (Dio 60.8.5). The sentence seems to have been capital at first: Seneca thanks Claudius for asking the Senate for his life (Consolation to Polybius 13.2). He was relegated to Corsica and spent eight years there.

Two works survive from the island, and they pull in opposite directions. The Consolation to Helvia consoles his mother for his exile with Stoic doctrine: exile is only a change of place, nature and virtue go with the exile wherever he goes, and the barren rock of Corsica is no worse than the city (6–9). The Consolation to Polybius, addressed to Claudius' powerful freedman on his brother's death, consoles Polybius conventionally and spends its later chapters on Claudius: the emperor's clemency, the hope that he will one day look on the exile's case, and praise of his learning (12.3–13.4). It reads as a petition. Claudius did not answer it, and Dio says Messalina later had Polybius put to death (61(60).31.2; here and below, Dio by the Loeb's book with the traditional book in brackets).

Where sources disagree. Dio presents the adultery as Messalina's invention to destroy a rival (60.8.5); Suillius in Tacitus, an enemy, treats it as fact, calling Seneca an adulterer in the house of Germanicus (Annals 13.42.3). Modern biographers divide in the same way, and the verdict is uncertain. A second question is whether the Consolation to Polybius is the book Dio says Seneca sent from exile in praise of Messalina and the freedmen and later suppressed in shame (61.10.2): the surviving text praises a freedman and Claudius but not Messalina, so the identification is plausible but unproved.

03Tutor and minister: Agrippina, Burrus and On Clemency

In 49 Claudius married Agrippina, who at once had Seneca recalled and made praetor, so that her son Domitius might grow up under such a teacher. Tacitus gives her reasons: Seneca's studies were famous, the recall would be popular, and he would be loyal to her for the favour and hostile to Claudius for the injury (Annals 12.8.2). Domitius, adopted as Nero in 50, was twelve. In 51 Agrippina had Afranius Burrus made sole prefect of the guard (12.42).

When Nero succeeded in October 54, at sixteen, the two men governed. Tacitus calls them the guides of the emperor's youth, of one mind and each strong in his own way: Burrus by military discipline and strict morals, Seneca by his teaching of eloquence and a dignified courtesy (Annals 13.2). Dio says they took the government into their own hands and ran it as well and as fairly as they could (61.4.1). Seneca wrote Nero's first speeches (Dio 61.3.1; module 7.1 gives their promises) and the funeral oration for Claudius, at which the senators laughed; Tacitus notes that Nero was the first emperor to need borrowed eloquence, and that Seneca's talent was pleasing and suited to the ears of his time (13.3). Seneca was suffect consul in 56. The poisoning of Britannicus in 55 (13.15–17) was followed by gifts of the dead prince's houses and estates to Nero's friends, and Tacitus records the reproach that men who professed austerity had shared them out like plunder (13.18.1).

On Clemency, addressed to Nero in his nineteenth year (1.9.1), so between December 55 and December 56 (Nero was born in December 37), was meant to act as a mirror in which the prince could see himself (1.1.1). It opens with Nero's imagined soliloquy: has he alone of mortals been chosen to act on earth in the gods' place, arbiter of life and death for nations (1.1.2)? The prince is the mind of the commonwealth and the people its body (1.5.1). Augustus' clemency came late, a cruelty grown tired (1.11.2); Nero's reign, by contrast, has kept the city free of blood (1.11.3). Book 2 defines clemency as restraint of the mind when it has the power to punish (2.3.1), and sets it apart from pity, which the Stoic counts a weakness: the wise man will not pity but will help (2.4–6). The work announces a division into three parts (1.3.1); the first book and the opening of the second survive.

Where sources disagree. Seneca tells Nero that his reign has shed no blood (On Clemency 1.11.3); Tacitus narrates the poisoning of Britannicus months earlier in full and the court's silence after it (Annals 13.15–17). Read with Tacitus the work looks like the regime's propaganda; read alone it is advice in the form of praise, binding the prince to the conduct it praises. Whether Seneca believed the mirror or held it up as a warning cannot be settled from the text.

The partnership broke over Agrippina. In March 59, when the collapsing boat had failed, Nero summoned the two men. Seneca, Tacitus writes, looked at Burrus and asked whether the soldiers should be ordered to kill her; Burrus answered that the guard would not harm a daughter of Germanicus (Annals 14.7). Afterwards Seneca drafted the letter to the Senate that accused Agrippina of plotting, and it was held against him that he had written a confession (14.11.3). Dio goes further and reports that Seneca was said to have urged the murder (62(61).12.1).

04The fortune and the charge of hypocrisy

The minister became one of the richest men in Rome. In 58 the old prosecutor Publius Suillius, facing charges himself, attacked Seneca openly. By what wisdom, he asked, by what precepts of philosophy had Seneca acquired three hundred million sesterces in four years of the emperor's friendship? At Rome he hunted the wills of the childless; Italy and the provinces were drained by his enormous rates of interest; he was hostile to Claudius' friends, under whom he had been justly exiled; Suillius had been Germanicus' quaestor, Seneca an adulterer in Germanicus' house; and he envied men who practised real eloquence in defence of citizens (Annals 13.42). Suillius was convicted, stripped of half his property and sent to the Balearic Islands (13.43). The three hundred million is Suillius' figure, given by an accuser.

Dio, writing a century and a half later, turns the charge into a list. While denouncing tyranny Seneca made himself the teacher of a tyrant; while attacking the rich he amassed three hundred million sesterces; he owned five hundred tables of citrus wood with ivory legs, all alike, and gave banquets on them; and Dio adds the adultery with Agrippina and the flattering book from exile (61.10). Module 7.1 records Dio's further claim that Seneca's sudden recall of forty million sesterces in loans helped provoke Boudica's revolt (62.2.1).

On the Happy Life, addressed to his brother Gallio, puts the objection in the reader's mouth: why do you speak better than you live, why does your wife wear a rich house's income in her ears, why do you own estates overseas (17.1–2)? Seneca answers first that he is not a wise man and never will be: he speaks of virtue, not of himself, and is waging war on his own vices (17.3–18.1). He then answers on wealth itself. The philosopher may have ample wealth, so long as it was taken from no one and stained with no blood (23.1); riches are a servant to the wise and a master to the fool (26.1; module 13.2 places the doctrine of preferred indifferents behind the reply). The date is not fixed, but the coincidence with Suillius' attack makes 58 or soon after the usual guess.

Where sources disagree. Tacitus frames Suillius as a corrupt informer and lets Seneca's fall in 62 come from the jealous courtiers' charges (Annals 13.42–43, 14.52); Dio states the charge of hypocrisy in his own voice (61.10). Among modern biographers, James Romm (Dying Every Day: Seneca at the Court of Nero, 2014) tells the career as a tragedy of entrapment, in which the hope of guiding Nero drew Seneca step by step into complicity, while Miriam Griffin (Seneca: A Philosopher in Politics, 1976) treated the works as philosophy to be read for doctrine, not as coded apology for a political life.

05Withdrawal, 62–65

Burrus died in 62, and with him, Tacitus says, Seneca's power broke. Tigellinus rose; the accusers told Nero that Seneca's wealth exceeded a private man's, that his gardens and villas outdid the emperor's, that he claimed the glory of eloquence for himself and wrote poems more often since Nero had taken to them (Annals 14.52). Seneca asked for an audience. He had attached himself to Nero's hopes fourteen years before, he said, and Nero had reigned eight; he offered his fortune back, to be managed by Nero's procurators, and asked leave to retire (14.53–54). Nero refused, embraced and kissed him. Seneca thanked him, as all conversations with a master end, and then changed his way of life: no more crowds of callers, no escort, rare visits to the city, as if kept at home by ill health or philosophy (14.55–56).

After the fire of 64 he asked again to retire to a distant estate, was refused, and kept to his room pleading illness. Tacitus reports a rumour that Nero had his freedman Cleonicus try to poison him, and that Seneca escaped by living on wild fruit and spring water (15.45.3). The withdrawal was the most productive period of his life: the Letters, the Natural Questions and probably the end of On Benefits belong to these three years.

06The works

Seneca wrote in every prose form a Roman moralist could use, and the tragedies besides (module 10.2 reads them as literature; the satire on Claudius is module 7.1's). The twelve books of Dialogues reach us chiefly through one eleventh-century manuscript at Milan, the Ambrosianus; the name is the manuscript's, and only some of them are dialogues in any sense, with an addressee who occasionally objects. Few can be dated securely.

On Benefits is the longest prose work and the most Roman in subject: it analyses the exchange of favours on which patronage, friendship and the court ran. It rejects Chrysippus' elaborate allegory of the three Graces as trifling (1.3.8–1.4.6) and argues that ingratitude, though the worst of vices, should not be actionable at law, since a benefit forced by law would cease to be one (3.6–17). The Natural Questions treat physics as a road to the moral: to study the heavens is to see how small are the things men fight over (1 preface). Book 6 begins from the Campanian earthquake; Seneca dates it to the consuls of 63 (6.1.2), Tacitus to 62 (Annals 15.22.2).

The Letters are addressed to Lucilius, an equestrian procurator of Sicily (Natural Questions 4a preface 1). Whether the correspondence was real or a literary fiction is debated; Lucilius' replies are never given, and the letters grow from a page with a closing maxim into treatises. Gellius quotes from a twenty-second book (12.2.3), so the collection was once larger. They are where most readers have met Seneca since antiquity, and where the Stoicism of the next section is clearest.

07Seneca's Stoicism: Epicurus, independence and the nightly review

Module 13.2 sets out the doctrines Seneca reshaped: the first movements, providence and the good man's suffering, the two commonwealths, the fates that lead the willing. What marks his own practice is a manner of using the school.

He claims the right to judge. He binds himself, he says, to no one of the Stoic masters (On the Happy Life 3.2); those who went before are guides, not masters (Letters 33.11). The first thirty letters each end with a small gift, usually a saying, and most of the sayings are Epicurus'. Seneca explains: he crosses into the enemy's camp not as a deserter but as a scout (2.5), and whatever is true is his (12.11). His friend the Cynic Demetrius, praised again and again in the Letters and On Benefits, belongs to module 13.3. On the Epicureans themselves he is fair: their teaching is holy and upright, and on closer view austere; the bad name comes from those who take the word pleasure for a licence (On the Happy Life 13.1–2).

The habit that has drawn most attention is the review at the day's end. The philosopher Sextius, Seneca writes, used to ask his mind each night what ill it had cured that day, what vice it had resisted, in what respect it was better (On Anger 3.36.1). Seneca does the same. When the lamp is taken away and his wife, who knows the habit, has fallen silent, he examines his whole day and measures his words and deeds, hiding nothing from himself (3.36.3). He gives samples: you spoke too sharply in that dispute; do not argue with the ignorant; you rebuked that man more freely than you should, and did not correct him but offended him (3.36.4). The verdict is always the same: this time I pardon you; see that you do not do it again. The practice has a Pythagorean pedigree, the three questions of the Golden Verses, and Sextius' school was Pythagorean in its habits (module 13.3).

Where sources disagree. Michel Foucault ("Technologies of the Self", 1988) read On Anger 3.36 as an administrator's audit, a review of acts against rules to correct the next day's conduct, not a judicial confession of hidden sins of the kind Christian monasticism later required. Pierre Hadot (Philosophy as a Way of Life, 1995) accepted the exercise as central but objected that Foucault's "care of the self" made ancient ethics too aesthetic: the Stoic examines himself in order to identify with universal reason, not to fashion a self.

08The death in 65: a Socratic performance

In April 65 Piso's conspiracy was betrayed (module 7.1). Nero, Tacitus says, was glad of a pretext against Seneca, though his guilt was unproved. The conspirator Antonius Natalis testified that he had been sent to Seneca, then ill, to complain that he would not see Piso; Seneca had replied that meetings served neither of them, but that his own safety depended on Piso's (Annals 15.60). Whether he knew more is uncertain; Tacitus reports a rumour that some conspirators meant to kill Piso after Nero and give the empire to Seneca (15.65).

The tribune Gavius Silvanus, himself a conspirator, found him at a villa four miles from Rome, dining with his wife Pompeia Paulina and two friends (15.60–61). Refused tablets to change his will, Seneca told his friends he left them the one thing he still had and the finest, the image of his life (imago vitae suae, 15.62). He checked their tears: where were the precepts of philosophy, the reasoning practised for so many years against what was coming? Who had not known Nero's cruelty? After killing his mother and brother, nothing remained but to kill his teacher (15.62).

Paulina chose to die with him, and both cut their arms. Seneca's old body, thinned by spare living, bled slowly, so he cut the veins of his legs and knees; to spare each other the sight, he had her taken to another room. Even then he called secretaries and dictated at length; Tacitus declines to paraphrase the words, since they had been published (15.63). Nero, having no grudge against Paulina, ordered her saved; she lived a few more years, her face and limbs pale (15.64). Seneca asked his doctor and friend Statius Annaeus for the poison long prepared, the one used to kill those condemned by the public court of the Athenians; it failed, his limbs already cold. He entered a pool of warm water, sprinkled the slaves nearest him, and said he offered the liquid to Jupiter the Liberator. Carried into a steam bath, he suffocated. He was cremated without ceremony, as he had directed in a codicil written when he was at the height of his wealth and power (15.64).

The script is Plato's, with a Roman precedent behind it: Cato's death at Utica, which Seneca had held up as a model (module 13.4 has Seneca's Cato). The friends round the condemned man, the rebuke of their tears, the conversation to the end, the Athenian poison and the libation all recall the Phaedo, where Socrates asks whether he may pour a libation from the cup (117b). The libation to Jupiter the Liberator turns death into the freedom Seneca had preached: the way out lies open (On Providence 6.7). The poison that failed and the steam that finished him make it a Socratic death that did not go to plan. James Ker (The Deaths of Seneca, 2009) traces how Seneca had rehearsed this death in his writings and how later readers re-staged it. Dio's epitome tells it otherwise: Seneca, whom it names with the prefect Rufus among the leaders of the plot (62.24.1), wished Paulina to die with him and opened her veins as well as his own, and the soldiers hastened his end (62.25.1–2).

Where sources disagree. Tacitus narrates Paulina's resolve to share her husband's death (Annals 15.63), then gives the crowd's reading, always ready to believe the worse: she sought the glory of dying with him while Nero seemed implacable and accepted life once he relented (15.64.2). Seneca's own letters show the other side, a husband who looks after his health because Paulina's life depends on his (Letters 104.1–2). Tacitus also leaves open how far Seneca's death was staged, and whether his narrative admires or quietly ironises the performance is still argued.

09Quintilian's verdict and the style

Seneca's style, short clauses, antithesis, the epigram that closes each thought, was the fashion of his youth taken to its limit. Quintilian, teaching a generation later, defers him to the end of his reading list on purpose (10.1.125). He had been thought to condemn Seneca, he explains, because he tried to call back to severer standards a corrupt style weakened by every fault, at a time when Seneca was almost the only author in the hands of the young; they loved him more than they imitated him, and fell as far below him as he had fallen below the ancients (125–127).

The verdict that follows is balanced. Seneca had many great virtues: an easy and abundant talent, great industry and wide knowledge, though he was sometimes deceived by those he set to research for him (128). He wrote speeches, poems, letters and dialogues; in philosophy he was not careful, but he was a splendid assailant of vices; in expression most of his work was corrupt, and the most dangerous because it abounds in sweet faults (abundant dulcibus vitiis, 129). One would wish he had written with his own talent and someone else's judgement; had he not loved everything of his own, and not broken the weight of his matter with tiny epigrams, he would be approved by the consensus of the learned rather than the love of boys (130). He is to be read by those already formed, for practice in judgement; there is much to approve and even to admire, if one chooses, as he should have chosen himself (131). Gellius, in the next century, was harsher, and recorded critics who found him trivial (12.2).

10The Christian Seneca

Christian writers claimed him early. Tertullian, citing him on the soul, calls him "Seneca, often ours" (Seneca saepe noster, On the Soul 20.1): often, not always, a pagan whose moral teaching ran close to the Church's. Lactantius, early in the fourth century, judged that Seneca could have been a true worshipper of God if someone had shown him the way (Divine Institutes 6.24).

Someone soon did. A correspondence of fourteen Latin letters, eight from Seneca and six from Paul, appeared in the fourth century: Seneca admires Paul's teaching and regrets his style; Paul answers with gratitude and urges caution at court. The Latin is not Seneca's, the content thin, and the chronology impossible in places. Jerome nonetheless admitted Seneca to his catalogue of Christian writers on the strength of these letters, "which are read by very many" (On Illustrious Men 12), and Augustine mentions them (Letters 153.14). The brother who had dismissed the charges against Paul at Corinth (Acts 18.12–17) made the meeting easy to imagine. Dante placed "Seneca the moralist" among the virtuous pagans in Limbo (Inferno 4.141); Renaissance editors, Erasmus among them, rejected the letters, and no modern scholar defends them.

Where sources disagree. Jerome treats the letters as grounds for counting Seneca among Christian writers (On Illustrious Men 12); Lactantius, writing earlier, evidently did not know them, since he says no one had shown Seneca the truth (Divine Institutes 6.24). Modern scholars agree the letters are a fourth-century forgery; why it was made is not known.

11Self-test

  1. What did the elder Seneca write, and what does his family show about where Roman careers could start?
    Answer

    The Controversiae and Suasoriae, the declaimers' sayings recalled for his sons; he had missed hearing Cicero because the civil wars kept him at Corduba (Controversiae 1 preface 11). An equestrian family from a Spanish colony produced a proconsul (Gallio), a rich equestrian (Mela, Lucan's father) and a consul and imperial minister (Seneca).

  2. Why did Seneca give up vegetarianism, and what does the episode show about his father?
    Answer

    In the first years of Tiberius foreign rites were being expelled and abstaining from certain meats could look like adherence to them, so his father asked him to stop; Seneca says his father did not fear slander but hated philosophy (Letters 108.22). The episode shows a provincial equestrian family's suspicion of philosophy as a way of life.

  3. How did Seneca nearly die under Gaius, and how did he escape?
    Answer

    Dio says Gaius ordered his death for pleading a case brilliantly in the Senate before him, then spared him because a woman of the court said he would soon die of consumption (59.19.7–8).

  4. How do the two consolations written on Corsica differ?
    Answer

    The Consolation to Helvia argues as a Stoic that exile is only a change of place and that virtue goes with the exile (6–9). The Consolation to Polybius praises Claudius' clemency and learning to a powerful freedman and hopes for recall (12.3–13.4), which makes it read as a petition; whether it is the book Dio says Seneca later suppressed (61.10.2) is unproved.

  5. Why did Agrippina recall Seneca in 49, according to Tacitus?
    Answer

    To have him teach her son, because his learning was famous and the recall would be popular, and because she expected him to be loyal to her for the favour and hostile to Claudius for the exile (Annals 12.8.2).

  6. What is the image that opens On Clemency, and why does the claim of 1.11.3 trouble readers?
    Answer

    The work is to act as a mirror in which Nero sees himself (1.1.1), followed by his soliloquy as the gods' agent with power of life and death (1.1.2). Its praise of a bloodless reign (1.11.3) was written months after Britannicus was poisoned (Annals 13.15–17).

  7. What exactly did Suillius charge in 58, and how does On the Happy Life answer such charges?
    Answer

    That Seneca had made three hundred million sesterces in four years of Nero's friendship, by legacy-hunting and usury, and was an adulterer exiled justly (Annals 13.42). Seneca replies that he is not wise but making progress (17.3–18.1), and that a philosopher may hold wealth taken from no one, which serves the wise and rules the fool (23.1, 26.1).

  8. What happened when Seneca asked to retire in 62, and how did he live afterwards?
    Answer

    He offered Nero his fortune and asked leave to withdraw; Nero refused and embraced him (Annals 14.53–56). Seneca gave up his receptions and escorts and rarely visited the city, and after a second refusal in 64 kept to his room (15.45); these were the years of the Letters and the Natural Questions.

  9. Which features of Tacitus' account make Seneca's death Socratic, and where did the script fail?
    Answer

    Friends round him, the rebuke of their tears, the image of his life as bequest, dictation to the end, the Athenian poison and the libation to Jupiter the Liberator recall the Phaedo (Annals 15.62–64). The veins bled slowly and the poison failed, so he died in a steam bath.

  10. What does Quintilian praise and blame in Seneca, and what does Tertullian's phrase "often ours" concede?
    Answer

    Quintilian praises talent, industry, knowledge and his attack on vices, and blames carelessness in philosophy and a corrupt style full of sweet faults that broke weighty matter into small epigrams (10.1.128–130). Tertullian's saepe noster (On the Soul 20.1) claims Seneca's moral teaching for Christians while admitting he was not one of them.

12Carry forward

  1. Seneca's works and his career cannot be read apart, and neither should be read as a simple key to the other: exile, ministry and withdrawal shaped what he wrote, but the dialogues argue doctrine, and dating them by their supposed political occasion is circular.
  2. Roman Stoicism became a practice of the person making progress: the letter with its closing maxim, the borrowing from Epicurus, the nightly review of the day. Watch for the same techniques in Musonius, Epictetus and the notebook of Marcus Aurelius.
  3. A philosopher's death could be composed as an argument: Seneca staged his on Socrates, and Tacitus wrote it so that it could be admired or doubted. Cato before him and Thrasea after him make the same claim, and each account must be read for who composed it.
