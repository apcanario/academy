From Actium to the sixth consulship, 31–28 BC

Module 5.5 ends with Egypt annexed in 30. The only continuous narrative of what follows is Cassius Dio, books 51–56, written two centuries later by a senator who thought monarchy the only workable government for an empire; Livy's books on these years are lost. Velleius Paterculus (2.88–123) wrote under Tiberius as an admirer, Suetonius' Augustus arranges its matter by topic, and Tacitus looks back from the opening of the Annals. Augustus' own Res Gestae is the fullest witness and the least neutral.

In August 29 Octavian held three triumphs on successive days, for Illyricum, Actium and Egypt (Suetonius, Augustus 22), and the temple of Janus was shut: closed, the Res Gestae boasts, only twice since the foundation before his birth and three times while he was princeps (13). As consul for the sixth time in 28, with Agrippa as colleague, he took the first completed census since 70 and revised the Senate roll (Res Gestae 8). In this consulship, Tacitus says, he annulled what he had ordered as triumvir (Annals 3.28), and an aureus of 28, published by John Rich and Jonathan Williams in 1999, proclaims that he restored to the Roman people their laws and rights. Legality was being rebuilt before the grand gesture.

The settlement of January 27 BC

On 13 January 27 Octavian, consul for the seventh time, laid down his powers before the Senate. Dio composes a long speech for him (53.3–10); the Res Gestae needs one sentence: having ended the civil wars, and being by universal consent in control of all affairs, he transferred the res publica from his own power to the judgement of the Senate and people (34.1). The senators begged him to keep a share. He accepted for ten years a single provincia of Spain, Gaul and Syria, with Egypt already his: the regions where most of the legions stood. The peaceful provinces went to proconsuls chosen by lot (Dio 53.12–13; Strabo 17.3.25). Dio saw the point: under pretence of taking the burden, he left the senators unarmed and kept the soldiers (53.12.3). His own legates governed his provinces, the legions swore to him, the grant was renewed until his death, and after Balbus' triumph in 19 BC no one outside his family triumphed. Module 6.4 takes the two kinds of province as administration.

On 16 January, by the Praenestine calendar (Ovid, Fasti 1.587–590, puts it on the Ides), the Senate on the motion of Munatius Plancus named him Augustus (Suetonius, Augustus 7.2). Romulus had been proposed, but Romulus was a king and a fratricide. The new name carried no power. Augustus meant hallowed, akin to augur, and Suetonius quotes Ennius on Rome founded by august augury (7.2). An oak crown over his door and a golden shield in the Senate house proclaiming his valour, clemency, justice and piety came with it (Res Gestae 34.2). His style was now Imperator Caesar Augustus: a victor's acclamation, a murdered dictator's name and a word with no constitutional meaning.

The theory came later. After that time, he wrote, he excelled all in auctoritas, influence, but had no more potestas, legal power, than his colleagues in each magistracy (Res Gestae 34.3); the word was long restored as dignitas until the copy from Pisidian Antioch settled it. True of each office, the claim is false of the whole, for no colleague held a consulship, the great provinces and the legions at once. Princeps, "first man", was a courtesy, not an office.

Where sources disagree. Velleius says that in 27 the ancient form of the res publica was recalled, with force restored to the laws and majesty to the Senate (2.89.3–4); Dio dates monarchy in the strict sense from the same moment (53.17.1). E. A. Judge ("Res publica restituta: a modern illusion?", 1974) argued that no Augustan text claims to have restored "the Republic" as a constitution: res publica meant the commonwealth, and what was restored was its order and legality, a reading the aureus of 28, published in 1999, fits.

The crisis of 23 BC and the second settlement

The settlement of 27 rested on a consulship Augustus held every year from 31 to 23, which left the nobility one consulship a year instead of two. In 23 he nearly died. Expecting the end, he gave his fellow consul Piso a record of the forces and revenues and Agrippa his signet ring, naming no successor (Dio 53.30.1–2). His doctor Antonius Musa's cold baths saved him (Suetonius, Augustus 81.1), but not his nephew and son-in-law Marcellus, who died late in the year. About this time Marcus Primus, governor of Macedonia, on trial for an unauthorised war, claimed instructions from Augustus or Marcellus; his advocate Murena, Maecenas' brother-in-law, was soon condemned with Fannius Caepio for conspiracy and killed (Dio 54.3).

In the summer of 23 Augustus resigned the consulship, so that more men could hold it, Dio says (53.32.3), and received two powers in its place (53.32.5). Tribunicia potestas for life gave him a tribune's rights without the office: to summon Senate and people, propose laws, veto and protect any citizen. He numbered his years by it (Res Gestae 4.4), and Tacitus calls it the title for supreme power that Augustus devised so as not to take the name of king or dictator (Annals 3.56). Proconsular imperium that did not lapse inside the city, and was greater than that of any proconsul, secured the provinces and armies. Agrippa went east with imperium of his own.

Where sources disagree. Dio puts Primus' trial and the conspiracy in 22, after the new settlement (54.3). But the list of consuls for 23 names a Varro Murena as Augustus' colleague, replaced by Piso, and many modern historians move the conspiracy into 23 and make it, with the illness, a cause of the resignation; others keep Dio's date and doubt that consul and conspirator were one man. Whether greater imperium came in 23 or later is argued too; a papyrus of Augustus' funeral speech for Agrippa shows such greater imperium voted to Agrippa by law by 13 BC.

Refusals and the father of his country

In 22, amid plague, flood and famine, the people shut the senators in their meeting-place and threatened to burn them unless they made Augustus dictator (Dio 54.1). He knelt, Suetonius says, and begged with bared breast to be spared (Augustus 52). The Res Gestae records the refusal and, in the same breath, the charge he did accept: the grain supply, by which at his own expense he freed the city from fear within a few days (5.1–2). He also refused a perpetual consulship (5.3) and, in 19, 18 and 11, the supervision of laws and morals, as an office contrary to ancestral custom; what the Senate wanted done he did through tribunician power (6). Caesar had accepted the dictatorship for life and the prefecture of morals (module 5.4). Augustus declined each title that looked like kingship and kept its function. The chief priesthood waited for Lepidus' death (module 6.2).

Where sources disagree. Against Res Gestae 6, Dio says that in 19 he accepted the supervision of morals for five years and consular power for life (54.10.5), and Suetonius that he received the supervision of morals and laws for life (Augustus 27.5). Most modern historians trust Augustus on the title and read the others as describing what he did. The lifelong consular power remains disputed, though the Res Gestae says he took two censuses "with consular imperium" (8.3–4).

On 5 February 2 BC the Senate, the equestrian order and the whole people hailed him pater patriae, father of his country, the honour on which the Res Gestae ends (35.1). Suetonius has the plebs offer it twice before Valerius Messalla spoke for the Senate and Augustus answered in tears that his prayers were fulfilled (Augustus 58). Cicero had been so hailed after 63 (Against Piso 6), and Caesar had borne the title (module 5.4); this father was the living patron of every citizen. Within months he exiled his daughter.

The Res Gestae: Augustus' own account

Augustus left with his will an account of his achievements to be inscribed on bronze before his Mausoleum (Suetonius, Augustus 101.4); module 0.1 introduces the text and its best copy, at Ancyra. It dates itself to his seventy-sixth year (35.2) and runs from honours (1–14) through spending (15–24) and conquests (25–33, module 6.4) to the constitutional claim (34–35). Module 5.5 has set its account of 44–30 against the record.

It tells few direct lies; it lies by arrangement and silence. Antony is only "a faction" (1.1); Lepidus is the colleague who seized the chief priesthood in a time of civil disturbance (10.2). Caesar's killers went into exile "by lawful judgements" (2), which means the court of the lex Pedia and then Philippi. The proscriptions, Perusia and the confiscations are absent; the triumvirate gets one sentence (7.1). He spared all citizens who asked for pardon (3.1), where Suetonius says that once the proscription began he was harsher than either colleague (Augustus 27.1). Gaius and Lucius appear as "my sons, whom fortune snatched from me" (14.1), Tiberius as stepson and then son, Marcellus only as the name on a theatre (21.1); Julia, Postumus and Varus' lost legions do not.

The inner circle and the problem of succession

Three people stood closest. Marcus Agrippa, of a family so obscure that he dropped its name Vipsanius, won Naulochus and Actium, was consul three times and rebuilt Rome's water supply. Maecenas, an equestrian of Etruscan royal descent (Horace, Odes 1.1.1), held no office yet ran Rome and Italy in Octavian's absences and patronised the poets; his eclipse after the Murena affair is in module 6.3. Livia, married to Octavian in 38 while pregnant by her first husband (deep dive module 11.4), held no office but unusual standing: sacrosanctity from 35 (Dio 49.38.1), and a husband who wrote out beforehand anything serious he meant to say to her (Suetonius, Augustus 84.2).

The Principate was not an office and could not be inherited, and Augustus had no son. Each plan tied a man to Julia, his only child, and gave him a share of the powers (Tacitus, Annals 1.3).

Agrippa's marriage was a necessity: Maecenas warned, Dio reports, that Augustus had made him so great that he must become his son-in-law or be killed (54.6.5). When Lucius and Gaius died within eighteen months, by fate's haste or their stepmother Livia's guile, as Tacitus insinuates (Annals 1.3), only Tiberius was left. On 26 June AD 4 Augustus adopted him, swearing that he did it for the sake of the state (Suetonius, Tiberius 21.3), and made him adopt Germanicus, soon to marry Augustus' granddaughter Agrippina, to keep Augustus' blood in the succession.

Julia's fall in 2 BC belongs here; the adultery law is module 6.2's. Augustus denounced her adulteries to the Senate by letter (Suetonius, Augustus 65.2) and sent her to the island of Pandateria. Her lovers were exiled, and Iullus Antonius, Antony's son and consul in 10 BC, died by his own hand in Velleius' account (2.100.4), by execution in Tacitus' (Annals 4.44). Augustus called Julia, her daughter and Postumus his three boils and three cancers (Suetonius, Augustus 65.4).

Where sources disagree. Velleius (2.100) and Seneca (On Benefits 6.32) tell a scandal of lust, with revels on the Rostra from which her father had proposed his adultery law. Pliny pairs her adultery with plans of parricide (Natural History 7.149), Dio says Iullus aimed at the monarchy (55.10.15), and Tacitus that Augustus charged adultery as sacrilege and treason (Annals 3.24). Syme (The Crisis of 2 B.C., 1974) read a conspiracy over the succession; others take the scandal as reported. Uncertain.

AD 14 and Tacitus, Annals 1.2–10

Augustus died at Nola on 19 August AD 14, having asked his friends whether he had played the comedy of life well (Suetonius, Augustus 99–100). Tacitus says Livia guarded the house and roads until his death and Tiberius' succession could be announced together, and that some suspected her of hastening the end (Annals 1.5). The will named Tiberius and Livia heirs (Suetonius, Augustus 101.2), and the first crime of the new principate was the killing of Agrippa Postumus (Annals 1.6). Tiberius' accession is module 7.1.

Where sources disagree. Tiberius claimed his father's standing order for Postumus' death. Tacitus thinks Tiberius' fear and Livia's hatred nearer the truth (Annals 1.6); Suetonius leaves open whether Augustus or Livia gave the order and whether Tiberius knew (Tiberius 22); Dio says Tiberius had it done (57.3.6).

Tacitus wrote a century later, a senator who had held office under Domitian. His first movement (1.2) is a cold narrative: with his rivals dead or stripped of power, Octavian dropped the title of triumvir and was content with tribunician power to protect the plebs; he won the soldiers with gifts, the people with grain and everyone with the sweetness of peace, and drew to himself the functions of Senate, magistrates and laws, unopposed, since the boldest had fallen and the surviving nobles found servility profitable. The second (1.3) traces the dynastic plans and ends: the magistracies kept their names, and how few were left who had seen the res publica?

The third (1.9–10) is the verdict of men talking at the funeral. For him: civil war forced on him by duty to his father, neither kingship nor dictatorship but a princeps, an empire bounded by ocean and rivers, the city adorned, force used rarely. Against him: duty a pretext for power, bribed veterans, the proscriptions, Sextus, Lepidus and Antony deceived in turn, the bloody peace of Lollius and Varus, temples to himself, and Tiberius chosen so that a worse successor would make him shine. The hostile case is longer and comes last; Tacitus never says which he believes, and the arrangement judges for him. Dio's obituary concludes the reverse: by mixing monarchy with democracy Augustus kept the Romans free and safe (56.43).

Syme and the favourable readings

Ronald Syme's The Roman Revolution (1939), written as Europe's dictators rose and in a Tacitean style, told the Principate as the victory of a faction: Caesar's generals and the new families of the Italian towns took the state from the old nobility, and the settlements of 27 and 23 were a facade over army, money and clients. His method was prosopography, and constitutional law counts for little beside who held which post. Deep dive module 11.8 places him among the explanations of the Republic's fall.

The favourable readings start from the forms. Mommsen's Römisches Staatsrecht called the Principate a dyarchy of princeps and Senate. Andrew Wallace-Hadrill ("Civilis princeps: between citizen and king", 1982) argued that the emperor's citizen manners (Suetonius, Augustus 53–56) were the price of aristocratic cooperation and a working part of the system, not a disguise. Karl Galinsky (Augustan Culture, 1996) made auctoritas an authority that drew consent, so that Augustan culture was made with him, not imposed. Syme asks who held power; his critics ask how a monarch made Romans willing to be governed.

Why the arrangement worked

Augustus ruled for forty-four years after Actium; Caesar lasted five after the Rubicon.

  • The army was his alone. It stood in his provinces under his legates, and from AD 6 its discharge bounties came from his military treasury (module 6.4). No senator could again raise a private army, as Octavian had in 44.
  • The forms survived. King and dictator were refused, each power had a republican name, Senate and magistrates went on working, and the consulship stayed a prize.
  • Exhaustion. After twenty years of civil war, peace, property and grain outweighed the liberty of a nobility the wars had thinned.
  • A wider governing class. Italian new men became consuls; equestrians became prefects of Egypt, the guard and the grain supply.
  • Flexibility and time. Powers were revised as needed, and by AD 14 few remembered anything else.

The succession failed. Powers voted to a person carried no rule of inheritance, only the family and the soldiers' loyalty to it.

Self-test

  1. What did Octavian give up in January 27 BC, what did he receive, and why did the division of provinces matter more than the gesture?
    Answer

    He transferred the res publica to the Senate and people (Res Gestae 34.1) and received for ten years Spain, Gaul and Syria, besides Egypt, governed by his own legates, while proconsuls chosen by lot took the rest (Dio 53.12–13; Strabo 17.3.25). His provinces held most of the legions, so, as Dio saw, the senators were left unarmed and the army stayed his (53.12.3).

  2. What did the name Augustus and the claim to auctoritas add to his position?
    Answer

    No legal power. The name came from religion, the hallowed, akin to augur (Suetonius, Augustus 7.2), and avoided Romulus' associations with kingship. Auctoritas was influence rather than potestas (Res Gestae 34.3): true for each office, misleading for a man who held consulship, provinces and legions at once.

  3. Why did Augustus give up the consulship in 23 BC, and what replaced it?
    Answer

    Holding one consulship every year blocked the nobility's highest prize, and his near-fatal illness and, on the modern dating, the Murena conspiracy exposed the regime's fragility. He received tribunician power for life and proconsular imperium that held inside Rome and was greater than any proconsul's (Dio 53.32.5); Tacitus calls tribunician power a name for supremacy that avoided king and dictator (Annals 3.56).

  4. How do the refusals in Res Gestae 5–6 answer Caesar's honours, and what complicates the record?
    Answer

    Caesar took the dictatorship for life and the prefecture of morals (module 5.4); Augustus refused the dictatorship in 22, a perpetual consulship and the supervision of laws and morals, accepting only the grain supply and acting through tribunician power. Dio 54.10.5 and Suetonius, Augustus 27.5, say he did accept the supervision of morals.

  5. Why is 2 BC a revealing year for the regime?
    Answer

    On 5 February Augustus was hailed father of his country by Senate, equestrians and people (Res Gestae 35.1; Suetonius, Augustus 58), the climax of his honours. In the same year he exiled Julia, the mother of his heirs, and Iullus Antonius died, a scandal that Pliny 7.149, Dio 55.10.15 and Tacitus, Annals 3.24, hint was also a political plot.

  6. Give three omissions in the Res Gestae and what each conceals.
    Answer

    Antony is only "a faction" and Lepidus unnamed (1.1, 10.2), hiding that his enemies were Romans and colleagues; the proscriptions and Perusia are absent, though Suetonius, Augustus 27.1, makes him the harshest triumvir; Julia, Postumus and Varus are absent, hiding the failures of the family and the frontier.

  7. What method lay behind the succession plans from Marcellus to Tiberius?
    Answer

    Each candidate was bound to Julia by marriage or descent, or adopted, and given a share of Augustus' powers, tribunician power or greater imperium, to mark him as successor to something that was not an office. Marcellus, Agrippa, Lucius and Gaius died in turn, leaving Tiberius, adopted in AD 4 for the sake of the state, as Augustus swore (Suetonius, Tiberius 21.3).

  8. How does Tacitus pass judgement on Augustus in Annals 1.2–10 without stating his own?
    Answer

    His narrative in 1.2 shows power gathered by gifts, grain and peace while the nobles were dead or servile. In 1.9–10 he gives two verdicts at the funeral; the hostile one is longer and comes last, ending with Tiberius chosen so that a worse successor would flatter Augustus' memory.

  9. How does Syme's reading of the Principate differ from the more favourable modern ones?
    Answer

    Syme (The Roman Revolution, 1939) made it the victory of a faction of generals and Italian new men, with the settlements a facade over army, money and clients. Mommsen read a constitutional dyarchy, Wallace-Hadrill (1982) citizen manners as a working part of the system, and Galinsky (1996) auctoritas as authority that drew consent.

  10. Why did the Augustan arrangement last where Caesar's did not, and where did it fail?
    Answer

    He monopolised the army while refusing the names of king and dictator, kept Senate and magistracies working, widened the governing class, and revised his powers as needed, among people exhausted by civil war. It failed on succession: powers voted to a person gave no rule of inheritance beyond family and the soldiers' loyalty.

Carry forward

  1. The Principate was a bundle of separately voted powers, provincial imperium, tribunician power and greater imperium, dressed in republican names with the army underneath; every later accession repeats the pattern, with the Senate voting the powers and the soldiers deciding who receives them.
  2. Augustus governed by refusing titles and keeping functions, and the Res Gestae lies by arrangement and silence rather than by statement; read every imperial claim as true word by word and test it as a whole.
  3. The succession was never solved: because power attached to a person, every transfer turned on adoption, the women of the family and the army's loyalty, which is the question the Julio-Claudians after AD 14 pose.
