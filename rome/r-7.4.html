01193: the murder of Pertinax and the auction of the empire

Module 7.3 ends with Commodus strangled on the last night of 192. His killers made the city prefect Pertinax emperor; on 28 March 193 the praetorians, resenting his discipline, killed him in the palace after eighty-seven days (Dio 74(73).10). Dio is cited here by the Loeb book numbers with the traditional book number, printed in the Loeb's margin, in brackets where the two differ.

Dio, a senator in Rome at the time, tells what followed as a bidding war. Pertinax's father-in-law Sulpicianus, inside the camp, and the rich senator Didius Julianus, at the wall, raised their offers until Sulpicianus reached 20,000 sesterces a man and Julianus jumped at once by 5,000 (74(73).11.2–5). Herodian has the soldiers cry the empire for sale from the walls (2.6). Dio then sat in the Senate as Julianus addressed it, ringed by soldiers (74(73).12).

Where sources disagree. Dio's 25,000 sesterces is the bid. The Historia Augusta says Julianus promised 25,000 and paid 30,000 (Didius Julianus 3.2); Herodian, that he could not pay what he had promised because the money was not there (2.7.1). The same biography calls false the story, which Dio tells (74(73).13), that he dined luxuriously while Pertinax's body lay in the palace (Didius Julianus 3.8).

The provincial armies refused the sale. Pescennius Niger was hailed in Syria; Clodius Albinus in Britain was bought off with the title of Caesar (Herodian 2.15); and on 9 April Septimius Severus, governor of Upper Pannonia, the army nearest Rome, was proclaimed at Carnuntum. The Senate deposed Julianus, who was killed in the palace after sixty-six days (Dio 74(73).17.4–5).

02Septimius Severus: Lepcis, the civil wars and the army

Severus was born at Lepcis Magna in Tripolitania on 11 April 145 (the city, Historia Augusta, Severus 1.2; the day, Dio 77(76).17.4, where the Life has six days before the Ides of April, 8 April, Severus 1.3, and Magie's Loeb note sides with Dio on the evidence of the Calendar of Philocalus), into a Punic family whose forebears, the Historia Augusta says, were Roman knights before the citizenship was given to all (Severus 1.2); he kept an African ring in his voice into old age (19.9).

Before entering Rome he had the praetorians parade unarmed, surrounded them and banished them beyond the hundredth milestone (Dio 75(74).1.1–2; Herodian 2.13). The new guard came from the legions instead of Italy, Spain, Macedonia and Noricum, and Dio complained that Italian youth turned to banditry and the arena (75(74).2.4–6).

Niger was beaten at Cyzicus, Nicaea and Issus in 193–194 and killed in flight (Dio 75(74).6–8; Herodian 3.2–4); Byzantium held out until 195 or 196 (Dio 75(74).10–14). Meanwhile Severus campaigned beyond the Euphrates, and Dio called the territory he claimed to have added "a source of constant wars and great expense to us" (75.1.1, 3.2–3). In 195 Severus declared himself son of the deified Marcus and brother of Commodus; his elder son, renamed Marcus Aurelius Antoninus and nicknamed Caracalla, was soon made Caesar. Albinus, displaced, was hailed Augustus. At Lugdunum on 19 February 197 each side had, by Dio's figure, 150,000 men (76(75).6.1). Albinus killed himself, and in the Senate Severus praised the severity of Sulla, Marius and Augustus, defended Commodus, released thirty-five senators accused of siding with Albinus, and put twenty-nine men to death, Sulpicianus among them (76(75).8.1–4).

He then sacked Ctesiphon (76(75).9.4) but failed twice before Hatra (76(75).10–12). Mesopotamia became a province under an equestrian prefect, held by two new legions, I and III Parthica; the third, II Parthica, was stationed at Albanum in the Alban hills, the first legion based in Italy under the emperors, and all three had equestrian commanders.

The army was his constituency. Herodian says he gave the soldiers large sums, was the first to increase their ration allowance, and let them wear gold rings and live with their wives, things earlier emperors had thought fatal to discipline (3.8.4–5). The legionary's pay had stood at 300 denarii a year since Domitian (Suetonius, Domitian 7.3); module 9.2 takes the army as an institution.

Where sources disagree. Herodian blames Severus for corrupting discipline (3.8.5), and Gibbon concluded that posterity justly considered him "the principal author of the decline of the Roman empire" (Decline and Fall, chapter 5). Against this, pay had not risen for over a century, soldiers' families already existed outside the law, and armies had made emperors since 69 (module 7.2). The size of the rise is unknown: J. Jahn (1984) and M. A. Speidel (1992) argued for a doubling; others assume a rise of half, to 450 denarii.

03The arch, Plautianus and the death at York

The arch the Senate and people dedicated in the Forum in 203 honours Severus and his sons for restoring the state and extending the empire; its four panels map the Parthian campaigns, and module 10.3 sets out how to read historical relief. In its inscription the fourth line, which named Geta, was recut after 211 as "best and bravest princes", and the dowel holes of the bronze letters still show the lost name.

Gaius Fulvius Plautianus, praetorian prefect from 197 and Severus' fellow townsman, married his daughter to Caracalla in 202 and, in Dio's portrait, wielded power equal to the emperors' own, with more and larger statues than theirs (76(75).14.6). On 22 January 205 he was killed in the palace on Caracalla's charge of a plot (Dio 77(76).3–4; Herodian 3.11–12), and Papinian took the prefecture. From 208 Severus campaigned in northern Britain; Dio's 50,000 Roman dead (77(76).13.2) cannot be checked. He died at York on 4 February 211, telling his sons, in Dio's version, "Be harmonious, enrich the soldiers, and scorn all other men" (77(76).15.2).

04Julia Domna and the Syrian women

Severus' second wife, Julia Domna, came from Emesa (Homs) in Syria, from the family that held the priesthood of its sun god Elagabal; the Historia Augusta has him choose her for a horoscope promising a royal husband (Severus 3.9). From 195 she was mother of the camps, the first empress so styled since Faustina the Younger, and under Caracalla mother of the Senate and the fatherland as well. Dio says Plautianus' hostility drove her to philosophy (76(75).15.6–7), and Philostratus wrote his Life of Apollonius at her request (1.3). Under Caracalla she handled petitions and correspondence in both languages (Dio 78(77).18.2), the business module 7.5 describes, and after his murder she starved herself (79(78).23–24).

Her sister Julia Maesa and Maesa's daughters, Soaemias and Mamaea, carried the dynasty once its men were dead. Dio and Herodian treat their power as a symptom of disorder; coins and inscriptions show the dynasty advertising it.

05Caracalla: fratricide, erasure and the baths

Severus left two Augusti who hated each other; Herodian has them consider dividing the empire until their mother asked how they would divide her (4.3.5–9). In December 211 Caracalla had centurions kill Geta in her arms (Dio 78(77).2.3–4), ran to the praetorian camp crying conspiracy, and promised each man 2,500 denarii and half as much again on his ration (Herodian 4.4.7). Dio puts Geta's partisans killed afterwards at about 20,000 (78(77).4.1).

To speak or write Geta's name became a capital offence (Dio 78(77).12.5). It was chiselled from inscriptions, and his face was scraped from a painted wooden tondo of about 200, probably from Egypt and now in Berlin, which shows Severus and Julia Domna behind their two sons: three faces and a blank. Dieter Metzler suggested instead that the erased boy is Caracalla, defaced in Egypt after his massacre of 215.

Caracalla spent most of his reign with the armies, imitating Alexander the Great down to a Macedonian phalanx (Dio 78(77).7–8), and in 215 had the young men of Alexandria massacred for their mockery (78(77).22–23; Herodian 4.9). Dio's account of his finances is a list of exactions: crown gold, new taxes and doubled ones, the soldiers paid without limit. Reproached by his mother, he pointed to his sword and said they would not lack money while they had this (78(77).9–10). In 215 he issued the radiate coin modern numismatists call the antoninianus (module 8.1 follows the debasement). His baths, inaugurated in 216, had 1,600 marble seats by the count of the fifth-century historian Olympiodorus, and yielded the Farnese Bull and Hercules in the 1540s (module 10.3). On 8 April 217, near Carrhae, a soldier stabbed him at the prompting of the praetorian prefect Macrinus (Dio 79(78).5; Herodian 4.13).

06The edict of 212

Ulpian, writing within a few years, states the fact:

in orbe Romano qui sunt ex constitutione imperatoris Antonini cives Romani effecti sunt.

"Those who are in the Roman world were made Roman citizens by a constitution of the emperor Antoninus" (Digest 1.5.17). A damaged Greek papyrus at Giessen, P.Giss. 40 col. I, is usually taken as a copy of the edict. The emperor thanks the gods who preserved him, which most connect with Geta's alleged plot, and grants the citizenship of the Romans to all [foreigners] in the world, with an exception restored as the dediticii, probably freedmen of criminal status or surrendered barbarians. After 212 the papyri swarm with Aurelii, new citizens taking their benefactor's family name.

Module 3.4 describes the graded statuses of Republican Italy; after the Social War (module 5.2) the empire ran on a cruder division between citizens and peregrines, with Latin rights as a step for provincial towns. Myles Lavan (Past & Present, 2016) judged it extremely unlikely that more than a third of free provincials were citizens on the eve of the grant: the edict was a break, not a formality.

Much did not change. Local law persisted beside Roman, as Ludwig Mitteis showed from the papyri (Reichsrecht und Volksrecht, 1891), and Junian Latins survived until Justinian. Penalties already followed rank, honestiores against humiliores, more than citizenship (Peter Garnsey, Social Status and Legal Privilege, 1970). But every free provincial could now enter a legion, and the line between legions and auxiliaries lost its legal basis (module 9.2).

Where sources disagree. Dio's motive is fiscal: Caracalla doubled the taxes on inheritances and manumissions to ten per cent and made everyone a citizen so that everyone paid them (78(77).9.4–5). The papyrus speaks of thanks to the gods, and later writers lost even the author: Aurelius Victor credits Marcus Aurelius (Caesares 16.12), Justinian credits Antoninus Pius (Novel 78.5). Kostas Buraselis (Theia Dorea, 1989) read it through Severan ruling ideology and judged it far more important than earlier scholars allowed; Alex Imrie (2018) combines the motives, more taxpayers and more men eligible for the legions, presented as piety and generosity after the fratricide.

07Macrinus and Elagabalus

Macrinus, a Moor from Caesarea in Mauretania (Dio 79(78).11), was the first emperor who had not been a senator, which Dio held against him (79(78).41). After an indecisive battle at Nisibis he bought peace from the Parthian king Artabanus for 200 million sesterces (79(78).26–27). In a letter to the city prefect Maximus, as Dio reports, he put the cost of Caracalla's pay rise at 280 million sesterces a year (79(78).36.3), and he enlisted recruits on the old terms fixed by Severus (79(78).28). Julia Maesa used her fortune and the claim that her grandson, the god's boy priest at Emesa, was Caracalla's son to win the legion camped nearby on 16 May 218 (79(78).31–32; Herodian 5.3), and Macrinus was beaten near Antioch on 8 June (79(78).38–40).

The boy reigned as Marcus Aurelius Antoninus; Elagabalus is his god's name, and the god was a black conical stone. Module 2.5 covers the eastern cults that reached Rome with traders and soldiers; this one arrived as the emperor's own, and that was the scandal. Herodian says he sent ahead a portrait of himself in Syrian priestly robes to hang above the statue of Victory in the Senate house, so that senators would grow used to it (5.5.6–7). He married the god to Urania of Carthage (5.6.3–5) and at midsummer ran backwards before its chariot through the city (5.6.6–8). He took the title of priest of the invincible Sun god Elagabal, and his coins show the stone in a chariot. He married a Vestal Virgin (Dio 80(79).9.3). For Dio the offence lay not in bringing a foreign god to Rome but in placing him above Jupiter and having himself voted his priest (80(79).11.1). Maesa had him adopt his cousin, renamed Alexander, as Caesar in 221; when he turned against the boy, the guard killed him and his mother Soaemias in March 222 (80(79).17–20).

Where sources disagree. Dio, Herodian and the Historia Augusta built Elagabalus from the stock vices of the tyrant and the effeminate easterner. Leonardo de Arrizabalaga y Prado (The Emperor Elagabalus: Fact or Fiction?, 2010) tested each claim against coins, inscriptions and papyri, found the sexual and violent stories unconfirmed, and calls him by his boyhood name Varius. Martijn Icks (The Crimes of Elagabalus, 2011) takes the cult and the priestly role as real and as the cause of his fall, while doubting most of the scandal.

08Severus Alexander, Mamaea and the Sasanians

Alexander was thirteen. Herodian says his grandmother and mother chose sixteen senators as his council (6.1.2); after Maesa's death Mamaea ruled, and she summoned Origen to Antioch (Eusebius, Church History 6.21.3–4). The guard remained master. Dio says the praetorians killed Ulpian, Alexander's prefect and chief minister, though he fled at night to the palace and took refuge with the emperor and his mother (80.1.1, 2.2), and that Dio himself, hated by them for disciplining the Pannonian legions, was told to spend his second consulship, held with the emperor in 229, outside Rome (80.4.2–5.1).

Meanwhile Ardashir, a Persian dynast from Fars, killed the Parthian king Artabanus about 224 and founded the Sasanian empire. Dio, writing as it happened, reports his threat to win back everything the ancient Persians had held as far as the Greek sea, and fears him less for his own strength than for the state of the Roman armies (80.3–4). Alexander went east in 231 and invaded in three columns in 232.

Where sources disagree. Herodian makes the campaign a humiliation: the southern army destroyed, the northern wasted in the Armenian mountains, the emperor, held back by fear or by his mother, never advancing (6.5–6). The Historia Augusta has Alexander read the Senate a dispatch announcing a crushing victory (Severus Alexander 55–57). Ardashir's quiet for several years afterwards suggests a costly stalemate rather than either version.

Then the Germans crossed the Rhine. At Mainz Alexander tried to buy peace (Herodian 6.7); the troops despised a youth ruled by his mother, and in March 235 the recruits proclaimed their trainer, Maximinus, and killed Alexander and Mamaea in their tent (6.8–9). Module 8.1 begins with him.

09Papinian, Ulpian and Paul

The Severan age is the summit of Roman jurisprudence, and its jurists held the highest equestrian office. Papinian, prefect from 205, wrote the Quaestiones and Responsa that the Law of Citations of 426 made decisive in a tie. He was killed in the purge after Geta's murder, with an axe, as Dio notes (78(77).4.1); the Historia Augusta's story that he refused to defend the fratricide (Caracalla 8) is probably invention. Paul records a document read in Papinian's auditorium (Digest 12.1.40): the jurists sat as the prefect's and the emperor's councillors. Ulpian of Tyre (Digest 50.15.1) died at the guard's hands as prefect; a papyrus showing his killer Epagathus already prefect of Egypt in 224 led J. Modrzejewski and T. Zawadzki (1967) to date his death to 223 rather than 228. Paul's own prefecture rests on late, doubtful sources. Ulpian is the largest single source of Justinian's Digest, and his sentences that the princeps is released from the laws (1.3.31) and that what pleases the princeps has the force of law (1.4.1) became texts of absolutism. Module 9.1 takes their law further.

10Cassius Dio and Herodian as witnesses

Cassius Dio of Nicaea, a senator's son, came to Rome around 180 and held office from Commodus to Alexander, from praetor in the 190s to consul with Alexander in 229 (module 0.1 introduces his history). A pamphlet on the portents of Severus' rise led to a history of the civil wars and then, at a dream's bidding, to the whole of Roman history: ten years of collecting to Severus' death and twelve of writing (73(72).23). He saw what he describes: Julianus' Senate, Severus' entry, the speech of 197, Caracalla's court. His standard is senatorial, his hostility to Caracalla and Elagabalus open, his verdict on Severus mixed. A Vatican manuscript keeps his own text for 217–219; the rest of these books survive in the eleventh-century epitome of John Xiphilinus and in Byzantine excerpts, and for Alexander's reign he warns that he was seldom in Rome (80.1.2–3).

Herodian, of uncertain origin, says he wrote of events he saw or shared in imperial and public service (1.2.5); his eight books run from 180 to 238. He has the better scenes and the worse facts: vague dates, invented speeches, a moral thread of boy emperors and greedy soldiers. Whether he read Dio is disputed. The Historia Augusta draws on Herodian and on the lost Marius Maximus; its life of Alexander, the longest in the collection, gives him a private shrine holding Christ, Abraham, Orpheus and Apollonius (Severus Alexander 29.2). Module 0.1 explains why it is not to be trusted.

11Self-test

  1. How does Dio's account of the auction of 193 differ from Herodian's and the Historia Augusta's, and why does Dio's carry particular weight?
    Answer

    Dio has a bidding war in which Julianus beat Sulpicianus' 20,000 sesterces by jumping 5,000 (74(73).11); Herodian has the empire cried for sale from the walls and says Julianus could not pay (2.6, 2.7.1); the Historia Augusta says he promised 25,000 and paid 30,000 (Didius Julianus 3.2). Dio was a senator in Rome and sat through Julianus' first Senate meeting.

  2. What did Severus change in the guard and the legions, and why did Dio object?
    Answer

    He disbanded the old praetorians and recruited the new guard from the legions of every province (Dio 75(74).1–2), raised three Parthian legions under equestrian commanders and stationed II Parthica at Albanum. Dio complained that Italian youth, shut out of the guard, turned to banditry and the arena.

  3. Why did Herodian and Gibbon blame Severus for the decline, and what can be said against them?
    Answer

    Herodian says his money, rations, rings and wives corrupted discipline (3.8.5), and Dio's deathbed advice to enrich the soldiers (77(76).15.2) sums up the policy; Gibbon called him the principal author of the decline. Against this, pay had stood still since Domitian, soldiers' families already existed, and armies had made emperors since 69; the size of the rise is unknown.

  4. Where can the erasure of Geta still be seen, and what made it necessary?
    Answer

    In the recut fourth line of the arch in the Forum and the blank face on the Severan tondo in Berlin. Caracalla had Geta killed in December 211 (Dio 78(77).2), presented it as self-defence against a plot, and made speaking or writing his name a capital offence (78(77).12.5).

  5. What roles did Julia Domna, Julia Maesa and Julia Mamaea play in the dynasty?
    Answer

    Domna was mother of the camps and of the Senate, patron of Philostratus and Caracalla's manager of petitions and correspondence (Dio 78(77).18.2). Maesa used her fortune and a claimed paternity to make Elagabalus emperor in 218 and replaced him with Alexander in 222. Mamaea governed for Alexander and died with him in 235.

  6. What motives do Dio and P.Giss. 40 give for the edict of 212, and how does Imrie combine them?
    Answer

    Dio: revenue, since new citizens became liable to the inheritance and manumission taxes he had doubled (78(77).9.4–5). The papyrus: thanks to the gods for the emperor's preservation, probably from Geta. Imrie (2018) sees more taxpayers and more men eligible for the legions, presented as piety and generosity after the fratricide.

  7. What did the edict end, and what did it leave untouched?
    Answer

    It ended the division between citizens and peregrines that had replaced the graded statuses of Republican Italy (module 3.4), enfranchising a majority: Lavan puts citizens at no more than a third of free provincials before it. Local law persisted (Mitteis), Junian Latins and probably the dediticii remained, and penalties followed rank, honestiores and humiliores (Garnsey).

  8. How did army pay bring down Macrinus?
    Answer

    Caracalla's rise cost, by Macrinus' reckoning in Dio, 280 million sesterces a year (79(78).36.3), on top of a peace with Parthia bought for 200 million. Macrinus put new recruits back on Severus' terms (79(78).28), alarming the soldiers, and Maesa's money and her claim that Elagabalus was Caracalla's son won the Syrian legion in May 218.

  9. What exactly was Elagabalus' offence in Dio's eyes, and what does the non-literary evidence confirm?
    Answer

    Not importing a foreign god but placing Elagabal above Jupiter and having himself voted his priest (Dio 80(79).11.1), with the marriage to a Vestal as sacrilege. Coins and inscriptions confirm the god's primacy and the priestly title; the sexual stories are unconfirmed, as Arrizabalaga y Prado (2010) argued.

  10. Why did the rise of Ardashir matter, and how do the sources judge Alexander's response?
    Answer

    The Sasanians replaced a weakened Parthian dynasty with one that claimed the old Persian lands to the Greek sea (Dio 80.3–4). Herodian makes the campaign of 232 a humiliation (6.5–6), the Historia Augusta a victory (Severus Alexander 55–57); the outcome was probably a costly stalemate, and on the Rhine his attempt to buy peace cost him his life in 235.

12Carry forward

  1. The Severans made explicit what Augustus had disguised: the emperor ruled because he paid the army. Each pay rise drove new taxes, the fiscal side of 212 and the debasement of the coinage, and the cycle of soldier emperors after 235 follows from it.
  2. The edict of 212 made nearly every free inhabitant a Roman citizen, ending the graded citizenship that had built Roman Italy; from now on "Roman" names a subject of the empire, while real status runs through rank, wealth and office, and local law lives on beneath.
  3. The dynasty's legitimacy ran through invented descent and through women, from Severus' self-adoption into the Antonines to Maesa's claim that Elagabalus was Caracalla's son; its history is told by senators who measured emperors by senatorial and Roman norms, so read the scandals of Elagabalus and the rule of women as judgements, not reports.
