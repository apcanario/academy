This is the complete syllabus the reports were generated from. Each module has its drop-in prompt; copy it into a new chat to regenerate a report yourself, extend one, or compare a fresh answer against the one here. Where a report exists it is linked.

How to run it

  1. Do the modules in order. Each prompt assumes the ones before it, and the Greek course before that: where Rome touches Greece, the prompt points back to the Greek module instead of repeating it.
  2. Tap copy prompt, paste the prompt into a new chat here, read the report, do the self-test at the end. If you score under 7/10, re-read before moving on.
  3. Tick the box when done. Progress is stored on this phone only.
  4. Every prompt asks for named primary sources and for the places where the accounts disagree. For early Rome that matters more than it did for Greece: almost everything before 300 BC reached us through writers of the first century BC, and the question is always what they could have known.

Units 11, 12 and 13 are deep dives: optional, and best taken after the core material they extend (unit 5; module 6.3; modules 4.5, 5.6, 7.3 and 10.2) or after the capstone.

Honest scoping: no syllabus gets you all of it. Rome left more words than Greece and fewer stories, and its stories come disguised as history. What this gets you is the skeleton, the main sources read in the right order, and enough structure that anything you meet later snaps into place.

UNIT 0

Foundations

Three modules that fix the evidence, the land and the dates before any story is told. Rome is a harder case than Greece: its early history was written down centuries late, by Romans with something to prove.

Give me an in-depth report on the sources for Roman history and mythology, with the emphasis on the problem that defines the subject: Rome's early history was written down centuries after the events, by Romans with agendas.
