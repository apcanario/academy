01Juno's anger and its causes

The poem asks its theological question before it tells a single event. The Muse is to recall the causes: what wound to her divinity drove the queen of the gods to roll a man famous for piety through so many trials, and then the line that every later reader of the poem has had to answer:

tantaene animis caelestibus irae? (1.11)

Can heavenly minds hold such anger? The answer comes at once, in a list (1.12–28). Carthage was Juno's city, loved above Samos, where her arms and chariot were kept, and she hoped to make it rule the nations; but she had heard that a race sprung from Trojan blood would one day overturn it, for so the Parcae spun (1.19–22). Behind that political cause lie the old personal ones, stored deep in her mind: the judgement of Paris and the insult to her slighted beauty, the hated race, and the honours given to the stolen Ganymede (1.26–28). The list runs from Homeric myth to Roman history, and the paragraph closes on a line that turns the anger into the measure of the achievement: tantae molis erat Romanam condere gentem, so great a labour it was to found the Roman race (1.33).

Her first speech gives the motive a goddess's form. Pallas could burn the Greek fleet for one man's crime; Juno, sister and wife of Jupiter, has fought one people for years, and who will worship her divinity or lay offerings on her altars after this (1.37–49)? The anger is a matter of numen and cult, of honour that must be visible. In book 7, seeing the Trojans settled on the Tiber, she admits she cannot win: the fates of the Phrygians run against her own (7.293–294), Lavinia is fixed by fate as Aeneas' wife, but she can drag the business out and add delays (7.313–316). The line between these two admissions is her programme:

flectere si nequeo superos, Acheronta movebo (7.312)

If she cannot bend the gods above, she will move the underworld. Juno is the poem's engine: the storm of book 1 (1.50–123), the pact with Venus that brings Aeneas and Dido to the cave (4.90–128), Iris sent to burn the ships (5.604–663), Allecto and the war in Latium (7.323–571), even the gates of war, which Juno pushes open with her own hand when Latinus refuses (7.620–622). The action of each scene is modules 12.2 and 12.3's; what matters here is that every delay the plot needs is hers.

Her anger is also Roman history read backwards. Carthage's Juno is the Punic goddess Tanit, worshipped by Romans as Juno Caelestis; Denis Feeney (The Gods in Epic, 1991) reads Virgil's Juno as several figures at once: Tanit, Homer's Hera, the physical allegory of air, and the Roman goddess of marriage. The allegory is old: Cicero's Stoic speaker says the air between sea and sky is consecrated under the name of Juno, sister and wife of Jupiter (On the Nature of the Gods 2.66), and Servius reads the poem's "sister and wife" the same way (on 1.47). Module 2.2 gives Juno's Roman cult titles and her Greek double.

Where sources disagree. Servius (on 12.841) says it is agreed that Juno was won over in the second Punic war and in the third was brought to Rome by Scipio with certain rites. Macrobius (Saturnalia 3.9) preserves the formula of evocation used at Carthage, but it calls on whatever god or goddess has the city in its care and names no Juno. Whether the Carthaginian goddess was ever formally summoned to Rome in 146, or Servius has run the Italian and the Punic Juno together, is uncertain.

02Jupiter: the prophecy, the council and the reconciliation

Jupiter appears first smiling at his daughter (1.254) and unrolls the secrets of the fates in the prophecy of 1.257–296, which module 6.3 reads. Two passages in it belong to this module. He says he will "move" the hidden things of the fates (fatorum arcana movebo, 1.262), and he forecasts that harsh Juno will change her counsels for the better and cherish the Romans with him (1.279–282). The poem thus announces its own divine ending in its first book.

The council of book 10 shows the limits of that confidence. Venus and Juno argue their cases (10.18–95); Jupiter, unable to reconcile them, declares himself neutral: rex Iuppiter omnibus idem. fata viam invenient, King Jupiter is the same to all; the fates will find the way (10.112–113). He opens it by rebuking the gods' quarrel: the lawful time for battle will come, they need not hasten it, when fierce Carthage sends great ruin on the Roman citadels through the opened Alps (10.11–13). Hannibal is written into heaven as the next round of the same quarrel.

The reconciliation of book 12 (12.791–842) is told as an event by module 12.3; its terms are what matter for the gods. Jupiter asks what end there will be (12.793) and forbids Juno to go further. She yields, but names a price: let the Latins keep their name, let them not become Trojans or change their language and dress, let there be Latium and Alban kings and a Roman stock strong in Italian valour; Troy has fallen, let it stay fallen with its name (12.819–828). Jupiter grants everything: do quod vis, et me victusque volensque remitto, I give what you wish, and yield, conquered and willing (12.833). The Ausonians will keep their speech and customs, the Trojans will sink into them, he will add the rites, and from the mixture a race will rise that surpasses men and gods in piety and honours Juno as no other people does (12.834–840). Then the line the commentators weigh:

adnuit his Iuno et mentem laetata retorsit (12.841)

Juno nods, and turns her mind round, glad. The price is real: the Trojan name disappears, and Rome is Italian, with a Trojan strain. The scene answers Juno's speech in Horace's Odes 3.3, which module 6.3 reads: there too Juno consents to Rome on condition that Troy is never rebuilt. And Jupiter's next act shows that peace in heaven is not gentleness. He sends one of the two Dirae who sit at his throne, sisters of the Fury Megaera, to drive Juturna from the field and terrify Turnus (12.843–886). The father of gods ends the war with an agent from the same world as Juno's Allecto.

Where sources disagree. Ennius, as Servius reports on 1.281, put Juno's peace with Rome in the second Punic war: then, appeased, Juno began to favour the Romans. Horace's Juno gives up her anger when Romulus is received into heaven (Odes 3.3.30–33). Virgil places a reconciliation at the end of the mythic war, but his own Jupiter forecasts a lawful time for battle under Hannibal (10.11–13). Denis Feeney ("The Reconciliations of Juno", Classical Quarterly, 1984) argued against the common view that book 12 shows a definitive transformation of the goddess: the poem's reconciliation is one in a series, and Juno's hostility resumes in Roman history.

03Venus as mother

Venus acts for one person. In book 1 she weeps to Jupiter over her son's sufferings (1.229–253) and wins the prophecy; then she meets Aeneas on the Libyan shore disguised as a Tyrian huntress, tells him Dido's story and reveals herself only as she leaves, by her walk (1.314–405). His reproach is the poem's sharpest line about divine love: cruel too, why does she mock her son so often with false images (1.407–408)? She arranges Dido's welcome by sending Cupid in the shape of Ascanius to inflame the queen (1.657–722), and in book 4 she accepts Juno's proposal of a marriage knowing it is a trick (4.90–128). Dido is the price of Aeneas' safety in Carthage; module 12.4 takes her further.

The mother also shows the son the truth. On the night of Troy's fall she checks his rage, asking why he is raving (2.594–595), and removes the mist from his eyes so that he sees the gods themselves destroying the city: Neptune heaving the walls, Juno at the Scaean gate, Pallas on the citadel, Jupiter rousing the gods against the Trojans. It is divum inclementia, divum, the harshness of the gods, that overturns Troy (2.602–603). Later she negotiates with the other gods for him: with Neptune for a safe passage, at the price of one life, unum pro multis dabitur caput (5.815), which turns out to be Palinurus; with Vulcan, her husband, for arms (8.370–406), which she brings herself (8.608–625); in book 12 she heals Aeneas' wound with dittany gathered on Cretan Ida (12.411–424). Her love is real and works by bargain and disguise. As mother of the Aeneadae and of the Julii, she is also the goddess Caesar had honoured as Venus Genetrix (module 2.2; Greek module 10.12).

04The agents: Neptune, Apollo, Allecto, Iris and Mercury

Below the three great gods the poem runs on messengers and instruments. Each is sent by someone, and each makes a cosmic will local.

Juno also works through lesser powers: she bribes Aeolus with a nymph to loose the storm (1.65–75) and urges Juturna, Turnus' sister, to snatch her brother from death if she can (12.134–160). One agent in the table has no Homeric model. Allecto, a Fury from below sent by the queen of heaven, has her nearest parallel in tragedy: in Euripides' Heracles Hera sends Iris with Lyssa, Madness, to drive the hero to kill his children. Apollo's words to Ascanius, macte nova virtute, puer, sic itur ad astra, blessings on your new courage, boy, this is the road to the stars (9.641), are the god's praise and his command to stop fighting at once: the boy's life belongs to the future. The system has a symmetry the table only half shows. Juno's agents come from the underworld or disguise themselves; Jupiter's come from Olympus until the end, when he too draws on the Dirae.

05Fate and the limits of Jupiter

Virgil's word is usually the plural, fata, and he uses it in several ways at once. The fates are spun by the Parcae (1.22); they are a script Jupiter reads and can unroll (1.262); they are the "fates of Jupiter" (Dido's fata Iovis, 4.614); and they are plural and opposed, the fates of the Phrygians against Juno's (7.293–294). Juno is "forbidden by the fates" (quippe vetor fatis, 1.39) and knows it.

The poem keeps marking what fate does not settle. It fixes the end, Lavinia and Latium and the walls of Lavinium, but not the time or the cost: Juno can delay (7.313–316), and Vulcan tells Venus that neither the father almighty nor the fates forbade Troy to stand for ten more years (nec pater omnipotens Troiam nec fata vetabant, 8.398). Dido dies neither by fate nor by a deserved death, so Proserpina has not yet cut her lock and Iris must be sent (4.696–705). Jupiter can grant delay but not reversal: he lets Juno snatch Turnus from the battle for a time, on condition that she knows the delay changes nothing (10.622–627). When Hercules weeps for Pallas, Jupiter answers with the poem's plainest statement of limit, stat sua cuique dies, each has his appointed day (10.467), and recalls that his own son Sarpedon fell under Troy (10.470–471): the scene of Iliad 16 that Greek module 10.9 reads, with the question of choice removed. At the last duel he lifts the scales with the two men's fates (12.725–727), the Homeric image of Iliad 22.209–213, and the poem does not say which pan sinks.

So Jupiter is supreme and limited at once. He knows fate, speaks for it and enforces it; whether he authors it the text never says. Deep dive module 13.2 hands this module the question of how far Virgil's fate is Stoic. The Stoic answer would make fate and Zeus one: Greek module 12.7 sets out Chrysippus' fate as the chain of causes and god as the active reason in matter, and Diogenes Laertius gives the school's formula that god, mind, fate and Zeus are one (7.135). The Aeneid has passages that read that way (Dido's fata Iovis, 4.614; the king of the gods allotting the fates, 3.375–376) and passages that do not (8.398, 4.696).

Where sources disagree. Richard Heinze (Virgils epische Technik, 1903) and Cyril Bailey (Religion in Virgil, 1935) identified fate with the will of Jupiter, which suits a Stoic reading. Graham Zanker (Fate and the Hero in Virgil's Aeneid, 2023) separates a Stoic world fate from Jupiter, its mouthpiece and occasional guardian, and leaves room for events held "in suspension". Sergio Casali (Bryn Mawr Classical Review 2024.10.47) answers that Virgil is not consistent on the point and that Stoic fate cannot simply be equated with Rome's destiny. Feeney (1991) denies the poem any neutral vantage at all: Jupiter is a character with a point of view, not the voice of the whole.

06Pietas against furor

The first adjective the poem gives its hero is in the proem: a man outstanding in pietas (1.10). He names himself by it, sum pius Aeneas (1.378). Pietas is duty owed to the gods, to parents and to the fatherland: Cicero's Cotta calls it justice towards the gods (On the Nature of the Gods 1.116), and Augustus' golden shield of 27 BC listed it with valour, clemency and justice among the virtues the Senate honoured in him (Res Gestae 34.2). Its image is the son carrying his father and his gods out of Troy (2.707–720; module 1.3).

Its opposite is furor, the madness of passion and of civil war. Jupiter's prophecy ends with impious Furor chained (1.294–296); the simile of the statesman in the first storm, which module 12.2 reads, sets a crowd's furor against one man's pietas. Juno's agents spread furor: Allecto's snake and torch, Amata's Bacchic frenzy, Turnus' rage, Dido raving through the city (4.68–69). The poem's moral scheme seems simple: the gods of order back pietas, Juno's underworld backs furor.

Aeneas' pietas is tested, and furor is in him too. On the night of Troy's fall he takes up arms out of his mind, furor and anger driving him headlong (2.314–317), until Venus stops him. In Carthage pietas means leaving: Italiam non sponte sequor, I seek Italy not of my own will (4.361), and at pius Aeneas turns from Dido to obey (4.393–396). After the ships burn he despairs, and old Nautes gives him the Stoic counsel: let us follow where the fates draw us and draw us back; every fortune is overcome by bearing it (5.709–710). After Pallas' death he takes eight young men alive to sacrifice to the shades (10.517–520) and kills suppliants. In book 12 he stands unarmed, his right hand outstretched, trying to keep the truce (12.311–317). At the end he kills Turnus "set on fire by furies and terrible in his anger" (12.946–947), saying that it is Pallas who sacrifices him (12.948–949): pietas towards the dead boy and his father, and furor, in one act. What that act means for the poem is the debate module 12.7 runs.

07Stoic and Epicurean readings

Virgil had both schools at hand. The Stoic doctrines of fate, providence and the passions are Greek module 12.7's and their Roman forms deep dive module 13.2's; the Epicurean gods and the swerve are Greek module 12.6's, and Lucretius, whom Virgil read closely, is module 5.6's. His own Epicurean teachers, Siro near Naples and the circle of Philodemus, belong to deep dive module 12.1.

The Stoic reading takes Aeneas as a man making progress towards wisdom, the figure deep dive module 13.2 calls the Romans' characteristic reader: he follows fate, at first unwillingly (4.361), and learns to bear what comes (5.709–710). Its emblem is the oak simile when Dido's sister pleads with him: the tree is shaken by the winds but holds, and mens immota manet, lacrimae volvuntur inanes, the mind stays unmoved, the tears roll in vain (4.449). Augustine applies the line to the Stoic sage who feels but does not assent (City of God 9.4); whose tears they are, Aeneas', Dido's or Anna's, the line does not say. Seneca's version of Cleanthes' prayer, the fates lead the willing and drag the unwilling (Letters 107.11; deep dive module 13.2), is the measure for Aeneas' progress from "not of my own will" to acceptance.

The Epicurean reading starts from the first question. Lucretius' gods live in deep peace, untouched by anger, nec bene promeritis capitur neque tangitur ira (Lucretius 2.651); the proem's "can heavenly minds hold such anger?" (1.11) asks the Epicurean question of a poem whose gods answer yes. Dido, scorning Aeneas' appeal to divine orders, uses the Epicurean picture as sarcasm: that, of course, is the gods' labour, that is the care that troubles their calm (4.379–380). Iopas' song at Dido's banquet is natural philosophy in Lucretius' manner, the wandering moon and the labours of the sun (1.740–746; module 12.1). Philodemus' On Anger and On the Good King According to Homer have given a third line, on the passions of a ruler.

Where sources disagree. C. M. Bowra ("Aeneas and the Stoic Ideal", Greece & Rome, 1933) read the hero as a Stoic in the making, and Zanker (2023) reads human responsibility in the poem through Chrysippus' compatibilism. The contributors to Vergil, Philodemus, and the Augustans (ed. David Armstrong and others, 2004) read it through the Epicureans at Naples: Jeffrey Fish argued from Philodemus' On Anger that the school allowed the wise man anger so long as he takes no pleasure in punishment, and read the Helen episode of book 2, where Venus checks his rage, as the corrective moment that transforms Aeneas' anger. Casali (2024) holds that Stoic psychology is largely irrelevant to Virgil's characters.

08Virgil's gods and Homer's

Greek module 10.9 sets out Homer's gods: the plan of Zeus, the scales, Sarpedon, the partisans and their reasons, double motivation, Olympian comedy, and the allegorists who defended it. Virgil keeps the machinery and changes its weight.

  • No comedy. There is no Deception of Zeus, no unquenchable laughter, no theomachy on the plain. The only battle of gods is on the shield, where Rome's gods and Apollo face the monstrous gods of Egypt (8.698–706). Jupiter's smile at his daughter (1.254) and at his wife (12.829) is as near as the poem comes to Olympian ease.
  • Partisanship as history. Homer's Hera hates Troy for the judgement and for no city's sake; Virgil's Juno hates it for Carthage's future (1.12–22). The gods' quarrels forecast Rome's wars.
  • Agents from below. Homer's gods work by disguise and impulse; Virgil's Juno and, at the end, Jupiter use Furies. The underworld enters the divine plot.
  • Double motivation kept. Allecto's torch is thrown into a Turnus already angry at losing Lavinia; Cupid works on a Dido already drawn to Aeneas. As in Homer, the god doubles the human cause without cancelling it.
  • Jupiter's limits stated. Where Homer's Zeus wonders whether to save Sarpedon and is talked out of it, Virgil's Jupiter tells Hercules only that his own son fell (10.464–473); the scales of 12.725–727 have no outcome.

Virgil's Neptune retells his own Homeric rescue of Aeneas (5.803–811), and Apollo's oracle at Delos turns Iliad 20.307–308 into a promise of world rule (3.97–98). The Homeric gods are not abandoned; they are given a Roman future.

09Roman religion inside the poem

The poem's gods are also Roman cult objects, and its rites are aetiologies.

The Penates. Hector's ghost entrusts Troy's sacred things and Penates to Aeneas (2.293–297); Anchises carries them (2.717); in Crete they appear in a dream to send the Trojans on to Hesperia (3.147–171). At Actium Augustus fights "with the fathers and the people, the Penates and the great gods" (8.679). These are the Penates Rome kept at Lavinium, where consuls sacrificed on taking office (module 1.3).

Rites. Helenus orders Aeneas to sacrifice with his head veiled in purple (3.403–409), the Roman manner, and to win Juno first by prayer and gifts (3.435–440). At the Ara Maxima Evander's people sacrifice to Hercules with Potitius and the Pinarian house as founders of the cult (8.268–272), the Greek rite with the head bare that module 1.3 describes.

The Sibyl and Apollo. At Cumae Aeneas vows Phoebus and Trivia a temple of solid marble and festal days named after Phoebus, and promises the Sibyl a shrine for her oracles in his kingdom (6.69–74). Readers have taken it as a forecast of Augustus' Palatine temple of Apollo, where, Suetonius says, Augustus placed the Sibylline Books in two gilded cases under the god's statue (Augustus 31.1). On the shield, Actian Apollo bends his bow from above and Egypt, India, Arabia and the Sabaeans turn to flee (8.704–706), and Caesar sits at the snow-white threshold of shining Phoebus to receive the conquered (8.720); module 5.5 reads the scene as Actium.

Juno's cult. The reconciliation itself is a cult promise: no people will honour Juno as Rome will (12.840). The anger of 1.48–49 was a fear of losing worship; the poem ends by guaranteeing it. The goddess who opened the poem by asking who would honour her is answered by the Roman calendar.

10Self-test

  1. What causes does the poem give for Juno's anger, and which of them is not in Homer?
    Answer

    Her love for Carthage and the fate that a Trojan race would overturn it (1.12–22), and the old grievances: Paris' judgement, the hated race, Ganymede's honours (1.26–28). The Carthaginian cause is not in Homer; it turns a Homeric hatred into a forecast of the Punic wars, a link Ennius' date for her appeasement, the second Punic war, already implies.

  2. What does Juno admit in book 7, and how does it define her role in the plot?
    Answer

    That fate has fixed Lavinia as Aeneas' wife and she cannot prevent it, but she can drag things out and add delays (7.313–316); if she cannot bend the gods above she will move the underworld (7.312). She becomes the source of every delay, and summons Allecto.

  3. What price does Juno exact at the reconciliation, and what does Jupiter add?
    Answer

    The Latins keep their name, language and dress, and the Trojan name dies with Troy (12.819–828). Jupiter grants it, says the Trojans will sink into the Italians, promises to add the rites, and foretells a people unmatched in piety and in honouring Juno (12.834–840).

  4. Why is the reconciliation of book 12 not obviously final?
    Answer

    Jupiter himself forecasts a time for battle when Carthage sends ruin through the Alps (10.11–13); Ennius, as Servius reports on 1.281, placed Juno's appeasement in the second Punic war, and Servius on 12.841 has her won over then and brought to Rome in the third. Feeney (1984) read book 12 as one reconciliation in a series.

  5. How does Venus help her son, and at whose cost?
    Answer

    She wins Jupiter's prophecy, guides Aeneas in disguise, sends Cupid to inflame Dido, accepts Juno's sham marriage, shows him the gods destroying Troy, bargains with Neptune and Vulcan, and heals him with dittany. Dido pays for his safety in Carthage, and Palinurus is the one life Neptune takes (5.815).

  6. Give three passages that show fate does not settle everything in the Aeneid.
    Answer

    Juno can delay what fate has fixed (7.313–316); Vulcan says neither Jupiter nor the fates forbade Troy ten more years (8.398); Dido dies neither by fate nor by a deserved death (4.696). Jupiter also grants Turnus a delay that changes nothing (10.622–627).

  7. How does Virgil's Jupiter differ from Homer's Zeus over a dying son?
    Answer

    Homer's Zeus wonders whether to save Sarpedon and yields to Hera (Iliad 16). Virgil's Jupiter tells the weeping Hercules that each has his appointed day and that his own son Sarpedon fell at Troy (10.467–471): the limit is stated, not debated.

  8. How do pietas and furor meet in Aeneas' last act?
    Answer

    He kills Turnus set on fire by furies and terrible in anger (12.946–947), but in the name of Pallas (12.948–949), out of duty to the dead boy and his father. The virtue and its opposite drive one act, which is why the ending is contested (module 12.7).

  9. What in the poem supports a Stoic reading, and what an Epicurean one?
    Answer

    Stoic: Nautes' counsel to follow the fates and overcome fortune by bearing it (5.709–710) and the unmoved mind of the oak simile (4.449), which Augustine applies to the Stoic sage (City of God 9.4). Epicurean: the proem's question about divine anger (1.11), which Lucretius' gods free from anger answer no (2.651), and Dido's sarcasm about the gods' troubled calm (4.379–380).

  10. Name two Roman cults or rites the poem founds or forecasts, and the passages.
    Answer

    Any two of: the Penates carried from Troy (2.293–297, 2.717, 3.147–171); sacrifice with the head veiled (3.403–409); the Ara Maxima's cult of Hercules (8.268–272); Aeneas' vow of a marble temple to Phoebus and a shrine for the Sibyl's oracles (6.69–74), read as Palatine Apollo; Actian Apollo on the shield (8.704–706).

11Carry forward

  1. Virgil's gods turn Roman history into divine quarrel: Juno's anger is Carthage's, her reconciliation is Rome's Italian identity, and the next round is already forecast for Hannibal. Read later epic gods, Lucan's absent ones and Silius' Juno (module 10.2), against that pattern.
  2. Fate in the Aeneid fixes the end and leaves the time, the cost and the manner open; Jupiter knows and enforces it, and the poem never decides whether he authors it. That gap is where every philosophical reading, Stoic or Epicurean, has to work.
  3. Pietas and furor are the poem's moral poles, but they are not distributed simply between Aeneas and his enemies: the hero carries both, and the gods of order use the agents of disorder when they need them.
