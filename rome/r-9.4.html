How many people

No ancient writer gives Rome's population; what survives are counts of a privileged subset. In 5 BC Augustus gave sixty denarii each to 320,000 of the urban plebs, and in 2 BC the plebs receiving public grain numbered "a little more than 200,000" (Res Gestae 15.2, 15.4). These were male citizens, mostly adult. Add women, children, slaves, foreigners and soldiers, and P. A. Brunt (Italian Manpower, 1971) and Keith Hopkins (Conquerors and Slaves, 1978) reach about a million. Most of that crowd, Seneca says, came from elsewhere (Consolation to Helvia 6.2–3), and Walter Scheidel ("Germs for Rome", 2003) argues that malaria and the diseases of crowding made Rome a demographic sink, kept full only by immigration, free and slave.

Where sources disagree. Against Brunt and Hopkins, Glenn Storey ("The population of ancient Rome", Antiquity 1997) applied the densities of excavated Pompeii and Ostia to Rome's area and reached about 450,000, since no known pre-industrial city packed a million into so small a space. The fourth-century regionary catalogues, with some 46,000 insulae, cannot decide it, because whether an insula there is a block or a rented unit is itself disputed.

Domus and insula

The rich lived in the domus, turned inward on atrium and peristyle, its street front let out as shops. Everyone else rented in the insula: shops (tabernae) at street level, flats (cenacula) above, rooms smaller, darker and cheaper with each flight of stairs. The Insula dell'Ara Coeli below the Capitol, a second-century block of at least five storeys, survives. Fire and collapse were the hazards. Augustus capped new buildings on public streets at seventy feet (Strabo 5.3.7) and in AD 6 created the vigiles, seven cohorts of freedmen, as night watch and fire brigade (Suetonius, Augustus 30.1; Dio 55.26.4–5). Collapse needed no fire. Two of Cicero's shops at Puteoli fell down and the rest were cracking, so that the mice as well as the tenants had moved out (Letters to Atticus 14.9.1). Nero's rebuilding after the fire of 64 is module 7.1's.

Juvenal's third satire turns this into a speech. Umbricius, leaving Rome for Cumae, flees a city propped on slender beams whose landlord's agent plasters over the cracks and bids the tenants sleep easy (3.190–196); fires in which the lodger under the tiles burns last (3.197–202); a year's rent for a dark garret that would buy a house at Sora (3.223–225); sleepless nights amid the carts (3.232–238); pots dropped from windows (3.268–277); and the Greeks and Syrians who have taken the city over (3.58–125). It is satire, and the xenophobia is part of the act, but the details are Cicero's and Strabo's.

Water: the aqueducts and Frontinus

For 441 years, Frontinus says, Rome drank from the Tiber, wells and springs (On Aqueducts 4). Then came the Aqua Appia of 312 BC and the Anio Vetus of 272 (modules 3.4 and 3.5), the Marcia of 144 and the Tepula of 125. Agrippa added the Julia and the Virgo, and as aedile in 33, Pliny says, built 700 basins, 500 fountains and 130 distribution tanks in a single year (Natural History 36.121); Claudius finished the Claudia and the Anio Novus in 52.

Sextus Julius Frontinus, a former governor of Britain, became curator of the waters under Nerva in 97 and wrote On Aqueducts as his own manual, so as not to depend on his subordinates (1–2). Measuring inflow and outflow in a unit of pipe bore, the quinaria, he found the records false, because landowners and the water-men (aquarii) diverted supply, fitted oversized nozzles and bored secret branch pipes (75–76, 112–115). In the city the water fed 247 distribution tanks and 591 public basins (78), tended by a public gang of about 240 and an imperial one of 460 (116). He sets these useful works against the idle pyramids (16). Few homes had running water: a private connection was a personal grant that did not pass to an heir or buyer (107), and most people carried water from the street basin.

Bread: the annona and the dole

Josephus has Agrippa II say that Africa feeds Rome for eight months a year and Egypt for four (Jewish War 2.383–386), and Tiberius told the Senate that the Roman people's life was tossed daily on sea and storm (Tacitus, Annals 3.54). The supply, the annona, was named from the year's harvest. Ostia was too shallow for the big grain ships, which long docked at Puteoli, so Claudius began a harbour to its north in 42 (Dio 60.11; Suetonius, Claudius 20.3). Trajan added the hexagonal basin, and in Ostia's Square of the Corporations shippers from Carthage, Narbo and elsewhere advertised themselves in mosaic. Pelted with crusts in the Forum during a shortage, when the city had fifteen days' grain left (Suetonius, Claudius 18.2; Tacitus, Annals 12.43), Claudius insured shippers against storms, and a Latin who built a ship of 10,000 modii and carried grain in it for six years became a citizen (Gaius, Institutes 1.32c).

The dole began as cheap grain under Gaius Gracchus in 123 and became free under Clodius in 58; those laws as politics are deep dive module 11.3's. As an institution it was a citizen's privilege, not poor relief. The list was closed: Caesar cut it and filled vacancies by lot (Suetonius, Julius 41.3; module 5.4), and Augustus held it at about 200,000 male citizens resident in Rome. Each drew five modii of wheat a month (Sallust, Histories 3.48.19, for 73 BC), enough for one man, not a household, against a token (tessera); Persius mocks the freed slave who, once enrolled in a tribe, owns a ticket for mangy grain (5.73–75). Augustus, who took charge of the supply in 22 BC (module 6.1), considered abolishing the dole and desisted, sure that some successor would revive it for popularity (Suetonius, Augustus 42.3). Late in his reign he created the equestrian prefect of the grain supply (praefectus annonae), responsible for procurement, storage and price; Gaius Turranius held the post in 14 (Tacitus, Annals 1.7). Most bread was still bought from bakers. The Historia Augusta credits Aurelian with handing out baked bread instead of grain.

The Roman day: salutation, baths, dinner

Martial gives the timetable (4.8): the first two hours wear out the callers, the next go to the courts and to business, the eighth to exercise, the ninth to dinner.

The salutation

At dawn clients in togas filled the patron's atrium to greet him (salutatio), hoping for help at law, a loan, a recommendation, a dinner, or the sportula, a cash handout Martial puts at a hundred quadrantes (1.59). The crowd measured the patron's standing: Quintus Cicero's election handbook sorts callers, escorts to the Forum and full-time attendants (Commentariolum petitionis 34–38). The satirists write from the client's side: Juvenal's crowd jostling for the handout on the threshold (1.95–96), and his Trebius served worse bread and wine than his host (Satire 5; compare Pliny, Letters 2.6). Richard Saller (Personal Patronage under the Early Empire, 1982; module 7.5) showed that such unequal exchange remained the working structure of imperial society, up to the senator recommending a protégé to the emperor.

Baths and dinner

Agrippa as aedile opened 170 bath-houses free (Pliny, Natural History 36.121) and later built the first imperial thermae; Nero, Titus, Trajan, Caracalla (module 7.4) and Diocletian built ever larger, with exercise yards, hot, warm and cold rooms, pools and libraries. An ordinary bath cost a quadrans (Horace, Satires 1.3.137). There all ranks met unclothed, and the parasite hunted dinner invitations (Martial 2.14). Seneca, lodging above one, lists the grunting weightlifters, the masseur's slaps and the hair-plucker's shrill cry (Letters 56.1–2); Marcus Aurelius thinks of bathing as oil, sweat, filth and greasy water (Meditations 8.24). Hadrian separated the sexes (Historia Augusta, Hadrian 18.10), so mixed bathing had gone on.

Dinner (cena) came at the ninth or tenth hour. In a domus up to nine diners reclined on three couches, placed by rank, and ate ab ovo usque ad mala, from egg to apples (Horace, Satires 1.3.6–7). Pliny scolds a friend who left his lettuce, snails and eggs for someone else's oysters and Spanish dancing girls (Letters 1.15). Petronius' Trimalchio (module 9.3) parodies the type. Most Romans had no dining room and often no kitchen: they ate bread, porridge, beans and greens and bought hot food at street bars, which emperors policed (Suetonius, Nero 16.2).

The arena: gladiators, beasts and the condemned

The origins of gladiatorial combat and Rome's first show in 264 BC are module 1.2's. The Latin word, munus, means a duty owed: the shows began as gifts to the dead at aristocratic funerals (Tertullian, On the Spectacles 12) and swelled with competition for votes, from twenty-two pairs in 216 (Livy 23.30.15) to Caesar's 320 in 65 (module 5.3). Augustus counted about 10,000 men fighting in eight shows given in his own and his family's names (Res Gestae 22.1). A senatorial decree of about 177, on bronze from Italica, capped gladiators' prices for the provincial priests obliged to give shows. Gladiators were slaves, captives, criminals and free volunteers who swore to be burned, bound and killed by the sword (Petronius 117.5; Seneca, Letters 37.1). They trained in schools (ludi) under a lanista, in Rome the imperial Ludus Magnus, and fought in fixed armatures, light against heavy: the murmillo with crested helmet and tall shield against the Thracian with small shield and curved sword, the helmetless net-man (retiarius) with trident against the smooth-helmeted secutor.

A show, posted on painted notices naming the giver, the pairs, a hunt and the awnings, ran beasts in the morning, the condemned at midday, gladiators in the afternoon. A beaten man appealed by raising a finger; the giver decided, prompted by the crowd, and both fighters might be reprieved standing. Trained men were costly, and many bouts ended without a death. Pompeian graffiti mark results V (vicit), M (missus, reprieved) and P (periit), and Flamma's tombstone in Sicily records thirty-four fights, twenty-one wins, nine draws and four reprieves before he died at thirty (CIL X 7297).

Where sources disagree. Juvenal says the crowd kills "with turned thumb" (verso pollice, 3.36–37) without saying which way; Gérôme's painting Pollice Verso (1872) fixed thumbs down for death. Anthony Corbeill (Nature Embodied, 2004) argues that the hostile sign was the thumb thrust up or out, and that favour was shown by pressing the thumb into the closed fist (Pliny, Natural History 28.25). No ancient text fixes the direction.

Executions were part of the programme; the legal penalty is module 9.1's. Seneca, at a midday show, found plain murder, unprotected men made to kill one another (Letters 7.3–5). Strabo watched the bandit Selurus, "son of Etna", dropped from a collapsing mock volcano among caged beasts (6.2.6), and Kathleen Coleman ("Fatal Charades", Journal of Roman Studies 1990) collected executions staged as myth or drama, such as Martial's criminal crucified and mauled by a bear as the bandit Laureolus (On the Spectacles). Beast hunts began, so far as Livy knew, with lions and panthers in 186 (39.22.2); at Pompey's games of 55 the elephants roused, Cicero wrote, not pleasure but a kind of pity (Letters to Friends 7.1.3); Augustus counted about 3,500 beasts killed in twenty-six hunts (Res Gestae 22.3).

The Colosseum, whose dedication in 80 and Alföldy's reading of its inscription are module 7.2's, was a machine. Beneath the arena the hypogeum, usually dated to Domitian, held cages and lifts: Heinz-Jürgen Beste of the German Archaeological Institute reconstructs, at its peak, about sixty capstans hoisting beasts and scenery up through the floor.

Where sources disagree. Dio says Titus suddenly flooded the amphitheatre for swimming horses and bulls and a sea fight (66.25.2–3); Suetonius mentions only a sea fight in the old naumachia (Titus 7.3), where Dio puts a second. Beste identified drains that he believes emptied a flooded arena, possible only before masonry substructures filled the space beneath the floor.

Circus and stage

The Circus Maximus, laid out in legend by Tarquinius Priscus (module 1.5), held races of seven laps round a central barrier, counted off by eggs and by Agrippa's dolphins (Dio 49.43.2). Four colours, Whites, Reds, Greens and Blues, were companies with stables, horses and drivers. Charioteers could grow rich: the Lusitanian Gaius Appuleius Diocles claimed 4,257 starts, 1,462 wins and 35,863,120 sesterces in prizes over twenty-four years (CIL VI 10048). Pliny marvelled that thousands cared only for a coloured cloth, and would follow it if the colours were swapped mid-race (Letters 9.6). Alan Cameron (Circus Factions, 1976) argued that the fans, at Rome and later in the Nika riot at Constantinople (module 8.6), were supporters' clubs without political programmes.

Where sources disagree. Dionysius, describing the circus of his own day, gives it 150,000 seats (3.68.3); Pliny, some eighty years later, gives Caesar's circus 250,000 (Natural History 36.102). One counted standing room, or the building grew between them; neither says how he counted.

The Senate refused a permanent theatre until Pompey's of 55, disguised, Tertullian says, as the steps of a temple of Venus (On the Spectacles 10); seats went by rank (Suetonius, Augustus 44). Comedy and tragedy yielded to mime, unmasked and with actresses, and to pantomime, a single masked dancer playing every part in a myth to sung text and music; Jerome's Chronicle makes Pylades, in 22 BC, the first at Rome to have a chorus and piper accompany him. Partisans rioted: soldiers died in a theatre riot in AD 15 (Tacitus, Annals 1.77), and the actors were expelled in 23 (4.14). Pylades told Augustus it was to his advantage that the people spent their time on Pylades and his rival Bathyllus (Dio 54.17.5).

"Bread and circuses"

Juvenal's phrase comes from the satire on vain wishes, beside the fall of Sejanus (module 7.1): the people that once gave out commands, fasces and legions now anxiously longs for two things only, panem et circenses (10.77–81). Fronto made it a maxim of government: the Roman people is held above all by the grain dole and the shows (Preamble to History). It gets four things wrong. The dole was grain, not bread, for a capped list of citizens, not for the poor or the whole city. The races came on fixed festival days, and gladiatorial shows were occasional gifts, never part of the republican festival calendar. The elite watched too, seated by rank, so that the shows displayed the social order. And the crowd spoke: Cicero counts the games among the three places where the Roman people's judgement was most clearly expressed (Pro Sestio 106), and imperial crowds demanded cheaper grain and the fall of ministers to the emperor's face, as with Cleander in 190 (module 7.3).

Where sources disagree. Paul Veyne (Le pain et le cirque, 1976) rejected the reading of the shows as depoliticisation: the emperor's gifts, like a town benefactor's (module 7.5), expressed his majesty rather than bought off a threat. Keith Hopkins (Death and Renewal, 1983) saw arena and circus as the place where emperor and people met face to face once the assemblies had lost their power. Juvenal's contempt and Fronto's maxim are both the view from the front rows.

Pompeii and Herculaneum: the town-level check

The towns buried in 79 (module 7.2) test the metropolitan picture; Pompeii held perhaps 8,000 to 12,000 people, on the estimates of Willem Jongman (1988) and Andrew Wallace-Hadrill (1991). Much is confirmed. Pompeii's amphitheatre, built about 70 BC by two magistrates at their own cost (CIL X 852), is the oldest in stone that survives. In 59 a brawl with Nucerians at a show became a massacre, and the Senate banned Pompeii's games for ten years (Tacitus, Annals 14.17). Public baths, an aqueduct feeding street fountains, bakeries with lava mills and street bars with masonry counters show the daily round. Much is corrected. There was no dole; houses were low; local magnates paid for shows and baths (module 7.5); and electoral notices from fullers, fruit-sellers and other trades show the town politics Juvenal thought Rome had lost. Wallace-Hadrill (Houses and Society in Pompeii and Herculaneum, 1994) found rich and poor side by side and the atrium a public room for the morning callers. Herculaneum's sewer under Cardo V, cleared in 2009, showed that the shopkeepers and tenants above it ate fish, sea urchin, figs and eggs, a diet no text records for such people.

Where sources disagree. The towns are often treated as Roman life frozen at a moment. Penelope Allison (Pompeian Households, 2004), studying the finds room by room, argued that many houses were in disorder, under repair or partly abandoned after the earthquake of 62 or 63, so that Pompeii in 79 was a town in trouble, not a snapshot. Wallace-Hadrill's analysis (1994) reads plans and decoration rather than the objects left in rooms, and so is less exposed to that objection.

Self-test

  1. Why are the Res Gestae figures of 320,000 and 200,000 not the population of Rome, and how do the high and low modern estimates differ in method?
    Answer

    They count male citizens, mostly adult, receiving largesse or grain (Res Gestae 15). Brunt and Hopkins add women, children, slaves and foreigners to reach about a million; Storey (1997) applied the densities of Pompeii and Ostia to Rome's area and reached about 450,000.

  2. What made the insula dangerous, and what did Augustus do about it?
    Answer

    Fire and collapse, which Cicero's cracking shops (Letters to Atticus 14.9.1) and Juvenal 3.190–202 both describe. Augustus capped new buildings at seventy feet (Strabo 5.3.7) and created the seven cohorts of vigiles in AD 6.

  3. Why is Juvenal's third satire both evidence and a trap?
    Answer

    Its collapses, fires, rents and night traffic match Cicero and Strabo. But it is a satirical speech by a xenophobic persona, built to exaggerate, and must be checked against other kinds of evidence.

  4. What did Frontinus discover when he measured the water supply, and why does his book matter?
    Answer

    The records were false because landowners and water-men diverted water, fitted oversized nozzles and bored secret pipes (On Aqueducts 75–76, 112–115). It is an official's own account of a working department, with figures for tanks, basins and staff (78, 116).

  5. Who received the grain dole, how much, and who ran the supply?
    Answer

    Male citizens resident in Rome on a closed list, about 200,000 under Augustus, each drawing five modii a month against a token: a citizen privilege, not poor relief. Procurement and price fell to the equestrian prefect of the grain supply, an Augustan creation (Turranius in 14, Annals 1.7).

  6. What did a client give and receive at the salutatio, and what did it show?
    Answer

    He gave his presence and escort; he received help, recommendations, dinners or the sportula of a hundred quadrantes (Martial 1.59). The crowd displayed the patron's standing (Commentariolum petitionis 34–38), and Saller showed that such patronage structured imperial society.

  7. Why is "fought to the death" a poor general description of gladiatorial combat?
    Answer

    Trained gladiators were costly, losers could appeal and be reprieved, and both could be sent off standing. Pompeian graffiti record reprieves (M) beside deaths (P), and Flamma's tombstone lists thirty-four fights with nine draws and four reprieves (CIL X 7297).

  8. What does Coleman mean by "fatal charades", and give two examples.
    Answer

    Executions of condemned criminals staged as mythological or dramatic scenes: Martial's criminal crucified and mauled by a bear as Laureolus (On the Spectacles), and Strabo's Selurus dropped from a collapsing mock Etna among beasts (6.2.6).

  9. Was the Colosseum ever flooded for a sea battle?
    Answer

    Possibly, at first. Dio says Titus flooded it (66.25.2–3), Suetonius mentions only a sea fight in the old naumachia (Titus 7.3), and Beste's drains suggest flooding was possible only before masonry substructures filled the space under the arena.

  10. What does Juvenal's panem et circenses (10.81) get wrong, and what does Pompeii add?
    Answer

    The dole was grain for a capped list of citizens, not bread for all; races came on festival days and gladiatorial shows were occasional; the elite attended in rank order; and the crowd used the shows to make demands (Pro Sestio 106; Cleander in 190). Pompeii shows a town with no dole, shows paid for by local magnates and lively electoral politics.

Carry forward

  1. The city of Rome lived on the empire: its numbers, water and grain depended on provincial surplus, migration and a growing administration (curator of the waters, prefect of the grain supply), so a supply crisis in the capital was always a political crisis.
  2. The capital's privileges, free grain and imperial shows, were citizen privileges and public displays of rank and imperial generosity, not poor relief or simple mob control; read "bread and circuses" as elite satire, not as description.
  3. Most literary evidence for daily life is satirical or moralising and about the capital (Juvenal, Martial, Seneca); test it against inscriptions, graffiti and the excavated towns, which both confirm the picture and correct it.
