Polybius: hostage, tutor, witness

Polybius was born at Megalopolis around 200 BC, son of Lycortas, a leading Achaean statesman. He carried Philopoemen's urn at his funeral in 182 (Plutarch, Philopoemen 21) and was the League's cavalry commander in 170/169 (Polybius 28.6). In 167 he was among the thousand Achaeans deported to Italy; Greek module 7.3 tells how few came home. He stayed in Rome because of a friendship. The sons of Aemilius Paullus had borrowed books from him, and the younger, Scipio Aemilianus, then about eighteen, asked why Polybius always addressed his brother: did he too think him idle and un-Roman? Polybius promised to help him speak and act worthy of his ancestors (31.23–24).

He was more than a tutor. He helped the Seleucid hostage Demetrius escape in 162 (31.11–15). When the detainees were freed in 150, Cato told him that asking for their old honours too was like Odysseus going back into the Cyclops' cave for his cap and belt (Plutarch, Cato Maior 9). In 146 he stood beside Scipio as Carthage burned (38.21–22), and after Corinth the Roman commissioners left him to settle the Achaean cities' laws (39.5). He died at eighty-two after a fall from his horse (Pseudo-Lucian, Long Lives 22). His forty books, planned to end in 167 and extended to 146 (3.4), are treated as a source in module 0.1.

Where sources disagree. Was Polybius Rome's analyst or its apologist? F. W. Walbank (Polybius, 1972) saw a Greek statesman who came to accept Roman rule and wrote to explain it to Greeks. Craige Champion (Cultural Politics in Polybius's Histories, 2004) reads a double message: Romans praised as near-Greeks for Roman readers, their barbarian traits left visible for Greek ones.

Book 6: three powers in one state

Polybius asks how, and under what constitution, Rome conquered almost the whole inhabited world in under fifty-three years (1.1.5). Greek module 7.3 gives the answer in brief; here it is in full. Book 6 answers by interrupting the narrative after Cannae, because a constitution, like a man's character, is proved in reversal (6.2).

The theory comes first (6.3–10). Three good simple constitutions, kingship, aristocracy and democracy, each decay into a corrupt twin: tyranny, oligarchy and mob rule (ochlokratia). The sequence, anacyclosis, runs by nature: kings' heirs become tyrants, the nobles who expel them rule as an aristocracy until their sons make it an oligarchy, the people who expel those found a democracy, and their grandsons, bored with equality, corrupt it until violence returns everything to a master. Only a mixture halts the wheel. Lycurgus designed one for Sparta by reasoning; the Romans reached it through many struggles, learning from each disaster (6.10).

The description follows (6.11–18). The consuls, commanding armies and presiding over Senate and assemblies, look like kings; the Senate, controlling the treasury and receiving embassies, like an aristocracy; the people, who confer honours and punishments, elect magistrates, pass laws and decide on war and peace, like a democracy. Even Romans could not say which the state was (6.11.11). Each part checks the others. A consul in the field needs the Senate for supplies and any extension of command, and the people to ratify his treaties and judge him afterwards; a single tribune can stop the Senate meeting; the people depend on the Senate for public contracts and judges (6.15–17).

Comparisons close the case. Athens is a ship without a master (6.43–44); Sparta's mixture kept it free but could not sustain conquest (6.48–50); Carthage had passed its prime, its people having gained the chief say where at Rome the Senate still held it, and it fought with mercenaries where Rome fought with citizens (6.51–52; module 4.1).

Camp, funeral and the fear of the gods

The other half of the argument is custom. Module 9.2 treats the army; here the camp is evidence. A Roman army built the same camp every night, laid out like a city with streets and fixed quarters (6.27–32), where Greeks fitted a camp to the ground (6.42). A sentry who failed was cudgelled to death by his comrades, a unit that fled was decimated, and men who fought when not obliged received spears, cups or crowns before the assembled army (6.37–39). Public reward and punishment made the legion a school of ambition and fear.

The funeral is the famous passage (6.53–54). A dead noble was carried to the Rostra and a son praised his deeds before the people. The family kept wax masks (imagines) of ancestors who had held office; at the funeral men of matching build wore them, in the robes of the offices each had held, and sat on ivory chairs while the speaker recounted every ancestor's achievements. No finer sight, Polybius says, could meet a young man in love with glory (6.53.10). Good men's fame was renewed, and the young were readied to endure anything for the common good.

Money and religion complete the case (6.56). A Greek official trusted with a talent cheats despite ten clerks and as many seals; a Roman magistrate handling vast sums keeps faith because of his oath. The reason is deisidaimonia, fear of the gods, which Greeks thought a weakness and Polybius calls what held Rome together: a fickle crowd must be restrained by invisible terrors and solemn display. A state of wise men would not need it.

Judging the analysis

Ancient readers saw the problems. Tacitus thought a blended constitution easier to praise than to create, and short-lived if created (Annals 4.33). Cicero adopted the theory in On the Republic but gave it a Roman turn through Cato, who said Rome's state excelled because it was built by many men over centuries, not by one lawgiver (2.1–2). Ennius had given the native answer in one hexameter: moribus antiquis res stat Romana virisque, the Roman state stands on ancient customs and on men (Cicero, On the Republic 5.1).

Modern critics add three points. The theory is incoherent: the mixture escapes a law of nature, yet 6.57 predicts Rome's own slide into mob rule once prosperity breeds luxury, a strain set out in Walbank's Historical Commentary on Polybius (1957–79). The account is formal, listing powers but omitting the nobility's grip on office, patronage, an assembly weighted by wealth and the allies. And it is Greek theory fitted to a foreign state. Its strength is the chapters on custom.

Where sources disagree. Fergus Millar ("The Political Character of the Classical Roman Republic", 1984) took Polybius' democratic element at its word: the people elected, legislated and judged, so Rome was in a real sense a democracy. Karl-Joachim Hölkeskamp (Reconstructing the Roman Republic, 2010) and Henrik Mouritsen (Plebs and Politics in the Late Roman Republic, 2001) reply that assemblies voted only on what magistrates proposed, in small crowds, so power stayed with the nobility. The debate in full, with North and Morstein-Marx, is deep dive module 11.1.

Latin literature begins as translation

As Denis Feeney stresses (Beyond Greek, 2016), no other ancient people set out to translate Greek literature into its own language. Rome began in 240 BC, the year after the First Punic War, when Livius Andronicus staged a play at the Roman Games (Cicero, Brutus 72); module 3.5 gives his Tarentine origin. He wrote tragedies and comedies on Greek models and the Odusia, the Odyssey in native Saturnian verse, whose first line survives (Gellius 18.9.5):

Virum mihi, Camena, insece versutum

Homer's Muse becomes a Latin Camena, polytropos becomes versutus. Horace still remembered it beaten into him at school (Epistles 2.1.69–71).

Gnaeus Naevius, a Campanian veteran of the First Punic War, staged his first play in 235 (Gellius 17.21.45). He is credited with inventing the play on Roman history (fabula praetexta), with a Clastidium on Marcellus' victory of 222, and wrote the Bellum Punicum, a Saturnian epic of his own war that reached back to Aeneas (Greek module 10.12). Gellius has him jailed for abusing leading men (3.3.15).

Quintus Ennius (239–169), from Rudiae in the heel of Italy, said he had three hearts, Oscan, Greek and Latin (Gellius 17.17.1). Cato brought him to Rome from Sardinia in 204 (Nepos, Cato 1.4); Fulvius Nobilior took him to Aetolia in 189, which Cato denounced (Cicero, Tusculan Disputations 1.3); and Fulvius' son made him a citizen in 184 (Cicero, Brutus 79). His Annales, eighteen books from Troy to his own day, replaced the Saturnian with Homer's hexameter, invoked the Greek Muses where Livius had a Camena, and opened with a dream in which Homer's soul passed into Ennius. The old Saturnians he dismissed as what Fauns and seers once sang (Cicero, Brutus 71). He also put Euripides on the Roman stage.

Where sources disagree. Cicero (Brutus 72) makes the play of 240 the beginning of Latin literature; Livy 7.2, probably following Varro, makes it one stage in a native theatre that had grown from Etruscan dancers in 364. Feeney treats 240 as a deliberate choice of the Roman elite after victory over Carthage; T. P. Wiseman (module 0.1) argues for a lost drama on Roman themes before and beside it.

Comedy in Greek dress: Plautus and Terence

Surviving Roman comedy is the palliata, played in the Greek cloak and set in Greek cities. Titus Maccius Plautus, from Sarsina in Umbria, wrote from about 205 until his death in 184, the year of Cato's censorship (Cicero, Brutus 60); twenty-one plays survive. He adapted Menander, Diphilus and Philemon, and said so with a joke at Rome's expense: Philemo scripsit, Plautus vortit barbare, Philemon wrote it, Plautus turned it into barbarian (Trinummus 19). Barbarian means Latin: Romans saw themselves through Greek eyes even while laughing. On his stage pergraecari, to play the Greek, means to carouse (Mostellaria), and clever slaves outwit fathers in an Athens full of Roman jokes and magistrates.

Publius Terentius Afer, born at Carthage according to Suetonius' life and freed by a senator, wrote six plays (166–160) that follow Menander and Apollodorus of Carystus closely in purer Latin. His prologues answer the charge of spoiling Greek plays by combining two (Andria 9–21) and the rumour that noble friends wrote for him, which he took as praise (Adelphoe 15–21). The Adelphoe was staged in 160 at the funeral games of Aemilius Paullus, given by his sons, Scipio Aemilianus among them. Caesar later called Terence half-Menander, pure but lacking force (Suetonius, Life of Terence).

Where sources disagree. How much of Plautus is Plautus? Gellius (2.23) set Caecilius beside his Menandrian original and found the Latin poorer, and nineteenth-century scholars mined Plautus for lost Greek plays. Eduard Fraenkel (Plautinisches im Plautus, 1922) identified systematic Plautine additions, and a papyrus of Menander's Dis Exapaton, presented by Eric Handley in 1968, confirmed against Bacchides that Plautus cut scenes, rewrote speeches and set dialogue to music.

Greek history and Latin prose: Fabius Pictor and Cato

History came to Rome in Greek. Quintus Fabius Pictor, senator and envoy to Delphi after Cannae, wrote around 200 BC in Greek for a Mediterranean public that read no Latin; module 0.1 covers the work and Polybius' criticism (3.8–9). A Roman who wished to explain Rome did it in the language of the world Rome was about to conquer.

Marcus Porcius Cato (234–149), a new man from Tusculum, made hostility to Greek manners his public character. Plutarch says he learned Greek late (Cato Maior 2) yet shows him at Athens in 191 speaking Latin through an interpreter though he could have spoken Greek (Cato Maior 12). To his son he wrote that the Greeks were a worthless and unteachable race who would corrupt everything once they gave Rome their literature, worse still if they sent their doctors, and that they called Romans barbarians (Pliny, Natural History 29.14). He called Socrates a babbler and said Rome would lose its empire once infected by Greek letters (Plutarch, Cato Maior 23). Yet his Origines, the first history in Latin prose, took its subject from the Greek genre of foundation stories and made the Aborigines, Rome's predecessors in Latium, Greeks from Achaea (Dionysius of Halicarnassus 1.11.1). On Agriculture, the earliest Latin prose work to survive whole, is a self-portrait in curt commands: its preface ranks the farmer above the trader and the moneylender and says the bravest soldiers come from farmers (preface 1–4); module 4.4 uses the estate as evidence.

The censorship of 184 made the persona policy (Livy 39.40–44; Plutarch, Cato Maior 16–19). Cato and Lucius Valerius Flaccus expelled seven senators, among them Lucius Quinctius Flamininus, brother of the liberator of Greece, for killing a man at a banquet to please a favourite; took the public horse from Lucius Scipio; assessed women's clothing, jewellery and carriages at ten times their value for tax; cut off public water piped to private houses; and built the Basilica Porcia. Luxury and the Scipios were linked targets: as quaestor in 204 Cato had attacked Scipio Africanus' spending and his hours in the palaestra at Syracuse (Plutarch, Cato Maior 3).

Where sources disagree. Plutarch and Cicero's On Old Age (26) have Cato learn Greek only in old age. Alan Astin (Cato the Censor, 1978) argued that he knew Greek literature throughout his career and attacked Greek manners in Roman leaders, not Greek learning. Erich Gruen (Culture and National Identity in Republican Rome, 1992) went further: his anti-Hellenism was a performance by which a Hellenised elite defined Romanness against a Greek foil.

Philosophers in the Senate, 155 BC

In 155 Athens sent the heads of three schools, Carneades of the Academy, Diogenes of Babylon of the Stoa and Critolaus of the Peripatos, to plead against a fine of five hundred talents for plundering Oropus; the Senate cut it to a hundred (Pausanias 7.11). They addressed the Senate through the senator Gaius Acilius as interpreter (Plutarch, Cato Maior 22) and lectured while they waited. On one day Carneades, whose scepticism Greek module 7.2 explains, praised justice; on the next he argued that justice is either nothing or the height of folly, and that Romans who wished to be just would have to give back their conquests and return to their huts. Cicero reworked the pair in book 3 of On the Republic, known for this part chiefly through Lactantius' Divine Institutes book 5. Cato urged the Senate to send the envoys home quickly, so that Roman youth would listen to the laws and magistrates as before (Plutarch, Cato Maior 22). The Senate had let a praetor expel philosophers and rhetoricians in 161 (Gellius 15.11.1), and in 181 had burned books ascribed to Numa, Greek works of philosophy that Valerius Antias called Pythagorean, a claim Livy rejects (40.29). Neither measure kept Greek thought out.

The "Scipionic circle" and captive Greece

Scipio Aemilianus kept Polybius and the Stoic Panaetius with him at home and on campaign (Velleius Paterculus 1.13.3); his friend Gaius Laelius was called the Wise; the satirist Lucilius, who served under him at Numantia, mocked Romans who affected Greek (Cicero, On Ends 1.9). Cicero made these men the cast of On the Republic and On Friendship, Greek learning lightly worn, and followed Panaetius in On Duties. Modern scholars named them the "Scipionic circle", a coterie of philhellene nobles. No ancient writer uses the phrase.

Where sources disagree. Hermann Strasburger ("Der Scipionenkreis", 1966) and James Zetzel ("Cicero and the Scipionic Circle", 1972) argued that the circle is largely Cicero's creation, a past built to dignify his own union of politics and learning; Alan Astin (Scipio Aemilianus, 1967) found no cultural programme or faction. Polybius, Velleius and Terence's prologues still attest the friendships; their coherence is what is doubtful.

Horace, writing to Augustus, put the century in a line: Graecia capta ferum victorem cepit et artis intulit agresti Latio, captive Greece took her savage conqueror captive and brought the arts to rustic Latium (Epistles 2.1.156–157). Greek module 7.3 uses it to frame loot, tutors and gods; in context it is narrower. Horace is arguing against admirers of the old Latin poets: the rough Saturnian gave way to elegance, yet traces of the farm remained, because the Roman turned late to Greek books, only in the peace after the Punic Wars (2.1.157–163). The epigram concerns literary taste, and its chronology is vague: post Punica bella fits Livius' first play of 240 only if Horace meant the first war.

Where sources disagree. Romans could not agree when Greek luxury began to corrupt them: Livy dated it to Manlius Vulso's army returning from Asia in 187 with bronze couches and lute-girls (39.6.7), Polybius to the years after Pydna (31.25), Calpurnius Piso to 154 (Pliny, Natural History 17.244), Sallust to the fall of Carthage in 146 (Catiline 10). Gruen and Andrew Wallace-Hadrill (Rome's Cultural Revolution, 2008) replace capture with choice: an elite switching between Greek and Roman codes as the setting required.

Self-test

  1. How did Polybius come to Rome, and why did he stay in the city rather than in an Italian town?
    Answer

    He was one of the thousand Achaeans deported after Pydna in 167. The sons of Aemilius Paullus, above all Scipio Aemilianus, sought his company, and he promised to help Scipio become worthy of his ancestors (Polybius 31.23–24).

  2. What is anacyclosis, and how did Sparta and Rome escape it according to Polybius?
    Answer

    The natural cycle in which kingship, aristocracy and democracy decay into tyranny, oligarchy and mob rule and give way to one another. A mixed constitution balances the three good forms; Lycurgus designed Sparta's by reasoning, while Rome reached its mixture through struggle and the lessons of disaster (6.3–10).

  3. Give two ways in which, for Polybius, one part of the Roman state checks another.
    Answer

    Any two of: the Senate controls a consul's supplies and the extension of his command; the people ratify his treaties and judge his conduct; one tribune can stop the Senate meeting; the Senate controls the public contracts and supplies judges on which the people depend (6.15–17).

  4. Why did Polybius think the aristocratic funeral was a cause of Roman strength?
    Answer

    Actors wearing the ancestral masks and insignia of office, and a eulogy of the dead man and every ancestor, kept the fame of good men alive and made the young ready to endure anything for the common good (6.53–54).

  5. What did Polybius claim Roman religion did, and what was his own attitude to it?
    Answer

    Fear of the gods (deisidaimonia) held the state together: oaths kept magistrates honest with public money, and invisible terrors restrained a fickle crowd. He treats it as a useful device that a state of wise men would not need (6.56).

  6. State one ancient and two modern objections to Polybius' constitutional analysis.
    Answer

    Tacitus (Annals 4.33) thought a mixed constitution easier to praise than to create, and short-lived. Modern critics note that a natural cycle and an escape from it sit uneasily together, especially as 6.57 predicts Rome's decline, and that the account is formal, omitting the nobility, patronage and the weighted assemblies.

  7. What does the first line of Livius Andronicus' Odusia show about early Roman translation, and how did Ennius change the approach?
    Answer

    Livius naturalised Homer: the Muse became a Latin Camena, polytropos became versutus, and the metre was the native Saturnian. Ennius invoked the Greek Muses, adopted Homer's hexameter and claimed to be Homer reborn.

  8. What does Plautus vortit barbare reveal about Roman comedy?
    Answer

    Plautus openly adapted Greek New Comedy and called his Latin version "barbarian", taking over the Greek view of Romans as a joke. Cato made the same complaint about Greek contempt in earnest (Pliny, Natural History 29.14).

  9. Why do Astin and Gruen doubt that Cato simply rejected Greek culture?
    Answer

    He knew Greek well, chose to speak Latin at Athens, brought Ennius to Rome and gave Rome's predecessors Greek origins in the Origines (Dionysius 1.11.1). His attacks targeted Greek manners in Roman leaders, and served to define Roman identity against a Greek foil.

  10. What happened during the philosophers' embassy of 155, and why did Cato want it ended quickly?
    Answer

    Carneades, Diogenes and Critolaus came to plead against the Oropus fine and lectured while waiting; Carneades argued for justice one day and against it the next. Cato feared that the young would prize eloquence over deeds and urged the Senate to send them home (Plutarch, Cato Maior 22).

Carry forward

  1. The first systematic explanation of Rome's success was Greek: Polybius' mixed constitution and his chapters on custom. Cicero took over the frame, and the modern argument over how democratic Rome was still begins from Book 6.
  2. Latin literature began as translation sponsored by the Roman elite at state festivals: Greek forms carrying Roman content. Every later Roman poet works inside this double inheritance, where allusion to a Greek model is the norm.
  3. Roman identity was defined against Greece by Romans who were themselves Hellenised. Cato's invective, the expulsions of philosophers and the dates of moral decline are ways of managing Greek culture, not refusing it; "captive Greece" is a metaphor, not a description.
