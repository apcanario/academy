01The ancient lives and what they rest on

Almost everything told about Virgil's life comes from one source passed through several hands. Suetonius wrote lives of the Latin poets in his On Poets early in the second century AD; the life of Virgil survives because the grammarian Aelius Donatus, in the fourth century, put it at the head of his commentary, with additions of his own. Scholars call it the Suetonius–Donatus life and cite it by section; it is "Donatus, Life" below. Servius, writing about AD 400, opens his commentary with a shorter life. Later lives under the names of Probus and Phocas add little that is independent. Every one of them was written at least a century after the poet died.

The frame is secure. Virgil (Publius Vergilius Maro) was born on 15 October 70 BC, in the first consulship of Pompey and Crassus, at Andes near Mantua (Life 2). His father was a potter, some said, or, as more said, a hired man who married his employer's daughter and grew rich on woodland and bees (Life 1). He spent his boyhood at Cremona, took the toga of manhood on his fifteenth birthday, in 55, under the same two consuls (Life 6; the transmitted numeral is seventeen, the consuls give fifteen), then moved to Milan and Rome (Life 7). He was tall, dark and countrified, often ill, slow of speech, so retiring that Naples called him Parthenias, "the maiden" (Life 8, 11, 16). He died at Brundisium on 21 September 19 BC and was buried on the road to Puteoli, beneath a couplet he is said to have written himself: Mantua me genuit, Calabri rapuere, tenet nunc Parthenope; cecini pascua rura duces (Life 35–36). Mantua bore me, Calabria took me, Naples holds me; I sang pastures, farms, leaders. The last three words list the Eclogues, the Georgics and the Aeneid.

Much of the rest is inference from the poems. The boy Alexander, Pollio's gift, is the Alexis of the second Eclogue (Life 9). The farm lost and recovered in the confiscations, with a veteran who nearly killed the poet (Life 19–20), is the first and ninth Eclogues read as autobiography; module 6.3 sets out those poems and warns that they name no one. The nickname Parthenias puns on Parthenope, the poetic name of Naples. The epitaph shapes the life to the career. Ancient biography worked this way for Greek poets too, building lives out of works.

The juvenilia show the same habit. Donatus (Life 17–18) credits the young Virgil with the Culex, the Ciris, the Catalepton and other pieces, the collection now called the Appendix Vergiliana. Two of the Catalepton poems (5 and 8) have the poet abandon rhetoric for the Epicurean Siro's school near Naples and later live in Siro's little villa; most of the collection is now thought spurious, and these two are disputed. Independent evidence exists in one place. Papyri from the library of the Epicurean Philodemus at Herculaneum name among his addressees Vergil, Varius, Quintilius Varus and Plotius Tucca, the poet's closest friends (David Armstrong and others, eds., Vergil, Philodemus, and the Augustans, 2004). The Epicurean circle on the Bay of Naples is no fiction.

Where sources disagree. How much of Donatus can be trusted? Nicholas Horsfall (in A Companion to the Study of Virgil, 1995) treated the life as little more than the poems turned into anecdote, padded with omens such as the laurel his mother dreamed of (Life 3). Against that, Suetonius had served in the imperial secretariat and could read Augustus' letters: Donatus quotes the princeps pressing for drafts (Life 31), and Macrobius preserves Virgil's reply (Saturnalia 1.24.11). The documentary core, the dates and the account of the manuscript, is better founded than the colour.

02The Eclogues as a book

The ten Eclogues, written over about three years (Life 25) and usually dated about 42–38, are Theocritus' pastoral Idylls carried into Latin and into history. Virgil called it Syracusan verse (Eclogue 6.1) and his Muse slender: the shepherd should sing deductum carmen, a finely spun song (6.5), Callimachus' programme in Latin, which module 10.1 traces into Ovid. Theocritus' herdsmen live in a timeless Sicily. Virgil's live in Italy during the triumviral years, and their world is broken into by land seizures, a consul and a dead Caesar who becomes a star (9.47).

The book is designed as a whole. Wendell Clausen (A Commentary on Virgil, Eclogues, 1994) argued that Virgil planned the collection first and wrote and rewrote the poems to fit it. The odd-numbered poems are dialogues, the even-numbered mostly single voices. Modern readers map the ten as pairs around a centre: 1 and 9 on the confiscations, 2 and 8 on love's laments, 3 and 7 on singing contests, 4 and 6 on songs that rise above pastoral to prophecy and the making of the world. Poem 5, Daphnis' death and deification, stands at the middle; poem 10 gives the elegist Gallus, lovesick in Arcadia, a farewell that closes the book.

The fourth Eclogue's child

The fourth poem announces its own ambition: Sicelides Musae, paulo maiora canamus, Sicilian Muses, let us sing of somewhat greater things (4.1). The last age of the Cumaean Sibyl's song has come; the great order of the centuries is born anew; the Virgin returns, Saturn's reign returns, a new generation is sent down from high heaven (4.4–7). A boy is about to be born, under whom the iron race will cease and a golden one rise; the age will ripen as he grows, from milk-and-honey infancy through a last round of wars, a second Argo and a second Achilles, to a world where the earth bears everything unploughed (4.8–45). It begins in Pollio's consulship: te consule (4.11), so in 40. Module 5.5 places it in the hopeful season of the Treaty of Brundisium, that autumn, when Antony married Octavia.

The materials are literary. The races are Hesiod's (Works and Days 109–201, Greek module 2.4); the Virgin is Aratus' Justice, who left the earth when the bronze race began (Phaenomena 96–136); the Sibyl gives the cycle of ages a Roman oracle; Catullus' Fates sing at a wedding of a hero's birth (Catullus 64.323–381). The identity of the child has been argued since antiquity.

Where sources disagree. Servius, on 4.11, reports Asconius Pedianus' story that Pollio's son Asinius Gallus claimed to be the child. W. W. Tarn ("Alexander Helios and the Golden Age", Journal of Roman Studies, 1932) proposed Antony's son by Cleopatra. Clausen's commentary makes the strongest case for the expected child of Antony and Octavia, the dynastic seal of Brundisium; the baby was a girl. Eduard Norden (Die Geburt des Kindes, 1924) traced the child to Hellenistic and Egyptian religious ideas of a divine child who opens a new age; read that way, the child is a figure of the age more than a particular baby. The Christian reading belongs to deep dive module 12.8.

03The Georgics: a didactic poem and its models

The four books of the Georgics, seven years in the writing (Life 25) and finished in 29, so begun about 36, and dedicated to Maecenas (module 6.3 covers him and the patronage of poets), teach farming: crops and weather (book 1), trees and vines (2), cattle and horses (3), bees (4). Module 6.3 has the reading to Octavian at Atella in 29 (Life 27), the passages on Caesar at the end of books 1, 3 and 4 and Servius' story of Gallus and the fourth book: they are not repeated here. The question for this module is what kind of poem it is.

Didactic verse was an old Greek form, and Virgil names his master: Ascraeumque cano Romana per oppida carmen, I sing Hesiod's song through Roman towns (2.176). Hesiod's Works and Days gave the farmer's calendar, the brother addressed and the moral weight of work. Virgil's version of that weight is the theodicy of book 1: Jupiter ended the easy life of Saturn's age so that need would sharpen human wits, and labor omnia vicit improbus, relentless toil conquered everything (1.121–146). The weather signs of book 1 come from Aratus' Phaenomena, the Hellenistic astronomical poem that Cicero had translated; Quintilian adds that Virgil followed Nicander, whose Georgica is lost (10.1.56).

The Roman model was Lucretius (module 5.6), and Virgil answers him openly. Felix qui potuit rerum cognoscere causas, happy the man who could learn the causes of things and trample fear and fate underfoot (2.490–492), is Lucretius' philosopher; the next lines bless instead the man who knows the country gods (2.493–494). The cattle plague that ends book 3 (3.478–566) rewrites the Athenian plague that ends Lucretius' poem (6.1138–1286), itself drawn from Thucydides. The fourth book ends in an epyllion, a short narrative poem inside the larger one: Aristaeus loses his bees, and Orpheus loses Eurydice for the second time.

Ancient readers already saw that the farming is a frame. Seneca said Virgil looked for what was most fitting, not what was most true, and wished not to teach farmers but to delight readers (Letters 86.15). Columella, writing a real agricultural manual under Nero, put his book on gardens into verse to complete the theme Virgil had left to others (Columella 10 preface; Georgics 4.147–148).

Where sources disagree. L. P. Wilkinson (The Georgics of Virgil, 1969) read the poem as a hard-won affirmation of work and of Italy, whose praise fills 2.136–176. Richard Thomas, in his commentary (Virgil: Georgics, 1988), stressed how often labour fails in it: the plague destroys the herds, the bees die and Orpheus' art loses what it won. The same split runs through the criticism of the Aeneid, which deep dive module 12.7 takes up.

04Making the Aeneid

Donatus describes the method in detail. Composing the Georgics, Virgil dictated many lines each morning and spent the day cutting them down to very few, saying that he bore his poem as a she-bear bears her cubs and licked it into shape (Life 22). Gellius gives the same simile, from Virgil's friends, for his verse in general (17.10). For the Aeneid, begun in 29 after the Georgics and unfinished at his death in 19, he first wrote the whole story in prose, divided into twelve books, then turned it into verse piece by piece, taking up whatever part he pleased and keeping to no order (Life 23). So as not to break his momentum he left some passages unfinished and propped others with makeshift lines, which he joked were struts, tibicines, holding up the building until the real columns arrived (Life 24). The work took eleven years (Life 25; some editions and translations print twelve).

The method left traces. The Harpy Celaeno foretells that the Trojans will eat their tables (3.255–257); when it happens Aeneas credits the prophecy to Anchises (7.122–127). Palinurus falls asleep and overboard on a calm sea in book 5 (5.835–871) but tells Aeneas in the underworld that he was swept off in a storm (6.347–361). These are the seams of a poem written out of order and not yet revised; deep dive modules 12.2 and 12.3 meet them in their books.

Augustus followed the work from a distance. From his Spanish campaign of 26–25 he wrote asking, with threats in jest, for a first draft or any portion (Life 31). Virgil's reply survives in Macrobius: so great a thing had he begun that he almost thought himself out of his mind to have undertaken it (Saturnalia 1.24.11). Propertius had already heard enough to announce that something greater than the Iliad was being born (2.34.65–66; module 6.3). Much later, the material finally worked out, Virgil read Augustus three whole books, the second, fourth and sixth (Life 32). Octavia, present, fainted at the lines on her dead son, tu Marcellus eris (6.883); since Marcellus died in 23, the reading came after that. Virgil read to other friends too, never to a crowd, choosing passages he was unsure of to hear their judgement (Life 33). His freedman Eros told of one reading at which he completed two half-lines on the spot, adding quo non praestantior alter and Martemque accendere cantu to the passage on Misenus (6.164–165; Life 34).

05The order to burn it

In 19, in what Donatus calls his fifty-second year, Virgil set out for Greece and Asia meaning to spend three years putting the last touches to the poem and then give the rest of his life to philosophy (Life 35). At Athens he met Augustus returning from the East and agreed to go back with him. Visiting Megara in the heat, he caught a fever; the voyage made it worse, and he died at Brundisium a few days after landing.

He had arranged with Varius, before leaving Italy, that if anything happened to him Varius should burn the Aeneid; Varius refused. In his last illness Virgil kept asking for his book-boxes so that he could burn it himself, and no one brought them (Life 39). His will left his writings to Varius and Tucca on condition that they publish nothing he would not have published (Life 40). Augustus overrode the wish: Pliny says he forbade the burning against the modesty of the poet's will (Natural History 7.114), and Donatus quotes verses by one Sulpicius of Carthage on the poem twice saved from the flames (Life 38). Gellius' informants said only that the dying poet asked his friends to burn it because the unrevised parts were unworthy of him (17.10).

Varius published it at Augustus' wish, corrected only lightly, leaving even the incomplete lines as they were (Life 41). Servius' preface adds the rule Augustus set the editors, to remove what was superfluous and add nothing. The grammarian Nisus, says Donatus, reported that Varius changed the order of two books and cut four opening lines that began Ille ego qui quondam, joining the Aeneid to the pastoral and farming poems (Life 42).

The unfinished state shows in the half-lines. A Latin hexameter should run to six feet, but dozens of lines in the Aeneid stop short: hic cursus fuit (1.534); disce omnis (2.66). Donatus remarks that later writers tried to complete them and failed, since almost all are complete in sense, the exception being quem tibi iam Troia (3.340; Life 41). No half-line appears in the Eclogues or the Georgics, which Virgil finished. John Sparrow (Half-lines and Repetitions in Virgil, 1931) catalogued them with the repeated lines and treated both as marks of a draft.

Where sources disagree. The editors' rule bears on the Helen episode (2.567–588), in which Aeneas, during the sack, sees Helen hiding at Vesta's shrine and thinks of killing her. None of the ancient or principal medieval manuscripts has it; it survives through Servius, who says Varius and Tucca removed it. R. G. Austin ("Virgil, Aeneid 2.567–88", Classical Quarterly, 1961) argued that Servius told the truth and the lines are Virgil's. G. P. Goold ("Servius and the Helen Episode", Harvard Studies in Classical Philology, 1970) argued they are a later forgery, noting that nobody cites them before Servius; Nicholas Horsfall's commentary (Virgil, Aeneid 2, 2008) also rejects them, pressing the clash with Deiphobus' account of Helen in the underworld (6.494–547) that Servius already gives as a reason for the cut.

06The poem's models

Donatus states the design: a work matching both Homeric poems, telling of Greek and Latin characters, with Rome's origin and Augustus' as its theme (Life 21). Books 1–6 follow the Odyssey, a hero's wanderings told partly in his own voice at a foreign court; books 7–12 follow the Iliad, a war over a bride, a friend killed and a final duel (Greek modules 4.2 and 4.4). G. N. Knauer (Die Aeneis und Homer, 1964) mapped the correspondences scene by scene. The charge of theft was ancient: when critics listed his borrowings, Virgil is said to have answered that it was easier to wrest his club from Hercules than a line from Homer (Life 46). Macrobius gives two books to the borrowings (Saturnalia 5–6).

Apollonius. The Argonautica (Greek modules 3.4 and 7.2) gave Virgil the Hellenistic epic of love: a hero on a mission, a foreign princess undone by a god-sent passion. Servius, on 4.1, says the whole of book 4 was taken from Apollonius' third book, Medea's love for Jason; deep dive module 12.4 reads Dido.

Naevius and Ennius. The Roman epic tradition (module 4.5; Greek module 10.12) was already Trojan. Naevius' Punic War began with Aeneas leaving Troy, and Macrobius says Virgil took the storm of book 1 and Venus' complaint to Jupiter from it (Saturnalia 6.2). Ennius' Annales gave the Latin hexameter its diction. Anchises' tribute to Fabius, unus qui nobis cunctando restituis rem (6.846), adapts Ennius' line on the same man, unus homo nobis cunctando restituit rem (quoted by Cicero, On Old Age 10).

Lucretius. The Georgics argued with him; the Aeneid absorbs his diction and sets his philosophy inside the poem, as in Iopas' song of the moon, the sun and the causes of things at Dido's banquet (1.740–746). Anchises' account of the world-soul in book 6 answers Lucretian mortalism with Platonic and Stoic doctrine (deep dive module 12.5).

Greek tragedy. Dido, raving, is compared to Pentheus seeing two suns and to Orestes driven across the stage by his mother's Furies (4.469–473): the theatre is named in the simile. Aeneas' last words to his son before the final battle, learn courage and true labour from me and fortune from others (12.435–436), translate Ajax's to his son (Sophocles, Ajax 550–551). Catullus is a model too: Aeneas tells Dido's shade that he left her shore unwillingly (6.460), in the words of the lock of hair in Catullus 66.39.

The Aeneid became in turn the model its successors had to answer. Ovid compressed it into a "little Aeneid" in the Metamorphoses (module 10.1); Lucan inverted it and Statius, at the end of the Thebaid, told his own poem not to rival the divine Aeneid but to follow at a distance and worship its footprints (12.816–817; module 10.2).

07The metre

The Aeneid is in dactylic hexameter, the metre of Homer, brought into Latin by Ennius. Latin verse is quantitative: syllables are long or short, and a line is a pattern of them, not of stresses. Each line has six feet. The first four may each be a dactyl (long, short, short) or a spondee (long, long); the fifth is almost always a dactyl, the sixth two syllables. A word-break, the caesura, usually falls in the third foot. The first line scans:

Arma vi | rumque ca | no, Tro | iae qui | primus ab | oris (1.1)

Two dactyls, two spondees, a dactyl and a closing foot, with the caesura after cano. Within the frame Virgil varies speed for sense. The galloping horses of 8.596, quadrupedante putrem sonitu quatit ungula campum, run on dactyls; the blinded Cyclops is a pile of spondees and elisions, monstrum horrendum, informe, ingens, cui lumen ademptum (3.658). Virgil also managed the relation between the metrical beat and the natural stress of Latin words, which tend to clash in the first half of the line and coincide at its end, so that each line settles as it closes. W. F. Jackson Knight studied the pattern (Accentual Symmetry in Vergil, 1939). Lines run over freely into the next, so that sentences and lines pull against each other. Nearly all of this is lost in English.

08Reading it in translation

Every translation chooses what to lose. The main choices are three: whether to match Virgil's line count or expand, whether to use English metre or free verse, and whether to aim at a poem in English or at a close crib. The opening shows the difference in miniature. The Latin's first word is arma, the object of the verb, and the man comes second. Dryden: "Arms, and the man I sing, who, forc'd by fate". Fitzgerald: "I sing of warfare and a man at war." Fagles: "Wars and a man I sing".

Translations that keep the line count (Ruden, Bartsch, close to it Ahl) can be read with a Latin text and a commentary, and line numbers cited by scholars find their place. The expansive ones (Dryden, Fitzgerald, Fagles) read better aloud and worse as evidence: they settle ambiguities the poem leaves open. That matters for the passages on which interpretation turns. In sunt lacrimae rerum et mentem mortalia tangunt (1.462), are these the tears shed for things, or the tears in things? At the end of the poem Aeneas buries his sword in Turnus fervidus, burning with rage (12.951), and the debate that deep dive module 12.7 runs depends on that one word. A translation that turns ambiguity into explanation has taken a side. The practical rule is to read one expansive and one line-for-line version, and to check the disputed lines against both.

09Self-test

  1. What is the Suetonius–Donatus life, and why should its anecdotes be treated with caution?
    Answer

    It is Suetonius' life of Virgil from his On Poets, preserved by Aelius Donatus in the fourth century at the head of his commentary. Much of its colour is inferred from the poems, as when the Alexis of Eclogue 2 becomes a real boy given by Pollio (Life 9), though its dates and its account of the manuscript have a firmer basis.

  2. What independent evidence places Virgil in the Epicurean circle near Naples?
    Answer

    Papyri of Philodemus from Herculaneum name Vergil, Varius, Quintilius Varus and Plotius Tucca among his addressees (Armstrong and others, 2004). The Catalepton poems on Siro's school (5 and 8) say the same but are of disputed authenticity.

  3. How is the book of Eclogues arranged?
    Answer

    Odd-numbered poems are dialogues, even-numbered mostly single voices, and modern readers map pairs around a centre: 1 and 9, 2 and 8, 3 and 7, 4 and 6, with Daphnis' deification in 5 at the middle and Gallus in 10 as the close. Clausen argued the book was planned as a whole.

  4. Name three candidates for the child of the fourth Eclogue and who proposed each.
    Answer

    Pollio's son Asinius Gallus, who claimed it himself (Asconius, in Servius on 4.11); Alexander Helios, Antony's son by Cleopatra (Tarn, 1932); the expected child of Antony and Octavia after Brundisium (argued most fully by Clausen, 1994). Norden (1924) took the child as a symbol of the returning age.

  5. What did Virgil take from Hesiod, Aratus and Lucretius in the Georgics?
    Answer

    From Hesiod the form of farming instruction and the moral weight of work, which he names at 2.176; from Aratus the weather signs of book 1; from Lucretius the didactic voice, the praise of the man who knows the causes of things (2.490–492) and the plague at the end of book 3.

  6. What does Seneca say about the purpose of the Georgics?
    Answer

    That Virgil sought what was most fitting rather than most true, and wished not to teach farmers but to delight readers (Letters 86.15).

  7. Which image does Donatus apply to the Georgics and Gellius to Virgil's verse in general, and how did Virgil draft the Aeneid?
    Answer

    A she-bear licking her cubs into shape (Donatus, Life 22; Gellius 17.10). For the Aeneid he wrote a prose draft in twelve books and versified it piece by piece in no fixed order, leaving struts where lines were not yet finished (Life 23–24).

  8. Give two inconsistencies in the Aeneid that reflect its unrevised state.
    Answer

    Celaeno foretells the eating of the tables (3.255–257), but Aeneas credits the prophecy to Anchises (7.122–127). Palinurus falls overboard on a calm sea in book 5 (5.835–871) yet says in book 6 that a storm swept him off (6.347–361).

  9. Who prevented the burning of the Aeneid, and how was it published?
    Answer

    Varius refused to burn it, nobody brought Virgil his book-boxes, and Augustus forbade the burning (Donatus, Life 39; Pliny, Natural History 7.114). Varius and Tucca published it at Augustus' wish with light correction, leaving the half-lines as they were (Life 41).

  10. Why does it matter whether a translation keeps Virgil's line count?
    Answer

    Line-for-line versions (Ruden, Bartsch) and Ahl's close hexameters can be read beside the Latin and keep its compression; expansive ones (Dryden, Fitzgerald, Fagles) read well but resolve ambiguities such as lacrimae rerum (1.462) or fervidus (12.951), so they take sides the poem leaves open.

10Carry forward

  1. Ancient biography built lives out of works: a fact about Virgil is only as good as its source, and much of the life is the Eclogues read as autobiography. Ask where each anecdote comes from before using it to interpret a poem.
  2. The Aeneid is an unfinished draft published against its author's dying wish: the half-lines, the seams between books and the edited opening are evidence of how it was made, and no reading should rest its weight on a line that may never have been revised.
  3. Virgil's three works are each a Latin answer to Greek and Roman models (Theocritus, Hesiod, Aratus and Lucretius, Homer, Apollonius and Ennius). The Aeneid then became the model every later Latin epic had to answer.
