Roman history and mythology

Four parts and three deep dives, 79 modules, plus a quality audit. History and myth are interleaved more tightly than in the Greek course, because Rome told its myths as history: the legends sit inside the periods that produced them. Every module is a long-form report with named primary sources, the places where the accounts disagree, a ten-question self-test and three ideas to carry forward.

How to read it

five rules
  1. Parts I to IV in order. Each report assumes the ones before it. Part I matters more here than in Greece: the evidence for Rome's first four centuries is late and partisan, and every later report assumes you know how late. Part IV ends with a capstone exam.
  2. Deep dives when you want them. Unit 11 (the fall of the Republic) extends unit 5; unit 12 (the Aeneid) extends module 6.3 and, for Aeneas, module 1.3; unit 13 (Roman philosophy) extends modules 4.5, 5.6, 7.3 and 10.2. Take each straight after the material it extends, or after the capstone.
  3. Greece behind you. Rome points back to the Greek course instead of repeating it: Greek 7.3 and 7.4 for the Greek side of the conquest, 2.2 for the Olympians, 9.3 and 10.12 for reception. Open the Greek module when a report names it.
  4. One or two a sitting, self-test before moving on. Under 7 of 10, re-read. The carry-forward ideas are the ones later reports lean on.
  5. A study text, not scripture. Citations are given so you can check them against the primary sources. Where sources disagree, the report says so in a red-edged box.

The reading spine

primary and secondary

The reports keep naming these. Read the primary ones at least once each, in this order, any decent translation: Livy, books 1 to 5 and then 21 to 30 · Polybius, books 1 to 6 · Virgil, Aeneid (book 8 with module 1.3) · Ovid, Fasti, then Metamorphoses · Sallust, The War with Catiline · Caesar, The Gallic War · Cicero, selected letters (Shackleton Bailey's translation) and the Second Philippic · Plutarch's Roman Lives as each name comes up · Augustus, Res Gestae · Tacitus, Annals · Suetonius, The Twelve Caesars · Seneca, Letters to Lucilius (a selection), Epictetus, Handbook, and Marcus Aurelius, Meditations (with deep dive 13) · Ammianus Marcellinus, books 14 to 31.

Secondary, three only: T. J. Cornell, The Beginnings of Rome; Mary Beard, John North and Simon Price, Religions of Rome; Mary Beard, SPQR, with Peter Heather, The Fall of the Roman Empire.
