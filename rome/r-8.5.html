01Two courts and a general, 395–408

Theodosius I died at Milan on 17 January 395, where module 8.4 ends. Arcadius, about seventeen, held Constantinople; Honorius, ten, took the West. Nobody announced a partition, and laws still went out in both names. But the empire was never again governed from one court, and each court had a chief minister with his own army and his own claim to the Balkan prefecture of Illyricum.

The West was run by Stilicho, magister militum, of Vandal descent (Orosius 7.38.1) and husband of Theodosius' niece Serena. He claimed that the dying emperor had entrusted both sons to him, as his poet Claudian repeated in poem after poem. The eastern prefect Rufinus was cut down by returning troops in November 395 (Zosimus 5.7); in 397 the eunuch Eutropius had Stilicho declared a public enemy at Constantinople (5.11), while Gildo, count of Africa, transferred his province and its grain to the East.

Between the courts stood Alaric, leader of the Goths settled in 382. He rebelled in 395 and ravaged Greece, burning Eleusis (Greek module 7.4); Stilicho cornered him twice and twice let him go. In 401 Alaric entered Italy; after Pollentia (402) and Verona he withdrew, and Honorius moved his court behind the marshes of Ravenna. In 405–406 Radagaisus brought another Gothic host into Italy, 400,000 by Zosimus' count (5.26.3), more than 200,000 by Orosius' (7.37); Stilicho destroyed it near Faesulae.

In 408 Stilicho made the Senate pay Alaric 4,000 pounds of gold, and the senator Lampadius called it "not peace but a pact of servitude" (Zosimus 5.29). Arcadius died in May. The official Olympius persuaded Honorius that Stilicho meant to put his son Eucherius on the eastern throne, and Stilicho was beheaded at Ravenna on 22 August (5.34). Roman soldiers then killed the families of barbarian troops in the Italian cities, and some 30,000 men went over to Alaric (5.35.5–6).

Where sources disagree. Orosius, writing in 417, makes Stilicho a traitor who nursed Alaric and set the peoples beyond the Rhine in motion to win the throne for Eucherius (7.38). Zosimus, following the contemporary Olympiodorus, calls him more moderate than almost anyone in power in his day (5.34). Alan Cameron (Claudian, 1970) showed that Claudian's poems, the fullest contemporary account, track Stilicho's policy of the moment.

02Alaric and the sack of Rome, 408–410

Alaric besieged Rome in autumn 408. The prefect Pompeianus let Tuscan diviners offer to save it (Zosimus 5.41.1–3; module 1.2), and the Senate bought the Goths off with 5,000 pounds of gold, 30,000 of silver, 4,000 silk robes, 3,000 scarlet fleeces and 3,000 pounds of pepper (5.41.4). Alaric wanted not loot but a place: land, grain and a Roman command. Honorius refused every version, even Noricum and grain alone (5.48–50). In 409 Alaric had the Senate proclaim Priscus Attalus emperor, then deposed him (6.6–12).

On 24 August 410 the Goths entered by the Salarian Gate and plundered the city for three days, the first capture of Rome by a foreign enemy since the Gauls eight centuries before. Orosius tells it as a story of restraint: Alaric, an Arian Christian, had the basilicas of Peter and Paul respected as sanctuaries, and church vessels were carried to St Peter's in procession, Goths and Romans singing hymns (7.39). Jerome, in Bethlehem, wrote that "the city which had taken the whole world was itself taken" (Letter 127.12).

Alaric took Honorius' half-sister Galla Placidia south, failed to cross to Africa and died at Consentia; Jordanes says he was buried in the diverted bed of the Busentus (Getica 158). His brother-in-law Athaulf led the Goths into Gaul and married Placidia at Narbonne in 414. Orosius heard a man of Narbonne tell Jerome that Athaulf had once meant to turn Romania into Gothia but resolved instead to restore the Roman name with Gothic strength (7.43.4–6). His successor Wallia returned Placidia, fought the Vandals and Alans in Spain as Rome's ally, and in 418 his people were settled in Aquitaine, the core of the later kingdom of Toulouse.

03Augustine's reply: the City of God

Refugees brought to Africa the charge that Rome had fallen for abandoning its gods. Augustine answered in the City of God, twenty-two books written between 413 and about 426 because, he says, pagans were blaspheming more bitterly than usual after the sack (Retractations 2.43). Books 1–5 show that Rome had suffered worse under the gods, from the Gauls to Cannae (book 3), and that in 410 Christ's name saved those who fled to the basilicas (1.1–7). Books 6–10 take on Varro's theology (module 2.1) and the Platonists. Books 11–22 trace two cities made by two loves, of self to the contempt of God and of God to the contempt of self (14.28). Rome is not the heavenly city, and "without justice, what are kingdoms but great robberies?" (4.4). Module 1.6 reads his chapter on Lucretia (1.19).

Augustine set the young Spanish priest Orosius to write the Seven Books of History against the Pagans (417–418), a catalogue of pre-Christian disasters beside which 410 looks mild. Pagans answered in kind. Rutilius Namatianus, sailing home to Gaul in 417, praised Rome for making one fatherland of different peoples (On His Return 1.63) and cursed Stilicho for burning the Sibylline Books (2.41–60). Zosimus set out to tell how Rome lost its empire as Polybius had told how it won it (1.57), and traced the loss to the end of public sacrifice (4.59).

Where sources disagree. Orosius makes the Christian empire providential and Roman history improve after Christ. Augustine ties God's purpose to no earthly state and treats Rome's glory as the earthly reward of its love of praise (5.12–15). R. A. Markus (Saeculum, 1970) argued that Augustine had shared the "Christian times" triumphalism of the 390s and then abandoned it, so that the City of God breaks with the optimism his protégé went on to publish.

04The Rhine crossing and the lost provinces

On the last day of 406, by Prosper's chronicle, Vandals, Alans and Sueves crossed the Rhine; Jerome lists the Gallic cities they took, from Mainz and Worms to Reims and Tournai (Letter 123). The army of Britain raised a usurper, Constantine III, who crossed to Gaul in 407. When his general Gerontius rebelled in Spain, the invaders crossed the Pyrenees (409) and in 411 shared the Spanish provinces by lot: Gallaecia to the Hasding Vandals and the Sueves, Lusitania and Carthaginiensis to the Alans, Baetica to the Siling Vandals (Hydatius, Chronicle, s.a. 411). Honorius' general Constantius ended Constantine III in 411, and the Goths, as Rome's allies, destroyed the Silings and broke the Alans in 416–418. The Burgundians held the middle Rhine by 413 (Prosper); in 436 Huns, probably in Aetius' pay, destroyed them, killing 20,000 by Hydatius' figure, and the survivors were resettled in Sapaudia, around Lake Geneva, in 443 (Gallic Chronicle of 452). Each settlement was a treaty with an army already standing on Roman soil.

Where sources disagree. Prosper dates the crossing to 31 December 406. Michael Kulikowski ("Barbarians in Gaul, usurpers in Britain", Britannia, 2000) argued for 31 December 405, which makes the British usurpations a response to the invasion and spares Stilicho, then fighting Radagaisus, the charge of ignoring Gaul. Most accounts keep 406; the evidence allows either.

Britain went first. Zosimus says that in 409 the Britons took up arms, freed their cities and expelled the Roman officials (6.5.2–3), and that in 410 Honorius told the cities of Britain to look to their own defence (6.10.2). New coin had stopped arriving soon after 402; within a generation towns and villas were abandoned. Gildas, in the sixth century, preserves an appeal to "Agitius, thrice consul", Aetius in or after 446, headed "the groans of the Britons" (On the Ruin of Britain 20.1).

Where sources disagree. Zosimus has the Britons expel Rome; Gildas has Rome abandon them, and the Saxons they hire betray them (On the Ruin of Britain 18–23). The Gallic Chronicle of 452 puts Britain under Saxon control in 441, Bede the Saxons' arrival in 449 (Ecclesiastical History 1.15), and some refer Honorius' letter to Bruttium in Italy. The end cannot be dated more closely than 409 to about 440.

05Aetius and Attila, 425–454

For a generation the West rested on Aetius, once a hostage with Alaric and then with the Huns (Gregory of Tours, History 2.8, quoting the lost Renatus Frigeridus). He brought a Hunnic army to the usurper John in 425 and was bought off with a command in Gaul by Placidia, regent for her six-year-old son Valentinian III. His rival Boniface beat him near Rimini in 432 but died of his wounds; Aetius returned with Hunnic backing in 433 and was master of the government thereafter, consul in 432, 437 and 446. With Hunnic auxiliaries he beat Goths, Burgundians and the Armorican rebels called Bagaudae.

Attila, sole king of the Huns from about 445, devastated the Balkans and, by Priscus' account, made the East pay 6,000 pounds of gold in arrears and 2,100 a year. In 450 Valentinian's sister Honoria sent Attila her ring, and he claimed her with a share of the empire (Priscus; Jordanes, Getica). In 451 he invaded Gaul; Aetius, with the Visigoths of Theoderic I, Alans, Franks and Burgundians, checked him on the Catalaunian Plains near Troyes, where Theoderic fell. Jordanes, writing a century later from Cassiodorus' lost Gothic history, puts the dead at 165,000 (Getica 217); Hydatius has almost 300,000. In 452 Attila sacked Aquileia, received Pope Leo I and two senators at the Mincio, and withdrew. He died in 453 on his wedding night (Getica 254, after Priscus), and his empire broke apart. The West had lost the army it hired against its other enemies.

Where sources disagree. Prosper of Aquitaine, of Leo's circle at Rome, credits the withdrawal of 452 to the pope's embassy (Chronicle, s.a. 452). Hydatius, writing in Gallaecia, has the Huns struck by famine and disease and attacked by troops of the eastern emperor Marcian (s.a. 452).

On 21 September 454 Valentinian killed Aetius with his own hand (Prosper, s.a. 454). In March 455 Aetius' guardsmen killed Valentinian, and the house of Theodosius ended in the West.

06Gaiseric: Africa and the sack of 455

In 429 Gaiseric led the Vandals and Alans from Spain to Africa; Victor of Vita, writing in the 480s, says they were counted at 80,000, old men and infants included (History of the Vandal Persecution 1.2). Augustine died in 430 in the third month of their siege of Hippo (Possidius, Life of Augustine 28–31). On 19 October 439 Gaiseric took Carthage in time of peace (Prosper, s.a. 439). It was the decisive loss: Africa fed Rome and paid much of the western budget, the senators' African estates passed to Vandal owners, and a law of 444 confesses that the treasury cannot supply the army.

The sack of 455 followed a broken betrothal. Gaiseric's son Huneric was promised to Valentinian's daughter Eudocia; Petronius Maximus, emperor after the murder, married her mother Eudoxia and gave Eudocia to his own son. Gaiseric sailed, Maximus was killed as he fled, and the Vandals entered Rome unopposed. Prosper says Leo persuaded Gaiseric to refrain from fire, slaughter and torture, and that the city was stripped for fourteen days (s.a. 455). Procopius has the palace treasures and half the gilded bronze roof of the Capitoline temple loaded aboard (Wars 3.5.3–5), and the Jerusalem Temple spoils that Titus had brought to Rome went too; Belisarius recovered them at Carthage in 534 (4.9.5). Eudoxia and her daughters were carried off, and Eudocia married Huneric.

Where sources disagree. Prosper's orderly fortnight was written in the pope's circle and credits the pope. Victor of Vita describes crowds of captives shipped to Carthage, husbands parted from wives, and the bishop Deogratias selling church plate to ransom them (book 1).

07Ricimer and the last emperors, 455–476

For two decades power lay with the general of the army of Italy. Ricimer, magister militum from 456, was the son of a Suevic father and a daughter of the Visigothic king Wallia (Sidonius, Poems 2). An Arian of barbarian royal blood could not wear the purple, so he made and unmade those who did.

Majorian, the last western emperor to govern as well as reign, legislated against extortionate tax collectors and the quarrying of Rome's public buildings; when his African fleet was destroyed on the Spanish coast in 460 (Hydatius), Ricimer had him put to death the next year. In 468 East and West tried once more: Basiliscus' armada, which Procopius says cost 1,300 centenaria of gold, 130,000 pounds (Wars 3.6.2), was burned by Vandal fireships off Cape Bon. The West never again had the revenues it needed.

The end was played out by men who had served Attila. Orestes, a Roman from Pannonia, had been Attila's secretary and in 449 went with one Edeco on a Hunnic embassy to Constantinople (Priscus). In 475 he drove out Nepos and made his young son Romulus emperor, called Augustulus, "little Augustus". The troops in Italy demanded a third of its land; Orestes refused, and they made Odoacer, probably Edeco's son, their ruler (Procopius, Wars 5.1.4–8). Odoacer killed Orestes at Placentia in August 476 and deposed Romulus, sparing him out of pity for his youth and, because he was handsome, sending him with an income of 6,000 solidi to live in Campania (Anonymus Valesianus 8.38). He sent the palace regalia to Constantinople, which returned them to Theoderic in 497 (12.64). The embassy to Zeno, and how 476 became a turning point only in retrospect, are in module 0.3; module 8.6 begins there.

08Gibbon and Pirenne: why and when

Edward Gibbon finished the western story in 1781, in his third volume, and closed chapter 38 with "General Observations on the Fall of the Roman Empire in the West". His answer is structural: the decline was "the natural and inevitable effect of immoderate greatness", and "instead of inquiring why the Roman empire was destroyed, we should rather be surprised that it had subsisted so long". He names the corrupted legions, Constantine's institutions and the division of East and West, and then religion: the clergy preached "patience and pusillanimity", and the soldiers' pay went to monks and nuns. Yet Constantine's "victorious religion broke the violence of the fall", softening the conquerors. He ends with reassurance: modern Europe was safe, since "before they can conquer, they must cease to be barbarous", and every age had added to the wealth, happiness and knowledge of mankind.

The phrase usually quoted for his thesis comes from the end of the work, in 1788, looking back from the ruins of the city: "I have described the triumph of barbarism and religion" (chapter 71). It summarises six volumes; the argument of 1781 made Christianity a cushion as much as a cause. The afterlife of Gibbon's verdict is module 10.4.

Henri Pirenne moved the ending. In Mohammed and Charlemagne, published in 1937 after his death (English 1939), he argued that the Germanic kings changed rulers, not civilisation: Merovingian Gaul still used gold coin, papyrus and eastern spices, and the Mediterranean remained one economy until the Arab conquests closed the sea and turned the Franks north. Without Muhammad, he concluded, Charlemagne would have been inconceivable. Archaeology has reversed his timing while keeping his question. Richard Hodges and David Whitehouse (Mohammed, Charlemagne and the Origins of Europe, 1983) showed from pottery and coin that long-distance exchange in the western Mediterranean had already shrunk in the sixth century, before the Arabs; Michael McCormick (Origins of the European Economy, 2001) traced its recovery from the late eighth.

09Transformation, and the return of fall

Peter Brown's The World of Late Antiquity (1971) turned the question from why Rome fell to what the centuries from about 150 to 750 created. Seen from Antioch or Alexandria, where nothing fell in 476, the period was inventive: the holy man, the bishop, a new art and a new inner life. The European Science Foundation's programme "The Transformation of the Roman World" (1993–1998) gave the word institutional weight. Walter Goffart argued that barbarian armies were settled on shares of tax revenue rather than confiscated land; Herwig Wolfram's Vienna school described the Goths as armies of mixed origin that became a people inside the Roman world. Module 0.3 names the camps; here is the evidence.

In 2005 two books restored the fall. Peter Heather (The Fall of the Roman Empire) argued that a strong empire was brought down from outside: the Huns drove two waves of Goths, Vandals, Alans and others across the frontier, in 376–380 and 405–408, and the loss of Africa in 439 removed the revenues that paid the army. Bryan Ward-Perkins (The Fall of Rome and the End of Civilization) turned to the material record. Fine tableware and amphorae from specialised workshops vanish from western sites; tiled roofs give way to thatch and stone to timber; coin drops out of everyday use; graffiti and stamped tiles, signs of ordinary literacy, disappear; cattle bones from Italian sites show smaller animals. Britain, on his account, fell below the economic complexity of the pre-Roman Iron Age.

Where sources disagree. Heather makes the Huns the external shock that started the chain. Guy Halsall (Barbarian Migrations and the Roman West, 376–568, 2007) reverses the causation: the western court's civil wars drew barbarian armies in as allies and recruits, and migration followed political collapse. Salvian of Marseille, in the 440s, already blamed the Romans, claiming that the poor fled the tax collectors to the barbarians or the Bagaudae (On the Governance of God 5).

The material evidence is not all one way. Nikola Koepke and Joerg Baten (European Review of Economic History, 2005), compiling skeletal heights, found that stature stagnated under the empire and rose in the fifth and sixth centuries. The Greenland lead curve that module 7.3 used for the Antonine plague had already fallen in the late second century, so it cannot date a fifth-century collapse by itself. And the collapse was regional: sudden in Britain, gradual in Gaul and Spain, heaviest in Italy during the wars of the sixth century, and absent in the East, where villages in the limestone hills of northern Syria went on building in stone.

Where sources disagree. Ward-Perkins reads the disappearance of goods as a fall in living standards for almost everyone. Chris Wickham (Framing the Early Middle Ages, 2005) traces the simplification chiefly to the end of the Roman tax system and the impoverishment of aristocracies; where landlords weakened, peasants may have kept more of what they grew, which would fit the heights. Both accept that specialised production and exchange collapsed in the post-Roman West; they disagree about who paid for it.

Weighed together, the camps answer different questions. The western state fell: by 476 western Roman government reached little beyond Italy, and the process, not the date, is what counts. The economy it sustained fell too, unevenly, mostly after 450 and for long after 476. Roman culture did not end: law, Latin, the Church, the senatorial families and the habit of calling oneself Roman passed to the successor kingdoms and the East (module 8.6). "Transformation" describes religion and culture; "fall" describes the state and the material life it paid for. The late army is module 9.2, and late antique art, the strongest case for transformation, module 10.3.

10Self-test

  1. Why did the division of 395 matter more than its makers intended?
    Answer

    It created two courts with rival chief ministers, each with an army and a claim to Illyricum. Stilicho's feud with Constantinople, which declared him a public enemy in 397 (Zosimus 5.11), let Alaric play one court against the other.

  2. What did Alaric want, and why did he end by sacking Rome?
    Answer

    Land for his people, grain and a Roman command, not plunder. Honorius at Ravenna refused every version, even Noricum and grain alone (Zosimus 5.48–50), and after his puppet emperor Attalus failed, Alaric took the city in August 410.

  3. How does Augustine answer the charge that Rome fell because it abandoned its gods?
    Answer

    Rome had suffered worse under the gods (City of God book 3), and in 410 Christ's name saved those in the basilicas (1.1–7). More deeply, Rome is not the heavenly city: the two cities are made by two loves (14.28), and kingdoms without justice are great robberies (4.4).

  4. How do Orosius and Augustine differ in reading Roman history?
    Answer

    Orosius makes the Christian empire providential and history improve after Christ. Augustine ties God's purpose to no earthly state; Markus (1970) read this as his break with the "Christian times" optimism of the 390s.

  5. Which peoples took which western provinces between 406 and 443?
    Answer

    Vandals, Alans and Sueves crossed the Rhine and shared Spain by lot in 411 (Hydatius); the Goths, as Roman allies, broke the Silings and Alans and were settled in Aquitaine in 418; the Burgundians held the middle Rhine from about 413 and, after their destruction in 436, Sapaudia from 443.

  6. Why can the end of Roman Britain not be dated more closely than 409 to about 440?
    Answer

    Zosimus has the Britons expel Roman officials in 409 and Honorius write to the cities in 410 (6.5, 6.10), a letter some refer to Bruttium; Gildas has Rome abandon Britain and preserves an appeal to Aetius in or after 446; the Gallic Chronicle of 452 gives Saxon control in 441 and Bede the Saxon arrival in 449.

  7. How did Aetius depend on the Huns, and why did Attila's death hurt the West?
    Answer

    A former hostage among them, he used Hunnic armies to reach power and to beat Goths, Burgundians and Bagaudae. When Attila died in 453 and his empire broke up, the West lost the army it hired against its other enemies, and Aetius was murdered the next year.

  8. Why was the Vandal conquest of Africa decisive?
    Answer

    Africa supplied Rome's grain and much of the western revenue, and the senators' estates there were lost; a law of 444 admits the treasury could not supply the army. The attempt to recover it in 468, which Procopius says cost 130,000 pounds of gold (Wars 3.6.2), ended in flames off Cape Bon.

  9. What does Gibbon argue in 1781, and how does Pirenne change the question?
    Answer

    Gibbon calls the fall "the natural and inevitable effect of immoderate greatness" and makes Christianity both a cause and a softener of it; "the triumph of barbarism and religion" is his chapter 71 summary of 1788. Pirenne moved the end of the ancient economy to the Arab conquests, a timing archaeology has since overturned (Hodges and Whitehouse, 1983).

  10. What does the archaeology show about living standards, and how is it disputed?
    Answer

    Ward-Perkins documents the disappearance of traded pottery, tiles, coin, everyday literacy and large cattle, worst in Britain. Koepke and Baten found heights rising in the fifth and sixth centuries, and Wickham ascribes the simplification to the end of the tax system and of aristocratic wealth, which may have left peasants better off.

11Carry forward

  1. The West fell through its revenues: each lost province, above all Africa in 439, shrank the taxes that paid the army, until the government depended on barbarian armies and on generals who made and unmade emperors. Follow the money and the army, not the battles.
  2. The "invasions" were mostly settlements negotiated with armies already inside the empire, often drawn in by Roman civil wars; Alaric, Athaulf and Odoacer wanted a place in the Roman system, not its destruction, and the successor kingdoms inherited Roman land, law and taxpayers.
  3. "Fall" and "transformation" answer different questions: the western state and its economy did fall, unevenly and over generations, while religion, law and Roman identity carried on, and the eastern empire did not fall at all. Ask of any claim which region, which class and which evidence.
