01Volunteers, levies and the poor

Module 5.2 sets out what Marius did in 107 (Sallust, Jugurtha 86) and why historians doubt it was a reform; module 9.2 follows the levy into the Principate. The question here is political. A soldier who owned nothing had nothing to go back to, so he depended on whoever could give him land or money at discharge, and a general who could give it held him. Appian put it most sharply, explaining the indiscipline of the armies of 41: their generals were not regularly chosen, their men were not raised by the ancestral levy, and they served the individuals who had gathered them rather than the public; desertion, once unpardonable, was now rewarded, and the generals knew that their authority rested on donatives, not on law (Civil Wars 5.17).

That chain, from proletarian recruits to personal armies, is Emilio Gabba's (articles of 1949 and 1951, in English as Republican Rome: The Army and the Allies, 1976). Its first link is weaker than it looks. Magistrates went on levying citizens throughout the century: Caesar enrolled two new legions in Cisalpine Gaul in 57 (Gallic War 2.2), Pompey swore in men there as consul in 55 (6.1), and in January 49 the Senate decreed a levy throughout Italy (Civil War 1.6). The recruits were mostly countrymen, not the urban plebs of deep dive module 11.3.

Where sources disagree. Sallust's volunteers of 107 and Appian's armies "not drawn from the enrolment" of 41 underlie Gabba's proletarian army of volunteers. P. A. Brunt (Italian Manpower, 1971) answered that the levy remained the normal way of raising legions throughout the first century, now falling mostly on the poor. Arthur Keaveney (The Army in the Roman Revolution, 2007) warns against overestimating the soldiers below the property qualification, whose lowering he treats as a long process, not a Marian break.

02Long commands, rich provinces

An army raised for a season belonged to the Republic; an army kept together for years by one man became his. Sulla spent 87–83 in the East, outlawed by the government at Rome (module 5.2), and bought his men while he waited. Wintering in Asia after Dardanus, he billeted them on the cities, each host paying his soldier sixteen drachmas a day and feeding him and his friends, a military tribune fifty drachmas and two suits of clothes (Plutarch, Sulla 25.2). Sallust says that Sulla, to bind the army of Asia to himself, indulged it against ancestral custom, and that there it first learned to make love and drink and to covet statues, paintings and plate (Catiline 11.5–6).

The young Pompey's army in Africa formed the same bond within months. Ordered by Sulla to send it home, his soldiers refused to forsake their general and told him not to trust the tyrant (Plutarch, Pompey 13). Lucullus, harsh with his men, lost his army to mutiny (Plutarch, Lucullus 33.2, 34; module 5.3). From 67 the great commands ran for years: three for Pompey against the pirates, five for Caesar in Gaul and five more after Luca, five for Pompey's Spains, which he held through legates while staying outside Rome (modules 5.3 and 5.4). A province with legions was a base where a proconsul recruited, paid and rewarded beyond the reach of the courts. Caesar raised legions at his own expense beyond those the state gave him, one of them, the Alauda, from Transalpine Gauls, whom he later made citizens (Suetonius, Julius 24.2), and he doubled the legionary's pay for good (26.3).

Legions were also counters between dynasts. In 50 the Senate ordered each to give up a legion for a Parthian war; Pompey reclaimed the one he had lent, so Caesar lost two, and both were kept in Italy (Gallic War 8.54–55, by Hirtius). Appius, who brought them back, told Pompey that Caesar's army would come over to him (Plutarch, Pompey 57); nine years together had made it Caesar's.

03Veterans and the land

The Republic paid its soldiers by the campaign and owed them nothing at discharge. Land came, when it came, from a measure forced through each time by a tribune, a consul or a dictator, usually against senatorial resistance: Saturninus' allotments for Marius' men and Sulla's colonies on confiscated land (module 5.2); Pompey's veterans, dismissed at Brundisium in 62 and kept waiting until Caesar's law of 59 (modules 5.3 and 5.4); Caesar's colonies and the triumvirs' eighteen towns (Appian 4.3; modules 5.4 and 5.5). Appian states the consequence for 41: the leaders needed the soldiers to keep their power, and the soldiers needed the leaders' rule to keep their land (Civil Wars 5.13). Brunt ("The Army and the Land in the Roman Revolution", Journal of Roman Studies 1962) made this the thread of the whole century: the veterans, like the Gracchan settlers of module 5.1, were countrymen who wanted land of their own, and men of that class, "with arms in their hands", brought the Republic down.

Settlement bred its own trouble. At Pompeii Sulla's colonists were still quarrelling with the old citizens over walks and votes when Cicero defended the colony's founder, Publius Sulla, in 62 (Pro Sulla 60–62). In Etruria some had spent the last of their plunder and joined Manlius in 63 (Sallust, Catiline 28.4); Cicero said they would need Sulla raised from the dead to clear their debts (Against Catiline 2.20). Caesar gave his veterans land in scattered plots so that no owner was dispossessed (Suetonius, Julius 38.1); the triumvirs dispossessed whole towns. Augustus' boast that he was the first and only founder of soldiers' colonies within living memory to pay for the land (Res Gestae 16.1) names what the Republic had seldom done: Caesar's law of 59 bought from willing sellers at the census value (Dio 38.1.4); his cash discharge and military treasury are module 6.4.

Where sources disagree. Cicero (Against Catiline 2.20) and Sallust (Catiline 28.4) show Sullan veterans ruined within twenty years, and Gabba read the soldiers' demand for land as a demand for capital they could turn into money. Cicero's picture is a consul's invective, and Brunt (1962) rejected Gabba's reading: the veterans were countrymen seeking a secure living from land they owned. Keaveney (2007) doubts again that most veterans wanted farms, noting how many re-enlisted.

04The oath, the donative and the general's name

The sacramentum was sworn to the commander in person, not to his office, and had to be sworn again when commanders changed (module 9.2 gives its Republican form). Because it bound men to a man, it went where he sent it: Pompey lent Caesar the men he had sworn in Cisalpine Gaul as consul and ordered them to report (Gallic War 6.1). In civil war it changed hands with the army. At Corfinium Caesar had Domitius' captured soldiers swear to him (Civil War 1.23); at Ilerda, as his men fraternised with Caesar's, Petreius made them swear afresh not to desert or betray the army and its commanders (1.76), an oath renewed because the first no longer held. Sulla's veterans in 83 swore of their own accord to stand by him and to harm Italy only on his orders, and offered him their savings, which he refused (Plutarch, Sulla 27.3).

Money spoke more plainly. Caesar paid his men 2,000 sesterces each at the start of the civil war and, at the triumphs of 46, 24,000 more praedae nomine, as booty (Suetonius, Julius 38.1): nearly twenty-seven years of a legionary's pay at his doubled rate of 225 denarii (module 6.4). In 44 the dynasts bid against each other. Octavian paid Caesar's veterans 500 denarii a man (Cicero, Letters to Atticus 16.8; module 5.5); Antony offered the Macedonian legions at Brundisium 100 drachmas, was laughed at, and executed men chosen by lot from the tribunes' lists of troublemakers (Appian, Civil Wars 3.43), and two of those legions went over to his rival. Caesar called his men commilitones, fellow-soldiers (Julius 67.2), a courtesy Augustus dropped (module 6.4). In 32 the oath of all Italy to Octavian (module 5.5) made the soldier's bond a political one; Anton von Premerstein (Vom Werden und Wesen des Prinzipats, 1937) derived the Principate itself from that oath, read as clientship and military allegiance extended to the whole citizen body.

Each army was given two reasons, one material and one constitutional. Sulla's officers in 88, all but one quaestor, refused to follow (module 5.2). His letter to the Senate from the East, in 85 or 84, promised vengeance on the guilty and no complaint against other citizens, old or new (Appian 1.77); he crossed in 83 and took Rome in November 82. At Ravenna in 49 Caesar told the Thirteenth that the tribunes' veto, which even Sulla had left free, had been crushed by arms, and asked them to defend the reputation and standing of the commander under whom they had served for nine years; they shouted that they were ready to avenge the wrongs of their general and of the tribunes (Civil War 1.7). Suetonius adds that when Caesar, in tears, pointed to his ring, men at the back thought he had promised each of them a knight's ring and 400,000 sesterces (Julius 33). His chief legate, Labienus, went over to Pompey (Dio 41.4.3–4); no other senior officer is recorded as leaving him.

In 43 the order looked reversed. After Mutina the Senate rewarded only two legions and sent its envoys to the troops past their commander; Octavian's centurions went to the Senate to demand the consulship for him, and when rebuffed the soldiers demanded to be led on Rome (Appian 3.86–88). Dio says Octavian had his soldiers take an oath, seemingly of their own motion, and then sent four hundred of them to the Senate (46.42); refused, one fetched his sword and said it would win the consulship if the senators would not (46.43). Suetonius makes him the centurion Cornelius, leader of the men sent to ask in the army's name (Augustus 26.1). The three legions in Rome went over against their commanders, one of whom, Cornutus, killed himself (Appian 3.92). Consul at nineteen, Octavian paid each man 2,500 drachmas from public funds and promised the rest (3.94). All three writers make the soldiers' voice his instrument.

06Caesar and his legions

Caesar built the bond deliberately. At Vesontio in 58, with the army panicking before Ariovistus, he said he would march with the Tenth alone if no one else followed and make it his bodyguard, and when he mounted the Tenth on Gallic horses for a parley a soldier joked that he was making them knights (Gallic War 1.40–42). Suetonius says there was no mutiny in the ten years of the Gallic war (Julius 69), and in 49 every centurion offered to equip a horseman from his savings and the soldiers to serve without pay or rations (68.1).

Civil war, with its clemency and little plunder, tested the bond twice. At Placentia in 49 the Ninth mutinied, complaining that the war was being dragged out and that the five minae promised at Brundisium were unpaid; Caesar threatened to decimate it, reduced the threat to 120 ringleaders and executed twelve chosen by lot (Appian, Civil Wars 2.47). Suetonius has him discharge the legion in disgrace and take it back only after pleas and punishment (Julius 69); Dio (41.26–35) and Lucan (Civil War 5.237–373) give him a speech. In 47 the legions in Campania, owed rewards promised after Pharsalus and kept beyond their term, refused to sail for Africa. They nearly killed Sallust, the future historian, sent to treat with them (Appian 2.92; Dio 42.52), killed two ex-praetors, Cosconius and Galba (Plutarch, Caesar 51), and marched on Rome. On the Campus Martius Caesar granted the discharge they demanded and addressed them as Quirites, citizens, not soldiers; they begged to follow him to Africa (Appian 2.93–94; Suetonius, Julius 70). Suetonius adds that he docked the most insubordinate a third of their booty and land.

Where sources disagree. Caesar's own narrative passes over Placentia, and Suetonius credits every recovery to his authority, not his indulgence (Julius 69–70); Appian (2.93–94) and Tacitus' Germanicus (Annals 1.42) make one word end the mutiny of 47. Dio has the mutineers kill two senators and enter Rome carrying their swords, and Caesar later rid himself of the boldest by spending them in Africa (42.52–55). Stefan Chrissanthos ("Caesar and the Mutiny of 47 B.C.", Journal of Roman Studies 2001) argued that Caesar, without loyal troops enough to crush so large a mutiny, gave way rather than prevailed.

07Imperator: acclamation and victory

The army also conferred standing. After a victory soldiers hailed their commander imperator, and he kept the title until he triumphed or laid down his command. Tacitus calls it an old honour, the army's joy acclaiming a successful general, with several imperatores at once, and says that Junius Blaesus in AD 22 was the last outside the emperor's family to receive it (Annals 3.74). Acclamation opened the road to a triumph, whose rules and ritual are module 9.2. The soldiers marched in it and expected their share, and they could withhold their part: Pompey's army, paid less than it hoped, threatened to disrupt his African triumph until he said he would rather give it up than truckle to them (Plutarch, Pompey 14).

Victory became a title of rule. Sulla called himself Felix (module 5.2), and his coins of 84–83 showed two trophies with IMPER ITERVM, imperator for the second time (RRC 359). Caesar after Munda took Imperator as a permanent title (Dio 43.44.2; Suetonius, Julius 76.1), and Octavian made it his first name, Imperator Caesar (Ronald Syme, "Imperator Caesar: A Study in Nomenclature", Historia 1958). What soldiers shouted on the battlefield became the ruler's name, and the acclamation of armies, not the vote of the people, would make emperors (module 9.2). Augustus confined the triumph to his own house (module 6.4): a victorious general with an army was what he had been.

08Was it a client army?

The modern argument turns on what bound soldiers to generals. Gabba's answer was need: proletarian volunteers whose only prospect was land became a military clientele, attached to their commander by economic interest alone. Brunt (1962, revised in The Fall of the Roman Republic and Related Essays, 1988) denied that the soldiers were clients. Their loyalty was conditional; the expectation of future rewards counted for more than gratitude for past ones, and the hope of plunder made them readier to follow generals into civil war. Erich Gruen (The Last Generation of the Roman Republic, 1974) added that service did not alienate soldiers from the Republic, since they followed a consul or proconsul as the state's lawful officer; his wider case is deep dive module 11.8.

Keaveney (2007) moved the turning point from Marius to Sulla, who first made the army a political instrument. Regular armies, he argued, saw their commander as a magistrate, and patron and client fit only the private armies of the mid-first century. Soldiers were reluctant to fight fellow citizens, and their leverage lasted only while a rival commander existed to desert to; after Actium none did. Lukas de Blois (The Roman Army and Politics in the First Century before Christ, 1987, and an essay of 2000 on the military "middle cadre") looked to the officers, the centurions, tribunes and prefects through whom generals reached the ranks, and insisted that soldiers did not follow automatically: a commander had to keep his promises and justify himself to his men.

Where sources disagree. Appian's armies that served individuals rather than the public (5.17) support Gabba's clientele; the same Appian shows soldiers sending deputies to force Antony and Octavian to terms at Brundisium in 40 (5.64), the independent actors of Keaveney's account. Brunt's bargain and de Blois's officers explain the defections of 83, 49 and 43 better than gratitude does. Yet Pompey's veterans, dismissed in 62, went home quietly (Velleius 2.40.3) and waited three years for land, which no model of a client army predicts.

What survives of the client army is narrower than Gabba's model. The Republic never made discharge its own business, the oath bound men to a man, commands lasted years, and in civil war each side bid for the other's soldiers. The soldiers' price, more than their dependence, was what the Principate had to meet.

09Self-test

  1. What did Appian say about the armies of 41, and why does it not prove that the legions after Marius were volunteers?
    Answer

    That their generals were not regularly chosen, their men were not raised by the ancestral levy, they served individuals rather than the public, and their generals' authority rested on donatives (Civil Wars 5.17). It describes the triumviral armies; levies continued throughout the century (Gallic War 2.2, 6.1; Civil War 1.6), and Brunt took the levy to be the normal method.

  2. How did Sulla bind the army of Asia to himself, and what does Pompey's African army show about the same bond?
    Answer

    He billeted it on the cities at sixteen drachmas a day per soldier and indulged it against ancestral custom (Plutarch, Sulla 25.2; Sallust, Catiline 11.5–6). Pompey's soldiers, ordered by Sulla to disband, refused to forsake their general (Pompey 13): a commander who rewarded his men could hold them against the government.

  3. Why was the veteran problem never solved under the Republic, and what does Augustus' claim in Res Gestae 16.1 reveal?
    Answer

    The state paid by the campaign and owed nothing at discharge, so each settlement had to be forced through by a tribune, a consul or a dictator, usually against senatorial resistance. Augustus' claim to be the first within living memory to pay for soldiers' land names what the Republic had seldom done: Caesar's law of 59 bought from willing sellers (Dio 38.1.4), but Sulla's and the triumvirs' colonies were made on confiscated land.

  4. Did veterans want farms or money? Give the ancient evidence and two modern positions.
    Answer

    Cicero (Against Catiline 2.20) and Sallust (Catiline 28.4) show Sullan colonists who spent their plunder and fell into debt, and Gabba read the demand for land as a demand for convertible capital. Brunt held that they were countrymen seeking a living from land of their own; Keaveney doubts that most wanted farms, given how many re-enlisted.

  5. Why did the soldier's oath fail to tie armies to the state?
    Answer

    It bound men to their commander, not his office, so it went with him: Pompey lent Caesar men sworn to himself (Gallic War 6.1). In civil war it changed hands with the army (Civil War 1.23) or had to be renewed to stop desertion (1.76), and Appian says desertion came to be rewarded (5.17).

  6. Compare how the armies of 88 and 49 were persuaded, and how their officers reacted.
    Answer

    Both were given a material motive and a legal cause: in 88 the threat to the eastern plunder and the claim to free Rome from tyrants; in 49 the overridden veto of the tribunes and the general's standing (Civil War 1.7), with rumoured rewards (Julius 33). In 88 all Sulla's officers but one quaestor refused to follow; in 49 Labienus is the only senior officer recorded as leaving Caesar.

  7. Who drove the march of 43, the soldiers or Octavian?
    Answer

    The soldiers appeared to: their centurions and envoys demanded the consulship, one threatening the Senate with his sword (Dio 46.43; Suetonius, Augustus 26.1). But Appian (3.87), Dio (46.42) and Suetonius all make Octavian the director, using the army's voice; the soldiers' reward was 2,500 drachmas a man from public funds (Appian 3.94).

  8. How did the mutinies of 49 and 47 end, and why is the record open to doubt?
    Answer

    At Placentia Caesar executed twelve of 120 ringleaders, chosen by lot (Appian 2.47); in 47 he granted the discharge and called the men Quirites, and they begged to follow him (Appian 2.93–94; Suetonius, Julius 70). Caesar's own narrative omits Placentia, Dio records two senators killed and the boldest later spent in Africa (42.52–55), and Chrissanthos argued that Caesar gave way rather than prevailed.

  9. What did salutation as imperator mean, and how did it become a title of rule?
    Answer

    An army hailed a victorious commander imperator, the first step towards a triumph, and several men could hold the title at once (Tacitus, Annals 3.74). Caesar made it a permanent title (Dio 43.44.2) and Octavian his first name, Imperator Caesar (Syme 1958); the acclamation of armies would make emperors.

  10. Set out the positions of Gabba, Brunt, Keaveney and de Blois on the client army.
    Answer

    Gabba: a proletarian clientele bound to its general by economic need. Brunt: not clients but countrymen whose loyalty was conditional on land and plunder. Keaveney: Sulla, not Marius, made the army a political instrument, and regular armies saw their general as a magistrate. De Blois: the officers were the link, and a general had to keep his promises and justify himself to his men.

10Carry forward

  1. The Republic never made the soldier's future its own business: pay by the campaign, nothing at discharge, land only by a contested law. Whoever could provide land and money held the army, which is why Augustus' military treasury (module 6.4) was a constitutional act.
  2. Armies were persuaded, not owned: oath, donative, officers and a legal cause all mattered, and soldiers deserted, mutinied or forced their generals to terms when the bargain failed. Test every claim about a "client army" against what the soldiers were offered.
  3. Victory became a title of rule: acclamation as imperator, the triumph and names such as Felix, Magnus and Imperator set a military source of legitimacy beside election, and the Principate made that source a monopoly.
