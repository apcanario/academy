Maecenas and the friendship of poets

Gaius Maecenas descended from Etruscan kings, as Horace never tired of saying (Odes 1.1.1; Satires 1.6.1–4), and never held a magistracy. He stayed an equestrian while acting as Octavian's negotiator in the triumviral years and his deputy in Rome during the Actium campaign (Velleius 2.88).

Patronage was friendship between unequal men, not employment. It meant dinners, introductions, protection and gifts of property: Horace's Sabine farm (Odes 2.18.14; Satires 2.6). Virgil and Varius presented Horace; Maecenas said little and sent for him nine months later (Satires 1.6.54–62). The poets paid in dedications. Each book of the Georgics names Maecenas, and the third calls its theme tua, Maecenas, haud mollia iussa, "your far from easy commands" (3.41); the first poems of Horace's Satires, Epodes, Odes and Epistles all address him; Propertius addresses him in 2.1 and 3.9. The friendship allowed refusal. In Epistles 1.7 Horace tells Maecenas he would hand everything back rather than lose his freedom, and Suetonius' Life of Horace records that he declined Augustus' offer of the post of private secretary without offence.

Maecenas was not the only patron. Asinius Pollio, consul in 40, protected the young Virgil (Eclogues 3 and 4) and wrote a history of the civil wars that Horace called a work full of dangerous hazard (Odes 2.1.6). Messalla Corvinus, once Brutus' officer, kept Tibullus, his niece Sulpicia and the young Ovid, who called him the instigator of his studies (Epistulae ex Ponto 2.3). Maecenas' own influence faded after 23 or 22, when he warned his wife Terentia that her brother Murena was under suspicion (Suetonius, Augustus 66.3); Tacitus says he kept the appearance of the princeps' friendship rather than its power (Annals 3.30). He died in 8 BC, leaving Augustus his heir. Horace died weeks later and was buried near him on the Esquiline.

Where sources disagree. Ronald Syme's chapter "The Organization of Opinion" (The Roman Revolution, 1939) set Maecenas and his poets beside coins and buildings as instruments of a managed consensus. Peter White (Promised Verse, 1993) replied that the "circle of Maecenas" is a modern construction: the poets had several patrons, gifts were the ordinary currency of Roman friendship, and no source shows Augustus or Maecenas prescribing content. The "commands" of Georgics 3.41 can be read either way.

Virgil before the Aeneid: the Eclogues and Georgics

Virgil's life belongs to deep dive module 12.1. The ten Eclogues, Theocritean pastoral in Latin, put the land confiscations of 41 inside the shepherds' world. In the first, Meliboeus is driven from his farm while Tityrus has been left in peace by a young man at Rome whom he will honour as a god: deus nobis haec otia fecit (1.6). In the ninth, Moeris has been evicted and songs have not saved Menalcas. Ancient commentators read Tityrus as Virgil and the god as Octavian; the poem names neither. The fourth Eclogue's child goes to deep dive module 12.1, and module 5.5 places it in the hopeful season of Brundisium.

The four books of the Georgics, finished in 29, were read to Octavian at Atella on his return from the East, Maecenas taking over when Virgil's voice failed (Donatus, Life 27). Book 1 closes with the portents after Caesar's murder, Philippi, and a prayer that the young man be allowed to rescue an age in which right and wrong are reversed, the world a chariot that no longer answers the reins (1.498–514). Book 3 promises a temple at Mantua with Caesar at its centre and, later, an epic of his battles (3.10–48). Book 4 ends with Caesar thundering at the Euphrates while the poet idles at Naples (4.559–566). The promised poem on Caesar's wars became, instead, a poem about his ancestor.

Where sources disagree. Servius (on Eclogue 10.1 and Georgics 4.1) says the second half of Georgics 4 once praised Cornelius Gallus and was replaced at Augustus' order by Aristaeus and Orpheus after Gallus' fall. Franz Skutsch (Aus Vergils Frühzeit, 1901) defended the story; W. B. Anderson ("Gallus and the Fourth Georgic", 1933) and most since doubt it, since Donatus is silent and Orpheus is integral to the book's design.

The Aeneid in outline and its Augustan programme

The Aeneid, twelve books left unrevised at Virgil's death, gives the legend traced in module 1.3 its final form. Books 1–6 are the Odyssean half: storm, Carthage and Dido, the fall of Troy, the wanderings, the underworld. Books 7–12 are the Iliadic half: Latium, Allecto's war, Evander's Palatine, Pallas killed, and the duel that ends with Turnus' death. Deep dive modules 12.2 and 12.3 take it book by book; Greek module 10.12 shows how it answers Homer from the Trojan side. Propertius greeted it in progress: something greater than the Iliad is being born (2.34.65–66). Three passages carry its Augustan programme, and each is prophecy, Roman history seen forward from the mythic past.

Jupiter's prophecy (1.257–296). To console Venus, Jupiter unrolls a timetable: three years of Aeneas' rule, thirty of Ascanius, three hundred of Alba until Ilia bears the twins, then Romulus and the Romans, to whom he sets no limits of space or time, imperium sine fine dedi (1.279). Juno will come round and cherish Romanos, rerum dominos gentemque togatam (1.282). Then a Trojan Caesar, Iulius from great Iulus (1.286–290; Greek module 10.12 covers the naming), and a world at peace: Fides and Vesta, Remus beside his brother Quirinus giving laws, the gates of war shut, and impious Furor chained inside on a heap of weapons, roaring with bloody mouth (1.291–296). Each detail points at 29 BC: Janus closed, religion restored, fratricide ended. But Furor is bound, not dead.

Where sources disagree. Which Caesar is meant at 1.286? Most commentators since Heyne take him as Augustus, since the closing of Janus follows directly. Servius, and in a full modern defence Robert Dobbin (1995), argue for Julius Caesar, whose name Iulius the line presses and whose reception in heaven matches the deified dictator. The ambiguity may be deliberate: the heir's legitimacy rested on the god.

The parade of heroes (6.756–892). Anchises shows Aeneas the souls waiting to be born. Augustus appears out of sequence, straight after Romulus: Augustus Caesar, divi genus, who will found again the golden age in Latium and carry empire past the Garamantes and Indians, further than Hercules or Bacchus travelled (6.791–805). What follows is not simple praise. Brutus, who executed his own sons, is infelix (6.822); Caesar and Pompey are begged not to turn their strength on their country (6.826–835). The precept to spare the conquered (6.851–853) belongs to deep dive module 12.5. The parade ends not on Augustus but on Marcellus, his nephew and heir, dead in 23 BC (6.860–886); Donatus says Octavia fainted at the reading (Life 32).

The shield (8.626–728). Vulcan engraves Roman history: the wolf and twins, the Sabine women, Cocles and Cloelia, Manlius and the geese, Catiline punished in Tartarus and, among the righteous, his dantem iura Catonem (8.670), probably Caesar's enemy, as their lawgiver. At the centre is Actium (8.675–713), which module 5.5 reads; then the triple triumph of August 29, Caesar seated at the threshold of Apollo's temple as the conquered nations file past (8.714–728). Aeneas lifts it onto his shoulder, rerumque ignarus imagine gaudet (8.730): he rejoices in the image without knowing the events.

The technique is the programme. History becomes fate, foreseen by gods and ancestors, and every line ends at Augustus; the cost (chained Furor, the dead heir, the hero who cannot read what he carries) is written into the same passages. Whether that cost undermines the celebration is the debate between Augustan and pessimistic readers that deep dive module 12.7 runs.

Horace: lyric for the res publica

Quintus Horatius Flaccus, a freedman's son from Venusia, fought for Brutus at Philippi as a military tribune and made a joke of his lost shield (Odes 2.7.9–10). Pardoned, he bought a treasury clerkship. The three books of Odes, published in 23, claimed to have brought Aeolian song to Italian measures (3.30.13–14): Alcaeus and Sappho made Latin. In 1.2 Mercury takes the form of the young Caesar to avenge the murdered dictator; 1.37 celebrates Cleopatra's death (module 5.5).

The six "Roman Odes" that open book 3 (the label is modern) share one metre, the Alcaic, and one voice, the poet as priest of the Muses: odi profanum vulgus et arceo (3.1.1). Their themes are the regime's. Contentment over ambition (3.1); military hardiness, with dulce et decorum est pro patria mori (3.2.13); the just man, among whom Augustus will drink nectar with the gods, and Juno's speech forbidding Troy ever to be rebuilt (3.3.18–68), apt when rumour had Caesar planning to move the capital east (Suetonius, Julius 79.3). In 3.4 the Giants fall, since vis consili expers mole ruit sua, force without judgement collapses under its own weight (3.4.65). In 3.5 Augustus will be held a present god once Britons and Persians are added to the empire (3.5.2–4). In 3.6 Romans will pay for their fathers' sins until they repair the temples, and each generation is worse than the last. The cycle preaches the restoration that module 6.2 traces in stone.

The Carmen Saeculare was a commission. The inscribed record of the Secular Games of 17 BC states carmen composuit Q. Horatius Flaccus (CIL VI 32323); twenty-seven boys and twenty-seven girls sang it on the Palatine and the Capitol. Module 6.2 covers the ritual; as a poem it is a hymn in Sapphic stanzas to Apollo and Diana that compresses the Augustan myth into prayers: bless the senators' decrees on marriage (17–20); if Rome is your work and Trojans reached the Etruscan shore (37–38), grant the princeps, clarus Anchisae Venerisque sanguis (50), his asks; Fides, Pax, Honos, Pudor and neglected Virtus dare to return (57–59). Horace later made the commission his title to fame: the girl in the chorus will remember, married, that she learned the hymn of the bard Horace (Odes 4.6.41–44). Suetonius says Augustus also pressed for a fourth book of odes to celebrate Tiberius' and Drusus' Alpine victories (4.4, 4.14).

Livy: a history for these times

Titus Livius of Patavium held no office and had no known patron. Module 0.1 treats him as a source for early Rome, quotes the preface on poets' tales and dates book 1 by its single closing of Janus (1.19.3); here he is an Augustan author. The preface frames the whole work as a history of decline. Readers will hurry to recent times, in which a long-dominant people's strength destroys itself (Preface 4); Livy turns to antiquity to avert his eyes from what his own age has seen (5). Morals slid, then sank, then fell headlong, until haec tempora quibus nec vitia nostra nec remedia pati possumus, these times in which we can endure neither our vices nor their remedies (9). No state was ever greater or richer in good examples, nor so late in admitting greed and luxury (11).

The first decade, to 293 BC, offers founders. Romulus is taken up to heaven, though Livy reports that some even then believed the senators had torn him apart (1.16.4); Augustus considered the name Romulus before choosing his own (Suetonius, Augustus 7.2; Dio 53.16.7). Camillus, after the Gallic sack, is hailed as Romulus ac parens patriae conditorque alter urbis (5.49.7), and his speech keeps the Romans from migrating to Veii (5.51–54), the same refusal to abandon the city that Horace gives Juno. Most telling is the digression on Cossus (4.20.5–11). All Livy's sources made Cossus a military tribune when he won the spolia opima; Augustus told Livy he had read "consul" on the linen corslet in the temple of Jupiter Feretrius. The point was live, since Crassus had claimed the spoils as a mere proconsul in 29 (Dio 51.24; module 2.2 gives the modern dispute). Livy prints the princeps' testimony, judges it almost sacrilege to deny Cossus such a witness, and leaves his sources standing beside it.

Where sources disagree. Cremutius Cordus, in Tacitus, says Livy praised Pompey so warmly that Augustus called him a Pompeian, without harm to their friendship (Annals 4.34.3); Seneca reports him uncertain whether Caesar's birth helped or harmed the state (Natural Questions 5.18.4). Syme ("Livy and Augustus", 1959) concluded that Livy was an independent republican moralist; readers who stress Cossus and Camillus see a willing contributor to the image of the refounder. The remedies of Preface 9 may be moral legislation or, as John Moles read them, one-man rule itself.

Propertius and Tibullus: love instead of war

Latin love elegy had its first master in Cornelius Gallus, whose four books are lost but for a few lines on papyrus; Ovid gives the canon as Gallus, Tibullus, Propertius and himself (Tristia 4.10.53–54). The form came from Catullus (module 5.6). Its stance was a refusal: the lover is a slave to his mistress, his service is a campaign (militat omnis amans, Ovid, Amores 1.9.1), and the career of office and arms is declined. Gallus' fate shadowed the genre: prefect of Egypt, he boasted of his deeds, lost Augustus' friendship and killed himself in 27–26 (Dio 53.23.5–7; Suetonius, Augustus 66.2).

Propertius' first book, about 29, ends on the Perusine graves and an unburied kinsman (1.21–22): the civil war seen from the losing town. Taken up by Maecenas, he offered the patron a recusatio: were he able, he would sing Caesar's wars, but his gift is narrow (2.1). In 2.7 Cynthia rejoices that a law that would have parted them has been lifted, and he asks why he should supply sons for his country's triumphs: nullus de nostro sanguine miles erit (2.7.14). If all lived like lovers, the sea at Actium would hold no Roman bones (2.15). Book 4, about 16, turns to Roman aetiology as the "Roman Callimachus" (4.1.64): Tarpeia in love, Hercules and Cacus, the spolia opima, Actium (4.6, module 5.5).

Tibullus, Messalla's poet, sets the lover on a poor farm while his patron wages war by land and sea (1.1), and curses the inventor of the sword (1.10.1). He celebrated Messalla's triumph of 27 (1.7) and gave the Sibyl a prophecy of Rome to Aeneas (2.5), yet nowhere names Augustus.

Where sources disagree. Hans-Peter Stahl (Propertius: "Love" and "War", 1985) read the poems as an individual's resistance to the state's claims on him; Francis Cairns (Sextus Propertius: The Augustan Elegist, 2006) set Propertius within Maecenas' patronage and his book 4 as its natural product. Whether the law lifted in 2.7 ever existed is weighed in module 6.2.

Ovid: the calendar, the changes and Tomis

Publius Ovidius Naso was born at Sulmo in 43, the year both consuls fell at Mutina (Tristia 4.10.6). He declined a senatorial career and only saw Virgil: Vergilium vidi tantum (4.10.51). He was the first Augustan poet with no adult memory of civil war. The Ars Amatoria taught seduction in the city of the Julian adultery law, its first book sending readers to Pompey's portico, the theatres and Augustus' mock sea battle of 2 BC.

The Fasti, which module 2.3 reads as a source for festivals, is also an Augustan poem: an elegiac calendar after Callimachus' Aitia, six books for January to June, dedicated to Augustus (Tristia 2.551–552) and rededicated in exile to Germanicus (Fasti 1.3). Ovid calls it his military service: haec mea militia est (2.9). The new dates crowd the old. On 13 January the provinces were returned and Octavian named Augustus, a name shared with sacred things (1.587–616). On 5 February Augustus became father of his country, and Ovid compares him with Romulus to Romulus' cost: the founder took wives by force, the princeps makes them chaste (2.119–144). On the Ides of March Vesta says she snatched her priest Caesar away and left only his image to the daggers (3.697–710); in April she is lodged in Augustus' Palatine house (4.949–954); in May Mars Ultor inspects his new forum (5.545–598).

Where sources disagree. Geraldine Herbert-Brown (Ovid and the Fasti, 1994) reads the poem as a courtier's tribute alert to dynastic politics. Alessandro Barchiesi (The Poet and the Prince, 1997) and Carole Newlands (Playing with Time, 1995) find its Augustan days undercut by juxtaposition, an elegiac voice too light for its subject and an unfinished shape.

The Metamorphoses, fifteen books of hexameter from Chaos to Caesar's apotheosis, ends with Jupiter ruling the heavens and Augustus the earth, each father and ruler (15.858–860), then claims a fame that neither Jupiter's anger nor fire nor sword can destroy (15.871–872). Greek module 3.7 treats Ovid's handling of the Greek myths and the council of the gods; module 10.1 takes the poem further.

In AD 8 an edict of Augustus relegated Ovid to Tomis on the Black Sea. He insists he was relegated, not exiled, and kept his property (Tristia 2.135–138). Two crimes ruined him, carmen et error (2.207): the poem was the Ars; the mistake he will not name, saying only that he saw something, like Actaeon (2.103–108). He was never recalled and died at Tomis under Tiberius.

Where sources disagree. Ovid's relegation coincided with that of Augustus' granddaughter Julia (Tacitus, Annals 4.71), and many link the mistake to her adultery or to her brother Agrippa Postumus; others propose forbidden rites. John Thibault (The Mystery of Ovid's Exile, 1964) surveyed the theories and found none proven; A. D. Fitton Brown (1985) argued that the exile was a literary fiction, a view few accept.

Propaganda, collaboration or something freer?

The evidence for direction is thin and specific. Augustus commissioned the Carmen Saeculare, pressed for Odes 4 and asked for drafts of the Aeneid; Suetonius says he fostered the talents of his age, and objected to being written about except seriously and by the best writers (Augustus 89.3). Against that stand the refusals: the recusatio in Virgil (Eclogue 6.3–5), Horace and Propertius, Horace's declined secretaryship, Tibullus' silence, Livy's Pompeian sympathies. Repression came late. In Augustus' last years Titus Labienus' histories were burned by senatorial decree (Seneca the Elder, Controversiae 10 preface 5), Cassius Severus' libels drew the first use of the treason law against writing (Tacitus, Annals 1.72), and Ovid went to Tomis.

Syme's "organisation of opinion" makes the poets part of the apparatus. White denies any apparatus. Karl Galinsky (Augustan Culture, 1996) describes an evolving culture of shared values, in which the poets were participants with minds of their own. Duncan Kennedy (1992) argued that "Augustan" and "anti-Augustan" are readers' terms of reference, positions taken by critics, not properties of texts.

The texts themselves suggest a sequence. Virgil and Horace had lived through confiscation and Philippi and wrote as survivors for whom the peace was a deliverance with a price, which their poems name. The elegists declined the public voice, then borrowed it. Ovid, born into the settlement, played with its myths as if they were already literature, which by then they were. "Collaboration" fits the myths better than the men: Aeneas' piety, the returning golden age, Actium as foreign war and the refounder's line were made by these writers, and the regime quoted them back in its forum and on its altars (module 6.2). The poets made the Augustan myth more than Augustus made the poets.

Self-test

  1. What did Maecenas' patronage consist of, and what evidence shows that it left room for refusal?
    Answer

    Friendship with gifts, dinners, introductions and protection, such as Horace's Sabine farm (Odes 2.18.14), repaid by dedications. Horace tells Maecenas he would return everything rather than lose his freedom (Epistles 1.7) and declined Augustus' offer of a secretaryship (Suetonius, Life of Horace).

  2. How do the first and ninth Eclogues register the confiscations of 41, and why should Tityrus not simply be equated with Virgil?
    Answer

    Meliboeus and Moeris lose their land while Tityrus keeps his farm thanks to a young "god" at Rome (1.6). The equation of Tityrus with Virgil and the god with Octavian comes from ancient commentators; the poems name neither.

  3. What do Jupiter's prophecy, the parade of heroes and the shield have in common as narrative devices?
    Answer

    All three are prophecy: Roman history is seen forward from the mythic past as fate, and each line leads to Augustus (1.286–296; 6.791–805; 8.714–728). Each also writes in a cost: Furor bound but alive, Marcellus dead, Aeneas ignorant of what he carries (8.730).

  4. Why is the identity of the Caesar at Aeneid 1.286 disputed?
    Answer

    The name Iulius and the reception in heaven fit the deified Julius Caesar, as Servius and Dobbin argue; the closing of the gates of war that follows fits Augustus, the view of most commentators since Heyne. The overlap may be intended, since Augustus' standing rested on his divine father.

  5. How does the parade in Aeneid 6 complicate its own praise of Augustus?
    Answer

    Augustus is placed beside Romulus as refounder of the golden age (6.791–805), but the procession includes the infelix Brutus (6.822), Caesar and Pompey begged to lay down arms (6.826–835), and ends on Marcellus, the heir who died in 23 BC (6.860–886).

  6. Name three themes of the Roman Odes and say how 3.3 uses Juno.
    Answer

    Any three of contentment, military virtue and loyalty (3.2.13), the just man and Augustus among the gods, the fall of the Giants (3.4.65), Augustus as a present god after conquest (3.5.2–4), temple repair and moral decline (3.6). Juno forbids Troy ever to be rebuilt (3.3.18–68), a warning against moving the capital east.

  7. What makes the Carmen Saeculare different from the other Odes, and how did Horace present it afterwards?
    Answer

    It was a state commission, recorded in the inscribed acts of the Games (CIL VI 32323), sung once by a chorus of boys and girls, and its prayers set out the Augustan programme: marriage laws, Trojan origins, the returning virtues (17–20, 37–59). Horace later claimed it as his fame in Odes 4.6.41–44.

  8. What does the Cossus digression (Livy 4.20.5–11) reveal about Livy's relation to Augustus?
    Answer

    Livy reports Augustus' claim to have read "consul" on Cossus' corslet, a claim that helped deny Crassus the spolia opima, while leaving all his own sources, which made Cossus a tribune, beside it. He defers to the princeps without suppressing the conflict.

  9. In what ways did Propertius and Tibullus refuse the regime's values, and how did Propertius' fourth book change course?
    Answer

    They chose love over office and war: Propertius will father no soldiers (2.7.14) and declines epic for Maecenas (2.1); Tibullus curses the sword (1.10.1) and never names Augustus. Propertius' book 4 turns to Roman aetiology as the "Roman Callimachus" (4.1.64), including Actium (4.6).

  10. What does Ovid say caused his relegation in AD 8, and what changed in the regime's treatment of writers in its last decade?
    Answer

    A poem and a mistake (Tristia 2.207): the Ars Amatoria and something he saw but will not name (2.103–108). In the same years Labienus' books were burned (Seneca the Elder, Controversiae 10 preface 5) and Cassius Severus was prosecuted under the treason law for libel (Tacitus, Annals 1.72).

Carry forward

  1. The Augustan myth was made in words before it was quoted in stone: Aeneas' piety, the returning golden age, Actium as a foreign war and the refounder after Romulus and Camillus come from Virgil, Horace and Livy, and the Forum of Augustus and the Ara Pacis set them in stone (module 6.2).
  2. The characteristic Augustan device is prophecy: history told as fate from the mythic past, every line ending at Augustus. Later writers who attack the regime attack that teleology, and the cost the Augustan texts themselves record is where the attack begins.
  3. Patronage was friendship, not employment, and the recusatio let poets praise while declining; the space narrowed only in the reign's last decade. "Augustan" and "anti-Augustan" are labels the texts resist, so ask what each voice does within a work.
