How the Senate went to war

Greek module 7.3 narrates the wars with Philip V, Antiochus III and Perseus, and the sack of Corinth, from the Greek side; this module stays in Rome. Polybius knew the men who decided. Livy (books 31–45) wrote some 170 years later, partly from Polybius; after 167 he survives only in the Periochae, and for Spain and the Third Punic War we lean on Appian, writing three centuries later.

The war vote of 200 shows the machinery. The lot gave the consul Publius Sulpicius Galba the provincia Macedonia, and on the Senate's instruction he asked the centuriate assembly whether it wished and ordered war on King Philip for wrongs and attacks on allies of the Roman people. Almost every century voted no; the tribune Quintus Baebius accused the senators of sowing one war after another (Livy 31.6). Galba told a second assembly that the choice was not war or peace but Macedonia or Italy, as with Hannibal, and the vote carried (31.7–8).

The fetial rite itself is module 2.3. Overseas, the fetials became legal advisers. Asked in 200 whether the declaration must reach Philip in person, they ruled that announcing it at the nearest garrison in his kingdom would do (Livy 31.8); in 191 they repeated the ruling for Antiochus and judged that the Aetolians, by refusing redress and seizing Demetrias, had renounced their own friendship (36.3). The college did not decide whether to fight; it certified that a war already chosen met the form. Cicero states the doctrine: no war is just unless restitution has first been demanded or the war declared (On Duties 1.36). Control was weaker at the edges: Gnaeus Manlius Vulso attacked the Galatians in 189 without a vote of Senate or people and was nearly refused a triumph for it (Livy 38.45).

Defensive imperialism or a habit of war

Romans had one answer to why they fought: to defend allies and avenge wrongs. Cicero calls the empire, until Sulla, a patronage of the world rather than a dominion (On Duties 2.27). Theodor Mommsen (Römische Geschichte, 1854–56) largely believed it: a Senate that wanted Italy secure was dragged overseas against its will. Maurice Holleaux (Rome, la Grèce et les monarchies hellénistiques, 1921) argued that Rome had no eastern policy until fear of a pact between Philip and Antiochus, magnified by Rhodes and Pergamon, drove it to war in 200.

W. V. Harris (War and Imperialism in Republican Rome, 327–70 BC, 1979) overturned that consensus. Rome was at war almost every year. No man could stand for office before serving ten campaigns (Polybius 6.19.4); consuls wanted commands and triumphs; senators, soldiers and treasury profited from booty, land and slaves, and knew it. In 200 it was the people who had to be argued into war. Polybius himself says that after Hannibal the Romans first dared to reach for the rest of the world (1.3.6), and that before the Third Punic War they had long decided on war and wanted only a pretext that would look well abroad (36.2). On this view the fetial forms were not a brake but the way a society habituated to war told itself it was always provoked.

Where sources disagree. Harris's critics did not restore Mommsen. John North (Journal of Roman Studies, 1981) accepted the bellicosity but located it in a system that had to call out its allies' soldiers every year. Erich Gruen (1984) found Rome in the East repeatedly drawn in by Greek appeals and reluctant to annex; Arthur Eckstein (Mediterranean Anarchy, Interstate War, and the Arrival of Rome, 2006) held that every Hellenistic state was as warlike, and Rome differed in manpower, not appetite.

Provinces and governors

Module 0.2 gives the provinces in outline. A provincia was first a task, a magistrate's sphere of command: Macedonia was Galba's province in 200, half a century before it was a territory. To govern Sicily and Sardinia the praetors rose from two to four in 227 (Livy, Periochae 20), and in 197 to six, the new pair for the two Spains (Livy 32.27.6). There the number stayed until Sulla; later provinces were met by prorogation, a magistrate keeping his command for a further year.

A governor brought a quaestor for the money, a few senatorial legates, a staff of friends and the troops the Senate assigned. He commanded, heard cases on assize and issued an edict; below him cities, tribes and client kings governed themselves and raised the tax. He answered for his year only when he came home. In 171 Spanish envoys complained to the Senate of extortion. Recuperators were appointed to judge each accused governor, the Spaniards chose Cato, Scipio, Aemilius Paullus and Sulpicius Gallus as advocates, one governor was acquitted and two went into exile before judgement. The Senate then forbade governors to fix the price of Spanish grain or impose collectors on Spanish towns (Livy 43.2).

Where sources disagree. When a province was "created" depends on what counts. Module 4.2 gives J. S. Richardson's case (The Language of Empire, 2008) that provincia moved only slowly from command to territory. Robert Kallet-Marx (Hegemony to Empire, 1995) argued that Macedonia after 146 was a military command over a zone of hegemony, with regular administration decades later; Achaea is the hardest case (Greek module 7.3).

Publicani and the extortion court of 149

Rome had no civil service, so it hired one. The censors let five-year contracts for building, supplies, public land, mines and customs to companies of publicani, who pledged property as security; Polybius says almost everyone in Rome had an interest in them (6.17). In 215 three companies offered to supply the Spanish armies on credit (Livy 23.48–49), and by 169 a tribune acting for them could put the censors on trial (43.16). The silver mines near New Carthage, Polybius reports, employed 40,000 men and yielded the Roman people 25,000 drachmas a day (in Strabo 3.2.10). Closing the Macedonian royal mines in 167, the Senate reasoned that where there is a publican, either public law is empty or the allies have no freedom (Livy 45.18).

In 149 the tribune Lucius Calpurnius Piso Frugi created a permanent jury of senators under a praetor to recover money wrongly taken by magistrates (Cicero, Brutus 106); before it, Cicero says, there had been no such law at all (On Duties 2.75). The penalty was simple restitution, but it was the first quaestio perpetua, and Rome's later criminal courts grew from it. The occasion was probably Spain. Servius Sulpicius Galba had massacred surrendered Lusitanians in 150; in 149, despite a speech by Cato in the last year of his life, he escaped a special inquiry by parading his weeping children before the people (Cicero, Brutus 89–90; Livy, Periochae 49).

Cato's fig and the road to the Third Punic War

The peace of 201 forbade Carthage to make war without Rome's leave (Livy 30.37), and Masinissa of Numidia exploited the clause for fifty years while Roman arbitrators ruled for him or not at all. In 150 Carthage fought him without permission and lost. Cato, an envoy to Africa in 153, came home alarmed at its wealth (Plutarch, Cato Maior 26), and thereafter closed every speech with the opinion that Carthage must not exist (27); Carthago delenda est is a later compression. He showed the Senate figs picked at Carthage, he said, two days before: so close was the enemy (Pliny, Natural History 15.74–75). Publius Scipio Nasica answered that Carthage must be kept, so that fear would keep Rome disciplined (Plutarch, Cato Maior 27).

In 149 Carthage surrendered by deditio, which, Polybius explains, gave up land, cities, people, temples and tombs (36.4). The consuls Manius Manilius and Lucius Marcius Censorinus then raised their demands in stages: three hundred noble hostages; all weapons; then abandonment of the city for a site eighty stades, about fifteen kilometres, from the sea (Appian, Punica 76–81). Carthage refused and held out for three years.

Where sources disagree. Polybius 36.9 records four Greek verdicts: Rome had wisely removed a standing threat; it had abandoned the principles that won its empire for a lust of domination like that of Athens and Sparta; it had used deceit unworthy of it; or, Carthage having surrendered by deditio, it had acted within its rights. Moderns divide the same way. A. E. Astin (Scipio Aemilianus, 1967) found real fear of a revived Carthage; Harris saw the familiar appetite for glory and plunder.

146: Carthage and Corinth

In 147 the people elected Scipio Aemilianus, son of Aemilius Paullus and adopted grandson of Africanus, consul though he was under age (Appian, Punica 112). In spring 146 he broke in. Six days of street fighting ended at the Byrsa, from which, Appian says, 50,000 came out to surrender (Punica 130). Scipio, with Polybius beside him, watched the city burn, wept, and quoted Hector's lines on the day when sacred Troy would perish (Iliad 6.448–449), fearing that fate for Rome (Polybius 38.21–22; Appian, Punica 132). A senatorial commission of ten had the remains razed and the site cursed, rewarded Utica and the towns that had deserted Carthage, and made the rest a tributary province (Punica 135). No ancient source has the ground sown with salt; R. T. Ridley traced that story to modern writers (Classical Philology, 1986).

Corinth fell the same year to Lucius Mummius (Greek module 7.3). The Roman end is the coincidence: within months the Senate had two famous cities erased, each by a commander executing its decrees and each settled by ten commissioners. Cicero accepted the destruction of Carthage and Numantia but wished Corinth had been spared, supposing that its site was feared as a base for some future war (On Duties 1.35). Rome had passed from breaking kings to erasing cities, and the decision was taken in the Curia.

Spain: Viriathus and Numantia

Spain was Rome's longest war. From 197 two praetors went out every year. Tiberius Sempronius Gracchus, father of the tribune, made treaties with the Celtiberians in 179–178 fixing tribute and service (Appian, Iberica 43–44), and Spain was quieter for a generation. Then the Lusitanians rose in 155/154 and the Celtiberians in 153; to get a consul to Spain in time for the campaign, the consular year was moved to begin on 1 January (Livy, Periochae 47).

It was a war of bad faith. In 150 Galba promised Lusitanian bands land, divided them into three groups, disarmed them and had them cut down (Iberica 59–60). One survivor, Viriathus, shepherd turned hunter, bandit and general in Livy's summary (Periochae 52), beat a series of praetors from 147 and in 140, having trapped Quintus Fabius Maximus Servilianus' army, won a treaty, ratified at Rome, that made him a friend of the Roman people (Iberica 69). Servilianus' brother and successor Quintus Servilius Caepio called it unworthy of Rome, had it broken, and in 139 bribed three of Viriathus' companions to kill him in his sleep (Iberica 70, 74).

Where sources disagree. Appian speaks of the eight years of Viriathus' war (Iberica 75); Eutropius says he troubled Spain for fourteen (4.16), apparently counting from the rising of 155/154. Diodorus' just and frugal chieftain and Florus' Spanish Romulus ennoble the enemy and pin the treachery on individual Romans. Early modern Portugal made him a national founder, a role the evidence cannot bear.

Numantia, a Celtiberian hill-town above the Douro, humiliated consul after consul. In 137 Gaius Hostilius Mancinus, trapped in retreat, swore a treaty to save 20,000 men, negotiated by his quaestor Tiberius Gracchus (Plutarch, Tiberius Gracchus 5–7). The Senate repudiated it and handed Mancinus over naked to the Numantines, who refused him (Iberica 83); Quintus Pompeius, who had disowned an earlier agreement of his own (Iberica 79), begged off the same fate (Cicero, On Duties 3.109). It was the Caudine Forks again (module 3.4). In 134 Scipio Aemilianus was made consul a second time by special dispensation. He enclosed the town with some 60,000 men, Jugurtha's Numidians among them (Sallust, Jugurtha 7), behind a wall about nine kilometres round (Iberica 90–93); Adolf Schulten excavated the siege camps from 1905. In 133 the starving survivors surrendered. Scipio kept fifty for his triumph, sold the rest and razed the town before Rome knew of it (Iberica 97–98).

Triumphs, loot, slaves and the Italian estate

The Senate voted triumphs, and the period produced them in numbers; what the processions displayed was cash. Paullus' of 167 took three days, the first for statues and paintings, the last for gold, Perseus and his children (Plutarch, Aemilius 32–34; Greek module 7.3). Indemnities brought 10,000 talents from Carthage over fifty years (Polybius 15.18) and 15,000 from Antiochus (Polybius 21.17, 21.42); Aemilius Paullus brought so much from Macedonia that the citizens' direct tax, tributum, was not levied again after 167 (Cicero, On Duties 2.76; Pliny, Natural History 33.56). People came too: more than 80,000 Sardinians killed or captured in the elder Gracchus' campaign (Livy 41.28), 150,000 Epirotes enslaved in 167 (Polybius 30.15; Livy 45.34), some 50,000 Carthaginians. Delos, a free port from 166, could take in and ship out tens of thousands of slaves in a day (Strabo 14.5.2).

Cato's On Agriculture, written about 160, shows where the profits went. Its estate is a medium property near a town, with a road or waterway to market (1.3), worked by slaves under a slave overseer (vilicus) and his partner. An olive grove of 240 iugera needs thirteen people: overseer, housekeeper, five labourers, three ox-drivers, a donkey-driver, a swineherd and a shepherd (10); a vineyard of 100 iugera needs sixteen (11). Free hands are hired by contract for the olive harvest (144–145). The owner sells old oxen, worn tools, an old slave, a sick slave; he should be a seller, not a buyer (2.7). In Sicily slave ranching produced the first great slave revolt, under Eunus, about 135 (Diodorus 34/35.2).

Where sources disagree. Keith Hopkins (Conquerors and Slaves, 1978) built Cato's estate into a model of slaves replacing citizen farmers; Nathan Rosenstein (Rome at War, 2004) and the field surveys answer him in module 5.1. Cato proves that slave estates existed, not how much of Italy they covered.

Polybius' fifty-three years

Polybius gave the process its frame. He asked how, and under what constitution, almost the whole inhabited world fell under Roman rule in less than fifty-three years (1.1.5): from 220, the 140th Olympiad, to the end of the Macedonian monarchy in 168/167. From 220, he argues, history became an organic whole, Italy and Africa bound to Greece and Asia (1.3.4). His constitutional answer is module 4.5. Then he moved the end, carrying the work to 146 so that readers could judge whether Roman rule was to be welcomed or avoided (3.4). He had watched Carthage burn; Numantia he wrote up separately (Cicero, Letters to Friends 5.12.2).

The frame makes conquest one story with one cause and underweights Spain, where Rome fought almost without a break and won least cleanly. Roman moralists drew their own line: Sallust dated the Republic's decline from the fall of Carthage, when fear of an enemy no longer held Rome together (Catiline 10), one of several rival dates (module 4.5); Andrew Lintott (Historia, 1972) tied the theme of moral decline to the empire it explained. The year 133 closed the period: Numantia fell, Attalus left Pergamon to Rome, and Tiberius Gracchus, the quaestor of Mancinus' treaty, raised the land question as tribune (module 5.1).

Self-test

  1. How was war declared on Philip V in 200, and what does the first vote suggest?
    Answer

    The consul Sulpicius Galba, on the Senate's instruction, asked the centuriate assembly to order war; nearly every century refused, and only a second assembly, after his speech about fighting in Macedonia rather than Italy, carried it (Livy 31.6–8). Harris reads it as a Senate that wanted war and a people that had to be persuaded.

  2. What did the fetials contribute to the overseas wars of the second century?
    Answer

    Legal certification, not decisions. They ruled that a declaration could be delivered at a frontier garrison rather than to the king in person (Livy 31.8, 36.3) and that the Aetolians had renounced their own friendship, so that wars already chosen satisfied the rule Cicero states in On Duties 1.36.

  3. How do Holleaux and Harris explain Rome's wars, and what is North's correction?
    Answer

    Holleaux saw a Rome without eastern policy, driven by fear in 200; Harris an aristocracy and people that expected glory and profit from almost annual war. North accepted the bellicosity but placed it in a system that had to use its allies' troops every year.

  4. What did provincia first mean, and how did Rome staff its territorial provinces?
    Answer

    A magistrate's assigned task or sphere of command. Praetors rose to four in 227 for Sicily and Sardinia and to six in 197 for the Spains; after that new provinces were met by proroguing commands.

  5. What happened when Spanish envoys complained of extortion in 171?
    Answer

    Recuperators were appointed for each governor, with Cato, Scipio, Paullus and Gallus as advocates; one was acquitted and two went into exile; the Senate forbade governors to set grain prices or impose collectors on towns (Livy 43.2).

  6. What did the law of Calpurnius Piso in 149 create, and what was new about it?
    Answer

    A permanent jury of senators under a praetor to recover money extorted by magistrates (Cicero, Brutus 106; On Duties 2.75). It was the first standing court, the model for later criminal courts, though its penalty was only restitution.

  7. Why did the Senate close the Macedonian mines in 167, and what does it show?
    Answer

    Because they could not be worked without publicani, and where there was a publican either public law was empty or the allies had no freedom (Livy 45.18). The Senate already knew the contracting companies as a danger to provincials.

  8. List the Roman demands on Carthage in 149 and two of the Greek verdicts in Polybius 36.9.
    Answer

    Three hundred hostages, all arms, then removal of the city eighty stades from the sea (Appian, Punica 76–81). Greeks said Rome had wisely removed a threat, or turned to lust for domination, or used deceit, or acted within its rights after a deditio.

  9. How did the Senate treat Mancinus' treaty with Numantia, and what precedent did it follow?
    Answer

    It repudiated the treaty and handed Mancinus naked to the Numantines, who refused him (Appian, Iberica 83); Pompeius in the same case escaped (Cicero, On Duties 3.109). The precedent was the Caudine Forks of 321, where the commanders were surrendered to keep a renewed war just.

  10. What does Cato's On Agriculture show about the slave-run estate, and what can it not show?
    Answer

    A medium estate near a market run by slave gangs under a slave overseer, with fixed staffs (thirteen for 240 iugera of olives), hired free labour at harvest and the sale of old and sick slaves (10, 11, 2.7). It cannot show how widespread such estates were or whether they displaced the peasantry.

Carry forward

  1. Rome's wars were decided in Rome by Senate and assembly through a procedure (demands, vote, fetial certification) that made every war formally just; whether that was defence or habitual aggression is the central modern argument, Mommsen and Holleaux against Harris, and it returns with every later war.
  2. Rome governed provinces with almost no administration: a governor for a year, self-governing communities and contracting companies. The extortion court of 149 admitted that governors needed policing, and courts and publicani become political battlegrounds from the Gracchi on.
  3. Between 167 and 133 Rome moved from arbitration to erasing cities (Carthage, Corinth, Numantia) and poured booty and slaves into Italian land; Polybius framed the process as fifty-three years extended to 146, while Roman moralists dated their decline from the same victories.
