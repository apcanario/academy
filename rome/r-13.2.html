01What reached Rome, and in what words

Greek module 7.2 outlines the system: virtue the only good, the indifferents, the passions as false judgements, Chrysippus' cylinder (Cicero, On Fate 42–43), the city of gods and men. Greek module 12.7 tells the school from Zeno to Chrysippus and ends with Panaetius and Posidonius, the Stoics Romans heard in person; how Greek teachers got into Roman houses is deep dive module 13.1. This module follows the doctrines after the crossing.

The crossing shaped the evidence. Continuous Stoic prose survives only from the Roman period: Cicero's dialogues of 45–44 BC, Seneca's works of about AD 40–65, the excerpts of Hierocles, and Epictetus and Marcus in Greek (deep dive modules 13.6 and 13.8). Much of the early Stoa reaches us through Cicero's Latin, whose coinages carried Roman associations into the doctrine.

02The indifferents: room for a career

Zeno's word for the preferred, Cicero's Cato explains, came from the court: no one calls the king himself "promoted", only those whose rank comes next to his; so virtue stands alone in first place and the preferred come second (On Ends 3.52). Health, wealth, office and repute have value and are to be selected, their opposites, the dispreferred (reiecta), avoided; none is good or bad. The image suited Rome's ladder of ranked honours: a senator could climb it in earnest while holding that the climb added nothing to his happiness. Office is the archer's target (On Ends 3.22; Greek module 12.7), and defeat, exile or a lost fortune touch only the target.

Cicero's Latin made the point civic. Rendering kathēkon as officium, a word Atticus queried (Letters to Atticus 16.11.4, 16.14.3), gave the Stoic appropriate action the name of the duties of office, so that a consul's tasks and a son's became acts of the same kind as the sage's, done with or without wisdom (On Duties 1.8). Most of On Duties concerns these middle duties: the two Decii and the two Scipios, Cicero says, were not sages, only men with the likeness of wisdom (3.13–16). Seneca's fortune, and his reply that riches serve the wise and rule the fool (On the Happy Life 26.1), are deep dive module 13.5's.

Where sources disagree. On repute the Stoics split. Chrysippus and Diogenes of Babylon would not stretch out a finger for good repute apart from its use; their successors, Cicero's Cato reports, unable to withstand Carneades, made it preferred for its own sake (On Ends 3.57). Cicero in his own voice took such concessions as proof that the Stoics differed from the Peripatetics only in words (On Ends 4.72), the charge Greek module 12.7 records against Zeno.

03Hierocles' circles

Greek module 12.7 gives Chrysippus' oikeiosis. Its most memorable Roman-era statement is in Greek, by Hierocles, a Stoic of the second century AD whom Gellius calls upright and serious (9.5.8). A papyrus published in 1906 preserves part of his Elements of Ethics; Stobaeus excerpts his handbook of duties to gods, country, parents, kin and household.

The circles come from that handbook (Stobaeus, Anthology 4.27.23; LS 57G). Each of us stands at the centre of concentric circles: the first drawn round one's own mind and body; then parents, siblings, wife and children; then uncles and aunts, grandparents, nephews and cousins; then other relatives, fellow demesmen, tribesmen, citizens, people of neighbouring towns, countrymen, and last the human race. The task is to draw the circles towards the centre, moving people from each ring into the one inside, even in speech: call cousins brothers, and uncles and aunts fathers and mothers. Oikeiosis stops being something that happens to us as we grow and becomes something we do. Seneca gives it a Roman voice: we are limbs of a great body, made kin by nature, and Terence's line that nothing human is alien to him belongs in our hearts and on our lips (Letters 95.52–53).

Where sources disagree. Cicero, following Panaetius, sets out the degrees of fellowship from the human race down to the household (On Duties 1.53–55), then ranks the claims: country and parents first, children and household next, kin after (1.57–58). Hierocles shortens every distance. Martha Nussbaum ("Patriotism and Cosmopolitanism", 1994) made the circles a charter for cosmopolitan education; Cicero's ranking is the ancient text for a Stoic who still owes most to those nearest.

04The passions and their therapy

Greek module 7.2 gives the doctrine of passions as judgements. Cicero's Tusculan Disputations 3–4 (45 BC) put it into Latin: four kinds, distress, fear, desire and elation, and three good feelings, will, caution and joy, none answering to distress (4.11–14). He chose perturbationes over the literal "diseases" (3.7) but kept the frame: philosophy is the medicine of the mind (3.6).

Therapy attacked the judgement. Cleanthes would show the mourner that what happened is no evil, the Peripatetics that it is no great one, Epicurus would turn him to pleasures, Chrysippus would remove the belief that mourning is a duty owed (3.76). Cleanthes, Cicero objects, consoles the wise man, who needs no consolation (3.77); in his own Consolation, written after his daughter Tullia's death, he threw in every method at once (3.76).

Seneca's On Anger denies, against the Aristotelian view, that anger can serve courage, since once admitted it cannot be governed (1.9, 1.17). Its novelty is the anatomy of onset (2.1–4). A shiver at cold water, a blush, dizziness at a height, the stir of reading how Clodius drove Cicero out or Antony killed him: these first movements come without assent, are not passions, and touch even the wise (2.2). Anger adds a second movement, the still governable thought that I ought to be avenged since I have been wronged, and a third that wants revenge right or wrong and has overthrown reason (2.4.1). Gellius' Stoic, pale in a storm at sea, cited Epictetus' lost fifth book: impressions strike the wise, who withhold assent (19.1). Seneca's nightly self-examination (On Anger 3.36) is deep dive module 13.5's.

Where sources disagree. Galen's Posidonius explained unwanted reactions by irrational powers in the soul (Greek module 12.7). Richard Sorabji (Emotion and Peace of Mind, 2000) reads Seneca's first movements as a concession to Posidonius that keeps them outside emotion, which still needs assent; Brad Inwood ("Seneca and Psychological Dualism", 1993) finds early Stoic psychology extended to what falls below assent. Christian writers made first movements the thoughts of temptation, and Augustine retells Gellius' pale Stoic (City of God 9.4).

05Providence and the two commonwealths

Chrysippus answered the problem of evil from the whole (Greek module 12.7). Seneca's On Providence answers from the sufferer's side. Why, if providence rules, do evils befall good men (1.1)? None do: no evil can happen to a good man, since opposites do not mix (2.1). God, with a father's mind towards the good, hardens, tests and exercises those he loves (2.6, 4.7); disaster is the occasion of virtue (4.6). The examples are Roman: Mucius' burnt hand, Fabricius' poverty, Rutilius' exile, Regulus' torture (3); Cato at Utica (2.9–12) is deep dive module 13.4's. God's last word is that no one is held against his will: patet exitus, the way out lies open (6.7).

The cosmic city took a Roman form. Cicero, a man of Arpinum, gave every townsman two fatherlands, one by nature and one by citizenship, the second first in affection (On the Laws 2.5). Seneca enlarged the model: two commonwealths, one great and truly common, holding gods and men and bounded by the sun, the other assigned by the accident of birth (On Leisure 4.1). The greater can be served in leisure, by inquiry into virtue and nature. Epicurus' wise man stays out of politics unless something intervenes, Zeno's takes part unless something prevents (3.2); if no state can bear him, as Athens could not bear Socrates, the exception becomes the rule (8). Seneca's own withdrawal is deep dive module 13.5's, and Marcus' Rome and cosmos (Meditations 6.44) deep dive module 13.8's.

Where sources disagree. Plutarch charges that Zeno, Cleanthes and Chrysippus wrote much on politics, held no office and lived out their lives abroad at leisure (On Stoic Self-Contradictions 1033b–c). Seneca had already made the charge a defence: by teaching they did more than if they had led armies or passed laws, since their laws were for the whole human race (On Leisure 6.4). Whether the greater commonwealth meant service or an alibi for retreat, each generation of Roman Stoics argued again (deep dive module 13.4).

06Fate and responsibility in Latin

Chrysippus' compatibilism is Greek module 12.7's; Romans argued the problem in three registers. Cicero's On Fate, a conversation of 44 with the consul-designate Hirtius at Puteoli (1–2), survives with gaps at both ends; Cicero argues as an Academic (deep dive module 13.3). Natural causes, he grants, give inclinations: the thin air of Athens may sharpen wits, but it does not decide whom a man hears or whether he walks in Pompey's portico or on the Campus (7–8). The physiognomist Zopyrus read in Socrates' face a dull man given to women; will, study and discipline, Cicero answers, uproot such natural vices (10–11). With Carneades, Cicero holds that a voluntary motion of the mind needs no external cause, so that Epicurus need not have invented the swerve (23–25).

Gellius, in the second century AD, set out a reader's dossier (7.2): Chrysippus' definition of fate from book 4 of his On Providence, an everlasting and unbreakable sequence of things; the objection that crimes should then be charged to fate, not to men; the reply that fate acts on each mind according to its quality, well-formed minds passing unharmed, rough ones rushing into wrong by their own perversity; and Cicero's verdict, from a lost part of On Fate, that Chrysippus, sweating and labouring, tangled himself in keeping everything fated and something still in our power (7.2.15).

Seneca chose consent. Consoling Lucilius for runaway slaves, he turns Cleanthes' prayer to be led by Zeus and destiny (Epictetus, Handbook 53; Greek module 12.7) into Latin verse that ends with a line of its own (Letters 107.11):

Ducunt volentem fata, nolentem trahunt.

The fates lead the willing and drag the unwilling: freedom lies in the manner of following. Tacitus reports an unnamed school's view, usually read as Stoic: fate runs not from the wandering stars but from the chain of natural causes, yet leaves us the choice of a life, after which what follows is fixed (Annals 6.22); he himself left the question open. Deep dive module 12.6 asks how far Virgil's fate is Stoic.

Where sources disagree. Epictetus' Greek of Cleanthes has four lines, ending with the unwilling man following all the same, as a bad man (Handbook 53); Seneca's Latin has five, and the last has no counterpart in the Greek. Whether Seneca translated a fuller text or added a gloss is unknown. The thought matches the image Hippolytus gives Zeno and Chrysippus, a dog tied to a cart that follows willingly or is dragged (Refutation of All Heresies 1.21; SVF 2.975).

07Panaetius' four personae: an ethic of role

Panaetius' On Proper Functions was Cicero's model for the first two books of On Duties, written for his son in late 44 (Greek module 12.7 gives the evidence). Romans took from it the four personae, the masks or roles each of us wears (1.107–121). Panaetius is generally credited with the theory, and Christopher Gill ("Personhood and Personality: The Four-Personae Theory in Cicero, De officiis I", 1988) reconstructs its Panaetian form.

PersonaGiven byCicero's examples
Firstnature, to all: reasonthe source of the honourable and the fitting (1.107)
Secondnature, to each: body, talent, temperamentSocrates' irony; Ulysses bearing insults Ajax would have died rather than bear (1.108–113)
Thirdchance and circumstancekingdoms, commands, nobility, honours, wealth and their opposites (1.115)
Fourthour own choicephilosophy, civil law or oratory as a career (1.115)

The rule is to strive against universal nature in nothing and, within it, to follow one's own (1.110), so the same act can be right for one person and wrong for another: Cato had to die at Utica rather than look on the tyrant's face, while others who surrendered to Caesar might have been blamed for killing themselves (1.112). A career is chosen in youth, the age least fit to choose, as Hercules chose (1.117–118).

The third persona is where office enters. A magistrate must understand that he bears the persona of the city, upholding its dignity, keeping its laws and remembering what is entrusted to his good faith (1.124). The actor's mask (1.114) let Stoic ethics speak the language of Roman roles. Seneca knew the method as the part of philosophy that gives precepts suited to each persona, how a husband treats his wife, a father raises his children, a master rules his slaves (Letters 94.1). Aristo of Chios thought it trivial (94.2); Posidonius, Seneca reports, wanted persuasion, consolation and exhortation too, and the study of causes and of character (95.65–66). Epictetus' roles are deep dive module 13.6's.

08The sage, the person making progress, and the paradoxes

The sage is almost never found: the good man may be born, like the phoenix, once in five hundred years (Letters 42.1), and Seneca says of himself non sum sapiens, nor shall I be (On the Happy Life 17.3). Strict doctrine left no middle ground. Cicero's Cato says a man just below the surface drowns as surely as one deep down, and puppies about to open their eyes are as blind as newborns (On Ends 3.48).

Roman Stoics lived in the gap. Asked by a young man whether the wise man would fall in love, Panaetius said they would see about the wise man; the young man and he, far from wisdom, should avoid so disturbed a state (Seneca, Letters 116.5). We live, Cicero wrote, not among the perfectly wise but among those in whom it is much if likenesses of virtue appear (On Duties 1.46). Seneca grades progressors in three classes: those near wisdom but untested; those free of the greatest vices but liable to relapse; those free of many vices but not all (Letters 75). Romans made the progressor the reader their books address.

The paradoxes are the sage's most provoking portrait. Cicero's Stoic Paradoxes (46 BC), dedicated to Brutus, argues six as oratory, so called, he says, because they are surprising and against everyone's opinion (preface 4): only the honourable is good; virtue suffices for happiness; all wrongdoings and all right acts are equal; every fool is mad; only the wise man is free and every fool a slave; only the wise man is rich. Cato in On Ends gives them a Roman edge: the sage is more truly called king than Tarquinius, master of the people than Sulla, rich than Crassus (3.75).

09The critics: Cicero before a jury, Horace at the baths

In November 63 Cicero, as consul, defended the consul-designate Lucius Murena on a bribery charge brought by Servius Sulpicius and Cato, and turned on the prosecutor's philosophy (Pro Murena 61–66). Zeno's followers, he told the jurors, hold that the wise man is never moved by favour and pardons no fault; that he alone is handsome, rich and a king, however misshapen, poor or enslaved; that the rest of us are runaways, exiles and madmen; and that all faults are equal, so that needlessly wringing a cock's neck is no lesser crime than strangling one's father (61). Cato lived by this (62); Plato and Aristotle would have made him gentler (63–64). Plutarch has Cato smile and remark that Rome had a comic consul (Cato Minor 21). Murena was acquitted. Years later Cicero, in a dialogue with Cato, called the jokes concessions to an untrained crowd (On Ends 4.74).

Horace put the joke into verse. Satires 1.3 says the equality of sins founders on sense, custom and expediency (96–98), then shows the sage, rich, a good cobbler, alone handsome, a king, his beard plucked by boys, going to the bath for a quadrans escorted only by the absurd Crispinus (124–139). In Satires 2.3 the bankrupt art dealer Damasippus, converted by the Stoic Stertinius, catches Horace at the Saturnalia and preaches that all but the wise are mad; Horace's last line begs the greater madman to spare the lesser (326). The Epistles soften it: the wise man, king of kings, is above all sound, unless he has a cold (1.1.106–108). Under Nero, Persius, the satirist of module 10.2, wrote the Stoic sermon straight.

10Self-test

  1. Why did Zeno's word for the preferred indifferents come from a royal court, and how did the idea make room for a Roman public career?
    Answer

    As courtiers rank next to the king but are not the king, preferred things rank below virtue, which alone is good (On Ends 3.52). Office, wealth and repute could be pursued with full effort as things of selective value, while their loss touched only the target, not the agent's good.

  2. What did Cicero's choice of officium for kathēkon do to the Stoic doctrine of appropriate action?
    Answer

    It gave the Stoic act the Roman name for the duties of office and of social bonds, so that a consul's or a son's tasks became middle duties performable without wisdom (On Duties 1.8). That is why On Duties can treat the Decii and Scipios as men with the likeness of wisdom, not sages (3.13–16).

  3. How do Hierocles' circles turn oikeiosis into a practice, and how does Cicero's ranking of duties pull against them?
    Answer

    Hierocles sets the self at the centre of rings running out to the human race and asks us to draw the outer rings inward, for instance by calling cousins brothers (Stobaeus 4.27.23). Cicero ranks claims, country and parents first, then children and household, then kin (On Duties 1.57–58), keeping the distances Hierocles works to shorten.

  4. Why does Cicero say Cleanthes' method of consolation consoles only the wise man?
    Answer

    Cleanthes would show the mourner that what happened is not an evil, but only the wise man holds that belief, and he needs no consolation (Tusculan Disputations 3.76–77). Chrysippus aimed lower, at the belief that grieving is a duty.

  5. What are Seneca's first movements, and why are they not anger?
    Answer

    Involuntary jolts such as a shiver, a blush or the stir of reading about Antony killing Cicero, which come without assent and touch even the wise (On Anger 2.2). Anger begins only with the second movement, the assent that revenge is due, and runs wild in the third (2.4.1).

  6. How does Seneca's On Providence answer the suffering of good men differently from Chrysippus?
    Answer

    Chrysippus explained evil from the whole, by opposites and by-products. Seneca argues from the sufferer: no evil can befall the good man (2.1), hardship is a father-god's training of those he loves (4.7), and if it becomes unbearable the way out lies open (6.7).

  7. What are Seneca's two commonwealths, and how do they let him justify leisure?
    Answer

    The great commonwealth of gods and men, bounded by the sun, and the state birth assigned us (On Leisure 4.1). The greater can be served by inquiry in leisure, and Zeno's rule that the wise man takes part unless something prevents (3.2) becomes withdrawal when no state can bear him (8).

  8. Name two arguments in Cicero's On Fate against the view that nature or fate decides our acts.
    Answer

    Climate may give inclinations, such as Athenian sharpness, but does not decide where a man walks or whom he hears (7–8); will, study and discipline, not nature, uproot vices such as those Zopyrus read in Socrates' face (10–11). With Carneades, Cicero adds that voluntary motion needs no external cause (23–25).

  9. What does the third persona add to the first two, and how does Cicero apply it to a magistrate?
    Answer

    The first two come from nature, shared reason and individual endowment; the third from chance and circumstance, such as birth, office and wealth (On Duties 1.107, 1.115). A magistrate must understand that he bears the persona of the city, upholding its dignity and its laws (1.124).

  10. How could Cicero mock the Stoic paradoxes in 63 and argue them in 46, and what did Horace add?
    Answer

    In the Pro Murena he needed the jurors to laugh at the prosecutor Cato (61–66), and he later called the jokes concessions to an untrained crowd (On Ends 4.74); the Stoic Paradoxes showed the same doctrines could be argued as oratory. Horace made the sage a figure of satire, the king at the bath (Satires 1.3) and the preaching convert Damasippus (2.3).

11Carry forward

  1. Roman Stoicism is Stoicism translated: officium, persona, perturbatio and the courtier's "preferred" brought Roman office, role and rank into the doctrine, and much of the early Stoa reaches us through those words, so read each Latin term for what it imports.
  2. Romans moved the centre of gravity from the sage to the person making progress and from cosmos to role: the personae, Hierocles' circles, the two commonwealths and the first movements are ways of living the system without claiming to be wise.
  3. The paradoxes were the school's public face, ideal and joke at once; when Cato, Seneca, Epictetus and Marcus appear in the modules ahead, ask whether each claims the sage's role or the progressor's, and how his contemporaries answered.

The prompt this answers

Give me an in-depth report on Stoic doctrine as Romans received and reshaped it, extending Greek module 7.2, which already outlines the system (virtue the only good, the indifferents, the passions as false judgements, Chrysippus' cylinder in Cicero, On Fate 42–43, the cosmic city): point back to it, and to Greek module 12.7 for the school from Zeno to Chrysippus, instead of restating them. Cover: the preferred and dispreferred indifferents as the space in which a public career happens; Hierocles' circles of concern as a Roman-era statement of oikeiosis (Greek module 12.7 gives Chrysippus' doctrine); the passions and their therapy; providence and the good man's suffering (Seneca, On Providence), and Seneca's two commonwealths (On Leisure 4.1) as the Roman form of the cosmic city; fate and responsibility as Romans argued them (Cicero, On Fate; Gellius 7.2; Seneca, Letters 107.11, his Latin of Cleanthes on the fates that lead the willing and drag the unwilling), leaving Chrysippus' compatibilism to Greek module 12.7; Panaetius' four personae and Cicero's On Duties as an ethic of role and office; the sage, the person making progress and the Stoic paradoxes; and the critics: Cicero mocking Cato's paradoxes before a jury (Pro Murena 61–66) and Horace's satires on the Stoic sage (Satires 1.3 and 2.3). Format: about 2500 words, headed sections, name sources, flag disagreements, end with a 10-question self-test with answers and the three ideas I must carry forward.
