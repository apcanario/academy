Jupiter is not Zeus: names and layers

Jupiter and Zeus are the same word: Iuppiter is *Dyeu-pater, "Father Sky", cognate with Greek Zeu pater and Sanskrit Dyaus pitar. Yet the Capitoline Jupiter had no succession myth, no amours and, in cult, no children. Each Roman god has two layers: an Italic power known from its name, priest, calendar day and cult titles, and a Greek identity added later, which brought images, genealogy and stories. Epithets are the best evidence of function. Temple dates before about 300 BC come from Livy and Dionysius, writing four to five centuries later. Greek module 2.2 profiles the Olympians themselves; module 2.1 covers the archaic triad of Jupiter, Mars and Quirinus; module 2.3 the priests and festivals.

The Capitoline gods: Jupiter, Juno, Minerva

Jupiter

Origin and cult. The inherited sky father, guarantor of oaths: the fetial struck the victim with a flint, praying Jupiter to strike Rome likewise if it broke faith (Livy 1.24.8). The Ides of every month were his. The temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus, "Best and Greatest", shared with Juno and Minerva, was begun by the Tarquins and dedicated in 509 by the consul M. Horatius (Livy 2.8.6–8, nearly five centuries later; Polybius 3.22.1 also links Horatius to it). Vulca of Veii made its terracotta image (Pliny, Natural History 35.157); the triumph ended at its steps, and its birthday, the Ides of September, carried the Ludi Romani.

Epithets. Feretrius, from ferre or ferire, to carry or to strike (Plutarch, Romulus 16), received the spolia opima, arms a Roman commander took from an enemy commander he had killed; Livy makes his shrine Romulus' foundation and Rome's first temple (1.10.5–7). Stator, "the stayer", halted the Romans' flight from the Sabines (Livy 1.12.3–6), but his temple was vowed by M. Atilius Regulus in 294 (10.36.11). Latiaris was the god of the Latin League on the Alban Mount, where the consuls had to celebrate the Latin Festival, which Dionysius ascribes to Tarquinius Superbus (4.49), before taking the field.

Greek double. Zeus matched at once and gave Jupiter a family: Saturn as father, Juno as wife, the amours Plautus staged in the Amphitruo. The match hid a civic god whose priest, the Flamen Dialis, lived under taboos no Greek priest knew (module 2.3).

Where sources disagree. All Livy's sources made A. Cornelius Cossus, the second winner of the spolia opima, a military tribune, yet Augustus told him the linen corselet in the temple named Cossus consul (4.20.5–11). Hermann Dessau (1906) tied the claim to Octavian's refusal of the honour to M. Licinius Crassus in 29 BC (Dio 51.24.4); Harriet Flower (2000) argued that the canonical three winners were themselves shaped by Marcellus' publicity after 222.

Juno

Origin. Italic, worshipped across Latium. The name is usually linked with iuven-, youthful vigour, as the woman's Juno answered the man's Genius (module 2.4). The Kalends were hers; her Etruscan form, Uni, is in module 1.2.

Epithets and cult. Regina, queen, shared the Capitol with Jupiter; a second Regina, called out of Veii, received an Aventine temple in 392 (Livy 5.31.3; module 2.5). Moneta had a temple on the Arx, dedicated in 344 on the site of M. Manlius' razed house (Livy 7.28.4–6), with the mint beside it: hence "money". Lucina, who brings the child into the light, had an Esquiline temple of 375 (Pliny, Natural History 16.235), whose birthday, 1 March, married women kept as the Matronalia. Sospita, saviour of Lanuvium, wore goatskin and carried spear and shield (Cicero, On the Nature of the Gods 1.82); in 338 her cult was made common to Lanuvium and Rome (Livy 8.14.2).

Greek double. Hera, reached through Uni, brought marriage to Jupiter and a hatred of Troy that made Rome's queen goddess the enemy of its ancestors; Ennius had her reconciled to Rome in the Hannibalic war (Servius on Aeneid 1.281; deep dive module 12.6). Sospita and Moneta have no Hera behind them.

Where sources disagree. Cicero derives Moneta from monere, to warn, citing a voice from her temple before an earthquake (On Divination 1.101). Livius Andronicus, however, used Moneta to render Mnemosyne, Memory, and Andrew Meadows and Jonathan Williams (2001) read her as a goddess of remembrance, a fitting patron for coins as monuments.

Minerva

Origin and cult. Old Latin Menerva, from the root of mens: mind and skill. Her Quinquatrus of 19 March, named as the fifth day after the Ides, grew into a five-day feast of craftsmen, doctors and teachers (Ovid, Fasti 3.809–848). Her Aventine temple became the guild house of writers and actors in honour of Livius Andronicus' hymn of 207 (Festus, s.v. scribas).

Greek double. Athena came early, through Etruscan Menrva, who already wears the aegis on mirrors. The armour came with her; Roman cult stayed in the workshop.

Mars, Quirinus and Janus

Mars

Origin and cult. Archaic Mavors, Oscan Mamers, of unknown etymology: father of the twins (module 1.4) and lord of the month that opened the old year. His altar stood in the Campus Martius, outside the boundary, where the army mustered; his first temple, outside the Porta Capena, was dedicated in 388 (Livy 6.5.8). His festivals bracket the campaigning season, from the March horse races and Salian dances to the purification of arms on 19 October and the October Horse (module 2.3).

Greek double. Ares, the god Zeus calls most hateful (Greek module 2.2), was the worst of matches. Seated with Venus in 217, Ares' adultery became Rome's genealogy: the two parents of the nation through Romulus and Aeneas (Lucretius 1.31–40).

Where sources disagree. Cato's farmer asks Mars Pater to keep disease and storm from his fields and herds (On Agriculture 141). W. Warde Fowler (The Roman Festivals, 1899) took Mars for a farm god turned war god; Georges Dumézil (1966) read the prayer as a warrior guarding the boundary against enemies seen and unseen.

Quirinus

Origin and cult. The god of the Quirites, the citizen body, the name usually derived from *co-viria, "assembly of men", like curia; Ovid offered Sabine Cures or a Sabine word for spear (Fasti 2.475–480). L. Papirius Cursor dedicated his Quirinal temple in 293 (Livy 10.46.7). The Quirinalia fell on 17 February.

Greek double. None. Greeks glossed him as Enyalios (Dionysius 2.48; Polybius 3.25), and by Cicero's day he was the deified Romulus (On the Republic 2.20).

Janus

Origin and cult. From ianus, archway, and ianua, door: the god of passages and beginnings, named first in prayer (Cicero, On the Nature of the Gods 2.67), owner of January and of the Agonium of 9 January. His shrine was a gate, the Janus Geminus in the Forum, open in war and shut in peace (Livy 1.19.2–3; Res Gestae 13); C. Duilius added a temple after Mylae in 260 (Tacitus, Annals 2.49). His myth is in module 1.3.

Greek double. None, as he tells Ovid:

nam tibi par nullum Graecia numen habet (Fasti 1.90)

Hearth, seed and grain: Vesta, Saturn, Ceres

Vesta

Origin and cult. Perhaps cognate with Hestia. Her round Forum shrine, ascribed to Numa, kept the form of a hut, was no inaugurated templum (Gellius 14.7.7) and had no image, for the goddess was the fire (Ovid, Fasti 6.295–298). Its storeroom held the Palladium. The fire was renewed on 1 March; the Vestalia fell in June; the Vestals are module 2.3's.

Greek double. Hestia, a match so close it changed nothing, since neither had myths; Ovid had to borrow one for Vesta (Fasti 6.319–348).

Saturn

Origin and cult. A Latin sowing god to Varro (module 1.3 tells his myth), paired with Ops. His temple below the Capitol held the state treasury. Its image, filled with oil (Pliny, Natural History 15.32), had woollen bonds on its feet, loosed only at the Saturnalia of 17 December (Macrobius, Saturnalia 1.8), and he was worshipped in the Greek manner, head bare.

Greek double. Kronos, with the Golden Age and the Athenian Kronia, which Accius already called the Saturnalia's model (Macrobius 1.7.36–37). In 217 the Saturnalia was refounded with a public banquet (Livy 22.1.19–20). No Kronos kept a treasury.

Where sources disagree. Livy dates the dedication to 497 under the consuls Sempronius and Minucius (2.21.1–2); Varro, via Macrobius (1.8.1), credits Tarquinius Superbus and the dictator T. Larcius; Dionysius divides the honour (6.1.4). All wrote four or five centuries later and agree only on the years around 500.

Ceres, Liber and Libera

Origin and cult. Ceres, from the root of creare and crescere, had an Oscan form and a minor flamen; Liber was an Italic god of seed and wine, Libera his pair. A. Postumius vowed them a temple in a famine, on the Sibylline Books' advice, and Sp. Cassius dedicated it at the foot of the Aventine in 493 (Dionysius 6.17, 6.94, nearly five centuries later). Greek artists decorated it (Pliny, Natural History 35.154), and it became the plebeians' sanctuary and archive (Livy 3.55.7, 3.55.13). The Cerialia ran on 12–19 April, the Liberalia, when boys took the toga of manhood, on 17 March (module 2.4).

Greek double. Dionysius calls the three Demeter, Dionysus and Kore. A Greek rite of Ceres served by priestesses from Naples or Velia (Cicero, Pro Balbo 55) was apparently in place by 216 (Livy 22.56.4). Liber's double proved dangerous: the Bacchic cult of 186 was Dionysus outside the Aventine (module 2.5).

Where sources disagree. Cicero makes Libera Proserpina (Verrines 2.4.106); Ovid makes her Ariadne, renamed by Bacchus (Fasti 3.459–516). Whether the triad was Greek from the start or acquired Greek identities later is uncertain.

Venus, Apollo, Diana and Mercury

Venus

Origin and cult. First a common noun, "charm", akin to venerari, to worship, and venia, the gods' favour (Robert Schilling, La religion romaine de Vénus, 1954). Varro found no trace of her under the kings (Macrobius, Saturnalia 1.12); her early cult was Latin, at the federal sanctuary near Lavinium (Strabo 5.3.5). Rome's first temple, to Venus Obsequens (the epithet is Servius', on Aeneid 1.720), was paid for in 295 by fines on adulterous matrons (Livy 10.31.9). Venus of Eryx came to the Capitol by Sibylline order after Trasimene, vowed in 217 and dedicated in 215 (Livy 22.9.10, 23.31.9), and took over the Vinalia of 23 April; Caesar's Venus Genetrix followed in 46.

Greek double. Aphrodite, through Eryx and Aeneas (module 1.3), changed everything: a grace became a mother, Aeneadum genetrix (Lucretius 1.1).

Apollo

Origin and cult. None in Italy: Apollo is the one great god Rome never renamed. He came early, through Etruscan Aplu and Greek Cumae, source of the Sibylline Books (module 2.3). His temple in the Flaminian meadows was vowed in a plague in 433 and dedicated in 431 (Livy 4.25.3, 4.29.7); the Ludi Apollinares began in 212 (Livy 25.12).

Greek double. No pairing, only a narrowing. Rome's Apollo was long the healer, Apollo Medicus to the Vestals (Macrobius, Saturnalia 1.17.15); the god of music and poetry arrived with literature and with Augustus (module 6.2).

Diana

Origin and cult. Italic, from dius, bright: goddess of the wild margin, childbirth and slaves. Her grove at Aricia by Lake Nemi had a runaway slave as priest, who held office by killing his predecessor (Strabo 5.3.12). Servius Tullius, say Livy and Dionysius, founded her Aventine temple as a shrine of all the Latins (Livy 1.45.2–3; Dionysius 4.26), though Cato's list of the Latins who dedicated the Arician grove omits Rome. The temple's birthday, 13 August, was a slaves' holiday (Plutarch, Roman Questions 100).

Greek double. Artemis, through an Aventine image copied from the Ephesian Artemis of Massalia (Strabo 4.1.5), fixed by the couch shared with Apollo in 217.

Mercury

Origin and cult. From merx, merchandise: trade and nothing else, without flamen or old festival. His temple by the Circus Maximus was dedicated on 15 May 495 by a centurion, M. Laetorius, who was also put in charge of the grain supply and a merchants' guild (Livy 2.21.7, 2.27.5–6). On that day merchants sprinkled their goods from his spring and asked pardon for their lies (Ovid, Fasti 5.663–692).

Greek double. Hermes, probably from the start, adding messenger, guide of souls and patron of eloquence.

Neptune, Vulcan, Hercules and the Dioscuri

Neptune

Origin and cult. Italic, Etruscan Nethuns, of uncertain etymology. The Neptunalia of 23 July, kept in leafy shelters in the summer drought, suggests a god of springs before the sea. His one temple, by the Circus Flaminius, is attested from 206.

Greek double. Poseidon, matched by 217 at the latest, brought sea, trident and horse: Livy calls the Consualia a feast of Neptune Equester (1.9.6), and Sextus Pompeius styled himself Neptune's son.

Vulcan

Origin and cult. Volcanus had a minor flamen and an open precinct, the Volcanal, above the Comitium. He was fire as danger. At the Volcanalia of 23 August the people threw animals into the fire on their own behalf (Varro, On the Latin Language 6.20); live fish were given him in place of human lives (Festus, s.v. piscatorii ludi); and Vitruvius records the Etruscan rule that his temples stand outside the walls (1.7.1).

Greek double. Hephaestus, matched with him by 217, made a fire to be appeased into a smith with a forge, Cyclopes and the shield of Aeneas (Virgil, Aeneid 8).

Hercules

Origin and cult. Greek, through Etruscan Hercle; the Ara Maxima and its rite are in module 1.3; its feast fell on 12 August. Generals and merchants paid him a tithe of booty or profit, and Crassus feasted the people at ten thousand tables (Plutarch, Crassus 12). Temples multiplied: the round marble Hercules Victor in the Forum Boarium, Hercules of the Muses built by M. Fulvius Nobilior after 189.

Greek double. None needed. Rome added the invincible general's god and, later, the model of a man deified for his labours.

Castor and Pollux

Origin and cult. Greek, taken directly: a bronze plaque from Lavinium, of the sixth or early fifth century, dedicates an offering Castorei Podlouqueique qurois, to Castor and Pollux the kouroi. A. Postumius vowed them a temple at Lake Regillus, and his son dedicated it in the Forum in 484 (Livy 2.20.12, 2.42.5, nearly five centuries later). The epiphany at Juturna's spring and its Greek model at the Sagra are module 1.6's. The knights paraded past it every 15 July.

Greek double. They came as themselves; Rome made them patrons of its cavalry and gave the temple to one twin, as Bibulus complained (Suetonius, Julius 10.1). Women swore by Castor, men by Hercules (Gellius 11.6).

The system: triad, twelve and the lectisternium of 217

The Capitoline triad. Jupiter, Juno and Minerva shared one temple of three cellae, displacing the archaic triad of Jupiter, Mars and Quirinus (module 2.1); an older shrine of the three stood on the Quirinal (Varro, On the Latin Language 5.158). Servius cites Etruscan doctrine that a proper city needed temples to the three (on Aeneid 1.422), and builders, image and temple form were Etruscan. Yet no Etruscan temple to Tinia, Uni and Menrva together is securely known (module 1.2), and Greece had no such trio: the triad may be Roman in Etruscan dress. Capitolia later rose in colonies and provincial cities from Cosa to Thugga.

Ennius' twelve. A couplet of Ennius' Annales, preserved by Apuleius (On the God of Socrates 2), fits twelve gods into two hexameters (fr. 240–241 Skutsch):

Iuno Vesta Minerva Ceres Diana Venus Mars / Mercurius Iovis Neptunus Volcanus Apollo

Varro knew them as the twelve Di Consentes, whose gilded statues, six male and six female, stood in the Forum (On Agriculture 1.1.4); their portico was restored in AD 367 by the pagan prefect Praetextatus.

The lectisternium. A lectisternium set couches and a meal before images of the gods, like the Greek theoxenia. The first, in a plague of 399, honoured three pairs: Apollo and Latona, Hercules and Diana, Mercury and Neptune (Livy 5.13.5–8). After Trasimene in 217 the Sibylline Books prescribed six couches for Ennius' twelve (Livy 22.10.9):

Where sources disagree. Varro's Forum statues and the Etruscan council gods that Arnobius calls Consentes (Against the Nations 3.40) suggest an older Italian twelve, so 217 may have formalised rather than invented. Rome's list also has Vesta and not Liber, the Hestia version of Greek module 2.2 rather than the Parthenon's, and its model is uncertain.

Interpretatio graeca and interpretatio romana

The modern phrase interpretatio graeca, formed on Tacitus' interpretatio romana, names an old habit of reading foreign gods under one's own names. Herodotus made Amun Zeus (2.42); Polybius and Dionysius wrote Zeus for Jupiter. Romans did it to themselves when they began to write: Livius Andronicus' Latin Odyssey turned the Muse into a Camena and Moira into Morta (Gellius 3.16.11). The equation transferred what Roman cult lacked: Greek images, genealogies, marriages, myths. It left the priest, the day and the rite alone, so Rome had gods of the pontiffs and gods of the poets under one set of names, which Varro's three theologies tried to keep apart (module 2.1). It also hid functions with no Greek match: Sospita's goatskin, Vulcan as mere fire, Diana of slaves.

Turned outward, the habit became interpretatio romana. Tacitus says that by Roman interpretation the twin gods of the Naharvali are Castor and Pollux, though they are called Alcis (Germania 43); Caesar gives the Gauls Mercury, Apollo, Mars, Jupiter and Minerva (Gallic War 6.17). In the provinces the double name became the cult: Sulis Minerva at Bath, Mars Lenus at Trier.

Where sources disagree. Georg Wissowa (1916) treated interpretatio Romana as Roman names laid over native gods. Jan Assmann (Moses the Egyptian, 1997) saw translatability as a premise of ancient polytheism, which took gods to be the same cosmic powers everywhere. Clifford Ando (2005) stresses how provisional Roman equations were: Tacitus offers a reading and keeps the native name.

Gods with no Greek double

Janus and Quirinus are profiled above. Three more show what Rome kept for itself.

Terminus. The boundary stone as god, honoured at the Terminalia on 23 February (Ovid, Fasti 2.639–684). When Tarquin cleared the Capitol, the birds allowed every shrine to be moved except his (Livy 1.55.3–4), so his stone stayed inside Jupiter's temple under a hole in the roof (Ovid, Fasti 2.667–672): a sign, Romans said, that their frontiers would never retreat.

Bellona. Old Latin Duellona, war itself. Ap. Claudius Caecus vowed her temple in battle in 296 (Livy 10.19.17). It stood outside the boundary, so the Senate met there with envoys and with generals seeking triumphs, and before it a fetial threw the spear that opened a war (Ovid, Fasti 6.205–208). Greek Enyo was a name more than a cult; when Romans met the Cappadocian goddess Ma they called her Bellona, and Plutarch could not decide whether she was Selene, Athena or Enyo (Sulla 9).

Fides. Good faith, which bound treaties, clients and those who surrendered into Rome's fides. Numa, Livy says, had the flamens sacrifice to her with the right hand wrapped (1.21.4). Her temple stood beside Jupiter's on the Capitol, built by A. Atilius Calatinus and rebuilt by M. Aemilius Scaurus (Cicero, On the Nature of the Gods 2.61). Greek Pistis had almost no cult.

The gods without doubles guard the community's procedures: the gate, the citizen body, the boundary, the declaration of war, the sworn word.

Self-test

  1. In what sense is Jupiter the same god as Zeus, and in what sense is he not?
    Answer

    Both names descend from *Dyeu-pater, the Indo-European sky father. But Roman cult gave Jupiter no succession myth, amours or children; he was a god of Roman places and offices (the Capitol, the spolia opima, the Latin Festival, the fetial oath), and his Greek family came later through literature.

  2. What do the titles Regina, Moneta, Lucina and Sospita tell you about Juno, and which least resembles Hera?
    Answer

    Queenship beside Jupiter, warning or memory (and later the mint), childbirth, and armed protection of Lanuvium. Sospita, in goatskin with spear and shield (Cicero, On the Nature of the Gods 1.82), has no Hera behind her.

  3. Why is the temple of Saturn a good example of the problem of early foundation dates?
    Answer

    Livy gives a dedication by the consuls of 497 (2.21.1–2), Varro via Macrobius gives Tarquinius Superbus and the dictator Larcius (1.8.1), and Dionysius divides the credit. All wrote four or five centuries later and agree only on the years around 500.

  4. What happened at the lectisternium of 217, and why does it mark the official pairing of the Greek twelve?
    Answer

    After Trasimene the Sibylline Books prescribed a banquet for twelve gods on six couches (Livy 22.10.9): Jupiter and Juno, Neptune and Minerva, Mars and Venus, Apollo and Diana, Vulcan and Vesta, Mercury and Ceres. The state's priests arranged Rome's gods as Greek mythological couples.

  5. How does the lectisternium of 399 differ from that of 217?
    Answer

    In 399 three couches held Apollo and Latona, Hercules and Diana, Mercury and Neptune (Livy 5.13.5–8), an ad hoc appeal to Greek-friendly gods in a plague. In 217 the full twelve were seated as a system, matching Ennius' list.

  6. Why was Ares a poor double for Mars, and what did the pairing produce?
    Answer

    Ares was despised in Greek myth, while Mars was father of Romulus and guardian of army and fields (Cato, On Agriculture 141). Seated with Venus in 217, the Odyssean adultery became the parentage of the Roman people, celebrated by Lucretius (1.31–40).

  7. What did Rome first want from Apollo, and what is unusual about his name?
    Answer

    Healing: his first temple was vowed in a plague of 433 (Livy 4.25.3), and the Vestals invoked him as Apollo Medicus (Macrobius, Saturnalia 1.17.15). He is the one great god Rome never renamed, since no Italic god matched him.

  8. What does the Lavinium plaque to Castor and Pollux show, and how does it bear on the Regillus story?
    Answer

    It names the twins and calls them qurois, kouroi, in archaic Latin, so their cult reached Latium directly from Greeks by about 500 BC. That makes Postumius' vow plausible, though the epiphany at Juturna's spring (Dionysius 6.13) may be borrowed from the Sagra (Cicero, On the Nature of the Gods 2.6).

  9. What is the difference between interpretatio graeca and interpretatio romana, and what does Tacitus' Naharvali passage show?
    Answer

    The first reads foreign gods, Rome's included, by Greek names; the second reads foreign gods by Roman names. Tacitus calls the twins Castor and Pollux by Roman interpretation yet records their native name, Alcis (Germania 43): a reading, not a claim of identity.

  10. Name three gods with no Greek double and say what they have in common.
    Answer

    Any three of Janus, Quirinus, Terminus, Bellona and Fides. They govern the community's procedures (thresholds, the citizen body, boundaries, the declaration of war, the sworn word), for which Greek myth had no figures.

Carry forward

  1. Every Roman god has two layers: an Italic cult (priest, calendar day, epithet, place) and a Greek identity (image, family, myth). Read cult evidence for function and literature for the overlay, and never assume one tells you the other.
  2. The equation was a dated process, not a fact of nature: some gods arrived already Greek (Apollo, Hercules, Castor), others were matched by stages through Etruria, Cumae, Sicily and the poets, and 217 is the year the state seated its twelve as Greek couples.
  3. Interpretatio joins and hides at once. The gods with no double (Janus, Terminus, Fides) or a bad one (Mars, Venus, Vulcan) mark what was specifically Roman, and the same habit turned outward built the double-named pantheons of the provinces.
