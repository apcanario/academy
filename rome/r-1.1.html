01Before the Iron Age: Terramare and Proto-Villanovan

Rome's neighbours were not newcomers in 1000 BC. In Emilia and southern Lombardy the Terramare (from terra marna, the black earth later farmers dug from the mounds as fertiliser) were villages of the Middle and Recent Bronze Age, c. 1650–1150 BC: banked, moated, laid out on regular plans, with houses on piles and cremation cemeteries outside. Around 1150 the system collapsed and the central plain emptied; Andrea Cardarelli blames over-exploitation and climate, not invasion. The Final Bronze Age, c. 1200–950, is called Proto-Villanovan because it anticipates the Iron Age rite. From the Po delta to Sicily cremation cemeteries appear with striking uniformity: ashes in a biconical urn capped by a bowl, set in a small pit, with few goods. Frattesina in the Polesine (a workshop centre for glass and amber), Pianello di Genga in the Marche, Allumiere in the Tolfa hills and Timmari near Matera are the reference sites.

Where sources disagree. Wolfgang Helbig (Die Italiker in der Poebene, 1879) and Luigi Pigorini made the Terramare folk the ancestral Italic people, who moved south and became the Latins, and read the urnfields as immigrants from central Europe. Renato Peroni and Anna Maria Bietti Sestieri replaced migration with local development: the rite spread through elite networks over a continuous pattern of settlement. Small-group movement remains possible.

02The Villanovan culture

The name comes from Villanova near Bologna, where Count Giovanni Gozzadini excavated a cemetery in 1853. "Villanovan" labels the Early Iron Age, c. 900–720 BC, of the lands that became Etruscan: Etruria, Bologna and Verucchio, Fermo in the Marche, and Campania (Capua, Pontecagnano). It is the Etruscans before writing; their origins are module 1.2. The signature is the biconical urn, a hand-made vessel of two cones with one handle, incised with meanders and swastikas, capped by a bowl or, for some men, a crested helmet, and set in a pit. Some ashes went instead into a hut urn, a clay model of an oval hut with door and smoke-hole that matches the post-holes of real huts; hut urns are commoner still in Latium.

Two changes run through the ninth and eighth centuries. In southern Etruria inhumation in trench graves gained ground from the later ninth century and dominated by the late eighth, while cremation persisted at Bologna and Clusium. And the small villages were abandoned for a few huge plateaus, Veii (about 190 hectares), Tarquinia, Caere and Vulci, each the footprint of a future city. The graves show a warrior elite forming: at Quattro Fontanili at Veii the early burials are nearly uniform, but by the mid eighth century a minority of men lie with sword, shield, crested helmet and horse-bits, and women with heavy fibulae and weaving gear, beside Euboean cups. The Warrior Tomb at Tarquinia (late eighth century) is the type.

Where sources disagree. Velleius Paterculus (1.7.2–4), writing under Tiberius, reports Cato's date for the Etruscan foundation of Capua, 260 years before its capture in 211, and prefers a date near 800 BC. Capua's ninth-century Villanovan graves support Velleius; whether their people were Etruscan settlers or Campanians sharing Etruscan culture is still argued.

03Latial culture: Rome, the Alban Hills and Osteria dell'Osa

South of the Tiber the Latial culture, defined by Giovanni Colonna and refined by Bietti Sestieri, began with the miniature: the cremated dead went into a hut urn or jar with tiny vessels and, for men, tiny weapons and offering-figurines, all packed into a storage jar (dolium) in a shaft. The miniatures mark ritual roles, not wealth.

In Rome, Giacomo Boni found the Forum cemetery beside the Temple of Antoninus and Faustina from 1902: some forty graves, Latial I cremations in dolia, then inhumations. In 2006 a cremation of about 1000 BC turned up under the Forum of Caesar. When adults ceased to be buried in the valley, the valley had joined the settlement. The main cemetery moved to the Esquiline, dug hastily in the 1870s and 1880s and catalogued by Giovanni Pinza (1905), with graves from the ninth century into the orientalising period. The Palatine huts are module 1.4. In the Alban Hills, heartland of Latial I, hut urns found near Marino in 1817 beneath a layer of peperino were published by Alessandro Visconti as coming from "near ancient Alba Longa"; the cemeteries of Castel Gandolfo, Marino and Grottaferrata show small communities clustered round the lake, but no city of Alba.

Osteria dell'Osa, beside Gabii, is the best-excavated Latin community: some 600 graves of the ninth to seventh centuries, dug by Bietti Sestieri in the 1970s and 1980s (The Iron Age Community of Osteria dell'Osa, 1992). In phase IIA two clusters, which she reads as descent groups, each have a few cremated adult men with hut urns and miniature weapons at the core, ringed by inhumed men, women and children: cremation marks the heads of lineages, not a separate people. In a woman's grave of about 775 BC lay a local flask scratched with five letters, read as Greek eulin: the first writing in Latium.

Where sources disagree. The dates above rest on Greek pottery cross-dated to the Aegean. Radiocarbon and dendrochronology, pressed by Albert Nijboer and Johannes van der Plicht, raise the early phases by fifty to a hundred years; Bietti Sestieri and most Italian scholars keep the lower dates. The oldest Forum graves thus precede Varro's 753 by two and a half centuries, or by up to three and a half.

04The Latins: shared cults and the League

The Latins saw themselves as one people, the nomen Latinum, with mutual rights of trade and marriage (commercium, conubium) and cults no single city owned.

  • The Latin festival on the Alban Mount, for Jupiter Latiaris: each member people received a share of a white bull. Dionysius (4.49), writing five centuries later, credits Tarquinius Superbus with founding it for forty-seven peoples; the Alban setting points to an older cult. Pliny (Natural History 3.69) lists some thirty populi Albenses who once took meat there, most vanished by his day.
  • Diana at Aricia, by Lake Nemi. A fragment of Cato's Origines, written some three and a half centuries after the event, records that Egerius Baebius of Tusculum, "Latin dictator", dedicated the grove for eight peoples: Tusculum, Aricia, Lanuvium, Laurentum, Cora, Tibur, Pometia and Ardea. Rome is absent.
  • Lavinium, seat of "our Penates" (Varro, On the Latin Language 5.144). Ferdinando Castagnoli's excavations from the late 1950s found thirteen altars, built and rebuilt from the mid sixth century into the fourth, and a bronze plaque of about 500 to Castorei Podlouqueique qurois, "Castor and Pollux, the kouroi" (Weinstock, 1960): a Greek cult reaching Latium directly. Aeneas' shrine there is module 1.3.

The Latin League was the political shadow of these cults. The Latins met at the grove of Ferentina; the antiquarian Cincius, quoted by Festus, says they shared command there "from the destruction of Alba" until 340 BC. Cato's "Latin dictator" shows a federal office. Romans dated the formal alliance to Spurius Cassius' treaty of 493 (Dionysius 6.95), whose bronze column Cicero remembered behind the Rostra (Pro Balbo 53); everything earlier is reconstructed backwards.

Where sources disagree. Andreas Alföldi (Early Rome and the Latins, 1965) argued that the annalists inflated Rome: leadership passed from Alba to Lavinium and Aricia, and Rome was a minor member until after 500, as Cato's list implies. Tim Cornell (The Beginnings of Rome, 1995) answers with the first Carthage treaty, dated by Polybius (3.22) to 508/7, in which Rome speaks for Ardea, Antium and Laurentum; Cato's dedication then marks a league formed against Rome after the Tarquins fell.

05Sabellic Italy and the other peoples

East and south of the Latins lived speakers of Sabellic (Osco-Umbrian) languages. Ancient writers derived them from the Sabines through the ver sacrum: a community vowed all born in one spring and sent the grown children out behind an animal, a bull for the Samnites, a woodpecker (picus) for the Picentes, a wolf (hirpus) for the Hirpini (Strabo 5.4.2, 5.4.12). These are aetiologies of names, but the link is real: the Samnites called their land Safinim. The Samnites are archaeologically thin before 500; their expansion is fifth-century, when Oscan speakers took Capua (423) and Cumae (421; Livy 4.37, 4.44).

The Umbrians of the upper Tiber left the Iguvine Tables, seven bronzes found at Gubbio in 1444 and engraved in the third to first centuries BC, in Umbrian and Latin letters: the rites of a priestly college, the Atiedian Brethren. Tablets VIa–VIb purify the citadel. Auspices are taken from named birds within a marked space, as in the Roman templum; sacrifices are made at three gates to Jupiter, Mars and Vofionus, all "Grabovius", and to a second triad including Fisus Sancius, cousin of Rome's Semo Sancus; then the people are purified and the neighbouring Etruscans and Naharci cursed. The prayers pile up paired synonyms exactly as Cato's farmer does (On Agriculture 141). The text is Hellenistic, so how much Iron Age ritual it preserves is unknown; Dumézil's reading of its triad is weighed in module 2.1.

  • Veneti, at Este and Padua: figured bronze buckets (situlae), and alphabet tablets dedicated to the goddess Reitia. Livy, a Paduan, makes the Trojan Antenor their founder (1.1).
  • Ligurians, in the north-west, known from place-names and statue-stelae; Cato called them "illiterate and liars" (quoted by Servius on Aeneid 11).
  • Messapians, in the Salento heel, Cretans by Herodotus' account (7.170), which records their slaughter of the Tarentines c. 473.
  • Picenes, on the middle Adriatic, with weapon-heavy graves at Novilara and Numana and the sixth-century Warrior of Capestrano, inscribed in South Picene.

Within Italic the split is sharp: Latin keeps qu where Sabellic has p (Latin quis, Oscan pis). Giacomo Devoto thought the two groups arrived separately and converged; Helmut Rix and most Indo-Europeanists posit one Proto-Italic parent. The alphabet's route maps the contacts. Euboean Greeks brought a western alphabet; the Etruscans had it by about 700 (the model alphabet on the Marsiliana ivory tablet) and passed it to Umbrians, Oscans, Picenes, Veneti and Lepontii, while the Messapians took theirs from Taras. Latin wrote C for /k/ and /g/ alike, as Etruscan did, implying an Etruscan teacher, yet kept B, D and O, which Etruscans never used, implying Greek contact too. Writing arrived as an elite skill, naming owners and givers on gifts.

Where sources disagree. Livy (1.7.8) honours Evander for teaching letters; Tacitus (Annals 11.14) has the Etruscans taught by Demaratus of Corinth and the Aborigines by Evander. The supposed earliest Latin text, the Praeneste fibula reading Manios med fhefhaked Numasioi, "Manius made me for Numerius", was denounced as a forgery by Margherita Guarducci (1980); analysis by Daniela Ferro and Edilberto Formigli (2011) supports authenticity, though doubts persist.

07Greeks and Phoenicians, seen from Italy

For Greek colonisation itself, see Greek module 5.2. From Italy the Greeks appeared first as traders: Euboean cups reached Veii early in the eighth century, and Eugenio La Rocca published eighth-century Greek pottery from Sant'Omobono at Rome's river harbour. Pithekoussai on Ischia (c. 770; Strabo 5.4.9) was placed for Etruscan metal and worked Elban iron. Giorgio Buchner's excavation of its cemetery from 1952 (published with David Ridgway, 1993) showed a mixed population: Greeks, Levantines who scratched Semitic letters on amphorae, and Italian women who had married in. Grave 168 held the Nestor cup, a Rhodian cup of the later eighth century inscribed in the Euboean alphabet: "Nestor's cup, good to drink from", then two hexameters promising the drinker desire for "fair-crowned Aphrodite". It is a symposium joke on Iliad 11.632–637.

Where sources disagree. Whether the joke presupposes our Iliad is Greek module 10.8. Buchner took the bones for a boy of about twelve; Gigante and colleagues (PLoS One, 2021) found at least three people, apparently none a child.

Cumae (c. 750), planted on a native site, was the nearest Greek city for Etruscans and Latins: the source of their alphabet, the reclining banquet, Apollo and the Sibyl, and Tarquinius Superbus' last refuge (module 1.6). The cities of Magna Graecia and Sicily, from Naxos (734) and Syracuse (733; Thucydides 6.3), took Italian land; yet at L'Amastuola near Taras, Gert-Jan Burgers and Jan Paul Crielaard found Greek and native houses side by side c. 700–650. Latin loanwords show the channels: oliva from archaic elaiwa, machina from Doric machana.

The Phoenicians were in the west as early. Timaeus, writing around 300 BC (in Dionysius 1.74.1), put Carthage in 814/813, Rome's year too, and radiocarbon from the earliest levels comes close. The Nora stone names Sardinia in the ninth or eighth century; Sulcis, Tharros and Motya followed, and Thucydides (6.2.6) says Phoenicians once ringed Sicily. Nuragic bronzes in ninth-century graves at Vulci show a Tyrrhenian network before any colony. In the seventh century Phoenician silver bowls filled the princely tombs of Caere and Praeneste, and the orientalising style came through Phoenician and Greek hands alike. Ancient writers put Gadir near 1100 BC; tenth- and ninth-century Phoenician pottery at Huelva (González de Canales, 2004) has narrowed a gap once dismissed as legend.

08The Sabines: a first exercise in method

In legend (module 1.4) the Sabines are Rome's co-founders: the seized women, Titus Tatius of Cures as joint king, Numa from Cures, and the citizens called Quirites after Cures (Livy 1.13.5). The story is first attested in Fabius Pictor (Plutarch, Romulus 14.1), more than five centuries after the supposed events, and told in full by Livy and Dionysius, seven centuries after.

Archaeology finds a Sabine country but no distinct Sabine people. The lower Sabina by the Tiber (Cures, Eretum, Poggio Sommavilla) shares its pottery and luxuries with Faliscans and Latins; the inland basins of Reate and Amiternum look to the Abruzzo. Nothing in Rome marks a Sabine quarter. An older reading took the Forum's cremations for Latins and the Esquiline inhumations for Sabines; Osteria dell'Osa disposes of it, since there both rites coexist within one kin group. What the ground does show is traffic along the Tiber corridor.

Language cuts both ways. Sabine is almost lost; a flask from Poggio Sommavilla (c. 600) carries a short text whose language is disputed. Ancient "Sabine" words come mostly through Varro, a man of Reate, who says the altars Tatius vowed "smell of the Sabine tongue" (On the Latin Language 5.74), then lists Saturn, Diana, Vulcan and Sol among them. Genuine Sabellic loans in Latin exist, bos, lupus, rufus, but could have entered at any date: they prove contact, not a joint foundation.

The story must be explained as well as weighed. It accounts for things Romans saw: the name Quirites, the tribe of Titienses named from Tatius (Livy 1.13.8), Sabine-seeming cults on the Quirinal, and real Sabine families such as the Claudii, said to have arrived with Attus Clausus in 504 (Livy 2.16.4–5). It served moralists: Livy's Numa learned "the grim and austere discipline of the ancient Sabines" (1.18.4). And Rome conquered the Sabines in 290, less than a century before Fabius Pictor wrote. The method: keep ground, language and story apart, and date each witness.

Where sources disagree. Jacques Poucet (Recherches sur la légende sabine des origines de Rome, 1967) argued that the Sabine element in early Rome was largely built in the fourth and third centuries, after Rome absorbed the Sabines. Dumézil read the Sabine war as myth joining the third function, wealth, to the first two. Cornell keeps a middle line: the synoecism is myth, but Sabine clans in early Rome, like the Claudii, are plausible in a world of horizontal mobility.

09Self-test

  1. Why is the uniformity of Proto-Villanovan cremation no longer taken as proof of an invasion?
    Answer

    Helbig and Pigorini read it as migrants from the Terramare or central Europe; Peroni and Bietti Sestieri showed continuity of settlement and explained the rite as spreading through elite networks. A shared burial custom need not mean a new people.

  2. What evidence in Villanovan cemeteries shows a warrior elite forming in the eighth century?
    Answer

    Early graves at Quattro Fontanili (Veii) are nearly uniform; by the mid eighth century a minority of men have swords, shields, crested helmets and horse-bits, women rich fibulae and weaving gear, with Greek imports, as in the Warrior Tomb at Tarquinia.

  3. At Osteria dell'Osa, who was cremated in phase IIA, and what does that imply about rite and identity?
    Answer

    A few adult men, with hut urns and miniature weapons, at the core of each cluster of inhumations. The rite marked rank within a kin group, so a difference of burial rite is not a difference of people.

  4. Why does the end of adult burial in the Forum valley matter for the history of Rome?
    Answer

    Once adults were buried on the Esquiline and only infants in the valley, the valley had become part of the settlement: the hill villages were becoming one community.

  5. What does Pliny's list of populi Albenses tell us about early Latium?
    Answer

    About thirty peoples once shared the sacrificial meat on the Alban Mount (Natural History 3.69), most vanished by Pliny's day: Latium was once a mosaic of small communities bound by a cult older than Rome's leadership.

  6. Why is Rome's absence from Cato's record of the dedication at Aricia significant, and how do Alföldi and Cornell read it?
    Answer

    It shows a federal Latin act without Rome. Alföldi takes it as proof that Rome was a minor member before 500; Cornell, citing the Carthage treaty in Polybius 3.22, reads it as a league formed against a strong Rome after the Tarquins fell.

  7. What does the Castor and Pollux plaque at Lavinium show?
    Answer

    A Greek cult, with the Greek title kouroi written qurois, was established at a Latin sanctuary by about 500 BC, so Greek religion reached Latium directly and not only through Rome.

  8. What do the Iguvine Tables reveal about Umbrian religion, and what caution applies?
    Answer

    Auspices in a marked space, sacrifices to triads at the citadel gates, purification of the people and cursing of neighbours, with prayers like Cato's (On Agriculture 141). But the tablets date from the third to first centuries BC, so their Iron Age antiquity is assumed, not shown.

  9. What does Latin's use of C, and its keeping of B, D and O, suggest about where Latins learned to write?
    Answer

    C for both /k/ and /g/ follows Etruscan practice and implies Etruscan mediation; B, D and O, unused by Etruscans, imply direct contact with Greeks, most likely at Cumae. Probably both channels operated.

  10. On the Sabines, what can archaeology and language establish, and what can they not?
    Answer

    They show a Sabellic-speaking hill population in close exchange with Latins, Sabellic loanwords in Latin, and traffic along the Tiber. They cannot confirm a Sabine half of Rome or a joint foundation; the old cremation-Latin, inhumation-Sabine reading fails.

10Carry forward

  1. Rome was one Latin community among many, sharing language, burial rites and federal cults (the Alban Mount, Aricia, Lavinium) older than its own leadership; every claim of early Roman primacy should be read against that crowded Latium.
  2. Iron Age Italy was multilingual and connected before it was colonised: Phoenicians, Euboeans, Sardinians and Etruscans traded through the Tyrrhenian, writing arrived as an elite import via Cumae and Etruria, and Rome's culture was hybrid from the start.
  3. Keep the ground, the language and the story apart, and date each witness: archaeology shows rank and period more readily than ethnicity, and ethnic labels in legend (Sabine, Trojan, Etruscan) usually explain later institutions and family claims.

The prompt this answers

Give me an in-depth report on the peoples of Italy in the Iron Age, c. 1000 to 500 BC, the world Rome emerged from. Cover: the Final Bronze Age background (the Terramare, the Proto-Villanovan cremation cemeteries) and the Villanovan culture of Etruria and Bologna (biconical urns, hut urns, the shift to inhumation, the rise of a warrior elite); the Latial culture and its phases, the early cemeteries of Rome (the Forum burials, the Esquiline) and of the Alban Hills, and Osteria dell'Osa near Gabii as the best-excavated Latin community; the Latins as a people and their shared cults (the Latin festival on the Alban Mount, Diana at Aricia, the thirteen altars at Lavinium) and the Latin League; the Sabines, the Oscan- and Umbrian-speaking peoples (the Samnites, the Iguvine Tables as a window on Umbrian religion), the Veneti, Ligurians, Messapians and Picenes; the languages of Italy, Indo-European and not, and what the spread of the alphabet shows; the Greek colonies of the south (Pithekoussai and Cumae, then Magna Graecia and Sicily) seen from the Italian side, with the Nestor cup and the first writing in Latium; and the Phoenicians in the west. Point back to Greek module 5.2 for Greek colonisation itself. End with the Sabines as the first case of the course's method: what archaeology and language allow us to say about a people that Roman legend (module 1.4) made into Rome's co-founders. Format: about 2500 words, headed sections, name sources, flag disagreements, end with a 10-question self-test with answers and the three ideas I must carry forward.
