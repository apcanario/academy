Galba and the secret of empire

Module 7.1 ends with Nero's death in June 68 and the Senate's recognition of Servius Sulpicius Galba, governor of Nearer Spain. The narrative rests on Tacitus' Histories (module 0.1), which survive only for 69–70; on Plutarch's Galba and Otho; on Suetonius; and on Dio in Byzantine epitome. For 69 the first three agree so closely that a lost common source is usually assumed.

Galba, an old noble of severe reputation, lost it within months. He refused the donative promised in his name: he chose his soldiers, he said, and did not buy them (Tacitus, Histories 1.5; Suetonius, Galba 16.1). On 1 January 69 the legions of Upper Germany refused their oath, and the next day Lower Germany hailed its governor, Aulus Vitellius (Histories 1.55–57). Galba adopted a young noble, Piso Licinianus. Marcus Salvius Otho, who had expected the honour, bought the praetorians instead, and on 15 January Galba and Piso were cut down in the Forum (1.41–43). All would have judged him capable of ruling, Tacitus says, had he not ruled (1.49).

evulgato imperii arcano, posse principem alibi quam Romae fieri.

"The secret of empire was out: an emperor could be made elsewhere than at Rome" (Histories 1.4). Galba was made in Spain, Vitellius on the Rhine, Vespasian in Egypt and Judaea; only Otho was made in the city, by the guard. Module 6.1 closes on a succession that rested only on the family and the soldiers' loyalty to it; 69 showed that any provincial army could decide.

Otho, Vitellius and the burning of the Capitol

Otho reigned three months. Vitellius' generals crossed the Alps and in April beat his army at the first battle of Bedriacum, near Cremona. Otho, his forces still intact, killed himself to end the bloodshed: by two deeds, one most shameful and one most noble, Tacitus says, he earned as much good fame as bad (Histories 2.50). Vitellius reached Rome in July, a glutton in Tacitus and Suetonius alike, and filled a new guard from his German legions.

On 1 July the prefect of Egypt, Tiberius Julius Alexander, swore his legions to Titus Flavius Vespasianus, commander against the Jewish revolt; Judaea and Syria followed (Histories 2.79–81). Vespasian came of a Sabine family without ancestral masks (Suetonius, Vespasian 1.1). The Danube legions joined him and Antonius Primus invaded Italy; in late October the Vitellians were broken near Cremona, and the city was sacked for four days (Histories 3.33).

In Rome Vitellius agreed to abdicate, then could not carry it through. Vespasian's brother Flavius Sabinus, prefect of the city, seized the Capitol with his followers and the young Domitian. On 19 December the Vitellians stormed it and the temple of Jupiter Best and Greatest burned: for Tacitus the most lamentable and disgraceful crime since the city's foundation, committed with no foreign enemy at the gates (3.72). Sabinus was killed; Domitian escaped disguised as a worshipper of Isis (Suetonius, Domitian 1.2), a cult whose Flavian fortunes module 2.5 follows. Next day the Flavians fought their way in, and Vitellius was killed and thrown on the Gemonian steps (Histories 3.84–85). The Druids, Tacitus reports, read the fire as a sign that empire was passing to the peoples beyond the Alps (4.54).

The rebuilding began in June 70 on the old foundations (4.53). Vespasian carried rubble on his own neck and had copies sought of three thousand bronze tablets of laws and decrees lost in the fire (Suetonius, Vespasian 8.5).

Where sources disagree. Who lit the fire is unclear. Tacitus gives two versions: the attackers threw torches onto the roofs, or, the commoner report, the besieged fired the buildings to drive them back (Histories 3.71). Suetonius blames Vitellius' men, with Vitellius dining and watching from the palace (Vitellius 15.3). On Tacitus' commoner report, the dynasty that restored the Capitol rose through a fire its own partisans set.

The law on Vespasian's powers

With Vitellius dead, the Senate voted Vespasian "everything customary for emperors" (Histories 4.3), and an assembly made the decree law. Its end survives on a bronze tablet that Cola di Rienzo displayed in the Lateran in the 1340s as proof of the Roman people's lost sovereignty (ILS 244; CIL VI 930). Module 6.1 shows Augustus' powers voted piece by piece over decades; here the bundle arrives in one law. Its clauses allow Vespasian to make treaties; to convene the Senate and have its decrees passed; to have candidates he commends considered out of turn; to extend the pomerium; and to do whatever he judges in the interest of the state and the majesty of things divine and human, public and private. They also free him from the laws from which his predecessors were freed.

The precedents named are Augustus, Tiberius and Claudius only; Gaius, Nero and the emperors of 69 vanish. The last clause validates everything done by Vespasian or at his order before the law, as if by the people's command. He counted his reign from 1 July, the day of the Alexandrian oath (Suetonius, Vespasian 6.3), not from the Senate's vote. The law shows both faces of the Principate: the powers remain a legal bundle conferred by Senate and people, and the conferring follows the army's choice. Ulpian built on it: what pleases the emperor has the force of law, because by the law on his imperium the people confers on him all its own imperium and power (Digest 1.4.1).

Where sources disagree. Read alone, the discretionary clause looks like a blank cheque and the law like a new constitution for a family without Julio-Claudian blood. P. A. Brunt ("Lex de imperio Vespasiani", Journal of Roman Studies 1977) argued that its wording was inherited, probably from the grant to Gaius in 37, with prerogatives added since Claudius; that only the final clause may be new; and that it is the basis of the jurists' doctrine. Whether the lost opening conferred tribunician power and imperium is unknown.

The Jewish War and the fall of Jerusalem

The revolt began in 66, when the procurator Gessius Florus took seventeen talents from the Temple treasury (Josephus, Jewish War 2.293) and the Temple captain Eleazar son of Ananias stopped the sacrifices for the emperor, which Josephus calls the foundation of the war (2.409). The governor of Syria, Cestius Gallus, was routed at Beth Horon. Nero gave the war to Vespasian, who with his son Titus reduced Galilee in 67. At Jotapata the Galilean commander, the priest Joseph son of Matthias, surrendered and told Vespasian he would be emperor (3.399–408; Suetonius, Vespasian 5.6).

Titus besieged Jerusalem from the spring of 70 with four legions; the Temple burned in August and the upper city fell in September. Josephus gives 1,100,000 dead in the siege and 97,000 captives in the whole war (Jewish War 6.420), Tacitus 600,000 besieged (Histories 5.13): both more than the city could have held. Masada fell in 73 or 74 (Jewish War 7.252–406).

Josephus is almost the only narrative. Freed, he took the name Flavius and lived on a pension in Vespasian's former house (Life 423), and Titus signed the copies of the War for publication (Life 363). In his account the revolt is the work of brigands and tyrants, not of the nation or its priestly elite, and God has gone over to Rome. Tessa Rajak (Josephus: The Historian and His Society, 1983) reads him as the voice of that elite rather than a mere Flavian mouthpiece; Steve Mason stresses the irony and rhetoric of a work shaped for Roman readers.

Where sources disagree. Josephus has Titus resolve in council to spare the Temple and a soldier throw a firebrand unordered (Jewish War 6.236–266). Sulpicius Severus, about 400, has Titus urge its destruction to uproot the religion of Jews and Christians alike (Chronicle 2.30); Jacob Bernays (1861) argued that Severus drew on the lost part of Tacitus' Histories. T. D. Barnes ("The Fragments of Tacitus' Histories", 1977) accepted the passage as Tacitean; whether it records Titus' decision or Tacitus' reading of it is still argued.

In 71 Vespasian and Titus triumphed with the Temple's golden table and lampstand; Simon son of Gioras, a leader of the defence, was executed (Jewish War 7.123–157), and the vessels went into the new Temple of Peace (7.158–162). An arch in the Circus Maximus, of 80–81, claimed that Titus destroyed Jerusalem, which all before him had attacked in vain or never attempted (CIL VI 944): Pompey had taken it in 63 BC. The surviving Arch of Titus on the Sacred Way, dedicated to the deified Titus (CIL VI 945), shows the spoils carried in triumph and Titus crowned by Victory; module 10.3 reads it as art. Géza Alföldy's reconstruction (1995) of the Colosseum's dedication, from the dowel holes of its lost bronze letters, has it built from spoils (ex manubis), presumably this war's. The Temple tax diverted to Jupiter Capitolinus is module 2.5's.

Vespasian and Titus

An unexpected new emperor, Suetonius says, lacked authority and majesty, and they came to Vespasian at Alexandria when he healed a blind man and a lame man (Vespasian 7.2–3; Tacitus, Histories 4.81). In Rome he said forty thousand million sesterces were needed to set the state on its feet (Vespasian 16.3), raised taxes, and told Titus that money from the tax on urine did not smell (23.3). As censor in 73–74 he refilled the senatorial and equestrian orders from Italy and the provinces, and he gave Latin rights to all Spain (Pliny, Natural History 3.30), whose charters module 7.5 reads. Helvidius Priscus, who would not treat him as more than a private citizen, was exiled and killed (Vespasian 15), and philosophers were expelled (Dio 66.13); deep dive modules 13.4 and 13.6 take both further. His sons would succeed him, he told the Senate, or no one (Vespasian 25). He died in June 79, the first emperor followed by his own son.

Titus, his father's praetorian prefect, had been feared as another Nero and loved Queen Berenice, sister of Agrippa II, who lived with him on the Palatine from 75 (Dio 66.15). As emperor he sent her away, "he unwilling, she unwilling" (Suetonius, Titus 7.2), and ruled so mildly that Suetonius calls him the love and delight of the human race (1).

Within months Vesuvius buried Pompeii, Herculaneum and Stabiae. Pliny the Younger wrote two letters for Tacitus' Histories (Letters 6.16.1). In 6.16 his uncle, the elder Pliny, commander of the fleet at Misenum, sees a cloud shaped like an umbrella pine, turns a scholar's outing into a rescue, saying fortune favours the brave (6.16.11), and dies on the shore at Stabiae, his body later found intact (6.16.20). In 6.20 the seventeen-year-old nephew reads Livy at Misenum (6.20.5), then flees with his mother through a darkness like a closed room with the lamp out, among people who thought it the world's last, eternal night (6.20.14–15).

Where sources disagree. The oldest manuscript of Letters 6.16, the ninth-century Medicean, gives the ninth day before the Kalends of September, 24 August; other copies point to November. Autumn fruit and braziers at Pompeii, and a charcoal graffito found in 2018 dated 17 October without a year, have persuaded many to prefer 24 October. A coin naming Titus imperator for the fifteenth time is disputed evidence.

In 80 Titus dedicated the amphitheatre his father had begun on the drained lake of Nero's Golden House (Martial, On the Spectacles 2), with a hundred days of games in which Dio counts nine thousand animals killed (66.25); the games are module 9.4's. Titus died in September 81, aged forty-one.

Domitian and the senatorial memory of terror

Domitian reigned fifteen years, longer than anyone since Tiberius. From 85 he was censor for life and used the office on the Senate and on morals; module 2.3 tells how the chief Vestal Cornelia was buried alive. He raised legionary pay by a third, the first rise since Caesar's (Suetonius, Domitian 7.3; module 6.4). After a triumph over the Chatti in 83, two commanders died in Dacia (Domitian 6.1), and the peace of 89 paid King Decebalus subsidies. In January 89 Antonius Saturninus, governor of Upper Germany, rebelled at Mainz and was crushed within weeks.

The senatorial memory concentrates on 93–96. After Agricola's death, Tacitus writes, soldiers besieged the Senate house; Arulenus Rusticus and Herennius Senecio were killed for praising Thrasea and Helvidius, their books burned, and philosophers expelled (Agricola 2, 45; deep dive modules 13.4 and 13.6). Tacitus confesses complicity, "our hands dragged Helvidius to prison" (45.1), and calls the reign fifteen years of silence (2.3–3.2). Suetonius has Domitian complain that no one believes in a conspiracy against an emperor until he is killed (Domitian 21) and dictate a letter as "our lord and god" (13.2); Juvenal has Rome slave to a bald Nero (4.38). After he executed his cousin Flavius Clemens in 95 (15.1), Stephanus, steward of Clemens' widow Domitilla, stabbed him on 18 September 96 in a plot of palace staff (17). The Senate erased his name, while the soldiers wanted him deified (23.1). Module 7.3 begins with Nerva.

Agricola in Britain

Gnaeus Julius Agricola, from Forum Iulii, had served in Britain in the year of Boudica (module 7.1) and governed it for seven seasons; Tacitus, his son-in-law, wrote his life in 98. Agricola took Anglesey (Agricola 18), reached the Tay and garrisoned the Forth–Clyde isthmus (22–23), and thought Ireland could be held with one legion and some auxiliaries (24.3). In the last season he beat the Caledonians at Mons Graupius, where Tacitus gives their leader Calgacus the charge that Romans make a desolation and call it peace (30) and counts ten thousand enemy dead to 360 Roman (37.6). The fleet then rounded the north and proved Britain an island (38.4).

Domitian recalled him with honours and coldness, from envy, Tacitus says (39). Within a few years the north was given up, the legionary fortress at Inchtuthil dismantled unfinished and a legion sent to the Danube: Britain conquered and at once let go (Histories 1.2).

Where sources disagree. Tacitus, the son-in-law, makes the recall Domitian's envy (Agricola 39–42). Yet seven seasons was already a long command, and the withdrawal from the north followed the Dacian disasters Suetonius records (Domitian 6.1), so policy may explain what Tacitus calls envy. The governorship is dated 77–83 or 78–84, and Mons Graupius has no secure site.

Rehabilitating Domitian

The hostile portrait was written after 96 by men who had prospered under him. Tacitus owed his career to all three Flavians, Domitian most (Histories 1.1); Pliny held office to the end, and his Panegyricus praises Trajan by blackening his predecessor. Suetonius concedes that magistrates and governors were never more restrained or just (Domitian 8.2). The physical record is of a builder: the Capitol, the Palatine palace, the stadium preserved as Piazza Navona. Ian Carradice (Coinage and Finances in the Reign of Domitian, 1983) found the denarius' silver raised in 82 and lowered in 85: finance strained but managed.

Where sources disagree. Stéphane Gsell's Essai sur le règne de l'empereur Domitien (1894) began the reassessment, and K. H. Waters ("The Character of Domitian", Phoenix 1964) sifted the hostile stories for a credible character. Brian Jones (The Emperor Domitian, 1992) presents a benevolent despot whose personal monarchy met no widespread discontent for most of the reign. Pat Southern (Domitian: Tragic Tyrant, 1997) strips away the sensational detail but holds that the suffering he caused was real and must be explained. Sceptics add that good administration and terror are compatible.

The Flavian poets

Module 10.2 takes them as literature. Valerius Flaccus' unfinished Argonautica hails Titus black with the dust of Jerusalem (1.12–14); Quintilian lamented his recent death (10.1.90). Silius Italicus, consul in 68, wrote the Punica, seventeen books on the Second Punic War, the longest surviving Latin poem; he kept Virgil's tomb as a shrine and starved himself to death at seventy-five (Pliny, Letters 3.7). Statius' Thebaid ends by telling itself to follow the Aeneid at a distance (12.816–817), and his Silvae include Domitian's equestrian colossus (1.1). Martial wrote of an edict of "our lord and god" (5.8) and, after 96, that he would call no one that (10.72). The court had a culture as well as a terror.

Self-test

  1. What did Tacitus mean by the "secret of empire" (Histories 1.4), and how did the emperors of 69 illustrate it?
    Answer

    That an emperor could be made elsewhere than at Rome, by a provincial army. Galba was proclaimed in Spain, Vitellius on the Rhine and Vespasian in Alexandria and Judaea; only Otho was made in the city, by the praetorians who killed Galba.

  2. Why did Galba fall so quickly?
    Answer

    He refused the promised donative, saying he chose soldiers and did not buy them (Histories 1.5), and lost the German legions on 1–2 January 69. His adoption of Piso passed over Otho, who bought the guard; Galba was killed on 15 January (1.41).

  3. Who burned the Capitol in December 69?
    Answer

    Uncertain. Tacitus gives both versions and calls the commoner one the besieged Flavians firing the buildings to repel the attack (Histories 3.71); Suetonius blames Vitellius' men (Vitellius 15.3). Tacitus calls it the worst crime since the city's foundation (3.72).

  4. How does the law on Vespasian's powers both confirm and qualify the idea of the Principate as a bundle of voted powers?
    Answer

    It lists the powers with Augustan, Tiberian and Claudian precedents, voted by Senate and people (ILS 244; Histories 4.3). But its last clause validates everything done before the vote, and Vespasian dated his reign from the army's oath of 1 July (Suetonius, Vespasian 6.3): the legal grant ratified the soldiers' choice.

  5. Why must Josephus' account of the Temple's destruction be read with caution, and what is the alternative version?
    Answer

    He was a surrendered rebel general living on Flavian patronage, and Titus approved the publication (Life 363). He has Titus try to save the Temple (Jewish War 6.236–266); Sulpicius Severus, probably following Tacitus as Bernays argued, has Titus urge its destruction (Chronicle 2.30).

  6. Name three ways the Flavians turned the Jewish War into legitimacy at Rome.
    Answer

    Any three of: the joint triumph of 71 with the Temple spoils (Jewish War 7.123–157); the Temple of Peace housing the vessels; the Circus Maximus arch claiming Jerusalem never taken before (CIL VI 944); the Arch of Titus (CIL VI 945); the Colosseum built from spoils, on Alföldy's reconstruction.

  7. What do Pliny's Letters 6.16 and 6.20 describe, and why is the date of the eruption disputed?
    Answer

    6.16 tells the elder Pliny's rescue voyage and death at Stabiae; 6.20 the nephew's own flight from Misenum in darkness. The oldest manuscript gives 24 August, but other copies, autumn finds and a charcoal graffito of 17 October found in 2018 favour 24 October.

  8. What did Agricola achieve in Britain, and why did it not last?
    Answer

    Anglesey, an advance to the Tay, a garrisoned Forth–Clyde line, victory at Mons Graupius and the circumnavigation (Agricola 18, 22–23, 29–38). After his recall the north was abandoned, Inchtuthil dismantled and a legion sent to the Danube crisis: conquered and at once let go (Histories 1.2).

  9. On what grounds have modern historians rehabilitated Domitian's government, and what is the objection?
    Answer

    The hostile writers served him and wrote after 96 (Histories 1.1); Suetonius admits his governors were never more just (Domitian 8.2); building and coinage show competent rule (Carradice; Jones' benevolent despot). The objection is that efficient administration and terror against the Senate can coexist.

  10. What do the Flavian poets show about the regime, and how did Martial's position change after 96?
    Answer

    Valerius Flaccus, Silius, Statius and Martial show a court culture of epic and panegyric around the dynasty, from Titus at Jerusalem to Domitian's colossus (Silvae 1.1). Martial, who had written of "our lord and god" (5.8), declared after 96 that he would call no one that (10.72).

Carry forward

  1. The year 69 exposed the Principate's base: provincial armies chose emperors, and the Senate and people then voted the powers, as the law on Vespasian's powers did, retroactively. Every accession by force afterwards, 193 above all, replays this sequence.
  2. A new family could manufacture legitimacy: the Flavians built theirs from a foreign victory turned into triumph, arches, temples and an amphitheatre, from miracles and a restored Capitol, and from succession by blood sons, which failed when Domitian left no heir.
  3. The "bad emperor" is largely a senatorial verdict written after the fact by men who had served him: read Tacitus, Pliny and Suetonius on Domitian as evidence for the Senate's relationship with the princeps, and test it against inscriptions, coins, buildings and the provinces.
