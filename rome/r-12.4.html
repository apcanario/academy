01Dido before book 4

Module 4.1 has the queen Virgil inherited: Timaeus' Elissa, who leaps into a pyre rather than marry a Libyan king; Trogus' chaste widow in Justin's epitome; Varro's Anna, who died for love of Aeneas; and the ancients' own knowledge that the meeting was impossible. Virgil puts Trogus' story almost whole into Venus' account in book 1 (1.340–368), as module 4.1 shows, and the welcome, the banquet and the pictures of Troy in Juno's temple belong to deep dive module 12.2. Book 4 is where Virgil turns the widow who would not remarry into a woman who breaks her vow for a Trojan. The reading of books 2, 4 and 6 to Augustus and Octavia is deep dive module 12.1's.

02From Anna's advice to the cave

The book opens on a wound: the queen, long since hurt by a heavy care, feeds it in her veins and is consumed by a hidden fire (4.1–2). That night she confides in her sister. She admits that she recognises "the traces of the old flame" (agnosco veteris vestigia flammae, 4.23), and prays that the earth may swallow her before she violates Shame: the man who first joined her to himself has taken her love, and let him keep it in the grave (4.24–29). The vow to Sychaeus is stated so that the reader watches it break.

Anna's answer (4.31–53) is the hinge, and it argues on two levels. On the personal one: will Dido grieve alone through her youth and never know sweet children or the rewards of love? Does she think ashes or buried shades care (4.32–34)? On the political one: Dido has refused Iarbas and the African chiefs, she is ringed by Gaetulians, Numidians and the Syrtes, and her brother threatens war from Tyre (4.35–44); the gods brought the Trojan fleet, and with such a marriage, sister, think what a city and kingdom will rise, how far Punic glory will reach on Trojan arms (4.45–49). Virgil's verdict is one line: with these words she set her mind on fire, gave hope to her doubt and loosed her shame (4.54–55). The sacrifices that follow are useless; Dido wanders the city like a deer carrying an arrow the shepherd who shot it does not know he left (4.68–73), the building of Carthage stops, the cranes hang idle (4.86–89).

Then the goddesses bargain (4.90–128). Juno proposes a marriage that will keep the Trojans from Italy and let Dido serve a Phrygian husband (4.103); Venus, seeing through her, agrees and smiles at the trick. The scenes are given here; why each goddess acts is deep dive module 12.6's. Juno's plan is exact: a storm will break up the hunt, Dido and the Trojan leader will come to the same cave, and "I will join them in lasting marriage and make her his own; this will be their wedding" (4.126–127).

The hunt is the book's one bright scene, Dido in a Sidonian cloak, Aeneas as Apollo striding the ridges of Delos (4.136–150). The storm comes and the plan is carried out in almost Juno's words. Dido and the Trojan leader reach the same cave; Earth and Juno as matron of honour give the signal, the lightning flashes and the sky is witness to the union, the nymphs wail on the mountain top (4.165–168). Every element of a Roman wedding is there in a distorted form: the bride's escort, the torches, the witnesses, the cry. Then the narrator: that day was the first cause of death and of disaster, for Dido no longer cares for appearances or reputation, and no longer thinks of a secret love: "she calls it marriage, and with that name she covers her fault" (coniugium vocat, hoc praetexit nomine culpam, 4.172).

03Rumour, Iarbas and Mercury

Rumour (Fama) is at once a personification and a monster: the last child of Earth, sister of the Giants Coeus and Enceladus, with as many eyes, tongues, mouths and ears as feathers, tiny at first and then walking on the ground with her head among the clouds, clinging as fast to the false as she announces the true (4.173–188). Her report is half right: that Aeneas has come, that Dido has seen fit to join herself to him, and that they spend the winter in luxury, forgetful of their kingdoms, captive to shameful desire (4.191–194).

She takes it to Iarbas, son of Hammon by a Garamantian nymph (4.198), whose prayer to his father Jupiter supplies the hostile reading (4.206–218). A woman wandering in our lands, he says, founded a little city for a price, and refused my hand; now "that Paris with his half-man retinue" (ille Paris cum semiviro comitatu, 4.215), his hair oiled under a Lydian cap, enjoys his prize. The insult comes from Trogus' Iarbas, whom module 4.1 traces, turned from a suitor who forces a widow's death into a rival who mocks her lover.

Jupiter hears and sends Mercury: this is not the man his mother promised, the one to rule Italy and send the line of Teucer on to bring the world under laws; let him sail, that is the whole of it (4.223–237). Mercury finds Aeneas laying out citadels and new houses, with a sword starred with yellow jasper at his side and a cloak of Tyrian purple from his shoulders, Dido's gifts woven with gold thread by her own hand (4.259–264). The god's rebuke turns on one word: "are you now laying the foundations of lofty Carthage, and building a fine city like a wife's man (uxorius), forgetful of your own kingdom?" (4.265–267). If his own glory does not move him, let him think of Ascanius, to whom the kingdom of Italy and the Roman land are owed (4.272–276).

04The confrontation

Aeneas burns to go and is at a loss how to approach a queen so far gone; he orders Mnestheus, Sergestus and Serestus to ready the fleet in silence while he looks for the gentlest moment to speak (4.279–295). Who can deceive a lover? Dido senses the plot, and Rumour, again, tells her the ships are being fitted; she rages through the city like a Bacchant at the festival of Bacchus (4.296–303).

Three speeches carry the confrontation (4.305–387). Her first (4.305–330) is an appeal, not yet a curse. Did you hope, traitor (perfide), to hide so great a wrong and leave my land in silence? Does nothing hold you, "not our love, nor the right hand once given, nor Dido about to die a cruel death" (4.307–308)? She pleads by their marriage and the wedding begun (per conubia nostra, per inceptos hymenaeos, 4.316); for him the Libyan tribes hate her, for him her shame is lost; to whom does he leave her, "guest, since that name alone is left from husband" (4.323–324)? If only she had a little Aeneas playing in her hall to recall his face (4.328–329).

His reply (4.331–361) begins with his eyes held still at Jupiter's warning and the care pressed down in his heart (4.331–332). He will never deny what she deserves, and he will remember Elissa as long as he remembers himself (4.333–336). Then the legal point: he never held out the torches of a husband, nor came to such a compact (nec coniugis umquam / praetendi taedas aut haec in foedera veni, 4.338–339). Left to himself he would be rebuilding Troy; but Apollo and the Lycian oracles order Italy, "this is my love, this my homeland" (hic amor, haec patria est, 4.347). Anchises' troubled ghost warns him in dreams, Ascanius is being cheated, and the messenger of the gods came in daylight. He ends: stop inflaming yourself and me with your complaints; "I follow Italy not of my own will" (Italiam non sponte sequor, 4.361).

Dido's second speech (4.362–387) answers the divine argument with contempt. No goddess was his mother, no Dardanus his ancestor: the Caucasus bore him on its rocks and Hyrcanian tigresses nursed him (4.365–367), the charge Patroclus brings against Achilles (Iliad 16.33–35) and Catullus' Ariadne against Theseus (64.154–157). Did he groan at my weeping? Did he turn his eyes? Nowhere is faith safe (nusquam tuta fides, 4.373). Then the turn: go, seek Italy on the winds; I hope you drink your punishment on the rocks; absent, I will follow with black fires, and when cold death parts my soul from my limbs, my shade will be with you everywhere (4.381–386). She faints and is carried to her room. The next line is "But pious Aeneas" (at pius Aeneas, 4.393), groaning, shaken by great love, obeying the gods.

A last embassy follows (4.416–449). Dido sends Anna: she did not swear at Aulis with the Greeks to destroy the Trojans; she asks only an empty time, a respite for her madness (4.425–434). He is moved by no tears. Like an old Alpine oak that the north winds tear at, he is battered on every side and feels the care in his great heart, but "the mind stays unmoved, the tears roll in vain" (mens immota manet, lacrimae volvuntur inanes, 4.449).

05The pyre and the curse

Dido now prays for death. The omens close in: libations turn to blood, Sychaeus' voice calls from his marble shrine at night, an owl wails on the roof, and in dreams she is hunted by Aeneas or left alone on an endless road, like Pentheus or Orestes on the stage (4.450–473; deep dive module 12.1 has the tragic simile). She deceives Anna with the story of a Massylian priestess who can free her from love, and has a pyre built in the inner court, with Aeneas' arms, his clothes and "the marriage bed on which I perished" laid upon it (4.474–498). The night monologue (4.534–552) weighs and rejects the alternatives, to marry Iarbas or follow the Trojans, and ends in self-accusation: she was not allowed to live a life without marriage and without blame, like a wild creature; the faith promised to Sychaeus' ashes has not been kept (4.550–552). Mercury comes again to Aeneas in sleep with the warning that woman is a thing always various and changeable (varium et mutabile semper / femina, 4.569–570), and the Trojans cut their cables before dawn.

At first light Dido sees the empty harbour. She tears her hair and turns on herself: unhappy Dido, do impious deeds touch you now? That was the time, when you gave him the sceptre; "behold the right hand and the faith" (4.596–597). She might have torn him apart, scattered him on the waves, served Ascanius at his father's table (4.600–602), the crimes of Medea and Atreus. Then the curse (4.607–629).

exoriare aliquis nostris ex ossibus ultor (4.625)

It is built as a prayer in due form. She invokes the Sun, Juno "witness" (conscia, 4.608, the word the cave scene used of the sky), Hecate, the avenging Furies and "the gods of dying Elissa" (4.610), naming herself by her Tyrian name. The first half (4.612–620) concedes what she cannot prevent: if he must reach harbour, if the fates of Jupiter require it, then let him be harried by the war of a bold people, driven from his lands, torn from the embrace of Iulus, begging for help and seeing the undeserved deaths of his own; when he has yielded to the terms of an unequal peace, may he not enjoy his kingdom or the light he longs for, but fall before his day and lie unburied in the middle of the sand. The poem's second half fulfils it clause by clause. The Rutulian war fills books 7 to 12; Aeneas leaves Ascanius to ask help of Evander and the Etruscans in book 8; Pallas dies in book 10; Juno's terms in book 12 make the Trojans give up their name to the Latins (12.819–840). The last clause reaches past the poem. Jupiter gives Aeneas three years' rule in Latium (1.265–266), and the tradition module 1.3 sets out has him vanish in battle by the Numicus with no body found (Dionysius of Halicarnassus 1.64.4–5). A man unburied on the sand is also how book 2 left Priam, a headless trunk on the shore (2.557–558).

The second half (4.622–629) turns from Aeneas to the future. The Tyrians are to harry his whole race with hatred and send this as an offering to her ashes: "let there be no love between the peoples, and no treaties" (nullus amor populis nec foedera sunto, 4.624), in the future imperative of Roman law and treaty. Then the address to someone not yet born: arise, some avenger, from my bones, to pursue the Dardan settlers with fire and sword, now, later, whenever strength allows (4.625–627). The singular aliquis with a second-person verb names no one, and no Roman reader needed the name. The last two lines pile up opposed nouns, shore against shore, waves against waves, arms against arms, "let them fight, they and their grandsons" (4.628–629), and the final line is hypermetric: its last syllable is one too many and runs on into the next line, as the war it calls for runs on past the poem. Module 4.1 shows that Rome and Carthage were treaty partners for more than two centuries before 264; Dido's curse rewrites that history as a war decreed at the foundation, and makes Hannibal a Trojan inheritance. Silius Italicus took her at her word, with Juno driving Hannibal to avenge Dido (module 10.2).

06The death, Iris and the lock

The death scene runs from 4.630 to 4.692. Dido sends Barce, Sychaeus' old nurse, to fetch Anna, climbs the pyre, "pale with death to come" (pallida morte futura, 4.644), and unsheathes the Dardan sword, a gift not sought for such a use (4.646–647). Over his clothes and their bed she speaks her epitaph: I have lived, and run the course that Fortune gave me, and now a great image of me will go beneath the earth. I founded a famous city, I saw my walls, I avenged my husband and punished my brother: happy, too happy, if only the Dardan keels had never touched our shores (4.653–658). We shall die unavenged, but let us die: "thus, thus it pleases me to go beneath the shades" (sic, sic iuvat ire sub umbras, 4.660); let the cruel Dardan drink in this fire with his eyes from the sea and carry the omen of my death with him (4.661–662). She falls on the sword. The palace's cry is compared to the wailing if Carthage or ancient Tyre were falling to an enemy, with flames rolling over the roofs of men and gods (4.669–671): a Roman reader heard the sack of 146 (module 4.4) in the simile. Anna's lament asks whether this was what the pyre and the altars were for, and whether her sister meant to deceive her (4.675–685).

Dido cannot die (4.693–705). Juno, pitying the long agony, sends Iris to release the struggling soul, "for since she perished neither by fate nor by a death she had earned, but wretchedly, before her day, set ablaze by sudden madness, Proserpina had not yet taken a golden lock from her head" (4.696–699). Iris cuts it: "I carry this, as I am bidden, sacred to Dis, and I release you from that body"; all warmth slips away and life withdraws into the winds (4.702–705). Book 5 opens with Aeneas at sea looking back at walls lit by a fire whose cause he does not know (5.1–7).

Where sources disagree. Macrobius (Saturnalia 5.19) reports that Cornutus, Persius' teacher, called the lock a poet's invention, like the golden bough. Macrobius answers that Virgil had a source: Euripides' Alcestis, where Death comes on stage with a sword to cut the hair of Alcestis and so consecrate her to the gods below. The narrator's line on Dido's death, neither fated nor deserved (4.696), is the poem's own verdict, and it sits against the "fault" of 4.172.

07The meeting in the underworld

In the Fields of Mourning, among the women killed by love, Phoenician Dido, her wound still fresh, wanders in a great wood (6.450–451). Aeneas makes her out dimly through the shadows, as a man sees or thinks he sees the new moon through clouds at the month's beginning (6.453–454), and weeps, and speaks with sweet love. So the news was true, that she had died by the sword: "was I, alas, the cause of your death?" He swears by the stars and by whatever faith there is below the earth, "against my will, queen, I left your shore" (invitus, regina, tuo de litore cessi, 6.460), the line deep dive module 12.1 traces to the lock of Berenice in Catullus 66.39. The gods' commands drove him; he could not believe his going would bring her such grief. Stay, do not withdraw from my sight; whom are you fleeing? This is the last word fate allows me to speak to you (6.456–466).

She says nothing. She keeps her eyes fixed on the ground, turned away, her face no more moved by his words than hard flint or a crag of Marpessan marble; then she flees, hostile, into the shadowy grove, where Sychaeus, her first husband, answers her cares and returns her love (6.469–474). Aeneas follows her with tears from far off (6.475–476). The scene reverses book 4: there his eyes were held still (4.331) and the tears were in vain (4.449); here she is the stone. The line for her averted eyes (6.469) is used in book 1 of Pallas refusing the Trojan women's prayer, in the pictures in Juno's temple (1.482). The model is Ajax in Odyssey 11, who will not answer Odysseus and walks away into Erebus (Greek module 4.4). T. S. Eliot, in his address What is a Classic? (1944), called it "perhaps the most telling snub in all poetry". The rest of book 6 is deep dive module 12.5's.

08The questions

Was there a marriage?

The poem gives the relationship a different name in every mouth. Juno promises lasting marriage and a wedding (4.126–127); the cave stages a wedding with nature as officiants (4.165–168); the narrator says Dido calls it marriage to cover a fault (4.172); Rumour reports a union (4.191–194); Iarbas sees Paris and his plunder (4.215); Mercury calls Aeneas a wife's man (4.266); Dido appeals to "our marriage" and a wedding begun (4.316), and then concedes that only "guest" is left of "husband" (4.324); Aeneas denies that he ever held the torches or entered such a compact (4.338–339). D. C. Feeney ("The Taciturnity of Aeneas", Classical Quarterly, 1983) made this disagreement among the characters the starting point for reading Aeneas' speech, and Richard Monti (The Dido Episode and the Aeneid, 1981) read the whole episode through the Roman language of fides, the pledged right hand and dynastic marriage.

Roman law sharpens the question rather than settling it. A Roman marriage needed no priest or document; it rested on the consent of two people with the capacity to marry who meant to be married (module 9.3). That makes the absence of torches and witnesses irrelevant and intention everything, and the poem's point is that the two intentions differ. Dido's inceptos, "begun", concedes the ceremony was never completed.

Where sources disagree. Ovid, defending his own love poetry, says that no part of the Aeneid is read more than "love joined by no lawful bond" (Tristia 2.533–536), taking Aeneas' side of the legal point. Dante, in the Monarchia (2.3), counts Dido as the second of Aeneas' three wives, Creusa from Asia, Dido from Africa, Lavinia from Europe, quoting 4.171–172 as proof of his hero's nobility, though the same lines call it a fault. Feeney (1983) takes the disagreement itself as Virgil's design rather than a puzzle to be solved.

Is Aeneas at fault?

The case against him is Dido's: he prepared the fleet in secret (4.289–295), his reply is cold and legalistic, and he never says plainly that he loves her. The case for him is the gods': Mercury's two visits, Anchises' ghost, Ascanius' inheritance, and the narrator's insistence on the cost to him, the care pressed down (4.332), "groaning much and shaken in mind by great love" (4.395), the oak that feels the storm in its great heart (4.448). The word pius at 4.393, set directly after Dido's collapse, can be read as the poem's endorsement or as its irony. So can 4.449: if the unmoved mind is Aeneas' and the tears are his, the line shows a man mastering grief; if the tears are Dido's and Anna's, it shows their pleading wasted on a wall.

Where sources disagree. Augustine (City of God 9.4) quotes 4.449 as the picture of a steadfast mind that feels emotion without yielding to it, and so gives the mind and the tears to Aeneas; deep dive module 12.6 sets the line among the poem's Stoic readings. Ovid's Dido (Heroides 7) reads the departure as plain desertion and ends with an epitaph: Aeneas supplied both the cause of death and the sword, Dido fell by her own hand (7.195–196). The modern form of this quarrel, between Augustan and pessimistic readers, is deep dive module 12.7's.

How far is Dido Cleopatra, and Carthage Egypt?

The poem was written in the decade after Actium, and its first readers had seen an eastern queen and a Roman general who forgot his country. The parallels are verbal as well as situational. Dido on the pyre is "pale with death to come" (pallida morte futura, 4.644); Cleopatra on Aeneas' shield is "pale with death to come" amid the slaughter (pallentem morte futura, 8.709), the scene module 5.5 reads. Aeneas in Tyrian purple, building a foreign city, called uxorius by a god (4.261–266), wears Antony's charges; Iarbas' "half-man retinue" (4.215) recalls the wrinkled eunuchs of Horace's ninth Epode (module 5.5) and the queen's "contaminated herd" of diseased men in Odes 1.37.9–10; Rumour's lovers forgetful of their kingdoms (4.194) recall Antony and Cleopatra wintering in Alexandria. Dante set the two queens side by side among the lustful (Inferno 5.61–63).

Against that, Dido is everything Cleopatra was said not to be: a founder and lawgiver, a widow faithful until gods intervened, admired by the narrator, dead by the sword on a pyre rather than by a snake. Carthage is a closer fit than Cleopatra. The sack-simile (4.669–671) and the curse look to 146, and while Virgil wrote, Rome was refounding Carthage as a colony, the plan Caesar made and Augustus carried out (Appian, Punica 136); Mercury finds Aeneas laying out Carthage's citadel (4.260).

Where sources disagree. A. S. Pease's commentary on book 4 (1935) detailed the parallels; Janice Benario ("Dido and Cleopatra", Vergilius, 1970) traced them to Horace's Cleopatra of Odes 1.37 as well as the historical queen, and Karl Galinsky (2003) argued that Horace's Cleopatra shapes Dido more than the historical one does. Antonio La Penna, in the Enciclopedia Virgiliana (1985), judged that Dido resembles Cleopatra very little, beyond Rumour's report and Mercury's rebuke, where a reader could think of Antony. Whether the resemblance makes Dido a warning or makes Cleopatra tragic is the political question deep dive module 12.7 takes up.

Dido returns twice in the Iliadic half: Ascanius promises Nisus an ancient mixing bowl that Sidonian Dido gave (9.266), and Aeneas dresses the dead Pallas in one of two robes that Sidonian Dido once wove for him with her own hands (11.72–75).

09Dido after Virgil

Ovid. Ovid answered book 4 three times: in Dido's letter, Heroides 7, the poem reread by its victim, opening with the swan singing at the Maeander, calling Aeneas a liar who never carried his gods or his father (7.79–82), hinting that she may be carrying his child (7.133–134), and ending with her epitaph; in the four lines the Metamorphoses give her (14.78–81); and in the Fasti, where Anna flees to Italy and becomes Anna Perenna (3.523–696). Module 10.1 places all three in Ovid's work, and module 10.2 traces Dido into Valerius Flaccus' Medea and Silius' Hannibal.

Augustine. The Confessions (about 397–400) make Dido the emblem of a misdirected schooling. As a schoolboy in Africa, Augustine was made to learn "the wanderings of some Aeneas", forgetting his own, and to weep for dead Dido "because she killed herself for love" (plorare Didonem mortuam, quia se occidit ab amore), while he watched dry-eyed his own soul dying away from God (1.13.20–21). The wooden horse, burning Troy and the shade of Creusa were, he says, the sweetest of spectacles to his vanity (1.13.22). His own departure from Carthage for Rome, deceiving his mother Monica and sailing by night while she prayed at the shrine of Cyprian (5.8.15), is told as Aeneas' flight with the roles changed.

Dante. In the second circle of the Inferno, written in the early fourteenth century, among the souls of the lustful whirled by the wind, Virgil points out "the one who killed herself for love and broke faith with the ashes of Sychaeus" (colei che s'ancise amorosa, / e ruppe fede al cener di Sicheo, 5.61–62), and next to her lustful Cleopatra. Francesca and Paolo come "from the troop where Dido is" (5.85). Dante damns her for the broken vow to Sychaeus, Virgil's own charge at 4.552, not for a false marriage: in the Monarchia she is Aeneas' wife.

Marlowe. The Tragedy of Dido, Queen of Carthage, printed in 1594 with Marlowe and Thomas Nashe named on the title page and played by the boy actors of the Children of the Chapel, was probably written between 1587 and 1593. It opens with Jupiter dandling Ganymede, keeps Cupid in Ascanius' shape from Aeneid 1, and gives Anna a love of her own, for Iarbas. Dido dies on the pyre speaking Virgil's Latin, sic, sic iuvat ire sub umbras, and the play then outdoes the poem: Iarbas kills himself for Dido, and Anna for Iarbas.

Purcell. Dido and Aeneas, with a libretto by Nahum Tate adapted from his own play Brutus of Alba (1678), is known from a printed libretto for a performance by the girls of Josias Priest's boarding school in Chelsea, usually dated 1689, though a letter of 1688 already links the opera to the school; no score in Purcell's hand survives, and the earliest copy is from the eighteenth century. Tate removes the gods. A Sorceress and her witches plot Carthage's ruin and send a spirit in the form of Mercury; Aeneas, confronted, offers to stay, and Dido refuses him: "For 'tis enough, whate'er you now decree, / That you had once a thought of leaving me." There is no pyre, no sword and no curse. Dido's lament over a descending ground bass asks the opposite of Virgil's queen: "Remember me, but ah! forget my fate."

Where sources disagree. The traditional account, built on the printed libretto for Priest's school, makes the opera a school piece of 1689, a loyal entertainment for the new reign of William and Mary. Andrew Pinnock and Bruce Wood ("'Unscarr'd by turning times'?: the dating of Purcell's Dido and Aeneas", Early Music, 1992) argued that this account rests on weak evidence and that the opera began earlier as a court work, perhaps as early as 1684, with the Chelsea performance a revival. A letter of 1688 has since linked the opera to the school by that summer, before the change of reign, which undoes the loyal occasion on either view.

Berlioz. Berlioz wrote both words and music of Les Troyens, the libretto in 1856 and the score by 1858, in five acts that join book 2 to book 4. Only the Carthage acts were staged in his lifetime, cut, as Les Troyens à Carthage at the Théâtre-Lyrique in 1863; the whole was first staged at Karlsruhe in 1890 over two evenings under Felix Mottl. His Didon is a ruler first, at peace and building, before the love duet "Nuit d'ivresse". On the pyre, after the farewell "Adieu, fière cité", she has a vision of Hannibal avenging her, then of Rome triumphant, and dies with Carthage's destruction before her eyes: the curse made into prophecy. Deep dive module 12.8 sets Purcell and Berlioz in the poem's wider afterlife.

10Self-test

  1. What does Anna argue in 4.31–53, and why does it matter that her case is political as well as personal?
    Answer

    That Dido should not waste her youth on the dead, and that a Trojan marriage would protect a city ringed by hostile Africans and threatened by Tyre, and raise Punic glory (4.32–49). It makes Dido's choice a queen's as well as a woman's, which is why the poem can treat its failure as Carthage's.

  2. How does the cave scene (4.165–172) both stage and undercut a wedding?
    Answer

    Earth and Juno as matron of honour give the signal, lightning serves as torches, the sky is witness and the nymphs wail, all the elements of a Roman wedding in distorted form. The narrator then calls the day the first cause of death and says Dido calls it marriage to cover her fault (4.172).

  3. On what word does Mercury's rebuke turn, and what does it imply?
    Answer

    Uxorius, a wife's man (4.266): Aeneas, in Dido's purple and building Carthage, has forgotten his own kingdom and Ascanius' Italy. It treats the union as a marriage that unmans him.

  4. What is Aeneas' legal answer to Dido, and how does Roman law bear on it?
    Answer

    That he never held out a husband's torches or entered such a compact (4.338–339). Roman marriage rested on the intention of both parties, not on ceremony (module 9.3), so the absence of torches settles nothing; what matters is that the two intentions differed.

  5. Divide Dido's curse (4.607–629) into its two halves and say where each is fulfilled.
    Answer

    The first (4.612–620) curses Aeneas with war, separation from Iulus, the deaths of his own, an unequal peace and an early death unburied, fulfilled in books 7–12, Juno's terms (12.819–840) and the tradition of his vanishing by the Numicus. The second (4.622–629) curses his race with eternal enmity and calls up an avenger, Hannibal and the Punic wars.

  6. Name two formal features of the curse's last lines and what they do.
    Answer

    The future imperative sunto (4.624) speaks in the language of law and treaty, turning a lover's curse into a decree against all treaties. The last line piles up opposed nouns and is hypermetric, its final syllable running into the next line as the war runs past the poem (4.628–629).

  7. Why does Iris have to be sent, and how did Macrobius defend the detail of the lock?
    Answer

    Because Dido died neither by fate nor by a deserved death, so Proserpina had not cut the lock that releases the soul (4.696–699). Against Cornutus, who called it an invention, Macrobius (Saturnalia 5.19) cited Euripides' Alcestis, where Death cuts Alcestis' hair to consecrate her.

  8. How does the meeting at 6.450–476 reverse book 4?
    Answer

    Aeneas now weeps and pleads, swearing he left unwillingly (6.460), and Dido is the one unmoved, eyes fixed on the ground, silent as flint, before she returns to Sychaeus (6.469–474). In book 4 his eyes were held still (4.331) and the tears were in vain (4.449). The model is Ajax's silence in Odyssey 11.

  9. What verbal echo links Dido to Cleopatra, and what is the strongest objection to equating them?
    Answer

    Dido is pallida morte futura (4.644), Cleopatra on the shield pallentem morte futura (8.709). Dido is a founder, faithful until the gods intervene, admired by the narrator and dead by the sword, so La Penna judged the resemblance slight.

  10. How do Augustine, Dante and Tate each change the meaning of Dido's death?
    Answer

    Augustine makes his boyhood tears for her a sign of a soul weeping for fiction while dying to God (Confessions 1.13.20–21). Dante damns her among the lustful for breaking faith with Sychaeus (Inferno 5.61–62). Tate removes the gods, the pyre and the curse, so that Dido refuses an Aeneas who offers to stay and asks to be remembered with her fate forgotten.

11Carry forward

  1. Book 4 refuses to name what Dido and Aeneas were to each other: every speaker, the narrator included, gives the union a different name, and Roman marriage by consent makes the divergence of intention the whole question.
  2. Dido's curse turns a love story into Roman history: the founding queen decrees the Punic wars, the treaties of two centuries vanish, and Aeneas' own end is foretold, so the poem's second half and Rome's wars with Carthage are both read as the working out of her words.
  3. Every later Dido takes a side in the quarrel the poem leaves open: Ovid's deserted wife, Augustine's warning against fiction, Dante's broken vow, Marlowe's three suicides, Tate's queen who forgives, Berlioz's ruler who foresees Rome.
