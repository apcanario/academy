01Musonius Rufus: a knight under Nero

Gaius Musonius Rufus came from Volsinii in Etruria, the Suda says, and Tacitus calls him a man of equestrian rank (Histories 3.81). He taught in Greek, which is why his lectures survive in Greek. Module 13.2 follows the Roman Stoics who made the doctrine an ethic of office and role; Musonius made it a regimen of food, dress, work and marriage, and his pupil Epictetus made it a discipline of judgement.

He first appears beside Rubellius Plautus, a descendant of Tiberius whom Nero in 60 told to withdraw to his estates in Asia (Tacitus, Annals 14.22). When the killers came in 62, Tacitus says, Plautus' teachers, the Greek Coeranus and the Etruscan Musonius, had urged him to wait for death with constancy rather than live in uncertainty and fear (14.59). After the conspiracy of Piso in 65 Musonius was himself sent away:

Verginium Flavum et Musonium Rufum claritudo nominis expulit.

The fame of their names drove out Verginius Flavus and Musonius Rufus, Tacitus writes: Verginius fostered the young with eloquence, Musonius with the precepts of wisdom (Annals 15.71). He was not accused of a part in the plot; his reputation was the charge. He went to Gyaros, a bare island of the Cyclades that Tiberius had once refused as a place of exile because it had no water (Annals 4.30). Philostratus, writing in the third century, says that Musonius found a spring there, that Greeks sailed out to see him in exile and still sail out to see the spring, which they praise as loudly as the horse's spring on Helicon (Life of Apollonius 7.16). The same romance has Demetrius the Cynic find him chained among the convicts digging Nero's canal through the Isthmus of Corinth, begun in 67; Musonius drives his spade into the ground and asks whether Demetrius would rather see him playing the lyre like Nero (5.19). Both stories come from a novelistic life written some 150 years later, and the second was evidently popular: the dialogue Nero, transmitted among Lucian's works, also sets Musonius at the canal.

He was back in Rome after Nero's fall. In December 69, when Vitellius sent envoys to the Flavian army, Musonius went with them, walked among the soldiers and lectured them on the blessings of peace and the dangers of war; the soldiers jeered, and he abandoned what Tacitus calls his untimely wisdom (Histories 3.81). In 70 he prosecuted Publius Egnatius Celer, a Stoic who under Nero had given evidence against Barea Soranus, his patron (Annals 16.32), and won (Histories 4.10, 4.40); Demetrius, module 13.3's Cynic, defended Celer. Juvenal's unnamed old Stoic informer, who killed Barea though Barea was his friend and pupil, is usually taken to be Celer (3.116–117). When Vespasian expelled the philosophers from Rome, Dio's epitome says he expelled all of them except Musonius (Dio 65(66).13.2); module 13.4 tells the expulsion itself. Some modern accounts add a second exile late in Vespasian's reign and a recall by Titus; the evidence is thin. He was dead by the time Pliny, who admired him, wrote of his son-in-law Artemidorus (Letters 3.11).

02The lectures: what Lucius wrote and Stobaeus kept

Musonius wrote nothing. Twenty-one discourses survive, written up in Greek by a pupil called Lucius, otherwise unknown, and excerpted by Stobaeus in his Anthology of the early fifth century; Cora Lutz's edition with translation (Musonius Rufus: "The Roman Socrates", 1947) numbers them 1 to 21, as below, and a few sayings survive in Epictetus and Gellius. The pattern recurs: a question, often put by someone present, an argument from nature, a Greek example. The voice is Lucius' report, and the reader should hear it as such.

Women and philosophy (Lecture 3). Women have received from the gods the same reason as men, the same senses and the same natural inclination to virtue, so philosophy, which is training in virtue, belongs to them too. Musonius meets the objection that philosophical women will grow bold, neglect their homes and sit arguing with men: philosophy will make a woman a better manager of her household, chaste, self-controlled and just, and brave enough to defend her children.

Daughters and sons (Lecture 4). The virtues are the same in both sexes, and trainers of horses and dogs train females as they train males; so daughters should be educated as sons are. He allows a division of labour, the heavier work to men, spinning and indoor tasks to women, but adds that tasks can be exchanged and that all human work is common to both.

Sex and marriage (Lectures 12–15). Sex is just only within marriage and for children. A master who sleeps with his slave woman would not tolerate his wife sleeping with a male slave, and if men claim to be the stronger sex they must show more self-control, not less (12). Marriage exists for a shared life and for children, husband and wife holding everything in common, even their bodies (13A); choose a spouse for character, not family or wealth or looks (13B). Marriage is no hindrance to philosophy: Socrates and Crates married, and Crates shared the Cynic life with Hipparchia (14). Lecture 15 condemns the exposure of children, even by the rich who wish to keep their inheritance whole.

Food and simple living (Lectures 18–21). Self-control begins at table, because the pleasure of eating is met daily. Musonius preferred food needing no fire, fruit, vegetables, milk, cheese and honeycomb, and thought meat heavier and more fit for wild beasts, dulling the mind (18A); one should eat to live, not live to eat, as Socrates said (18B). One cloak is better than two and bare feet better than shoes (19); furniture should be plain (20); hair should be cut only to relieve its burden, and the beard not shaved (21). The philosopher's best livelihood is farming, with his pupils working beside him (11), and the training of body and soul by cold, heat, hunger and a hard bed is philosophy's other half (6, 7). Lecture 9 argues, perhaps from experience, that exile is no evil, since the cosmos is the common fatherland.

Where sources disagree. Martha Nussbaum's title gives her verdict, "The Incomplete Feminism of Musonius Rufus, Platonist, Stoic, and Roman" (2002): an equal capacity for virtue is argued in principle, while the household's roles in Lecture 4 stay Roman. Gretchen Reydams-Schils, who introduced the reissue of Lutz's translation (That One Should Disdain Hardships, 2020), called his view that relations between women and men, above all marriage, can be a vehicle of the good life significant and revolutionary, and took Gregory Hays' review to dismiss the apparent proto-feminism as a rhetorical ploy against male promiscuity; Hays answered that she was struck by how far Musonius transcended his time, he by how far he did not (exchange in the New York Review of Books, 29 April 2021).

03Epictetus: slave, pupil, exile

Epictetus was born at Hierapolis in Phrygia, the Suda says, about the middle of the first century; his name means "acquired". He was the slave of Epaphroditus, usually identified with the freedman of Nero who held the emperor's petitions office, helped him to his death (Suetonius, Nero 49.3) and was executed for it by Domitian (Domitian 14.4). The Discourses know Epaphroditus' house from inside: he sold a useless cobbler named Felicio, who then became Caesar's cobbler, and you should have seen, Epictetus says, how Epaphroditus honoured him (1.19.19–22). Fergus Millar ("Epictetus and the Imperial Court", 1965) read such passages as the court seen from below.

While still a slave he heard Musonius. The memories are of a teacher who tested his pupils: Rufus would say, to try me, that my master was going to do this or that to me (Discourses 1.9.29); he rebuked Epictetus for missing a flaw in a syllogism as if he had burned the Capitol (1.7.32); and he spoke so that each of us thought someone had denounced him (3.23.29). When Thrasea said he would rather be killed today than exiled tomorrow, Rufus asked whether he had chosen death as the heavier or the lighter: if heavier, what folly; if lighter, who gave him the choice (1.1.26–27)?

Freed, Epictetus taught at Rome until the senatorial decree against philosophers under Domitian, when, Gellius says, he left Rome for Nicopolis (Gellius 15.11); the decree and its date are module 13.4's. Nicopolis in Epirus, Augustus' city of victory over Actium, lay on the sea route between Italy and Greece, and his school drew young men of good family preparing for careers, with visiting officials looking in. Hadrian, the Historia Augusta says, was on terms of the closest friendship with him (Hadrian 16.10). He lived poorly; after his death, Lucian jokes, someone paid 3,000 drachmas for his clay lamp (Against the Ignorant Book-Collector 13). He never married, though Lucian has him urge marriage on Demonax (Demonax 55), and Simplicius says he took in, late in life, a child that would otherwise have been exposed. The year of his death is unknown; Gellius, writing in the mid-second century, speaks of his memory as recent (2.18).

Where sources disagree. Epictetus calls himself a lame old man (Discourses 1.16.20) but says nothing of the cause. Celsus, quoted by Origen, tells that his master twisted his leg; Epictetus smiled and said "you will break it", and when it broke, "did I not tell you?" (Against Celsus 7.53). The Suda blames rheumatism, and Simplicius says he was lame from youth. The torture story suits the doctrine too well to be trusted, and it may have grown from it.

04Arrian's Epictetus

Epictetus wrote nothing for publication. What survives was written down by Lucius Flavius Arrianus of Nicomedia, who heard him as a young man and became a consul under Hadrian, governor of Cappadocia and the historian of Alexander. In a prefatory letter to Lucius Gellius, Arrian says that he did not compose these words as one composes a book and did not publish them; he tried to write down what he heard in the speaker's own words, as notes for himself of his thought and frankness, and they had got out without his knowledge.

Photius read eight books of Discourses (Diatribai) and twelve of Conversations (Homiliai) by Arrian (Library, codex 58). Four books of the Discourses survive; Gellius quotes the lost fifth (19.1, told in module 13.2), and Stobaeus keeps fragments. The Handbook (Encheiridion), fifty-three short chapters, was compiled by Arrian from the discourses, choosing, Simplicius says, the most essential and most moving parts (preface to his commentary). The language is the plain common Greek (koinē) of speech, not the Attic Arrian wrote in his own histories, which is the strongest argument that the voice is not simply his.

Where sources disagree. A. A. Long (Epictetus: A Stoic and Socratic Guide to Life, 2002) takes Arrian at his word: notes taken in class, filled out from memory and polished, keeping an urgent and vivid voice that is Epictetus' own. Robert Dobbin (Epictetus: Discourses Book 1, 1998), following Stellwag (1933), argues that no shorthand of the time could have taken them down so fully, that they are too finished to be improvised, and that Epictetus wrote them himself, with Arrian's letter a literary convention. Most readers stand near Long, while granting that Arrian chose and arranged.

05What is up to us

The Handbook opens with a division (1.1). Some things are up to us (eph' hēmin), some are not. Up to us are judgement, impulse, desire and aversion: in a word, whatever is our own doing. Not up to us are body, property, reputation and office: whatever is not our own doing. The first are by nature free, unhindered and unimpeded; the second weak, slavish, hindered and another's (1.2). Mistake the second for your own and you will be thwarted, grieve and blame gods and men; keep to the first and no one will compel or hinder you, and you will have no enemy (1.3).

The Discourses open with the same thought as theology (1.1). Of all our faculties only reason studies itself and judges the rest. Zeus, Epictetus imagines, would have made our bodies and possessions free if he could; since he could not, he gave us a portion of himself, the power of impulse and repulsion, desire and aversion, the power to use impressions (1.1.10–12). So: you will chain my leg, but my prohairesis not even Zeus can overcome (1.1.23). Lameness is an impediment to the leg, not to the prohairesis (Handbook 9).

Prohairesis, in Aristotle a deliberate choice of means (Nicomachean Ethics 1113a10–11), becomes in Epictetus the whole self as a power of choosing: you are not flesh or hair but prohairesis, and if you make that beautiful you will be beautiful (Discourses 3.1.40). It is translated "will", "volition" or "moral choice", and nothing outside it can hinder it. Michael Frede (A Free Will, 2011) found here the first notion of a free will: a power that no force in the world can compel while it remains free, though a person can enslave it himself by wanting what is not his.

06Impressions, assent and the three fields

Everything turns on impressions (phantasiai), the way things strike us, and on assent, the yes we give them. Greek module 7.2 gives the passions as assents and Greek module 12.7 Zeno's cognitive impression; Epictetus made the use of impressions the whole task. Impressions come unbidden; assent is ours. Hence the exercise: to every harsh impression say, you are an impression and not at all what you appear to be, then test it by the rule of what is up to us (Handbook 1.5); or say, wait for me a little, impression, let me see what you are and test you (Discourses 2.18.24). What disturbs people is not things but their judgements about things; death is nothing terrible, or it would have seemed so to Socrates (Handbook 5).

Assent cannot be forced against what appears. Medea kills her children because revenge appears to her better than sparing them; show her plainly that she is deceived and she will not do it (Discourses 1.28). Wrongdoers are mistaken, not monsters, and anger at them is anger at the blind.

Epictetus ordered the training in three fields (topoi, Discourses 3.2.1–5).

The first, the most urgent because it governs the passions, trains desire and aversion: desire only what is up to us. The second trains impulse to action, the appropriate acts (kathēkonta) owed to others. The third secures assent against error even in sleep, drink or depression, and belongs to those already advanced; Epictetus mocks students who polish syllogisms while still grieving and envying (3.2.6–8). Pierre Hadot ("Une clé des Pensées de Marc Aurèle", 1978) took this triad as the key to the Meditations; module 13.8 weighs that debt.

07Roles and duties

The second field is where Epictetus' ethic of roles lives. Consider who you are, he says: first a human being, whose ruling faculty is prohairesis, and so a citizen of the world; then a son, a brother, a councillor, a young man or an old man, a father; each name, rightly considered, sets out its own acts (Discourses 2.10). Duties are measured by relationships. He is your father: care for him, yield to him, bear his abuse. But he is a bad father? Nature related you to a father, not to a good one (Handbook 30).

The roles that circumstance gives are not chosen. You are an actor in a play whose author decides; if he wants you to play a beggar, a lame man, a ruler or a private citizen, play it well: to choose the part belongs to another (Handbook 17). Within a role, though, each person must know his own price. For one man it is reasonable to hold the chamber pot for his master, for another not; Agrippinus would not go on stage at Nero's festival, and when Florus asked why, said that he did not even consider it, as Florus did (Discourses 1.2.8–18). Agrippinus wanted to be the purple stripe on the toga, the small bright part that makes the rest look well (1.2.17–18). Epictetus' example of keeping such a role to the death, Helvidius Priscus before Vespasian (1.2.19–24), is module 13.4's; the four personae of Panaetius and Cicero, which this ethic recalls, are module 13.2's. Epictetus' version is harsher: the given role is accepted as god's casting, and only its performance is ours.

08The Cynic as Epictetus imagines him

A pupil asks whether to take up Cynicism, and the answer is the longest portrait in the Discourses (3.22). The real Cynic is not a cloak, a wallet and a staff (3.22.10). He is a messenger sent by Zeus to show people that they have gone astray about good and evil, and a scout sent ahead to report what is friendly and what hostile (3.22.23–25). He must first purify his own ruling faculty; his frankness of speech rests on a conscience with nothing to hide (3.22.93–96). Beaten, he must love those who beat him, as a father or a brother (3.22.54). Diogenes is the model, and the Cynics Epictetus saw about him, begging and abusing passers-by, are the counterfeit; Demetrius and the Cynics of Rome are module 13.3's.

In the present order of things, a world not a city of sages, the Cynic should not marry or raise children: like a soldier on campaign he must be undistracted, free for the service of god, going about among people rather than tied to kettles and cradles (3.22.67–72). Crates married Hipparchia, but she was another Crates, and that was a love match in special circumstances (3.22.76). His children are all humanity (3.22.81–82). The figure is the sage made into a calling, and Epictetus warns the young man that without god's summons he will only disgrace himself.

Where sources disagree. Master and pupil answer differently. Musonius uses Crates and Hipparchia to show that marriage is no hindrance to philosophy and that the philosopher, as teacher of his city, should marry (Lecture 14); Epictetus makes the same couple the exception that proves the Cynic must stay single (Discourses 3.22.76). How far the portrait is Cynic at all, and how far a Stoic ideal in borrowed dress, is still argued; the difference with Musonius is one measure of it.

09The reach of the Handbook

The second century read him. Gellius tells how Herodes Atticus had a volume of the Discourses fetched to shame a young man posing as a Stoic (1.2), and reports Favorinus' account of the two words Epictetus said to keep, bear and forbear (17.19). Marcus Aurelius, given Epictetus' notes by Junius Rusticus (Meditations 1.7, told in module 13.7), quotes him: you are a little soul carrying a corpse, as Epictetus used to say (4.41); module 13.8 weighs the debt. Cleanthes' prayer to be led by Zeus and destiny, which closes the Handbook (53), is Greek module 12.7's.

Christians claimed him early. Origen grants that Plato is read only by the learned, while Epictetus is admired by ordinary people who want to be helped (Against Celsus 6.2). The Handbook was adapted for monks three times; one paraphrase is ascribed to Nilus of Ancyra in the fifth century, and in these versions the pagan names give way to Christian ones and the philosopher's life becomes the monk's (Gerard Boter, The Encheiridion of Epictetus and its Three Christian Adaptations, 1999). In the sixth century the Neoplatonist Simplicius wrote a long commentary on it as a first course in ethics. Poliziano put it into Latin in the fifteenth century, Elizabeth Carter into English in 1758, and James Stockdale, a navy pilot held prisoner in Hanoi from 1965 to 1973, said it carried him through.

Modern psychotherapy claims him most loudly. Albert Ellis cited Handbook 5 as a forerunner of his rational therapy, and Aaron Beck and his colleagues wrote that the philosophical origins of cognitive therapy can be traced back to the Stoic philosophers (Cognitive Therapy of Depression, 1979). Donald Robertson (The Philosophy of Cognitive-Behavioural Therapy, 2010) works the comparison out in detail: disturbance comes from judgements, judgements can be examined, and examining them is a skill to be practised.

Where sources disagree. Beck and Robertson see a therapy anticipated. Epictetus' own text resists the fit: judgements are corrected because they are false about what is good, the goal is virtue rather than relief, and a teacher who tells a father kissing his child to remember that he kisses a mortal (Handbook 3), and who denies that exile is an evil (Discourses 3.24), asks more than any therapist would. Whether the shared technique survives the loss of the providential cosmos that grounded it is the question Stoicism's modern revival leaves open.

10Self-test

  1. Why does Tacitus' sentence on Musonius' exile (Annals 15.71) matter for the relation between philosophers and the Principate?
    Answer

    It says the fame of Verginius' and Musonius' names drove them out: they were not charged with a part in Piso's plot, but their influence over the young was itself treated as dangerous. A teacher's reputation was enough.

  2. Which two stories about Musonius' exile does Philostratus tell, and why are they doubtful?
    Answer

    That he found a spring on waterless Gyaros, which Greeks still sailed to see (Life of Apollonius 7.16), and that Demetrius found him chained among the diggers of Nero's Isthmus canal (5.19). Both come from a novelistic life written about 150 years later.

  3. On what grounds does Lecture 3 argue that women should study philosophy, and what limit does Lecture 4 keep?
    Answer

    Women have the same reason, senses and inclination to virtue as men, and philosophy is training in virtue (Lecture 3). Lecture 4 still assigns heavier work to men and spinning and indoor work to women, though it allows that tasks can be exchanged.

  4. What arguments does Musonius use to bind men to the same sexual standard as women?
    Answer

    That a master who sleeps with his slave woman would not tolerate his wife doing the same with a male slave, and that men who claim to be stronger must show more self-control, not less (Lecture 12).

  5. Whose slave was Epictetus, and what do the Discourses show of that household?
    Answer

    Epaphroditus, usually identified with Nero's freedman who helped him die (Suetonius, Nero 49.3). Epictetus tells how Epaphroditus sold a useless cobbler who later became Caesar's cobbler and then honoured him (Discourses 1.19.19–22): the court seen from below.

  6. What does Arrian claim in his prefatory letter, and what is the strongest internal argument that the voice is Epictetus'?
    Answer

    That he wrote down what he heard in Epictetus' own words as private notes and did not publish them. The Discourses are in plain koinē, unlike the Attic of Arrian's own histories, though Dobbin argues Epictetus wrote them himself.

  7. Which things does Handbook 1 put up to us, and why are they called free?
    Answer

    Judgement, impulse, desire and aversion, whatever is our own doing. They are free because nothing outside us can compel or hinder them, while body, property, reputation and office are hindered and belong to another.

  8. What does Medea illustrate about assent in Discourses 1.28?
    Answer

    That no one assents to what appears false: Medea kills her children because revenge appears better to her. Show her plainly that she is deceived and she will not act, so the wrongdoer is mistaken and deserves correction, not anger.

  9. How do Handbook 17 and 30 divide what is given from what is ours in a role?
    Answer

    The role is given, like an actor's part chosen by the author (17), and the relationship fixes the duty, a father being owed care whether good or bad (30). Only the performance of the part is up to us.

  10. Why should Epictetus' Cynic not marry, and how does this differ from Musonius?
    Answer

    In the present order of things he must be undistracted, like a soldier on campaign, free for god's service, with all humanity for his children; Crates' marriage was an exception (Discourses 3.22.67–82). Musonius uses Crates and Hipparchia to argue that marriage is no hindrance to philosophy (Lecture 14).

11Carry forward

  1. Both men reach us through a pupil's record, Lucius' lectures in Stobaeus and Arrian's notes; every quotation of Musonius or Epictetus is a report, and how much of the voice is the reporter's is part of reading it.
  2. Epictetus narrowed the self to prohairesis, the power to judge and assent, and made the line between what is up to us and what is not the whole of ethics; Marcus, the Christian monks and modern cognitive therapy each inherited that line and changed what lay behind it.
  3. Roman Stoicism after Seneca was taught by an exiled knight and a lame freedman to the sons of the governing class: a philosophy for people who could lose everything external, and who were told that losing it touched nothing that was theirs.

Give me an in-depth report on Musonius Rufus and Epictetus, extending module 13.2.
