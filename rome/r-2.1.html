Religio, superstitio and the question of belief

Latin had a word Greek lacked (Greek module 8.1), but religio did not name a faith. Its core sense is scruple: a felt obligation before what must be done or must not be touched. A day could carry religio; a tomb was religiosus, left to the gods below (Gaius, Institutes 2.4). Cicero's Stoic speaker claims that Romans surpass all peoples in religio, "that is, the worship of the gods" (On the Nature of the Gods 2.8).

The same speaker derives religiosi from relegere, to go over again, because they carefully retraced everything belonging to the gods' worship, and superstitiosi from those who prayed all day that their children might survive them (superstites) (2.72). Lactantius, a Christian, preferred religare, to bind (Divine Institutes 4.28). Superstitio was excess: fear of the gods beyond custom, private and anxious where religio was public and measured. Cicero ends On Divination urging that superstition be uprooted and religion kept (2.148–149); under the empire the word labels foreign cults (module 2.5).

Hence the claim that belief is the wrong question. No Roman assented to a creed; what was required was correct performance by the right person at the right time. John Scheid's title puts it: Quand faire, c'est croire (2005), when doing is believing. Beard, North and Price (Religions of Rome, 1998) call belief as an inner test of membership a Christian category read backwards.

Where sources disagree. Charles King (Classical Antiquity, 2003) and Denis Feeney reply that Romans held beliefs, only unsystematic ones that nobody policed. Cicero's pontiff Cotta says he must believe (credere) the ancestors even without a reason (On the Nature of the Gods 3.5–6). The issue is whether belief was ever the test, not whether it existed.

The pax deorum, do ut des and the vow

Public cult aimed at the pax deorum, the peace of the gods: a state of agreement in which they were well disposed to the community. Livy, writing some three and a half centuries later, says the lectisternium of 364 BC was held to secure it (7.2.2); plague, defeat or prodigy showed it broken, and ritual repaired it. The logic was reciprocal. Cicero's dialogue defines piety as justice towards the gods (On the Nature of the Gods 1.116):

Est enim pietas iustitia adversum deos.

Justice gives each his due; piety gives the gods theirs. Modern scholars call this do ut des, "I give so that you may give". Cato's farm prayers (On Agriculture 132, 139, 141) name the god, state the offering and attach the request "for the sake of this thing". The vow (votum) made the bargain conditional. The vower was voti reus, answerable; when the god delivered he was voti damnatus, condemned to pay like a debtor, and thousands of inscriptions end V.S.L.M., votum solvit libens merito, "paid the vow willingly and deservedly". After Trasimene in 217 BC, since the pontifex maximus ruled that it could not be vowed without them, the people voted a "sacred spring": every pig, sheep, goat and ox born that spring if the state survived five years, with clauses saving the vow if a beast was stolen, died or was offered on the wrong day (Livy 22.10).

Where sources disagree. Warde Fowler (The Religious Experience of the Roman People, 1911) and Kurt Latte (Römische Religionsgeschichte, 1960) read the contract as cold legalism that lost its hold on feeling and decayed in the late Republic. Beard, North and Price reject both coldness and decline, tracing the decline story to Christian polemic and Roman nostalgia. The contract was lopsided anyway: the human party was bound, the god free to refuse.

Ritual exactness and instauratio

A relationship founded on performance required exact performance. When the highest magistrates prayed, Pliny says, one man read the formula ahead from a text so that no word was skipped or misplaced, another checked it, a third ordered silence, and a flute-player drowned ill-omened sounds (Natural History 28.11). A god of uncertain identity was addressed "whether god or goddess" (Cato, On Agriculture 139).

A flawed rite was void and had to be done again: instauratio. If the dancer stopped, the flute-player fell silent, the boy with both parents living let go of the sacred cart, or the aedile slipped in a word or with the ladle, the games were not duly performed, and the gods were appeased by repeating them (Cicero, De haruspicum responso 23). Livy often records games repeated once, twice or more in a year; Plutarch knew of a sacrifice performed thirty times because faults kept appearing (Coriolanus 25.3). The sacred spring of 217, paid in 195, was judged improperly performed by the pontiffs and done again in 194 (Livy 33.44; 34.44). A flaw (vitium) in election auspices likewise forced magistrates to resign (below).

The state as worshipper

The religious actor that mattered was the Roman people. Sacra publica were rites performed at public expense for the people (Festus); households and clans had their own (module 2.4). Magistrates took the auspices before every public act, sacrificed, vowed and dedicated temples, presided at games and carried out expiations; the senate decided, with the priestly colleges as expert advisers (module 2.3). Priests were not a caste but senators, often the same men who held consulships; Cicero praised the ancestors for putting the same men in charge of the worship of the gods and the highest interests of the state (De domo sua 1). In the plague of 363 BC a dictator was appointed solely to drive a nail into the Capitoline temple (Livy 7.3). Neglect was a public fault: Gaius Flaminius left Rome in 217 without taking the auspices or pronouncing the vows on the Capitol (Livy 21.63), and after Trasimene the dictator Fabius told the senate that Flaminius had erred more by neglecting ceremonies and auspices than by rashness (22.9.7). Unlike the Greek city, whose priests belonged to sanctuaries (Greek module 8.1), Rome put the auspices in the hands of elected magistrates.

Where sources disagree. Beard, North and Price make the community the frame of Roman religion, as Sourvinou-Inwood's "polis religion" does for Greece. Andreas Bendlin (2000) and Jörg Rüpke (2016) reply that this marginalises individual choice, private vows, healing cults and magic, always present but less documented.

Prodigies and their expiation

A prodigy was an event against nature showing the pax deorum disturbed. It foretold no particular future; it demanded a remedy. Livy lists prodigies year by year, usually at the start of the consular year before the consuls left Rome: lightning on temples and walls, rains of stones, blood or milk, sweating statues, an ox that spoke, monstrous births. In the winter of 218/17 a six-month-old in the vegetable market shouted the triumphal cry, and an ox climbed to a third floor in the cattle market and threw itself down (21.62).

The procedure was public. Reports came from Rome and the Italian towns; the senate decided which to accept (suscipere); the pontiffs, the decemvirs with the Sibylline Books or Etruscan haruspices prescribed the remedy; the consuls performed it with full-grown victims, supplication at all the gods' couches, a lectisternium or a new cult (module 2.5). A rain of stones required nine days of rites, a rule Livy, writing some six centuries later, traces to Tullus Hostilius (1.31.4).

Livy reflects on his lists. The neglect that now makes men think the gods portend nothing, he says, has stopped prodigies being reported; but writing of old things his mind grows old-fashioned, and a scruple (religio) keeps him recording them (43.13.1–2). That year two were refused, one on private ground, one on foreign soil (43.13). Julius Obsequens, a late antique excerptor, extracted the lists into a Book of Prodigies that survives from 190 BC into Augustus' reign.

Where sources disagree. Bruce MacBain (Prodigy and Expiation, 1982) saw reports cluster in crises and allied prodigies expiated at Rome, and read the system as a way of binding Italy and managing senatorial tension. Veit Rosenberger (Gezähmte Götter, 1998) and Susanne Rasmussen (Public Portents in Republican Rome, 2003) see a working method for turning anxiety into prescribed action. Whether the thinner late Republican lists mean less fear or less recording is uncertain.

Varro's three theologies and the gods without images

Varro's Antiquities (module 0.1) is lost; its divine books survive in Augustine's City of God, books 4, 6 and 7, quoted for demolition. Varro put human things before divine because, as the painter precedes the painting, cities precede the rites they establish (6.4). His frame was a threefold theology (6.5). The mythical belonged to poets and theatre and was full of things unworthy of gods: births from heads and thighs, thefts, adulteries. The natural belonged to philosophers asking what the gods are, fire, numbers or atoms, a discussion for the schools, not the forum. The civil belonged to cities: what citizens and priests should know and do, which gods to worship publicly and with which rites. The pontifex maximus Quintus Mucius Scaevola had already distinguished the gods of poets, philosophers and statesmen, and thought it expedient for cities to be deceived in religion (4.27). Augustine replies that civil theology cannot be separated from mythical: the games were rites, and the gods of the temple were mocked on the stage (6.6–8).

Varro, writing some five centuries after the period, also claimed that Romans worshipped without images for over 170 years, that the gods would be worshipped more purely had this lasted, and that those who introduced images removed fear and added error (4.31). Plutarch, some eight centuries after Numa's supposed reign, credits him, in agreement with Pythagoras, with the ban (Numa 8). Counted from 753, the 170 years end about 583, under Tarquinius Priscus, to whom Pliny, citing Varro, assigns Vulca's terracotta Jupiter (module 1.5).

Where sources disagree. Warde Fowler and the numen school (below) read the imageless Rome as memory; it may instead be philosophy, echoing the Stoic and Pythagorean critiques of images that Varro and Plutarch share. Some cults did use objects instead of images: the flint of Jupiter Feretrius, the spears of Mars in the Regia, Vesta's fire, which Ovid says had no statue (Fasti 6.295–298). Whether these are remnants of a general rule or local peculiarities is uncertain.

Origins: numen and the three functions

The animism theory and its collapse

For much of the twentieth century the standard account began with numen. Warde Fowler, H. J. Rose ("Numen inest", 1935) and Hendrik Wagenvoort (Roman Dynamism, 1947) held that the earliest Romans worshipped impersonal powers in places, objects and acts, a Latin mana on the model of R. R. Marett's "pre-animism"; personal gods with images came later from Etruria and Greece. The evidence: Varro's 170 years, the aniconic cults, the "god or goddess" formula and swarms of functional gods. Servius' expanded commentary lists, from Fabius Pictor, twelve gods invoked at the sacrifice to Tellus and Ceres, one for each farming task from breaking the fallow to bringing grain out of store (on Georgics 1.21); Augustine mocks the door shared by Forculus, Cardea and Limentinus (City of God 4.8).

The theory collapsed on its key word. Numen comes from nuere, to nod; Varro glosses it as command, named from the nod (On the Latin Language 7.85). In Republican Latin it is almost always the numen of a named god, his expressed will; numen as a free-standing divinity is Augustan and later. Dumézil (La religion romaine archaïque, 1966; English 1970) pressed the linguistic case; anthropologists abandoned mana as a universal stage; and archaeology found anthropomorphic, Greek-influenced gods in sixth-century Latium, from the Sant'Omobono terracottas to a Lavinium bronze of about 500 BC dedicated to Castor and Pollux as qurois, Greek kouroi. The functional gods now look like pontifical classification, not fossils of a primitive mind.

Jupiter, Mars, Quirinus

Dumézil replaced primitive powers with an inheritance. Indo-European societies, he argued (Jupiter Mars Quirinus, 1941), thought through three functions: sovereignty, magical and juridical; force; and fertility, wealth and the people. At Rome he found them in a triad older than the Capitoline one (module 2.2). The three major flamines served Jupiter, Mars and Quirinus, ranked after the rex and before the pontifex maximus (Festus, under ordo sacerdotum); a law ascribed to Numa gave the first spoils of honour to Jupiter Feretrius, the second to Mars, the third to Quirinus (Plutarch, Marcellus 8; Festus, quoting the law, has Janus Quirinus); and the Umbrian Iguvine Tables name Jupiter, Mars and Vofionus, all called Grabovius (module 1.1).

Where sources disagree. Quirinus is the weak point: armed, god of the citizen body in its curiae, a "tranquil Mars" for Servius (on Aeneid 1.292), he fits the third function only by argument. Momigliano (History and Theory, 1984) objected that the system cannot be falsified and ignores Roman evidence that does not fit. Most historians accept an early grouping of the three gods while doubting the Indo-European ideology built on it.

A people without myths?

The old claim had an ancient root. Dionysius of Halicarnassus praises Romulus for rejecting Greek myths of castration, swallowed children and gods at war (Greek module 2.1): Roman gods are not wounded, chained or mourned (Roman Antiquities 2.18–19). Modern scholars from Rose to Latte made this a thesis: Rome had cult without stories and borrowed a mythology from Greece. The modern answers take four forms (module 0.1 gave the source problem and a first sketch of this debate).

  • Dumézil: the myths were turned into history. The one-eyed Horatius Cocles and the one-handed Mucius Scaevola answer to Odin and Tyr (Mitra-Varuna, 1940; module 1.6); the gods lost their stories and the city's past gained them.
  • Wiseman (The Myths of Rome, 2004): Rome had abundant myths, of Faunus and Picus, Hercules and Cacus, Remus and the Lupercalia, staged at the games from the fourth century and lost with the drama that carried them.
  • Feeney (Literature and Religion at Rome, 1998): the question assumes a Greek definition. Roman myth was entangled with Greek from the start, and literature was itself a religious voice; following Paul Veyne, Feeney argues that Romans kept the poet's gods and the cult's gods in separate compartments.
  • Beard, North and Price: myths were embedded in places and rituals, told as the origin of a festival or monument, and concerned the city more than the gods' private lives.

Varro's scheme explains the impression: myth was filed under the poets, so anyone looking for it in civil cult found none.

One activity: religion and politics

Because magistrates held the auspices, every assembly was also a rite. Lightning on the left, otherwise the best of omens, stopped an assembly, a rule Cicero says was made for the state's sake so that the leading men should interpret it (On Divination 2.74). An augur or magistrate could announce adverse signs (obnuntiatio) and dissolve the meeting; Cicero counts the augur's power to halt public business with the words "another day" among the greatest in the state (On the Laws 2.31). Flaws voided results: Tiberius Gracchus, the father, presiding as consul in 163 BC, later found from the augural books that he had erred in taking the auspices, and the consuls he had returned resigned (On the Nature of the Gods 2.10–11). Bibulus' sky-watching against Caesar in 59 was the same weapon at full stretch (module 5.4).

Cicero, augur from 53, theorised the system. On the Nature of the Gods (45 BC) sets the Epicurean Velleius and the Stoic Balbus against the Academic Cotta, himself a pontiff; Cicero warns that without piety, good faith, human society and justice may vanish (1.4), and ends finding the Stoic case nearer the likeness of truth (3.95). On Divination (44 BC) gives his brother Quintus the Stoic defence and himself the demolition, with Cato wondering that one haruspex could see another without laughing (2.51); yet augury is kept out of respect for popular opinion and its great service to the state (2.70).

Where sources disagree. Polybius thought Roman fear of the gods a device to control the masses and the cement of the state (6.56.6–12). Mary Beard ("Cicero and Divination", Journal of Roman Studies, 1986) and Malcolm Schofield in the same volume argue that On Divination is no confession of disbelief: the dialogue puts both cases in play and makes divination a subject of Latin rational argument. Manipulation, in any case, works only in a system others take seriously.

Self-test

  1. How did Cicero and Lactantius derive religio, and what does each derivation reveal about its author?
    Answer

    Cicero's Stoic derives it from relegere, to go over again, so religion is scrupulous attention to rites (On the Nature of the Gods 2.72); Lactantius from religare, to bind, so religion is a bond with God (Divine Institutes 4.28). The first is a Roman's religion of practice, the second a Christian's of relationship.

  2. Why do Scheid and Beard, North and Price call belief the wrong question, and what text best answers them?
    Answer

    Membership required correct performance, not assent to a creed, and belief as an inner test is a Christian category. Cotta's statement that he must believe the ancestors even without a reason (On the Nature of the Gods 3.5–6) shows that belief existed, though not as a test.

  3. What do voti reus and voti damnatus tell you about the logic of a vow?
    Answer

    The vower is bound once he vows, and when the god grants the request he is "condemned" to pay like a debtor. The vow is a conditional contract in which only the human party is obliged; V.S.L.M. inscriptions record the payment.

  4. What is instauratio, and why could a flawed rite not simply be accepted?
    Answer

    The repetition of a rite, often games, spoiled by a fault such as a dancer stopping or an aedile's slip (Cicero, De haruspicum responso 23). A rite was valid only if performed exactly, so a flawed one counted for nothing, as with the sacred spring repeated in 194 (Livy 34.44).

  5. Describe the path of a prodigy from report to expiation, and give Livy's reasons for refusing two.
    Answer

    Reported from Rome or Italy, accepted by the senate as public business, referred to pontiffs, decemvirs or haruspices, and expiated by the consuls with victims, supplications or new rites. In 169 one was refused because it happened on private ground, the other because it was on foreign soil (Livy 43.13).

  6. Name Varro's three theologies and state Augustine's objection to the scheme.
    Answer

    Mythical (the poets'), natural (the philosophers') and civil (the cities') (City of God 6.5). Augustine argues that civil theology cannot be separated from mythical, since the games were rites and the gods of the temple were the gods mocked on the stage (6.6–8).

  7. What did Varro claim about images, and why is the claim doubted?
    Answer

    That Rome worshipped without images for over 170 years and that images added error (City of God 4.31). It fits a Stoic and Pythagorean ideal of pure worship too well, and the aniconic cults (Jupiter's flint, Mars' spears, Vesta's fire) may be local peculiarities rather than relics of a general rule.

  8. What was the numen theory, and what linguistic fact undid it?
    Answer

    That the earliest Romans worshipped impersonal powers like mana before personal gods (Rose, Wagenvoort). Numen means the nod or will of a god (Varro, On the Latin Language 7.85) and in Republican Latin belongs to a named god; the free-standing numen is Augustan.

  9. What is the evidence for an archaic triad of Jupiter, Mars and Quirinus, and where is Dumézil's reading weakest?
    Answer

    The three major flamines and their rank in the order of priests (Festus), the three grades of spoils of honour (Plutarch, Marcellus 8), and the Umbrian triad of the Iguvine Tables. Quirinus fits the function of fertility and the people only by argument, since he is also armed.

  10. Give two ways augury made elections religious events, and say how Cicero justifies keeping augury in On Divination.
    Answer

    Lightning or an announcement of adverse signs could halt an assembly, and a flaw in the presiding magistrate's auspices forced elected consuls to resign, as in 163 (On the Nature of the Gods 2.10–11). Cicero keeps augury out of respect for popular opinion and for its service to the state (On Divination 2.70).

Carry forward

  1. Roman religion is a relationship with the gods maintained by exact performance on behalf of the community: for any rite, ask who performed it, for whom, and whether it was done right, before asking what anyone believed.
  2. Religion and politics were one activity carried on by the same men under the same rules; every prodigy, vow and auspice in Livy or Cicero is also a political act, and manipulating the system presupposed that others took it seriously.
  3. Roman religion reaches us through systematisers and critics (Varro through Augustine, Livy's archaising lists) and through modern theories (numen, the three functions) that have risen and fallen; separate the attested practice from the frame laid over it.
