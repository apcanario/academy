The defining problem: a past written down late

Varro dated Rome's foundation to 753 BC; the first history of Rome by a Roman was written about 200 BC. Romulus reached the page five and a half centuries after his supposed date, the first consuls three centuries after theirs. Most of what we read is later still: Livy and Dionysius of Halicarnassus wrote under Augustus, seven centuries after Romulus; Plutarch nearly eight and a half. In between lay the Gallic sack of about 390 BC, which Livy says destroyed most of the records (6.1.2).

The writers who filled the gap were senators or their dependants, writing for families that competed through ancestral glory, and they made the early centuries an argument about the present: liberty against tyranny, patricians against plebeians, ancestral virtue against decline. The Roman problem is the reverse of the Greek one. Greek myth survives as many contradictory poems with no canon; Greek module 0.1 covers those sources, and they are not repeated here. Roman tradition survives as one apparently continuous prose history, dated year by year and largely constructed in its details. The task is not to choose between versions but to strip one back to what lies beneath.

Where sources disagree. Whether Rome had a mythology of its own is disputed. H. J. Rose held that Romans had gods without stories and borrowed myths from Greece; Georges Dumézil (Mythe et épopée, 1968–73) and T. P. Wiseman (The Myths of Rome, 2004), in different ways, find Roman myth told as history. Module 2.1 weighs the answers.

The first historians: Roman history in Greek

Poets came first: Naevius' Punic War (late third century) and Ennius' Annales (c. 180s–170s BC) made Romulus Aeneas' grandson (Servius on Aeneid 1.273), and Cicero quotes Ennius' augural contest of the twins (On Divination 1.107–108).

Prose began with Quintus Fabius Pictor, a senator of the patrician Fabii, who fought the Gauls in 225 (Orosius 4.13.6) and was sent to Delphi after Cannae (Livy 22.57.5; 23.11.1–6). His history, written in Greek around 200 BC, ran from Aeneas to his own day. Lucius Cincius Alimentus, praetor in 210 and once Hannibal's prisoner (Livy 21.38.3), wrote a similar Greek work. Dionysius says both were exact on what they had witnessed and summary on the centuries after the foundation (1.6.2): an hourglass, full at the origins and the present, thin between.

Why Greek? Latin had no prose literature, and Greek was the language of history and of the Mediterranean public that judged Rome. Like Berossus for Babylon and Manetho for Egypt, Fabius presented his people to the Hellenistic world in its own idiom, answering historians who wrote from Carthage's side: Philinus of Acragas, and Silenus and Sosylus in Hannibal's camp. He took the foundation story itself from a Greek, Diocles of Peparethus (Plutarch, Romulus 3.1). The habit lasted: Aulus Postumius Albinus, consul in 151, wrote in Greek and apologised for his Greek, earning Cato's mockery (Polybius 39.1).

Where sources disagree. Polybius calls Fabius a partisan who blamed the Second Punic War on Hasdrubal's ambition (1.14.1–3; 3.8–9); Dionysius treats Fabius and Cincius as careful witnesses of their own times. Matthias Gelzer ("Römische Politik bei Fabius Pictor", Hermes 1933) read Fabius as propaganda aimed at Greeks; Dieter Timpe ("Fabius Pictor und die Anfänge der römischen Historiographie", ANRW I.2, 1972) replies that the Roman elite read Greek and was itself the first audience.

Cato and the Latin annalists

Cato the Elder's Origines, begun after 168 and unfinished at his death in 149, was the first history in Latin prose: the kings in book 1, the origins of Italian peoples and towns in books 2–3 (hence the title), then Rome's wars to his own day, with his speeches inserted. He named no generals, only their offices (Nepos, Cato 3.4), though he named a Carthaginian elephant, Surus (Pliny, Natural History 8.11). The fragments of all these writers are in T. J. Cornell (ed.), The Fragments of the Roman Historians (2013).

The annalists, who arranged events year by year under the consuls, came in two waves:

  • Lucius Cassius Hemina (mid-second century) wrote from the origins with an antiquarian bent.
  • Lucius Calpurnius Piso Frugi (consul 133) moralised, dating the onset of decline to 154 BC (Pliny, Natural History 17.244), and rationalised: his Tarpeia tries to trick the Sabines out of their shields (Dionysius 2.38–40).
  • Quintus Claudius Quadrigarius (Sullan era) began with the Gallic sack, as if conceding that what lay before was unknowable.
  • Valerius Antias, his contemporary, wrote at least 75 books; Livy catches him "exaggerating the number of everything without limit" (33.10).
  • Gaius Licinius Macer (tribune 73) cited the "linen books" kept in the temple of Juno Moneta (Livy 4.7.12; 4.20.8), yet disagreed with Aelius Tubero, who cited the same books (4.23.1–3); Livy discounts him for seeking glory for his own family (7.9.5).

The charge is invention. The further the annalists stood from the early Republic, the more they knew: speeches, casualty figures, family heroes, political struggles that read like their own. Valerii loom large in Antias, Licinii in Macer. Ernst Badian ("The Early Historians", 1966) traced how each generation lengthened the early centuries.

Where sources disagree. Badian and, later, Gary Forsythe treat the annalists as free inventors. Wiseman (Clio's Cosmetics, 1979) recasts the charge: ancient historiography expected plausible "creative reconstruction", so they filled gaps by convention rather than lying. Cornell and Stephen Oakley (A Commentary on Livy Books VI–X, 1997–2005) see elaboration around a transmitted core of lists, laws, buildings and cults.

The raw material, and what the Romans said about it

The Annales Maximi. Each year, Cicero says, the pontifex maximus wrote up the year's events on a whitened board at his house, until Publius Mucius Scaevola's time, about 130 BC (On the Orator 2.52); an edition in eighty books followed (Servius on Aeneid 1.373). The entries were magistrates, prodigies, famines and eclipses. Cato would not copy "how often grain was dear, how often darkness obscured the moon or sun" (Gellius 2.28.6); Cicero found nothing "more jejune" (On the Laws 1.6). Even this record was reconstructed at its start: the first eclipse actually entered fell around 400 BC, and earlier ones, including one under Romulus, were calculated backwards (Cicero, On the Republic 1.25).

Lists. The consular and triumphal fasti survive chiefly in the Augustan Fasti Capitolini, found in the Forum in 1546. The triumphal list opens with Romulus over the Caeninenses, a record no one could have kept.

Documents. A few survived: Polybius read the Carthage treaties in the aediles' archive (3.22–26), and Cicero learnt the Twelve Tables by heart as a boy (On the Laws 2.59).

Families. Noble houses kept wax ancestor masks (imagines) captioned with offices held; at funerals actors wore them while a son praised the dead and his forebears (Polybius 6.53–54). Cicero says these eulogies filled Roman history with errors:

false triumphs, too many consulships, false genealogies too (Brutus 62)

Livy agrees that the record was "corrupted by funeral eulogies and false captions on the masks", and notes that no writer contemporary with those times survives (8.40.4–5). Cato recalled songs in praise of great men once sung at banquets, lost by his day (Cicero, Brutus 75).

Where sources disagree. The traditional view, broadly shared by Cornell, is that Scaevola published the eighty books around 130 BC and the annalists mined them. Bruce Frier (Libri Annales Pontificum Maximorum, 1979) argued that the edition was an Augustan reworking and that its role in shaping the tradition has been exaggerated. How much of the chronicle reached Livy is uncertain.

Polybius, a Greek hostage in Rome after 167, asked an outsider's question: how did Rome conquer nearly the whole inhabited world in under fifty-three years (1.1.5)? His answer, the constitution and army of book 6, is analysis, not tradition. Livy follows him from the fourth decade, calling him "not an unreliable authority" (33.10).

Livy (c. 59 BC–AD 17) wrote Book 1 between 27 and 25 BC: it has Augustus closing the temple of Janus once, not twice (1.19.3). His preface will neither affirm nor refute stories from before the city's foundation, being

more fitting for poets' tales than for the incorrupt records of history (Preface 6)

Antiquity may mingle human and divine to make the beginnings of cities more august (Preface 7); history supplies examples to imitate and to avoid (Preface 10). But the line falls at the foundation: from Romulus on Livy narrates as history, hedged with "they say", and even offers the rationalised version in which the she-wolf was a prostitute (1.4.7). He worked from the annalists, rarely from documents.

Dionysius came to Rome in 30 BC and published in 7 BC (1.7.2; 1.3.4). His thesis is that Rome was a Greek city, founded by successive waves of Greeks (1.5.1; 1.89–90), an argument to reconcile Greek readers to Roman rule; module 1.3 takes up its content. He is fuller than Livy because more rhetorical, and he names the same annalists (1.7.3).

Diodorus gives sparse Roman notices for the fifth and fourth centuries, with a chronology often at odds with Livy's. Plutarch's Roman Lives (Romulus, Numa, Publicola, Coriolanus, Camillus for the early period) collect variants; he admits he learnt Latin late (Demosthenes 2.2–4). For the later Republic, Appian's Civil Wars is the only continuous narrative from the Gracchi to Sulla, and Dio's surviving books run from 69 BC to AD 46.

Where sources disagree. Mommsen, and later Otto Leuze (1909), held that Diodorus' Roman notices descend from Fabius Pictor and so preserve the tradition before annalistic expansion. Gerhard Perl traced the consular list to a separate magistrate list, Andrew Drummond treats anomalies as Diodorus' own errors, and Cornell leaves the question open.

The antiquarians: religion, custom and words

For religion and custom the key writers are antiquarians. Varro (116–27 BC) wrote the Antiquities Human and Divine in 41 books, 25 human and 16 divine (Augustine, City of God 6.3), the divine part dedicated to Caesar as pontifex maximus. It is lost, known mainly through Augustine, who quotes it to demolish it, including its three theologies: mythical (the poets'), natural (the philosophers') and civil (the city's) (City of God 6.5). Books 5–10 of On the Latin Language survive, rich in etymologies of hills, gates and festivals.

Verrius Flaccus, Augustan freedman and tutor to Augustus' grandsons, set up an annotated calendar at Praeneste (Suetonius, On Grammarians 17), fragments of which survive as the Fasti Praenestini. His lexicon On the Meaning of Words survives only through Festus' abridgement, itself in one fire-damaged manuscript, and Paul the Deacon's eighth-century epitome of Festus. Aulus Gellius' Attic Nights (later second century AD) quotes Cato, the annalists and the Twelve Tables verbatim. Macrobius' Saturnalia (early fifth century) explains the calendar and festivals in book 1.

The antiquarians' facts about ritual are often old and good; their explanations are scholarship, frequently etymological guesswork, not memory.

The imperial historians in outline

Velleius Paterculus published a compressed history in two books in AD 30, a soldier's panegyric of Tiberius. Tacitus (c. AD 56–c. 120) wrote the Histories (AD 69–96) and the Annals (AD 14–68), each surviving in part: senatorial, hostile to the principate, and supremely artful. Suetonius, imperial secretary under Hadrian, wrote the Lives of the Caesars from Julius to Domitian, arranged by topic, quoting Augustus' letters. The Historia Augusta gives lives of emperors and usurpers from Hadrian to Carinus (AD 117–284) under six names, with forged documents and a gap for 244–260. Ammianus Marcellinus, a Greek from Antioch writing Latin in Rome around 390, covered AD 96–378; books 14–31 (353–378) survive, much of it eyewitness.

Where sources disagree. Hermann Dessau (1889) showed that the Historia Augusta's six "authors" under Diocletian and Constantine are one writer of the late fourth century; Ronald Syme (Ammianus and the Historia Augusta, 1968) called him a rogue grammarian. Arnaldo Momigliano (1954) urged caution, but the single late author is now consensus. The live question is how much sound material the main lives take from Marius Maximus and the lost imperial history posited by Alexander Enmann (1884).

Material sources

Inscriptions. The Lapis Niger, a black paving in the Comitium found by Giacomo Boni in 1899, covers a cippus inscribed boustrophedon in archaic Latin around 570–550 BC (CIL I² 1). It names recei, "to the king", a herald (kalatorem) and a curse on violators (sakros esed): contemporary proof of a rex, whether king or the priestly rex sacrorum. The Duenos vase, a triple pot found near the Quirinal in 1880 (CIL I² 4), carries a sixth-century text that includes "Duenos made me". The Satricum stone, inscribed around 500 BC and found in 1977 reused in a temple's foundations, names the companions (suodales) of Poplios Valesios, perhaps the consul Publius Valerius (module 1.6). The Scipio epitaphs on the Via Appia are family self-advertisement in Saturnian verse. The Res Gestae, Augustus' own account set up before his Mausoleum (Suetonius, Augustus 101.4) and best preserved at Ancyra, claims he freed the republic from "the domination of a faction", leaving Antony unnamed (1.1). Claudius' speech of AD 48 on admitting Gallic nobles to the senate, on the bronze found at Lyon in 1528 (ILS 212), can be set beside Tacitus' version (Annals 11.24): same argument, wholly rewritten, a measure of what historians' speeches are.

Where sources disagree. The epitaph of Lucius Cornelius Scipio Barbatus, consul 298 (CIL I² 7), says he took Taurasia and Cisauna in Samnium and subdued all Lucania; Livy gives him Etruria that year and Samnium to his colleague (10.12). Either the family inflated the record, as Cicero and Livy complain, or the annalists confused the provinces. The text's date is debated too: traditionally a later replacement for an erased epitaph, it is original in Rudolf Wachter's analysis (1987).

Coins. A didrachm of about 269–266 BC already shows the wolf and twins. From the 130s BC the annual moneyers advertised their families: Sextus Pompeius Fostlus set Faustulus beside the wolf, punning on his name (c. 137 BC; RRC 235/1), and Caesar issued Aeneas carrying Anchises. Under the emperors the centre chose every type, though how much users noticed is debated (A. H. M. Jones, "Numismatics and History", 1956).

The Pyrgi tablets, three gold sheets found in 1964 at the port of Caere, two in Etruscan and one in Phoenician, record a dedication to Uni-Astarte around 500 BC by Thefarie Velianas, called king in the Phoenician text. Their bearing on Rome's first treaty with Carthage, which Polybius dates to 508/7 (3.22), is weighed in module 1.6.

Archaeology. In the Forum Boni found an Iron Age cemetery; the valley became a public square only after drainage, landfill and a first pavement in the later seventh century. On the Palatine, Iron Age hut post-holes lie near the "hut of Romulus" Romans preserved (Dionysius 1.79.11), and Andrea Carandini dated a wall at the hill's foot to about 730–720 BC (module 1.4). Sant'Omobono and the Regia show a monumental sixth-century city. Archaeology gives settings and sequences, not names.

Method: how far to trust the tradition

The hypercritical tradition

Louis de Beaufort (1738) first argued that the first five centuries were uncertain. Barthold Niebuhr (Römische Geschichte, 1811–32) derived the legends from lost banquet ballads; the theory is abandoned, his separation of source layers is not. Theodor Mommsen (Römische Geschichte, 1854–56) set the regal narrative aside and rebuilt early Rome from institutions and law. Ettore Pais (Storia di Roma, 1898–99) went furthest, reading Tarpeia and others as faded deities and much of the fifth century as doublets of later events. Gary Forsythe (A Critical History of Early Rome, 2005) restates the position: the fasti, a few documents and archaeology are usable; the narrative before the fourth century is reconstruction.

Cornell, Carandini, Wiseman

Cornell (The Beginnings of Rome, 1995) separates structural facts (institutions, cults, treaties, the sequence of kings, the Servian reforms) from narrative superstructure. The first survived through institutions and monuments and fits the archaeology of a large sixth-century city; plebeian names in the early consular list are not what a patrician forger would invent. Carandini (La nascita di Roma, 1997; Remo e Romolo, 2006) goes further, treating the Palatine wall as evidence of a deliberate eighth-century foundation and legend as compressed memory; Wiseman ("Reading Carandini", 2001) rejects this as reading archaeology through Livy. Wiseman's own view (Remus, 1995; Roman Drama and Roman History, 1998) is that many early stories were created in the fourth and third centuries as dramatic performances at the games, Remus himself entering the story late. Plutarch already reports that some found the Romulus story "dramatic and fictitious" (Romulus 8).

Where sources disagree. Wiseman points to stage games at Rome from 364 BC (Livy 7.2) and to lost plays on Roman subjects such as Naevius' Romulus; critics reply that hardly any are attested before the late third century, and Cornell's structural memory needs no stage.

Judging a story first found in Livy

  1. Count the centuries. Name the earliest witness and its date; a story first found in Livy has passed through Fabius, the annalists and Livy's rhetoric.
  2. Separate structure from narrative. A law, cult, temple or name in the fasti may be old; a speech, motive or casualty figure is not.
  3. Look for aetiology. The Lacus Curtius has a Sabine Mettius Curtius (Livy 1.13.5), a self-sacrificing Marcus Curtius of 362 (7.6.1–6) and a consul fencing off a lightning strike (Varro, On the Latin Language 5.148–150): three explanations of one monument.
  4. Look for Greek models. Tarquin's poppies (Livy 1.54.6) are Thrasybulus' advice to Periander (Herodotus 5.92); Sextus Tarquinius at Gabii is Zopyrus at Babylon (Herodotus 3.153–158). In Cicero's Brutus (41–43) Atticus teases him for giving Coriolanus a Themistocles-like suicide, since rhetoricians may lie in histories.
  5. Look for later politics and family interest. Spurius Cassius and Spurius Maelius, would-be tyrants offering land or grain, read like the Gracchi; the 306 Fabii dying at the Cremera (Livy 2.48–50) are a family epic Fabius Pictor was placed to shape.
  6. Seek an outside control. Aristotle knew the Gauls took Rome but named its saviour Lucius, not Marcus Camillus (Plutarch, Camillus 22.4); an inscription, treaty or stratum can confirm or contradict.

The main traps

Treating Livy book 1 as history. Livy suspended judgement only on the stories before the foundation; the kings he narrates as history, seven centuries late. Seven kings filling 244 years, each founding a neat set of institutions, is a scheme. Read Book 1 for what Augustan Romans believed, and for the structural facts that survive scrutiny.

Treating Dionysius as independent of Livy. They were contemporaries in the same city, using the same annalists. Their agreement is one tradition reported twice; Dionysius' extra detail is usually extra rhetoric.

Trusting the Historia Augusta. It is a late fourth-century fiction on a factual spine. Use it only where Dio, Herodian, coins or inscriptions confirm it.

Reading Roman myth only through Ovid. Greek module 0.1 warned against treating Ovid as Greek; the Roman trap is the mirror image. The Metamorphoses ends with Roman stories, Vertumnus and Pomona (book 14) and Numa as Pythagoras' pupil (book 15), a link Livy rejects as chronologically impossible (1.18.2); the Fasti, an often playful calendar poem, covers only January to June. Roman myth lives in Livy, Dionysius, Virgil, Propertius book 4, the antiquarians, cult and topography.

Self-test

  1. Why did Fabius Pictor write Roman history in Greek around 200 BC?
    Answer

    Latin had no prose tradition; Greek was the language of history and of the Mediterranean public whose opinion mattered during the Hannibalic war, and Fabius was answering pro-Carthaginian Greek historians such as Philinus and Silenus.

  2. What does Dionysius 1.6.2 reveal about the shape of the earliest Roman histories, and why does it matter?
    Answer

    They were detailed on the origins and on their own times but summary on the centuries between. The full year-by-year narrative of the early Republic in Livy must therefore have been added later, largely by the annalists.

  3. What did Cicero and Livy say had corrupted the record of early Rome?
    Answer

    Family funeral eulogies and the captions on ancestral masks, which invented triumphs, consulships and genealogies (Cicero, Brutus 62; Livy 8.40.4–5); Livy adds the loss of records in the Gallic fire (6.1.2).

  4. How does Cicero's eclipse passage (On the Republic 1.25) undermine trust in the pontifical chronicle's early entries?
    Answer

    It shows that the first eclipse actually recorded fell around 400 BC and that earlier ones, including one under Romulus, were calculated backwards. The early part of the chronicle was itself reconstructed.

  5. What does Livy's preface claim about legend, and where does he draw the line?
    Answer

    He will neither affirm nor refute stories from before the foundation, which suit poetry better than history (Preface 6–7). From Romulus on he narrates as history, with hedges such as the rationalised she-wolf (1.4.7).

  6. Why is agreement between Livy and Dionysius not corroboration?
    Answer

    They wrote at the same time in the same city from the same annalistic sources, which Dionysius names (1.7.3). Two reports of one tradition are not two witnesses.

  7. What does the Lapis Niger prove, and what does it not?
    Answer

    It proves that a rex functioned in the Comitium around 570–550 BC, in contemporary Latin. It does not prove any named king of the tradition, and recei may mean the rex sacrorum.

  8. What does the Barbatus epitaph, set against Livy 10.12, teach about Roman sources?
    Answer

    A family record in stone and Livy's annalistic narrative disagree about where the consul of 298 fought. Either family display inflated the claim or the annalists confused the provinces; neither can be trusted by default.

  9. State Cornell's distinction and give one argument he uses for the authenticity of the early fasti.
    Answer

    Structural facts (institutions, cults, treaties, the sequence of kings) can be old; narrative detail is later elaboration. The plebeian names in the early consular list are not what a patrician forger would have inserted.

  10. Who exposed the Historia Augusta, and what did he show?
    Answer

    Hermann Dessau (1889): its six supposed authors of the Diocletianic and Constantinian age are one writer of the late fourth century who invented sources and documents.

Carry forward

  1. Keep the date of the event and the date of the source apart: everything before about 300 BC reached the page at least a century later, the kings five to eight centuries later, and the fullest accounts are the latest.
  2. Separate structure from narrative. Institutions, cults, treaties, monuments and names in the lists may preserve something old; speeches, motives, numbers and heroics belong to the annalists and their successors.
  3. Roman tradition is history-shaped and agenda-laden: ask whose family, faction or regime a story served when it was first told, and look for an outside control in an inscription, a coin, a Greek witness or a stratum.
