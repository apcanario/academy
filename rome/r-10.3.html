The inheritance: an Etruscan plan and a Greek grammar

Roman art was assembled from borrowed parts. Etruria gave the temple (module 1.2): a high podium, a frontal stair and a deep porch with columns only at the front, the form Vitruvius calls Tuscan (4.7). Pliny reports Varro's claim that the Greek modellers and painters Damophilus and Gorgasus decorated the temple of Ceres, dedicated by tradition in 493 BC, and that before it everything in Roman temples was Tuscan (Natural History 35.154): an antiquarian's claim, made some four and a half centuries later. Greece gave the orders, sculpture and painting, whose grammar Greek module 9.1 sets out.

The Roman synthesis put Greek columns on the Etruscan podium. The temple of Portunus in the Forum Boarium, of about 100 BC, is raised and frontal, its Ionic columns free only in the porch and engaged round the cella; beside it a round temple in Pentelic marble is a Greek building on Roman ground. Velleius makes Metellus Macedonicus' temple of Jupiter Stator, built after 146 BC, the first of marble in Rome (1.11.5). Rome added the plain Tuscan column and the Composite capital, Ionic volutes over Corinthian acanthus, whose earliest prominent surviving use is on the Arch of Titus.

The bronze Capitoline Wolf, long the textbook Etruscan casting, warns against dating on style alone: module 1.4 gives the case for the eleventh or twelfth century AD.

The republican portrait: verism and the ancestors

The portrait that looks most Roman is the head of an old man, bald, furrowed and hollow-cheeked, which modern scholars call veristic. It is a claim more than a likeness: age, toil and severity, the virtues of a man who has held office. Most such heads date to the first century BC, and freedmen soon borrowed the manner for the grave reliefs of husband and wife described in module 9.3.

The ancestors explain why a face mattered. The funeral masks, and Flower's argument that they were made in life, are module 2.4's. The Barberini Togatus in the Centrale Montemartini, of the last decade BC, shows the custom in marble: a man in a toga holds the portrait busts of two ancestors. His own head is ancient but not the statue's. He claims descent as the funeral did, by displaying faces.

Where sources disagree. A. N. Zadoks-Josephus Jitta (1932) and H. Drerup (1980) derived verism from the ancestral masks. Gisela Richter (Journal of Roman Studies, 1955) credited Greek sculptors working for Roman patrons, and R. R. R. Smith (Journal of Roman Studies, 1981) read the style as a Greek artistic response to a new kind of foreign patron. Chronology is the difficulty: the masks were displayed for generations before the style appears, so they cannot by themselves explain why it arose in the first century BC.

Greek statues in Roman settings: what "Roman copy" means

Rome met Greek art first as plunder: Marcellus, Paullus and Verres are modules 4.3, 4.4 and 5.3. Velleius' Mummius warned the contractors shipping the masterpieces of Corinth that if they lost any they would have to supply new ones (1.13.4): a Roman who could not tell an original from a replacement. A market followed. Cicero had Atticus buy statues suited to a gymnasium for his villa at Tusculum (Letters to Atticus 1.8–1.10), and scolded Fadius Gallus for buying him Bacchants and a Mars, useless to a man of peace (Letters to Friends 7.23).

Greek module 9.1 explains how Roman marbles preserve lost bronzes; the bronze Boxer in the Palazzo Massimo, a Hellenistic original found on the Quirinal, shows what the marbles lost. The Roman side adds four points. Versions changed scale, material and setting, chosen for the room. Greek bodies took Roman heads. Workshops made new works in old styles: Pasiteles wrote five books on famous works (Pliny 36.39–40), and his pupil Stephanus signed an athlete in the manner of four centuries before. And colossal versions furnished imperial buildings. The Farnese Hercules from the Baths of Caracalla (module 7.4) is signed by Glykon of Athens after a Lysippan type; the Farnese Bull is usually taken as a version of the group of Zethus, Amphion and Dirce by Apollonius and Tauriscus that Pliny saw in Asinius Pollio's collection (36.34). For a Roman patron a type was a word in a vocabulary, used in a sentence of his own.

Reading historical relief

Greek public sculpture told myths; Roman relief told what Romans had done. Its ancestor was the triumphal painting: Manius Valerius Messalla showed his Sicilian victory of 263 BC on the side of the Curia, and Lucius Hostilius Mancinus won a consulship by explaining his painting of Carthage under siege to the crowd (Pliny, Natural History 35.22–23).

The Ara Pacis (module 6.2 for its figures) takes the procession of the Parthenon frieze (Greek module 9.1) and makes the marchers a living family. Figures in front are cut deep and those behind shallow, so that a crowd stands in a few centimetres of marble; inside, the walls carve the wooden fence of the ritual enclosure hung with garlands and ox skulls, a rite turned to stone. The Arch of Titus (module 7.2) shows in its passage the spoils of Jerusalem carried through a gate seen at an angle, and Titus in his chariot crowned by Victory. The procession swings into and out of the plane, and Franz Wickhoff (Die Wiener Genesis, 1895) made this illusionism his proof that Roman art was not decadent Greek art. In the vault an eagle carries Titus to heaven.

The columns. Trajan's spiral frieze unrolls both Dacian wars up a column of a hundred Roman feet, in 155 scenes by Conrad Cichorius' numbering. Marching, building, sacrifice and speeches outnumber battles, and the emperor is the hinge of each episode (modules 7.3 and 9.2 for its value as evidence). The column of Marcus, finished by 193, keeps the form and changes the tone: deeper cutting and drilled shadows that carry further, an emperor more often frontal, villages burned and prisoners beheaded. The Arch of Septimius Severus (module 7.4) maps the Parthian campaigns on four panels in stacked registers seen from above, like the battle paintings Herodian says Severus sent to Rome (3.9.12). On his arch at Lepcis Magna he and his sons ride in a chariot facing straight out: the frontal ruler of late antiquity has arrived.

Read any such relief in four steps. Find the emperor: he is largest, central or frontal, and the action turns on him. Name the ritual, since most scenes belong to a small repertory. Separate persons from personifications: Victory, Roma, Virtus or the Genius of the Senate turn an event into a statement of value. Then look for recut heads.

Where sources disagree. Paul Veyne ("Conduct without belief and works of art without viewers", 1988) argued that above its lowest turns Trajan's frieze was invisible, the column working by height and presence alone. Penelope Davies (American Journal of Archaeology, 1997) answered that it was a tomb as well as a trophy, meant to be walked round as mourners circled a pyre, and that the circling viewer completed its meaning.

Painted walls and mosaic floors

August Mau's four Pompeian styles (1882), anticipated by Vitruvius (7.5.1–4), still organise Roman wall painting. The First, about 200–80 BC, models the wall as blocks of coloured marble in painted stucco (House of the Faun). The Second, about 80–15 BC, paints architecture that opens the wall into depth, as at Boscoreale, or dissolves it, as in the garden room from Livia's villa at Prima Porta, an orchard full of birds now in the Palazzo Massimo. The Third closes the wall again into flat red or black fields with spindly columns and small framed pictures (the black room from Boscotrecase). The Fourth, about AD 50–79, combines the others in theatrical fantasies (House of the Vettii). Vitruvius condemned reeds in place of columns and monsters in place of real things (7.5.3–4).

The Golden House (module 7.1) took the Fourth style into the palace. Pliny calls its painter Famulus, Fabullus in some manuscripts, grave and severe yet florid, a man who painted in his toga and for whom the palace was the prison of his art (35.120). Rediscovered in its buried "grottoes" around 1480, the ornament gave Europe the word grotesque.

The Villa of the Mysteries outside Pompeii has the grandest Second-style room, of about 60 BC, its life-size frieze on a red ground: Silenus with a lyre, satyrs with a bowl and a mask, Dionysus in Ariadne's lap, a woman unveiling a covered object, a winged figure whipping a kneeling girl, a bride dressing her hair.

Where sources disagree. Amedeo Maiuri (La Villa dei Misteri, 1931) read the frieze as a woman's initiation into the Dionysiac mysteries. Paul Veyne (Les mystères du gynécée, 1998, with François Lissarrague and Françoise Frontisi-Ducroux) argued that it shows a secular wedding day, the bride's preparation set beside the divine couple, not a secret rite.

Mosaic began as Hellenistic floor art. Pliny names Sosus of Pergamum, whose "unswept room" showed the debris of a meal and a dove drinking from a bowl (36.184), and dates figured pavements at Rome from Sulla's in the temple of Fortune at Praeneste (36.189). The Alexander Mosaic from the House of the Faun, of about 100 BC, renders a lost painting, perhaps the battle of Alexander and Darius that Philoxenus of Eretria painted for Cassander (35.110). Imperial Italy favoured black-and-white floors, as at Ostia; Africa and Sicily made polychrome carpets of hunts and games, as in the villa at Piazza Armerina of the early fourth century. Pliny already knew glass mosaic rising from floors to vaults (36.189), where it became the medium of the Christian apse.

Concrete, arch and dome

Roman architecture changed when builders learned to pour. Concrete, rubble in lime mortar, set hard even under the sea when mixed with the volcanic dust of Puteoli, Vitruvius says (2.6.1). Faced with stone or brick, it freed the plan from post and lintel into barrel vaults, cross vaults and domes, so that the interior became the subject: the domed octagon of the Golden House and Domitian's palace on the Palatine led to the Pantheon.

Rome also made the arch a system. The Tabularium of 78 BC framed its arches with engaged half-columns, a Greek order used as a picture of structure on a structure of arches; the Theatre of Marcellus and the Colosseum stacked the motif in Tuscan, Ionic and Corinthian storeys. The building types followed:

  • The temple on a podium, set axially at the end of a square, as Mars Ultor closed the Forum of Augustus (module 6.2).
  • The basilica, a roofed hall for courts and business, its nave lit by windows above the aisles. Cato built the Basilica Porcia in 184 BC (Livy 39.44.7); Maxentius vaulted the type in concrete, and Constantine's churches took its timber-roofed form.
  • The bath, cold, warm and hot rooms over heated floors, symmetrical round a vaulted hall, probably from Nero's baths and at full scale from Trajan's. The cross-vaulted cold hall of Diocletian's baths survives as Michelangelo's church of Santa Maria degli Angeli (module 9.4 for bathing).
  • The amphitheatre, an oval of seats, banked on earth at Pompeii and carried on radial vaults in the Colosseum (modules 7.2 and 9.4).

The Pantheon, Hadrian's rebuilding with Agrippa's name on the porch, probably begun under Trajan (module 7.3), sets a porch of sixteen granite columns before a drum carrying a concrete dome whose span equals its interior height. The aggregate grades from heavy stone at the base to light pumice at the top, stepped rings weigh down the haunches, five rings of twenty-eight coffers lighten the shell, and the oculus is the only window. A block between porch and drum shows a second pediment above the first. Paul Davies, David Hemsoll and Mark Wilson Jones (Art History, 1987) argued that the porch was designed for fifty-foot shafts and built with forty-foot ones.

Where sources disagree. Dio, who knew the Hadrianic building, could not say why it was called the Pantheon: perhaps for its statues of many gods, but in his own view because its vaulted roof resembled the heavens (53.27.2). William MacDonald (The Pantheon, 1976) developed the cosmic reading; against a temple of all the gods stand the absence of any such cult and Dio's report that Hadrian held court there (69.7.1).

The imperial portrait and its erasure

The emperor's face was the most reproduced image of antiquity: a type designed at the centre, copied in marble, bronze and paint, and struck on every coin. Modern scholars sort the replicas by the locks over the forehead. Augustus kept one ageless type for four decades (module 6.2). Style followed policy. Vespasian's lined, balding head answered Nero's fleshy curls; Hadrian's full beard, which the Historia Augusta says hid blemishes (Hadrian 26.1), set the fashion until Constantine; Caracalla scowls like a soldier. Constantine returned to a clean-shaven, Trajanic face, and his colossal head in the Capitoline courtyard looks upward with enormous eyes, as Eusebius says his coins did (Life of Constantine 4.15).

What was made could be unmade. At Domitian's death the Senate ordered his inscriptions erased and his memory abolished (Suetonius, Domitian 23.1), and Pliny recalled the pleasure of dashing his proud faces to the ground (Panegyricus 52.4–5). Marble was too costly to waste, so heads were recut: on one of the Cancelleria reliefs Domitian setting out for war now wears Nerva's face. Geta's erasure is module 7.4's. Damnatio memoriae is an early modern phrase, not a Roman penalty; Harriet Flower (The Art of Forgetting, 2006) speaks instead of memory sanctions varied case by case, and a scarred inscription or a blank face kept the condemned in view.

Sarcophagi: myth for the dead

When the elite turned from burning to burial (module 2.4 for the change), carved marble chests became, from Hadrian's reign, the main field of mythological art. The myths were of death, loss and love: Meleager carried from the hunt, Endymion in endless sleep, Persephone seized by Hades, Achilles holding the dying Penthesilea, Medea, Phaedra. The dead entered the myth, hero and heroine carved with the portrait features of the couple inside, or with faces left blocked out on stock awaiting a buyer. The third century added battle chests, like the Great Ludovisi of about 250–260 in the Palazzo Altemps, a mass of Romans and barbarians round a bareheaded general. Christians kept the format: Junius Bassus' sarcophagus of 359 sets biblical scenes in columned niches.

Where sources disagree. Franz Cumont (Recherches sur le symbolisme funéraire des Romains, 1942) read the myths as allegories of the soul's journey and its hope of immortality. Arthur Darby Nock ("Sarcophagi and Symbolism", 1946) replied that most were conventional decoration from which belief cannot be read. Paul Zanker and Björn Ewald (Mit Mythen leben, 2004) turned to the mourners: the myths praised the dead and consoled the living, like a funeral speech in stone.

Late antique style: change, not decline

Around 300 the image changes. The porphyry Tetrarchs in Venice (module 8.2) are squat, cubic and nearly identical, with staring eyes and hard planes of drapery: the subject is the concord of four rulers, not four faces, and the purple stone itself carries meaning. On the Arch of Constantine (module 8.3) old and new meet. Above, Hadrian's roundels show elegant hunters in airy space; below, the Constantinian frieze sets the emperor frontal and central, speaking from the Rostra and distributing money, between rows of short figures of equal height, rank marked by size. Against the roundels it looks clumsy. On its own terms it is a diagram of order, legible at a distance. The same aims, frontality, hierarchy and light rather than mass, made the apse mosaic and Hagia Sophia's dome floating on its ring of windows (module 8.6).

Where sources disagree. Bernard Berenson (The Arch of Constantine, or the Decline of Form, 1954) saw in the frieze the end of classical competence. Alois Riegl (Spätrömische Kunstindustrie, 1901) had argued that late Roman art pursued a different aim, and Ranuccio Bianchi Bandinelli traced its squat, frontal figures to a "plebeian" strand in Roman relief. The reuse, once put down to haste, Jaś Elsner (Papers of the British School at Rome, 2000) read as a deliberate claim on Trajan, Hadrian and Marcus.

Self-test

  1. What did the Roman temple take from Etruria and what from Greece, and which building in Rome shows the combination?
    Answer

    From Etruria the high podium, frontal stair, deep porch and axial approach (Vitruvius 4.7; module 1.2); from Greece the orders, marble and ornament (Greek module 9.1). The temple of Portunus in the Forum Boarium sets Ionic columns, free in the porch and engaged round the cella, on an Etruscan podium.

  2. Why is the veristic portrait better read as a claim than as evidence of Roman honesty?
    Answer

    Its wrinkles and baldness advertise age, office and severity, and the heads cluster in the first century BC, when freedmen soon copied the manner. Whether it came from the ancestral masks (Zadoks-Josephus Jitta, Drerup) or from Greek sculptors working for Romans (Richter, Smith), it was a chosen style.

  3. What is wrong with the phrase "Roman copy"?
    Answer

    It implies one Greek original and a servile maker. Roman workshops changed scale, material and setting, put Roman heads on Greek bodies, made new works in old styles (Pasiteles and Stephanus) and colossal versions such as the Farnese Bull, usually taken as a version of the group Pliny saw in Pollio's collection (36.34); patrons chose types for a room, as Cicero's letters show.

  4. What did historical relief inherit from triumphal painting, and where is the inheritance plainest?
    Answer

    The display of a campaign to the people, as in Messalla's and Mancinus' pictures (Pliny 35.22–23). The four panels of the Arch of Septimius Severus map the Parthian war in bird's-eye registers, like the battle paintings Herodian says Severus sent to Rome (3.9.12).

  5. Give four steps for reading a Roman historical relief.
    Answer

    Find the emperor by size, centrality or frontality; name the ritual from the repertory (profectio, adventus, adlocutio, lustratio, clementia, largitio, triumph); separate real persons from personifications such as Victory or Roma; look for recut heads.

  6. How does the column of Marcus change Trajan's model?
    Answer

    It keeps the spiral frieze but cuts deeper, with drilled shadows that carry to the viewer, shows the emperor more often frontal, and dwells on brutality, burned villages and beheaded prisoners, where Trajan's frieze favours marching, building and sacrifice.

  7. What separates the Second Pompeian style from the Third, and what did Vitruvius dislike?
    Answer

    The Second opens the wall into painted architecture or dissolves it into a garden (Boscoreale, Livia's garden room, the Villa of the Mysteries); the Third closes it into flat coloured fields with thin columns and small framed pictures. Vitruvius condemned reeds in place of columns and monsters in place of real things (7.5.3–4).

  8. What let the Pantheon's dome stand?
    Answer

    Concrete made with Puteolan dust (Vitruvius 2.6.1), aggregate graded from heavy stone below to pumice above, stepped rings loading the haunches, coffers lightening the shell, and an open oculus that removes weight at the crown.

  9. How did Rome treat the images of a condemned emperor, and why did he not simply vanish?
    Answer

    Inscriptions were chiselled and statues smashed or recut as successors, as Domitian became Nerva on a Cancelleria relief (Suetonius, Domitian 23.1; Pliny, Panegyricus 52.4–5). A scarred inscription or blank face kept the dishonour visible, which is why Flower speaks of memory sanctions rather than a fixed penalty.

  10. Is the frieze of the Arch of Constantine evidence of decline?
    Answer

    Berenson thought so. Riegl argued that late Roman art pursued different aims, which the frieze's frontality, hierarchy and legibility at a distance share with the Tetrarchs; Bianchi Bandinelli traced the style to a long "plebeian" strand, and Elsner read the reused reliefs as a deliberate claim on the good emperors.

Carry forward

  1. Roman art speaks in borrowed parts recombined for Roman ends: an Etruscan plan with Greek orders, a Greek body with a Roman head, a Greek style chosen for what it says, verism for the republican noble and classicism for Augustus. Ask of every work what its manner claims.
  2. Images of rulers are ritual and type, not reportage: the relief shows the emperor in a fixed role, the portrait in a fixed type, and both were recut or erased when power changed, so read them as statements by whoever paid for them.
  3. Concrete turned architecture into the shaping of interior space, from the Golden House to the Pantheon and the vaulted basilica, and late antique style turned the image from illusion into sign; both are changes of aim, not failures of skill, and both lead to the church, the apse mosaic and Hagia Sophia.
