Brick and marble

Suetonius ends his survey of Augustus' building with the emperor's own boast. Rome had been unworthy of the empire's majesty and exposed to flood and fire; he improved it so far that he could justly claim

marmoream se relinquere, quam latericiam accepisset (Suetonius, Augustus 28.3)

that he left of marble a city he had found of brick. The inventory is his own: Res Gestae 19–21 lists the Curia, the temples of Apollo, the deified Julius, Jupiter Feretrius, Jupiter Tonans, Quirinus and Magna Mater, the Lupercal, the Capitol and Pompey's theatre restored without his name, aqueducts and roads, and a forum on his own land. Others built at his urging, Agrippa most of all (Suetonius, Augustus 29.5). Each project argued that the new order was the old one restored. Style is module 10.3, the walk module 9.5; this module reads the argument.

Where sources disagree. Suetonius takes the boast literally. Dio sets it among Augustus' last words and says he meant the strength of the empire, not its buildings (56.30.3–4). Since much Augustan building was tufa, travertine and concrete faced with marble, even the literal claim was partly a figure.

The house on the Palatine and the temple of Apollo

Octavian lived on the Palatine in the orator Hortensius' former house, modest, with short colonnades of Alban stone and no marble floors (Suetonius, Augustus 72.1). After Naulochus in 36 BC he bought neighbouring houses; when lightning struck part of the property, the haruspices declared that the god wanted it, and he made it public and vowed a temple to Apollo (Velleius 2.81.3; Suetonius, Augustus 29.3). Dedicated on 9 October 28 BC, it shared the hilltop with his house, and in its Greek and Latin libraries the ageing princeps often held the Senate.

Propertius describes it (2.31): the daughters of Danaus between the columns of a golden portico, Myron's cattle round the altar, ivory doors carved with the Gauls hurled from Parnassus and Niobe's dying children, and inside Apollo the lyre-player between his mother and sister. Actian Apollo (module 5.5) was now the princeps' neighbour, and his doors showed what befell those who insulted him.

The house became a monument too, with laurels at the doorposts and the oak crown over the door from 27 BC (Res Gestae 34.2). Pontifex maximus from 12 BC and bound to live in a public house, Augustus made part of his own public and gave the house of the rex sacrorum, which adjoined their quarters, to the Vestals (Dio 54.27.3); a shrine of Vesta was dedicated in his home, so that, in Ovid's arithmetic, Phoebus held one part, Vesta a second and Caesar the third (Fasti 4.949–954). Below stood the hut of Romulus and the Lupercal (module 1.4). The hill's name, Palatium, became the word for a ruler's residence.

The Forum of Augustus and Mars Ultor

Before Philippi in 42 BC Octavian vowed a temple to Mars the Avenger if he punished his father's murderers (Suetonius, Augustus 29.2; Ovid, Fasti 5.569–578). He built it, with a forum round it, on private land from the spoils of war (Res Gestae 21.1), keeping the square narrower than he wished rather than evict owners (Suetonius, Augustus 56.2). It was dedicated in 2 BC (Velleius 2.100.2), the year of the title Father of his Country, inscribed beneath his chariot group in the square (Res Gestae 35.1). There the Senate would debate wars and triumphs, governors set out and victors bring back their insignia (Suetonius, Augustus 29.2); the temple held the standards recovered from Parthia (Res Gestae 29.2). The colonnades' upper storey carried copies of the Erechtheum caryatids and shields with the head of Jupiter Ammon, the god of Alexander's sonship (Greek module 11.4).

The exedrae held the argument. On one side stood Aeneas carrying Anchises and leading Ascanius, with the Alban kings and the Julii; on the other Romulus with the spolia opima and the great men of the Republic (Ovid, Fasti 5.563–566; modules 1.3 and 1.4). Each statue stood above an inscription of name, offices and deeds; fragments survive, with copies at Arretium and Pompeii, Marius among them. Suetonius gives no total; he says Augustus honoured these leaders next to the gods and declared by edict that citizens should hold him, and later principes, to their standard (Augustus 31.5). The Julian descent from Venus and the Republic's roll of honour faced each other as one story, ending in the man in the chariot.

Where sources disagree. Suetonius and Ovid make Mars the avenger of Caesar. Dio says that in 20 BC a temple of Mars Ultor was decreed on the Capitol to receive the standards (54.8.3), and coins show a small round one; yet the Res Gestae puts the standards in the Forum temple. Ovid fuses the two causes, making Mars twice avenger (Fasti 5.579–596). Whether the Capitoline temple was ever built is uncertain.

The Campus Martius: Mausoleum, obelisk and Ara Pacis

In 28 BC Octavian built his tomb between the Via Flaminia and the Tiber (Suetonius, Augustus 100.4). Antony had asked to be buried in Alexandria (module 5.5); the largest tomb Rome had seen declared where the new dynasty belonged. Strabo describes a mound on a high marble base, planted with evergreens and crowned by a bronze Augustus (5.3.8). Marcellus entered it first in 23 BC, and the Res Gestae was set up in bronze before it (Suetonius, Augustus 101.4). Its models were probably both Italic tumuli and Hellenistic royal tombs, above all Alexander's, whose body Octavian had crowned in 30 BC, declining to view the Ptolemies because he had come to see a king, not corpses (18.1).

In 10 BC an obelisk from Heliopolis, dedicated to the Sun on the conquest of Egypt and now at Montecitorio, became the gnomon of a pavement inlaid with bronze rods on which its noon shadow measured the length of the days (Pliny, Natural History 36.72–73). In 1979–80 a stretch of the line was found under the Via di Campo Marzio, with Greek labels for signs of the zodiac and the seasons.

Where sources disagree. Edmund Buchner (Die Sonnenuhr des Augustus, 1982) reconstructed a vast sundial whose shadow reached the Ara Pacis on Augustus' birthday, 23 September, binding birth, Sun and peace. Peter Heslin (Journal of Roman Studies 2007), following Michael Schütz, argued for a meridian line only, the excavated stretch being Flavian; Bernard Frischer's computer simulations (2013–2017) found that Buchner's alignment fails.

On Augustus' return from Spain and Gaul in 13 BC the Senate decreed an altar of Augustan Peace, at which magistrates, priests and Vestals were to sacrifice every year (Res Gestae 12.2); it was dedicated on 30 January 9 BC (Ovid, Fasti 1.709–722). The peace was one won by victories (Res Gestae 13). On the long sides of the enclosure a life-size procession moves: Augustus with lictors, flamines in spiked caps, Agrippa veiled with a small boy clutching his toga, the women and children of the house. The short sides carry the Lupercal, Roma, a seated goddess and a sacrifice. The goddess holds two infants and fruit, with an ox and a sheep at her feet. The sacrificer is bearded and veiled, with attendants leading a sow and a shrine with two seated figures above: traditionally Aeneas sacrificing the sow of Lavinium, with the Penates in their shrine (Virgil, Aeneid 8.81–85). Reassembled in 1938 for Mussolini's bimillenary of Augustus' birth, away from its original site, the altar we see is a modern reconstruction.

Where sources disagree. Paul Rehak ("Aeneas or Numa?", Art Bulletin 2001) argued that the sacrificer is Numa, older, bearded and unarmed, the founder of peace and cult answering Romulus, the founder in war, opposite. For the goddess, Karl Galinsky argued for Venus (1966), Nancy de Grummond for Pax (1990) and Barbette Spaeth for Ceres (1994); the old name Tellus, Earth, survives, and many now read her as several at once.

The Prima Porta statue and the image of the princeps

The statue was found in 1863 at Livia's villa at Prima Porta, where an eagle had dropped into her lap a white hen holding the laurel sprig from which the triumphal laurels grew (Pliny, Natural History 15.136–137). It shows the general addressing his troops, cuirassed, right arm raised. But the feet are bare, as a hero's or god's, and a Cupid on a dolphin names his ancestress Venus. Stance and proportions follow Polykleitos' Doryphoros. Paul Zanker (The Power of Images in the Age of Augustus, 1988) read this classicism as policy: Octavian's early portraits had the agitation of Hellenistic kings, while the type adopted about 27 BC is calm and ageless, and stayed unchanged until his death at seventy-five. Suetonius' real man was short, with decayed teeth and eyebrows that met (Augustus 79). The breastplate, a Parthian handing back a standard, is module 6.4. Whether the marble copies a bronze of about 20 BC or was made for Livia after AD 14, when bare feet would suit a god, is uncertain.

The same man appears veiled for sacrifice on the Ara Pacis, and the golden shield set in the Curia named his courage, clemency, justice and piety (Res Gestae 34.2). General, priest and moral exemplar: the images assign roles, not powers.

The restored religion: temples, priesthoods, pontifex maximus

In 28 BC, consul for the sixth time, Octavian repaired eighty-two temples in the city, by his own count passing over none that needed it (Res Gestae 20.4). Livy calls him founder or restorer of every temple (4.20.7); Horace warns the Roman that he will pay for his fathers' sins until he rebuilds the gods' crumbling shrines (Odes 3.6.1–4). New temples tied gods to his life: he vowed Jupiter Tonans when lightning killed the slave lighting the way before his litter in Spain, and later called it Capitoline Jupiter's doorkeeper (Suetonius, Augustus 29.3, 91.2).

He sat in all four great colleges and among the fetials, Arvals and Titii (Res Gestae 7.3; the colleges are module 2.3). He revived the augury of Safety, the Flamen Dialis, the Lupercalia, the Secular Games and the Compitalia (Suetonius, Augustus 31.4), and had his name sung in the Salian hymn (Res Gestae 10.1). He refused to take the chief pontificate from the living Lepidus and was elected at his death in 12 BC by a crowd from all Italy (10.2); he then burned more than two thousand unreliable prophetic books (Suetonius, Augustus 31.1). In 8 BC Sextilis became Augustus (Macrobius, Saturnalia 1.12.35). Head of state and head of state religion were henceforth one man.

Where sources disagree. The Augustans wrote of decay: Varro composed his Divine Antiquities lest the gods perish through neglect (Augustine, City of God 6.2), and Livy laments the neglect of the gods in his own age (3.20.5). Beard, North and Price (Religions of Rome, 1998) treat the decline as largely the restorers' own story: republican nobles had competed for priesthoods and vowed temples, and Augustus rebuilt religion around himself.

The Secular Games of 17 BC

A saeculum was the longest human life, set at 110 years by the Sibylline oracle preserved in Zosimus (2.6) and by Horace (Carmen Saeculare 21–22). The republican games had honoured Dis Pater and Proserpina at night at the Tarentum in the Campus (Zosimus 2.1–3). In 17 BC the quindecimviri, with Augustus and Agrippa presiding, fixed the date, and the jurist Ateius Capito expounded the rules (Zosimus 2.4.2). The inscribed record, found by the Tiber in 1890 (CIL VI 32323), gives the programme. On three nights Augustus sacrificed by Greek rite to the Moerae, the Ilithyiae of childbirth and Terra Mater; on three days he and Agrippa offered to Jupiter and Juno on the Capitol, while 110 matrons prayed, and to Apollo and Diana on the Palatine. The prayers asked that the empire of the Roman people grow and that the Latin should always obey. Then twenty-seven boys and twenty-seven girls sang a hymn on the Palatine and the Capitol; Quintus Horatius Flaccus composed the song, the record notes (module 6.3). Underworld gods had given way to Fates, birth and Earth; expiation became the inauguration of an age, and the rite ended at the princeps' own Apollo.

Where sources disagree. Censorinus (De die natali 17) sets the quindecimviri's 110-year series, leading to 17 BC, beside that of Valerius Antias, Varro and Livy, with games in 249 and 149 or 146 BC, a century apart. Claudius reverted to the century in AD 47, and those who had seen Augustus' games laughed at the herald's promise of a spectacle no one had seen or would see again (Suetonius, Claudius 21.2).

Marriage, adultery and the failure of law

The Julian laws on the marriage of the orders and on adultery came in 18 BC (Dio 54.16.1–2); the Papian-Poppaean law of AD 9 was carried by two consuls who were both unmarried and childless (Dio 56.10.3). The unmarried could not inherit from outside their kin, the childless took half, and lapsed shares could fall to the treasury; parents gained precedence in office, and a freeborn mother of three escaped guardianship. Adultery became a public crime with its own court: the husband had to divorce or face a charge of pandering, and the guilty were relegated to islands (Digest 48.5). Horace's hymn asks the goddess to prosper the senators' decrees on marrying women (Carmen Saeculare 17–20). Equestrians demanded repeal; Augustus displayed Germanicus' children at them (Suetonius, Augustus 34.2). His daughter Julia was exiled for adultery in 2 BC (module 6.1) and his granddaughter in AD 8. Tacitus' verdict, from AD 20: marriage and child-rearing grew no commoner, and informers had made every household a target (Annals 3.25).

Where sources disagree. Propertius rejoices that a law which would have parted him from Cynthia was lifted (2.7); Gordon Williams took it for a marriage law withdrawn about 28 BC, while Ernst Badian ("A Phantom Marriage Law", 1985) denied that any such law existed or that the poem was evidence for one. On purpose, Dio's Augustus speaks of a shrinking citizen body (56.2–9), Tacitus of the treasury; Andrew Wallace-Hadrill (1981) noted that the sanctions work through inheritance, making the laws chiefly about how property passed within the propertied orders.

Genius, Lares and divus: the cult of the princeps

At Rome the princeps was honoured in existing forms. In 30 BC the Senate ordered libations to Octavian at all banquets (Dio 51.19.7), and Horace's farmer mingles his divine power with the Lares (Odes 4.5.31–35). Module 2.4 describes the crossroads reform of 7 BC and the freedmen serving the Lares Augusti and the Genius of Augustus; Dio adds that these officials wore magistrate's dress with two lictors in their own districts on set days (55.8.6–7). A household honoured its master's Genius; freedmen now honoured Augustus' across a city treated as his household, as the title Father of his Country made explicit.

Caesar's deification in 42 and his comet are modules 5.4 and 5.5. Octavian became son of a god, and in 29 BC dedicated the temple of the deified Julius on the site of the pyre, with prows from Actium (Dio 51.19.2, 51.22.2). For himself he refused a temple in Rome and melted his silver statues to pay for golden tripods for Apollo (Suetonius, Augustus 52); when Agrippa wished to set his statue in the Pantheon he declined, so Caesar stood inside and Augustus in the porch (Dio 53.27.3).

In 29 BC Octavian allowed Romans at Ephesus and Nicaea precincts to Roma and the deified Julius, and Greeks temples to himself and Roma at Pergamum and Nicomedia (Dio 51.20.6–7). In 9 BC Asia began its year on his birthday, the decree found at Priene calling the god's birth the beginning of good news for the world. At Lugdunum an altar of Roma and Augustus received an Aeduan priest (Livy, Periochae 139); it was dedicated in 12 BC according to the epitome and Dio (54.32.1), on 1 August 10 BC according to Suetonius (Claudius 2.1).

The idiom was Hellenistic. Greek cities had given kings cult for benefactions, as Athens did Antigonus and Demetrius in 307 (Greek module 7.1), and Smyrna gave the goddess Roma a temple in 195 BC (Tacitus, Annals 4.56). Augustus borrowed Alexander's apparatus with care: Alexander's head on his seal (Suetonius, Augustus 50), and a tale that Apollo as a serpent fathered him on Atia (94.4), answering Olympias' snake (Greek module 11.4). What he added was gradation: god in the East, Roma and Augustus in the provinces, a Genius at Rome, divinity at death. In AD 14 a senator swore he had seen him ascend, as Proculus had seen Romulus (Dio 56.46.2; module 1.4).

Where sources disagree. Suetonius says he refused such honours in the city (Augustus 52), and Dio that no one in Rome or Italy dared them (51.20.8). Tacitus' critics at the funeral say he wished to be worshipped with temples, images, flamines and priests (Annals 1.10.6), and the temple to Rome and Augustus at Pola, built in his lifetime within Augustan Italy, supports them. The line between Genius and person, city and province, leaked.

Self-test

  1. What did Augustus mean by leaving a city of marble, and how does Dio's version change the claim?
    Answer

    Suetonius takes it literally: Augustus adorned a city unworthy of the empire and boasted that he left it of marble (Augustus 28.3). Dio sets it among his last words and says he meant the strength of the empire, not its buildings (56.30.3–4).

  2. Why did the princeps' house on the Palatine become a religious site after 12 BC?
    Answer

    As pontifex maximus he had to live in a public house, so Augustus made part of his own public, gave the house of the rex sacrorum, which adjoined their quarters, to the Vestals (Dio 54.27.3) and housed a shrine of Vesta, so that Apollo, Vesta and Caesar shared it (Ovid, Fasti 4.949–954). His temple of Apollo already stood beside it, built on ground struck by lightning.

  3. What did the two exedrae of the Forum of Augustus show, and what argument did they make?
    Answer

    Aeneas carrying Anchises with the Alban kings and the Julii on one side, Romulus with the spolia opima and the great men of the Republic on the other (Ovid, Fasti 5.563–566). Family and Republic became one history culminating in Augustus, who by edict made them the standard for himself and his successors (Suetonius, Augustus 31.5).

  4. Why is Mars Ultor twice an avenger?
    Answer

    He was vowed at Philippi as avenger of Caesar, and the standards recovered from Parthia were later housed in his Forum temple (Res Gestae 29.2). Ovid combines the two (Fasti 5.579–596), though Dio reports a separate Capitoline temple decreed for the standards in 20 BC (54.8.3).

  5. What is the case for reading the sacrificer on the Ara Pacis as Numa rather than Aeneas?
    Answer

    Rehak (2001) pointed out that the figure is older, bearded and unarmed, and that Numa, founder of peace and cult, would answer Romulus, the founder in war, on the opposite panel. The traditional reading sees Aeneas sacrificing the sow of Lavinium, with the Penates in their shrine.

  6. What did Buchner claim about the obelisk in the Campus Martius, and what have his critics said?
    Answer

    That it was the gnomon of a vast sundial whose shadow reached the Ara Pacis on Augustus' birthday. Schütz and Heslin argue from Pliny (Natural History 36.72–73) and the excavation for a meridian line, the excavated stretch being Flavian, and Frischer's simulations found that the birthday alignment fails.

  7. Which features of the Prima Porta statue go beyond the portrait of a general?
    Answer

    The bare feet of a hero or god, the Cupid on a dolphin naming Venus as ancestress, and the Polykleitan model that makes the face calm and ageless. Zanker read this classicism as a deliberate break with the agitated Hellenistic royal portrait.

  8. How did the Secular Games of 17 BC change the republican rite?
    Answer

    The night offerings went to the Moerae, Ilithyiae and Terra Mater instead of Dis Pater and Proserpina, day sacrifices to Jupiter, Juno, Apollo and Diana were added, and the rite ended with Horace's hymn at Palatine Apollo (CIL VI 32323). A rite of expiation became the inauguration of an age, on a 110-year cycle built to fit 17 BC (Censorinus, De die natali 17).

  9. How did the marriage laws try to compel marriage, and why did Tacitus think they failed?
    Answer

    Mainly through inheritance: the unmarried could not inherit from outside their kin and the childless took half, while parents gained privileges. Tacitus says that by AD 20 marriage and children were no commoner and that informers had made every household a target (Annals 3.25).

  10. How did cult of the princeps differ between Rome and the provinces, and where did the provincial forms come from?
    Answer

    At Rome he received libations and Genius worship at the crossroads but no temple (Suetonius, Augustus 52); in the provinces Greeks had temples to Roma and Augustus at Pergamum and Nicomedia (Dio 51.20.6–7), and the Gauls an altar at Lugdunum. These continued the cults Greek cities had offered to Hellenistic kings (Greek module 7.1) and to Roma since 195 BC.

Carry forward

  1. Augustus argued through places: a house shared with Apollo and Vesta, a forum where Roman history ended in his chariot, and a Campus binding tomb, sun and peace. Read later imperial building as the same kind of claim.
  2. The religious restoration was a relocation: priesthoods, calendar, games and crossroads were renewed with the princeps at their centre, and from 12 BC the head of state religion and the head of state were one man.
  3. Ruler cult was graded by place and time: god in the Greek East, Roma and Augustus in the provinces, Genius and son of the deified at Rome, divus after death. Law could not achieve what images and ritual did, as the marriage laws showed in his own family.
