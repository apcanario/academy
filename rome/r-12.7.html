Augustan or anti-Augustan?

The ending, the 'two voices', and the long critical war over what the poem means.

01Where module 6.3 leaves the question

Module 6.3 sets out the frame and is not repeated here: Syme's account of Augustan literature as managed opinion (The Roman Revolution, 1939), Peter White's denial that there was any apparatus to manage it (Promised Verse, 1993), Karl Galinsky's culture of shared values (Augustan Culture, 1996), and Duncan Kennedy's argument (1992) that "Augustan" and "anti-Augustan" are readers' terms of reference rather than properties of texts. It also reads the poem's three prophecies, Jupiter's in book 1, Anchises' parade in book 6 and the shield in book 8, and ends on the observation that each writes its cost into its praise. This module keeps to the criticism of the Aeneid itself: who argued what about the poem's politics, on which passages, and what ancient readers thought.

The explicitly Augustan passages are few and placed with care. Jupiter grants the Romans empire without end (1.279) and foretells a Trojan Caesar and the gates of war shut on chained Furor (1.286–296). Anchises points out Augustus Caesar, divi genus, who will found again the golden age in Latium (6.791–793). Vulcan sets him at the centre of the shield, leading Italy into battle at Actium with the senate, the people, the Penates and the great gods (8.678–679), then seated at Apollo's threshold for the triple triumph (8.714–728). In book 3 Aeneas holds games at Actium, where Octavian would found his own (3.280; module 12.2). Donatus gives the design as a poem on the origin of Rome and of Augustus (Life 21; module 12.1), and Servius opens his commentary by stating Virgil's intention: to imitate Homer and to praise Augustus through his ancestors, Homerum imitari et Augustum laudare a parentibus. That was the schools' reading. The modern quarrel is over everything else in the poem: what its grief, its losses and its final killing do to the praise.

02The "Harvard school" and the pessimistic reading

The pessimistic reading was assembled in American journals and books between 1953 and 1965. R. A. Brooks's article on the golden bough, "Discolor aura" (1953), found the poem suspended in limbo, its mood a splendid despair. Adam Parry's "The Two Voices of Virgil's Aeneid" (Arion, 1963) gave the reading its formula: beside the "public voice of triumph" the poem keeps a "private voice of regret", which records what Rome cost. Parry heard it first in the catalogue of Italians, where the priest Umbro, killed in the war to come, is mourned by the grove of Angitia, the glassy water of the Fucine lake and the clear lakes (te nemus Angitiae, vitrea te Fucinus unda, 7.759–760): an Italian landscape weeping for an Italian whom the Trojans' arrival will destroy. Wendell Clausen's "An Interpretation of the Aeneid" (Harvard Studies in Classical Philology, 1964) called the poem a "long Pyrrhic victory of the human spirit" and observed that it ends with no sense of triumph. Michael Putnam's The Poetry of the Aeneid (1965) read four books, 2, 5, 8 and 12, through their repeated words and images, and made the ending the key to the whole.

Putnam's reading of the ending set the terms for everything after. Jupiter promises a Rome in which impious Furor sits chained (1.294–296); Anchises charges the Roman to spare the conquered and war down the proud (6.851–853). In the last lines Aeneas, set on fire by furies and terrible in anger (12.946–947), kills a beaten suppliant. On Putnam's reading the hero becomes what the poem has set against him, furor, the force of Juno and of Turnus, and the poem ends not with a city founded but with the victory of the passion the founding was meant to bind. He returned to the scene for half a century; in The Humanness of Heroes (2011) book 12 traces Aeneas' decline from a man famed for piety to the killer of a wounded enemy begging at his feet.

None of these critics called themselves a school. W. R. Johnson gave them the name in Darkness Visible (1976), dividing modern critics into a "Harvard" or pessimistic school (Brooks, Parry, Clausen, Putnam) and a "European" or optimistic one (T. S. Eliot, Viktor Pöschl, Karl Büchner, Brooks Otis), and joined neither: his own reading made the poem's darkness a matter of the human condition more than of Augustus. Eliot's Virgil, the classic of a civilisation, belongs to module 12.8.

Where sources disagree. Is the pessimistic reading a modern projection? Galinsky ("The Anger of Aeneas", American Journal of Philology, 1988) argued that condemning Aeneas' last act imposes on Virgil a wholesale disapproval of anger that Aristotle and most Romans did not share, and later called the Harvard school an overdue reaction that narrowed the debate to for or against ("Reflections of an Infidel", Classical World, 2017). Craig Kallendorf (The Other Virgil, 2007) traced a continuous tradition of "pessimistic" readings through early modern Europe and the Americas, and Lactantius had already denied that Aeneas was pious at all (below). Most of the founding papers also predate the escalation of the American war in Vietnam in 1965, by which the school is often explained.

03The optimistic or Augustan school

Viktor Pöschl's Die Dichtkunst Virgils (1950; in English as The Art of Vergil, 1962) read the poem through its images and symbols and found an Augustan order in them. The storm calmed by Neptune and the simile of the statesman (module 12.2 gives his reading) announce a poem in which chaos is subdued by pietas, and Turnus is the "dark demon of passion" the founder must overcome. Pöschl's method, close reading of recurring images, was the one the pessimists took over and turned against his conclusions. Brooks Otis's Virgil: A Study in Civilized Poetry (1963) analysed Virgil's "subjective style", a narrator who enters his characters' feelings, and read the poem as the moral education of its hero: the Aeneas of the first half, still Trojan and looking back, learns in book 6 what Rome will be and fights the second half as a man who has mastered himself. On that reading Dido and Turnus are the passions the Roman future requires him to overcome, and the poem's sadness is the price of a victory it endorses.

Galinsky's "The Anger of Aeneas" (1988) answered Putnam on his own ground, the last scene. The ending looks like a moral failure, he argued, only to a reader who assumes, with the Stoics, that all anger is a vice. Aristotle praises the man who is angry at the right things, with the right people, as and when and for as long as he ought (Nicomachean Ethics 1125b31–32), and Roman moralists did not condemn just anger. Aeneas' anger is roused by the belt of a boy entrusted to him by Evander, worn by the man who killed him wishing his father could watch (10.441–443). The killing is punishment, and the anger that drives it is the right emotion.

The Augustan reading has its later advocates. Hans-Peter Stahl (Poetry Underpinning Power, 2016) argued that the poem guides its Roman readers to side with pious Aeneas and draws Turnus and Dido as morally flawed opponents, Turnus above all as the impious one. David West, reviewing Putnam's 2011 book with Richard Tarrant's commentary on book 12 (2012), pointed out that no suppliant in the Iliad or the Aeneid is spared, and that Anchises' sentence does not stop at sparing the conquered: it goes on, et debellare superbos (6.853).

04Further voices and the end of the binary

R. O. A. M. Lyne's Further Voices in Vergil's Aeneid (1987) multiplied Parry's two. The poem has an epic voice, which tells the story as the regime would wish it told, and further voices, heard in allusion, simile, the narrator's interjections and the words given to characters, which add to that story, question it and at times undo it. Parry had heard one private sentiment beside the public; Lyne located many voices in Virgil's technique.

Richard Thomas's Virgil and the Augustan Reception (2001) turned the argument from the poem to its readers. Thomas holds that the Aeneid is ambivalent towards Augustus and that the Augustan Virgil is a construction, made by readers who needed one. Horace is its first Augustan reader; the commentators who explained difficulties away, the textual critics who emended or deleted uncomfortable lines (among them the gates of ivory and the Cato on the shield), Dryden, whose translation of 1697 removes ambiguity while claiming to keep the spirit, and the twentieth-century German classicists who were the Augustan Virgil's strongest advocates carried it on (module 12.8 has Virgil in fascist Italy). Thomas argued that a poet under an autocrat had reason to veil his criticism and that veiled speech was a recognised category of Roman rhetoric. His conclusion was more modest than his opening: the poem compels neither reading.

The binary has been attacked from three sides. Kennedy (module 6.3) made the labels positions of critics, so that a passage is Augustan or anti-Augustan only for a reader who has already decided what Augustus meant. Johnson had refused both camps from the start. Galinsky (2017) charged the Harvard school with reducing a complex poem to a single question. What replaced the binary in much recent work is ambivalence, the view that the poem holds both voices without resolving them, which Greek module 10.12 gives as the majority position. Its weakness is the mirror of its strength: a reading that can absorb any evidence is hard to test against any passage.

Where sources disagree. Where are the voices? Parry (1963) and Lyne (1987) place them in the text, in Virgil's own technique. Kennedy (1992) places the opposition in the reader: the same lines change sides with the reader's idea of Augustus. Thomas (2001) holds both: the poem is ambivalent, and its Augustan reading is a history of readers suppressing that ambivalence.

05Test case: the ending (12.950–952)

Module 12.3 tells the duel and the death, and module 12.6 reads the last act as pietas and furor meeting in one deed; here is the debate they hand on. The facts both sides use are few. Turnus, wounded, concedes the bride and the war and asks for his life or his body (12.930–938). Aeneas checks his hand, and the words begin to bend him (12.938–941). He sees Pallas' belt, is set on fire by furies and terrible in anger (12.946–947), says that it is Pallas who sacrifices him, immolat (12.949), and strikes:

hoc dicens ferrum adverso sub pectore condit / fervidus (12.950–951)

Saying this, he buries the sword in the breast before him, burning (12.950–951). The poem stops a line later (12.952; module 12.3), without a funeral, a marriage or a city.

The pessimistic reading counts the words. Furiis and ira are the vocabulary of Allecto, Juno and Turnus; immolat recalls the eight young men Aeneas took alive after Pallas' death to sacrifice at his pyre (10.517–520); and the killing of a man who has conceded breaks the first half of Anchises' charge (6.853). The Augustan reading counts the circumstances. Turnus' side broke the sworn treaty (module 12.3); Turnus killed a boy in Aeneas' care and wears his spoils; and Anchises' charge ends by warring down the proud. The hesitation itself, which Homer's Achilles never shows over Hector, proves the man who could have spared him and judged that he should not.

Where sources disagree. Putnam (The Poetry of the Aeneid, 1965; The Humanness of Heroes, 2011) reads the killing as the triumph of furor and a breach of Anchises' precept. Galinsky (1988) reads it as justified anger in Aristotle's sense, the punishment of Pallas' killer. Tarrant's commentary (2012) takes the anger as directed in some degree at Aeneas himself; West (2013) objected that this undercuts Aeneas' merciful hesitation at 12.940, which he called unique among the heroes of Homer and the Aeneid. Servius, on 12.940, had already found pietas on both sides of the hesitation (below).

06Test cases: Marcellus, the shield and Dido

Marcellus (6.860–886)

Module 6.3 places the passage at the end of the parade, module 12.1 tells how Octavia fainted at the reading (Donatus, Life 32), and module 12.5 takes book 6 as a whole. Aeneas sees a young man outstanding in beauty and shining arms, but with downcast eyes (6.861–862), and asks who he is. Anchises weeps: the fates will only show him to the earth, and the gods thought the Roman stock too powerful had this gift been lasting (6.869–871). He calls him pitiable boy, if only he could break the harsh fates, tu Marcellus eris (6.882–883), and asks for lilies to scatter, a gift he calls empty, inani munere (6.885–886). The Augustan reading hears the dynasty's grief made the nation's: the parade of Rome's future ends on the heir of the house, mourned by the founder's own father, and the gods' envy is a compliment to Rome's greatness. The pessimistic reading hears the parade of glory end at a grave: the future Aeneas has been shown breaks off, the man performing the rite calls it futile, and a few lines later Aeneas leaves the underworld by the gate of false dreams (6.893–898; module 12.5 weighs why). Both are possible because the passage was written as the regime's own mourning; the question is whether a lament inside a panegyric qualifies the panegyric or completes it.

The shield (8.626–731)

Module 6.3 reads the shield's Roman scenes, module 5.5 its Actium and module 12.3 what it changes in Homer's. For the debate it is the hardest case for the pessimists. The scenes run from the wolf and twins to the triple triumph with little irony: Antony comes with barbarian wealth and, the narrator exclaims, an Egyptian wife, nefas (8.685–688); Rome's gods fight the monstrous gods of Egypt (8.698–700); Apollo of Actium bends his bow (8.704–706). Pessimistic readers press three details instead. Catiline hangs in Tartarus while a Cato, probably Caesar's enemy, gives laws to the righteous (8.666–670). Actium, a civil war, is drawn as a foreign one. And Aeneas lifts the shield rejoicing in the images without knowing the events, rerumque ignarus imagine gaudet (8.730). The Augustan reading takes the same line as a climax of pietas: as he carried his father out of Troy, he now carries on his shoulder the fame and fates of his descendants (8.731).

Dido

Module 12.4 reads book 4 and weighs how far Dido stands for Cleopatra; the political question it hands on is what follows from her. On the Augustan reading Aeneas' departure is pietas obeyed at great cost, Dido is the passion and the East that Rome must leave behind, and her curse (4.607–629) is the aetiology of the Punic wars, Rome's founding enmity. On the pessimistic reading Dido is the mission's first great victim: a founder and lawgiver ruined by gods who use her, left by a man who never says he loves her, and her curse brings Hannibal into Italy. The case is the reverse of the shield. Here the sympathy is all with the victim, and the Augustan reading must argue that the sympathy is the price of the mission, not a judgement on it. Ancient readers took Dido's side in their feelings whatever they thought of the poem's politics.

07What ancient readers thought

Ovid. Ovid read the poem as Augustus' and as a love story. Defending his own verse to the emperor from exile, he calls Virgil the fortunate author of "your Aeneid", tuae Aeneidos auctor, who brought arms and the man into a Tyrian bed, and says that no part of the whole work is read more than that love joined by no lawful bond (Tristia 2.533–536; module 12.4 weighs the legal point). He rewrote book 4 from Dido's side in Heroides 7, gave her four lines in the Metamorphoses' compressed Aeneid (14.78–81) and made Aeneas a god (14.581–608); module 10.1 reads all three. Thomas counts Ovid, with Lucan (module 10.2), among the first anti-Augustan readers: "your" Aeneid points the emperor to the part of his own epic least like his moral legislation. The plainer reading is self-defence: a poet relegated for an erotic poem notes that the regime's epic is read for its love affair.

Servius. Servius' commentary, written about 400 (module 12.8 has it as a school text), begins from the intention to praise Augustus through his ancestors, and Thomas treats it as a systematically Augustan reading that explains difficulties away. It is more various than that. Servius takes the Caesar of 1.286 as Julius (module 6.3), hears Pompey's death in Priam's headless body on the shore (on 2.557; module 12.2), and on the gate of ivory remarks that the poet wants all that has been said to be understood as false (on 6.893). On the hesitation at 12.940 he praises both halves of the last act: in thinking of sparing his enemy Aeneas shows himself pius, and in killing him he shows pietas too, out of regard for Evander. Thomas also found in the commentary the dissenting readers, alii, whom Servius names in order to dismiss: evidence that the poem's difficulties were felt in the schools.

Macrobius. The Saturnalia, a dialogue of the fifth century in which Servius appears as a young guest, makes Virgil an authority in every field: his religious law, his rhetoric and his borrowings from Homer and the Latin poets fill books 3 to 6. It preserves Virgil's letter to Augustus on the size of his undertaking (1.24.11; module 12.1), but it treats the poem as an encyclopaedia of learning, not as an argument about the princeps. Its sharpest remark on the poem's power is about Dido. Eustathius observes that the story of wanton Dido is known by everyone to be false, yet for so many centuries it has kept the appearance of truth: painters, sculptors and weavers take it as their subject, and actors perform it in gesture and song (Saturnalia 5.17.4–6). Beauty, on his account, has outweighed history.

Lactantius. One ancient reader went further than any Harvard critic. Lactantius, writing the Divine Institutes early in the fourth century, attacked Aeneas' epithet: a man who sacrificed captives to the dead and killed men who did not resist, even a suppliant who begged by his father's shade and Iulus' rising hopes (Aeneid 10.524), was not pious, and was called so only because he loved his father (Divine Institutes 5.10). Lactantius' target was pagan virtue, not Augustus; but the question the pessimists asked, whether the hero deserves his title, was asked in antiquity.

Where sources disagree. Against Servius' note on 12.940, Lactantius (Divine Institutes 5.10), arguing from the suppliants of book 10, denies Aeneas pietas altogether. Richard Gaskin ("On Being Pessimistic about the End of the Aeneid", Harvard Studies in Classical Philology, 2020) notes that Servius frames the hesitation as a conflict between two kinds of pietas, not as mercy or clemency set against duty.

08Self-test

  1. What did Ovid call the Aeneid in Tristia 2, and what has Thomas made of the phrase?
    Answer

    "Your Aeneid", tuae Aeneidos, addressed to Augustus, whose epic, Ovid says, is read above all for an unlawful love (2.533–536). Thomas reads it as pointing the emperor to the part of his own epic least like his moral legislation and counts Ovid among the first anti-Augustan readers; the plainer reading is self-defence.

  2. What are Parry's two voices, and how did Clausen sum up the poem?
    Answer

    A "public voice of triumph" and a "private voice of regret" for what Rome cost, first heard in the lament for Umbro (7.759–760) (Parry, 1963). Clausen (1964) called the poem a long Pyrrhic victory of the human spirit, ending with no sense of triumph.

  3. Why is it hard to dismiss the pessimistic reading as a product of the Vietnam years?
    Answer

    Most founding papers (Brooks 1953, Parry 1963, Clausen 1964) predate the escalation of 1965; Kallendorf (2007) traced pessimistic readings through early modern culture; and Lactantius (Divine Institutes 5.10) already denied Aeneas his pietas.

  4. On what grounds did Galinsky defend the killing of Turnus?
    Answer

    That the ending looks like a moral failure only if all anger is a vice, which is the Stoic position; Aristotle praises anger at the right things and for the right length of time (Nicomachean Ethics 1125b31–32), and Aeneas' anger at Pallas' killer is just (1988).

  5. How does Putnam read the ending against Jupiter's prophecy and Anchises' charge?
    Answer

    Jupiter promises Furor chained (1.294–296) and Anchises orders the Roman to spare the conquered (6.853); Aeneas, set on fire by furies (12.946–947), kills a suppliant, so the poem ends with furor victorious in its hero rather than with a city founded.

  6. How does Lyne's model differ from Parry's, and what objection does Kennedy's position raise against both?
    Answer

    Lyne (1987) hears an epic voice and many further voices, located in allusion, simile and the narrator's interjections, where Parry heard one private voice beside the public. Kennedy (1992) puts the opposition in the reader: whether a line is Augustan depends on the critic's idea of Augustus, not on the line.

  7. What does Thomas mean by "the Augustan reception", and give two of its instances.
    Answer

    The tradition of readers who built an unambiguous Augustan Virgil by suppressing the poem's ambivalence (2001). Instances: Horace as first Augustan reader, commentators explaining difficulties away, textual critics deleting lines such as the gates of ivory, Dryden's translation of 1697, twentieth-century German classicists.

  8. Why can the Marcellus passage serve both camps?
    Answer

    It was written as the dynasty's mourning, so it can be read as the heir's grief made Rome's, the gods' envy a compliment (6.869–871). It can also be read as the parade of glory ending at a grave, with a rite its performer calls empty (6.885–886), just before the exit by the false gate (6.893–898).

  9. Why is the shield the hardest test case for pessimists, and what do they press?
    Answer

    Its scenes celebrate Actium and the triumph with little irony (8.685–728). Pessimists press Cato giving laws among the righteous (8.666–670), civil war drawn as foreign war, and Aeneas rejoicing in images he does not understand (8.730).

  10. How did Servius and Lactantius judge Aeneas' pietas?
    Answer

    Servius (on 12.940) found pietas both in the wish to spare Turnus and in the killing done for Evander. Lactantius (Divine Institutes 5.10) denied it outright: a man who sacrificed captives and killed suppliants was called pious only for loving his father.

09Carry forward

  1. "Augustan" and "pessimistic" are readings of the same passages, not lists of different ones: the ending, Marcellus, the shield and Dido each carry both, because the poem writes its costs into its praise. Ask of any passage what it does with the cost it records.
  2. The ending is the crux. One act is described in the words of pietas and of furor, and every reading of the poem's politics comes down to whether Aeneas' anger is justice, as Galinsky argued, or the furor Jupiter chained, as Putnam argued.
  3. The quarrel is as old as the readers. Ovid, Servius and Lactantius already divide over the poem, and the Augustan reading has its own history of construction (Thomas); before trusting a verdict on Virgil's politics, ask who is reading, when, and why.
