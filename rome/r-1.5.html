The seven and who tells them

The canonical list runs from Romulus to Tarquinius Superbus: seven kings across 244 years, 753–509 BC on the Varronian count (module 0.3 has the variants). Every narrative is late. The first Roman historian, Fabius Pictor, wrote in Greek around 200 BC, three centuries after the last king and five after the first; he survives only in fragments quoted by later writers. Livy's Book 1 dates from about 27–25 BC, Dionysius of Halicarnassus' Roman Antiquities books 2–4 from 7 BC on, Cicero's sketch in On the Republic 2 from the 50s BC, Plutarch's Numa from around AD 100. Livy admits that most records perished when the Gauls burned the city (6.1.2). Between the kings and their historians lie family tradition, antiquarian aetiology, priestly lists of uncertain age, and monuments that carried a king's name.

Romulus to Ancus: founder, priest, warrior, builder

Romulus (his story is module 1.4) is the author of the political frame. He creates a hundred senators, the patres, whose descendants are the patricians (Livy 1.8.7); twelve lictors on the Etruscan model (1.8.2–3); thirty curiae named after the Sabine women and three centuries of cavalry, Ramnenses, Titienses and Luceres (1.13.6–8); and a bodyguard of 300 Celeres (1.15.8). The curiae were real: they voted the lex curiata into the late Republic, by then through thirty lictors, and the few names known, Faucia (Livy 9.38.15), Foriensis and Veliensis, look like places or clans, not abducted brides. Varro reports that the three tribe names were Etruscan (On the Latin Language 5.55).

Numa, chosen after a year's interregnum, is the priest-king: inaugurated by an augur on the Capitol (Livy 1.18.6–10), he closes the gate of Janus (1.19.2), divides the year into twelve lunar months with intercalation and fixes the days for public business (1.19.6–7), and founds the flamines of Jupiter, Mars and Quirinus, the Vestals, the twelve Salii and the pontiff (1.20). His authority comes from night meetings with the nymph Egeria in a grove outside the Porta Capena (1.21.3; Plutarch, Numa 4, 8). The institutions are genuinely archaic; unit 2 (module 2.3) treats them. Their gathering under one founder is the move Sparta made with Lycurgus (Greek module 5.3). The "books of Numa" found on the Janiculum in 181 BC and burned (Livy 40.29.3–14) show claims on him still being made in the Republic.

Where sources disagree. Some made Numa a pupil of Pythagoras. Livy 1.18.2 and Cicero, On the Republic 2.28–29, reject it on chronology, since Pythagoras came to Italy under Servius Tullius (Livy) or Tarquinius Superbus (Cicero); Plutarch, Numa 1 and 8, reports both sides. The legend probably grew when Rome set up a statue of Pythagoras in the Comitium during the Samnite wars (Pliny, Natural History 34.26).

Tullus Hostilius, "fiercer even than Romulus" (Livy 1.22.2), fights Alba Longa. The war is settled by champions, three Roman Horatii against three Alban Curiatii; one Horatius survives (1.24–25). He kills his sister for mourning her betrothed Curiatius, is condemned for perduellio, appeals to the people and is acquitted (1.26), which supplies aetiologies for provocatio and the Tigillum Sororium, the "sister's beam" he passed under in expiation. When the Alban dictator Mettius Fufetius betrays Rome, Tullus has him torn apart by chariots (1.28), razes Alba except its temples, moves the Albans to the Caelian and builds the Curia Hostilia (1.29–30). He dies by Jupiter's lightning after a botched rite (1.31.8). Alba itself is module 1.3.

Where sources disagree. Livy himself says sources differ on which triplets were Roman, though most call the Horatii Roman (1.24.1). The combat of champions is a type: Herodotus 1.82 has 300 Spartans fight 300 Argives for Thyrea. Georges Dumézil read Horatius as the Indo-European warrior who defeats a triple foe and must be purified of his fury; critics prefer Roman aetiology of a beam, a tomb and a legal procedure.

Ancus Marcius, Numa's grandson, combines both predecessors (Livy 1.32). He institutes the fetial ritual for declaring war (1.32.5–14), conquers Latin towns and settles their people on the Aventine, fortifies the Janiculum, builds the wooden Pons Sublicius and the prison (1.33.1–8), and carries Roman territory to the sea, founding Ostia and its salt-works (1.33.9). The excavated castrum at Ostia is no older than the fourth century BC, so a regal Ostia, if real, was control of the salt beds at the river mouth rather than a town. The plebeian Marcii Reges claimed descent from him (Suetonius, Julius 6.1) and had reason to enlarge him.

The Tarquins and Servius in the tradition

Tarquinius Priscus is a Corinthian's son. Demaratus, a Bacchiad, fled when Cypselus seized Corinth (Greek module 5.3), settled at Tarquinii and married there (Livy 1.34.2; Dionysius 3.46–47; Cicero, On the Republic 2.34). The craftsmen Pliny puts in his train, Eucheir, Diopos and Eugrammos (Natural History 35.152), "Skilled-hand", "Line-sight" and "Fine-drawer", are too apt to be true. His son Lucumo, barred from honour at Tarquinii as an immigrant's son, comes to Rome with his wife Tanaquil; at the Janiculum an eagle lifts his cap and replaces it, and Tanaquil, versed in Etruscan augury, reads kingship (1.34.8–9). As Lucius Tarquinius he becomes guardian of Ancus' sons, then the first king to canvass for the throne (1.35.2). He adds a hundred senators from "lesser families", lays out the Circus Maximus and the Roman Games (1.35.6–9), drains the low ground and lays the Capitoline foundations (1.38.6–7). Ancus' sons have him murdered, and Tanaquil installs Servius (1.40–41).

Servius Tullius was born in the palace to Ocresia, a captive of Corniculum; a flame played round the sleeping boy's head (1.39.1–4). Livy argues against the slave birth his name suggested (1.39.5–6); others had him fathered by a phallus rising from the hearth (Dionysius 4.2; Ovid, Fasti 6.627–636) and loved by Fortuna. He reigns "without the people's order, by the will of the senators" (Livy 1.41.6), takes the census, creates classes, centuries and tribes (1.42–43), counts 80,000 citizens on Fabius Pictor's authority (1.44.2), extends the pomerium and walls, and builds the Aventine temple of Diana as a shrine for the Latins on the model of Ephesus (1.45.2). His daughter Tullia and her husband Lucius Tarquinius have him killed, and she drives her carriage over his body (1.48.7).

Tarquinius Superbus rules "neither by order of the people nor with the senators' authority" (1.49.3). He finishes the Capitoline temple with Etruscan workmen and plebeian labour, digs the Cloaca Maxima and plants colonies at Signia and Circeii (1.55–56). He is drawn as a Greek tyrant: his capture of Gabii is built from Herodotus (module 1.6), and the forced building matches Aristotle's list of tyrants' devices for keeping subjects poor and busy (Politics 5.1313b). His tyranny and fall belong to module 1.6.

Where sources disagree. Fabius Pictor made Superbus the son of Priscus. Dionysius 4.6–7 objects that the ages cannot work, since Priscus died old in 579 and Superbus lived until 495 (Livy 2.21.5), and follows Calpurnius Piso in making him a grandson; Livy 1.46.4 notes the doubt and keeps "son" with the majority. The chronology has been stretched to fill the space.

Seven reigns for 244 years

Seven reigns in 244 years average thirty-five years each. That is implausible in any monarchy, more so in an elective one choosing grown men, in which five of the seven vanished, were killed or were expelled. Well-documented dynasties average around twenty years a reign; module 0.3 states the problem, and here it is weighed. Either kings have dropped out, the foundation date is too early, or both. The date of 753 is a computation; the archaeology (below) puts the urban transformation in the later seventh century; and the end around 509 is anchored by the consular lists and the Capitoline dedication (module 1.6). Most modern accounts therefore compress the regal period, or treat its start as unknowable.

The first four kings look schematic. Warlike Romulus is paired with peaceful Numa, warlike Tullus with Ancus who combines both; Latin alternates with Sabine; each king owns a sphere: politics, religion, war, expansion and the plebs. Dumézil (Mitra-Varuna, 1940, and later work) mapped them onto the Indo-European functions: Romulus and Numa as the violent and the juridical sides of sovereignty, Tullus as the warrior, Ancus as the third function of wealth and people. The Tarquins double each other: both fight Latins and Sabines, both drain the city, both build the Capitol.

Where sources disagree. Momigliano's objection is weighed in module 2.1. Cornell (The Beginnings of Rome, 1995) offers a different defence of the list: Numa, Tullus and Ancus are first names no Republican Roman used, and no later forger would choose them. The stories may be invented while the names are remembered.

Servius Tullius as the Etruscan Mastarna

In AD 48 the emperor Claudius urged the senate to admit nobles from Gallia Comata, arguing that Rome had always taken in outsiders, even as kings. The bronze text was found at Lyon in 1528 (CIL XIII 1668); Tacitus gives a reworked version (Annals 11.24). Claudius, who had written twenty books of Etruscan history (Suetonius, Claudius 42.2), says that by Roman authors Servius was born of the captive Ocresia, but by Etruscan ones he was the faithful companion of Caelius Vivenna, led the remnant of his army to Rome, occupied the Caelian and named it after his leader, and changed his name:

"Tusce Mastarna ei nomen erat": in Etruscan his name was Mastarna.

The François Tomb at Vulci, found by Alessandro François in 1857 and painted about 340–310 BC, shows the episode. Facing Achilles' slaughter of Trojan prisoners, a local scene: Macstrna cuts the bonds of Caile Vipinas; Larth Ulthes stabs Laris Papathnas Velznach, "of Volsinii"; Rasce kills Pesna Arcmsnas Sveamach; Avle Vipinas kills a third captor; Marce Camitlnas raises his sword over Cneve Tarchunies Rumach, "Gnaeus Tarquinius of Rome". Prisoners break free and kill captors drawn from several cities, one a Roman Tarquin.

The Vibennae are not fiction. A bucchero vessel dedicated by Avile Vipiienas at the Portonaccio sanctuary of Veii dates to the second quarter or middle of the sixth century. Roman antiquarians named the Caelian after Caeles Vibenna (Varro, On the Latin Language 5.46; Tacitus, Annals 4.65), and derived the Capitol from the head of Olus found in its foundations (Arnobius, Against the Pagans 6.7). Macstrna looks like Latin magister with an Etruscan suffix: a title, "the master", perhaps the magister populi that the early dictator was called.

Where sources disagree. Livy and Dionysius never mention Mastarna, and Roman tradition is unanimous for a Latin mother from Corniculum. Andreas Alföldi (Early Rome and the Latins, 1965) took the Etruscan version as the truth Roman patriotism suppressed. Momigliano and Cornell judge the equation to be Claudius' learned combination of two traditions; Cornell thinks Servius and Mastarna were probably different men. The identification is uncertain; the world it implies is not.

The great Rome of the Tarquins

Giorgio Pasquali's phrase of 1936, "la grande Roma dei Tarquini", became the title of the 1990 Rome exhibition. The archaeology supports it in outline.

  • The Forum. The valley between Palatine and Capitol held Iron Age burials and huts. It was raised by deliberate fill, up to two metres in places (Ammerman 1990), and received its first gravel floor in the later seventh century, about 650–600 BC; Gjerstad's date was c. 575.
  • The Regia. At the Forum's east end Frank Brown excavated in the 1960s a sequence of buildings from the late seventh century to the later sixth, ending in the irregular plan the Regia kept to the end. A bucchero bowl fragment of about 530–500 found there in 1899 bears the word rex. Tradition made it Numa's house (Plutarch, Numa 14; Ovid, Fasti 6.263–264); it was too small for a palace and reads better as the king's cult house.
  • The Comitium and the Lapis Niger. The assembly place before the Curia was first floored in the later seventh century. Beneath a late Republican pavement of black stone, Giacomo Boni found in 1899 an altar, a column stump and a tufa cippus inscribed boustrophedon on four faces and a bevelled edge (CIL I² 1), of about 570–550 BC. The text is a sacred law: sakros esed, "let him be accursed"; recei, "to the king", the dative of rex; kalatorem, "the herald"; iouxmenta, "yoked beasts". The ancient identifications of the spot are module 1.4.
  • Sant'Omobono. By the river harbour stand the temples of Fortuna and Mater Matuta that tradition gives to Servius (Dionysius 4.27; Livy 5.19.6). Cult begins in the late seventh or early sixth century; the first temple, usually dated to the early sixth, was rebuilt about 540–530 with terracotta decoration including Hercules led by Minerva, a theme popular in Peisistratid Athens. The temple went down about 500 under a great fill. Imported Greek pottery and an ivory lion with an Etruscan name show the harbour's reach.
  • The Capitoline temple. Foundations of cappellaccio tufa, about 54 by 62 metres, were re-exposed in the 1990s and 2000s in the Capitoline Museums' garden. Three cellae for Jupiter, Juno and Minerva; a terracotta Jupiter by Vulca of Veii (Pliny, Natural History 35.157, citing Varro); dedication by the consul Horatius in the Republic's first year (Livy 2.8.6–8). At full size it rivalled the Heraion of Samos and Peisistratid Athens' unfinished Olympieion.
  • The Cloaca Maxima. Livy credits Priscus with draining the low ground (1.38.6) and Superbus with the great sewer (1.56.2). The surviving vaulted conduit is mid-Republican or later; the sixth-century work was an open, stone-lined channel along the Forum stream to the Velabrum.
Where sources disagree. John Stamper (The Architecture of Roman Temples, 2005) reads the foundations, and Dionysius' 200 feet a side (4.61), as a platform carrying a temple of roughly 34 by 38 metres. Anna Mura Sommella, Gabriele Cifani and John Hopkins (the last in The Genesis of Roman Architecture, 2016) accept the full size. On the cippus, most take recei as a real king, since the stone predates 509; Gjerstad, who lowered the end of the monarchy to about 450, and others who doubt the date allow the rex sacrorum.

The ground confirms the tradition's structure: between about 625 and 500 a village cluster became a city with paved public spaces, monumental temples, drainage, Greek imports and Etruscan craftsmen. It confirms no individual king.

The Servian reforms

Livy 1.42–43, Dionysius 4.16–21 and Cicero, On the Republic 2.39–40, describe a census of property dividing citizens into classes, each class into centuries of iuniores (17–46) and seniores, armed according to wealth.

Eighteen centuries of cavalry, a few of engineers and musicians and one of those below the census brought the total to 193 (Dionysius 4.16–18; Livy differs in detail). Voting ran from the top, so cavalry and first class held a majority and the poor rarely voted (Livy 1.43.10–11). Servius also replaced Romulus' three tribes with four urban tribes, Suburana, Palatina, Esquilina and Collina, and rural ones; by 495 there were twenty-one (Livy 2.21.7).

Rome struck no coin before about 300 BC, so the figures in asses are anachronistic. The 193-century assembly as described is that of the third century. No army was ever drawn up in five graded panoplies. Plinio Fraccaro (1931) found the original underneath: the old distinction between classis and those infra classem (Gellius 6.13), and sixty junior centuries in the first three classes matching a legion of 6,000 hoplites. A sixth-century Servius could plausibly have created a single hoplite classis levied through territorial tribes, as Greek cities did in the same century; Solon's property classes and Cleisthenes' territorial tribes are the obvious parallels (Greek module 5.5).

Where sources disagree. Cornell (1995) accepts a Servian core: territorial tribes, a census and a hoplite army in the sixth century, with the voting assembly developing from the army later. Gary Forsythe (A Critical History of Early Rome, 2005) and others put the class system in the fifth or fourth century, with military pay from 406 BC. The tradition has the centuriate assembly elect the first consuls in 509 (Livy 1.60); few modern scholars think a voting assembly of 193 centuries existed under any king.

The nature of the kingship

No king succeeded his father. On a king's death the patres took turns as interrex for five days each (Livy 1.17.5–6); the interrex proposed a candidate, the people in their curiae voted, the patres ratified, the augur inaugurated him (1.18.6–10), and Cicero has each king confirm his power by a curiate law (On the Republic 2). The interrex survived into the Republic, last attested in 52 BC. The kingship was open to outsiders: a Sabine, a Corinthian's son, the son of a captive. Carmine Ampolo's studies of archaic social mobility treat this as normal for central Italy: Demaratus moves to Tarquinii, his son to Rome, and in 504 the Sabine Attus Clausus arrives with his clients (Livy 2.16.4).

The Tarquins break the procedure. Priscus canvasses; Servius rules without the people's vote; Superbus without either. Elective sacral kingship gives way to something like Greek tyranny, a non-hereditary sole rule backed by force and public works (Greek module 5.3). Sixth-century Rome was a prize for warlords with armed companions: the Vibennae and Mastarna, Gnaeus Tarquinius, and Porsenna of Clusium, who in one tradition took the city (module 1.6).

The sacral side outlived the kings. Livy says the Republic created a rex sacrorum to perform the kings' rites, subordinate to the pontifex maximus so that the name should not threaten liberty (2.2.1–2). Patrician and barred from other office, he kept the king's calendar rites, among them the Regifugium of 24 February and the days marked Q.R.C.F., "when the king has attended the assembly". He is the strongest proof of a priest-king. Some suggest the split began under the Etruscan rulers, a war-leader or magister holding power beside an old sacral rex; it cannot be shown.

How much is history: the modern positions

Barthold Niebuhr (1811–12) imagined lost banquet ballads behind Livy. Ettore Pais (Storia di Roma, 1898–99) led the hypercritics: most kings were gods, doublets or annalists' inventions. Einar Gjerstad (Early Rome, 1953–73) used his Forum stratigraphy to argue that the city was founded about 575 and the monarchy ran on to about 450. Alföldi (1965) made sixth-century Rome a small dependency of Etruscan cities and its regal hegemony in Latium Fabius Pictor's patriotic invention.

Cornell (1995) shifted the debate by separating the narrative superstructure, largely fiction, from the structural facts: institutions, topography, the Etruscan dynasty, the size of late sixth-century Rome. Those, he argues, came down independently and are confirmed by archaeology and by documents such as the Carthage treaty. Andrea Carandini (La nascita di Roma, 1997; Roma: il primo giorno, 2007) goes furthest: a Palatine wall of the later eighth century and a regal house by the Vesta temple make even Romulus and Numa historical figures, not personifications.

Where sources disagree. T. P. Wiseman, reviewing Carandini ("Reading Carandini", Journal of Roman Studies 2001), argued that the excavations are read through Livy and so cannot confirm him; Christopher Smith (Early Rome and Latium, 1996) and Francesca Fulminante see a gradual process of urbanisation from the ninth century to the sixth, not a foundation by a founder. The consensus, such as it is, lies between Cornell and the sceptics: the last three kings belong to history, the first four to aetiology, with names that may be old.

Self-test

  1. Why is it hard to treat Livy's account of the kings as testimony?
    Answer

    He wrote Book 1 around 27–25 BC, about five centuries after the last king; the first Roman historian, Fabius Pictor, wrote around 200 BC, and Livy says most records perished in the Gallic sack (6.1.2).

  2. What is wrong with seven reigns for 244 years, and what are the possible solutions?
    Answer

    An average of thirty-five years a reign is implausible, especially in an elective monarchy with several violent deaths. Either kings have been lost from the list, the foundation date is too early, or both.

  3. Give two signs that the first four kings are schematic, and one argument that their names are nonetheless old.
    Answer

    They alternate warlike and peaceful, Latin and Sabine, and each owns one sphere (politics, religion, war, expansion), which Dumézil read as Indo-European functions. Cornell notes that Numa, Ancus, Tullus and Servius are archaic names out of use in the Republic, which no forger would pick.

  4. What did Claudius claim about Servius Tullius, and where?
    Answer

    In his speech of AD 48 on the Lyon tablet (CIL XIII 1668) he said that Etruscan authors made Servius the companion of Caelius Vivenna, who brought Caelius' army to the Caelian and changed his Etruscan name, Mastarna.

  5. What does the François Tomb show, and why does the figure of Cneve Tarchunies Rumach matter?
    Answer

    Macstrna freeing Caile Vipinas while companions kill captors from several cities, painted at Vulci about 340–310 BC. "Gnaeus Tarquinius of Rome" is a Tarquin unknown to Livy, which suggests the two-Tarquin tradition simplifies a more crowded history.

  6. What is the Lapis Niger cippus, and what does recei tell us?
    Answer

    A tufa pillar of about 570–550 BC under the black pavement of the Comitium, inscribed boustrophedon with a sacred law (CIL I² 1). Recei, "to the king", shows a rex with a herald acting in the Comitium before 509, unless it means the rex sacrorum.

  7. What does the archaeology of the period 625–500 BC confirm, and what can it not confirm?
    Answer

    It confirms an urban transformation: the paved Forum and Comitium, the Regia, the Sant'Omobono temple, a huge Capitoline platform and drainage. It cannot confirm any individual king or deed.

  8. Why is the centuriate assembly as Livy describes it anachronistic for Servius?
    Answer

    Its census figures are in coined asses, unknown before about 300 BC, and its 193 centuries match the third-century assembly. Fraccaro's single hoplite classis of sixty centuries is the plausible sixth-century core.

  9. How was a king chosen, and how did the Tarquins depart from it?
    Answer

    Interreges from the patres in turn; a candidate proposed, voted by the curiae, ratified by the patres and inaugurated. Priscus canvassed, Servius ruled without the people's vote (Livy 1.41.6) and Superbus without either (1.49.3).

  10. What is the rex sacrorum evidence for?
    Answer

    That the king had sacral duties important enough to be kept after 509 by a priest bearing his title, deliberately subordinated to the pontifex maximus and barred from politics (Livy 2.2.1–2).

Carry forward

  1. Separate the narrative from the structure: the stories of the kings are late and shaped by aetiology, family pride and Greek models, but institutions, names and monuments can carry older information, and archaeology tests the structure, not the stories.
  2. Late sixth-century Rome was a large, Etruscanised city open to mobile warlords and their armed bands; the Tarquins, Mastarna and Porsenna belong to that world, and the Republic was born out of it rather than out of a tidy expulsion.
  3. Roman institutions are routinely projected back onto founders (Numa's priesthoods, Servius' centuries); before believing any "regal" institution, ask what form it took in the age of the historian who describes it.
