01The text: twelve books, a borrowed title and two place notes

The Meditations are several hundred short entries in Greek, divided into twelve books. They range from a single line to a page, are written mostly in the second person, and address no reader but their writer. The only surviving manuscript of the whole text, Vaticanus Graecus 1950 of the fourteenth century, is untitled. The title To Himself (ta eis heauton) stood in the lost manuscript from which the first edition was printed and was already used by Arethas about 900; the twelve-book division is first attested in the Suda, in the late tenth century (John Sellars, introduction to The Cambridge Companion to Marcus Aurelius' Meditations, 2025). Neither is likely to be Marcus' own. The English title comes from Meric Casaubon's translation of 1634, Meditations Concerning Himselfe. Module 13.7 tells the life; this module takes the book.

Two notes in the manuscripts tie the book to a place. Between books 1 and 2 stands "among the Quadi on the Granua", the Gran (Hron), a tributary flowing into the Danube from the north in what is now Slovakia; between books 2 and 3, "at Carnuntum", the legionary fortress and capital of Upper Pannonia east of Vienna. Module 7.3 places Marcus on the Danube for most of the 170s, at Carnuntum and Sirmium, against the Marcomanni, the Quadi and the Iazyges; it gives the notes and hands the book here. The notes show that at least some of the writing was done in camp, during the northern wars, and so after Lucius Verus' death in 169. They do not date the other ten books, and none of the entries names a battle, an enemy or a year.

The internal evidence agrees with a date late in the reign. The writer speaks of himself as an old man and as one already dying (2.2), urges haste because understanding may fail before life does (3.1), and returns again and again to death. Most scholars put the bulk of the work in the 170s, which is as far as the evidence goes.

Where sources disagree. Whether each note closes the book before it or heads the book after it is not settled. George Long's translation (1862) prints the Quadi note as the close of book 1 and suggests that book 1 was written during that war; C. R. Haines, introducing his Loeb edition (1916), reads the notes as showing that book 2 was written among the Quadi and book 3 at Carnuntum, and Sellars (2025) gives both as the titles of books 2 and 3. If they are endings, book 1 was written among the Quadi; if headings, book 1 is undated and may, as many readers suspect, have been composed separately, even last.

02Notebook or exercises: date and purpose

Antiquity is almost silent about the book. The Historia Augusta's life of Avidius Cassius has Marcus, asked before the Marcomannic war to publish his precepts of philosophy, expound them in public for three days instead (Avidius Cassius 3); the orator Themistius tells Valens in 364 that he has no need of Marcus' exhortations (Orations 6). Neither certainly means this text, and nothing quotes it before Arethas. A book Marcus meant for readers would have been copied in Rome and Athens; this one nearly vanished.

P. A. Brunt ("Marcus Aurelius in his Meditations", Journal of Roman Studies 64, 1974) set the modern consensus: Marcus wrote for his own moral improvement, rehearsing Stoic doctrine to himself, and the book is evidence for his inner life only with that purpose kept in view. The form supports him. The entries repeat, abbreviate and break off; they quote other authors from memory; they command and rebuke a "you" who is the writer. R. B. Rutherford's The Meditations of Marcus Aurelius: A Study (1989) took the book's style, imagery and rhetorical training seriously as part of its moral purpose: a man taught by Fronto persuading himself with the tools of persuasion.

Pierre Hadot's The Inner Citadel (La Citadelle intérieure, 1992; English translation by Michael Chase, 1998) gave the private reading a method. The entries are hypomnēmata, personal notes, and their writing is a spiritual exercise: the Stoic dogmas are known already, and the task is to make them present and effective at the moment of action by restating them, each time with new force. Epictetus had told his pupils that these were the things to write down daily and to exercise themselves in (Discourses 1.1.21–25). On this view the repetition is the point; a notebook that said each thing once would have failed.

Where sources disagree. Nicephorus Callistus, a Byzantine church historian of the fourteenth century, says Marcus composed the book for the instruction of his son, and the Historia Augusta's public lectures imply a teaching text. Brunt (1974) and Hadot (1992) answer that the second-person address, the repetitions and the near-total absence of ancient readers fit only a private notebook. Peter Stothard, reviewing the translation (Times Higher Education, 9 October 1998), found Hadot unpersuasive on whether Marcus lived up to the ideals he wrote down, which is a different question from privacy: a man can write for himself and still pose.

03Book 1: a catalogue of debts

Book 1 is unlike the rest. It is a single sustained list of what Marcus learned and from whom, each entry opening "from my grandfather Verus", "from my mother", "from Rusticus". Module 13.7 uses two entries for the life, Rusticus and the Stoic turn (1.7) and the long portrait of Antoninus (1.16); read as a whole, the book is a self-portrait drawn in what others gave.

It moves in four stages. The family comes first: from his grandfather gentleness, from his father's memory modesty and manliness, from his mother piety, generosity and simple living, and from his great-grandfather the advice to be taught at home by good teachers and to spend freely on it (1.1–4). Then teachers and friends (1.5–15). The tutor taught him to take no side between the Greens and the Blues or the gladiators' factions, to endure work and want little (1.5); Diognetus to ignore wonder-workers and to sleep on a plank bed and a skin (1.6); Apollonius to be the same in pain, in illness and at the loss of a child (1.8); Sextus the idea of living according to nature (1.9); the grammarian Alexander to correct a solecism by using the right word, not by pouncing (1.10). Fronto's entry is short and does not mention eloquence: from him Marcus learned what envy, duplicity and hypocrisy are in a tyrant, and that Roman patricians are somewhat lacking in natural affection (1.11). Alexander the Platonist taught him not to plead that he was too busy (1.12). Then comes Antoninus, the longest entry (1.16), and last the gods, thanked for good parents and teachers, for his not having been raised longer with his grandfather's concubine, for his not having made more progress in rhetoric and poetry, for meeting no sophist when he turned to philosophy, and for remedies shown in dreams against spitting blood and dizziness (1.17).

The entry for Severus is the most political in the book (1.14). Through him Marcus came to know Thrasea, Helvidius, Cato, Dion and Brutus, and formed the idea of a state with one law for all, governed by equality and free speech, and of a monarchy that values above all the freedom of the governed. The emperor names men who died opposing tyrants or emperors as models of his own rule (module 13.4 follows Thrasea and Helvidius). What the book leaves out is as telling as what it includes: no victory, office, building or decree, no Hadrian except by implication, almost no doctrine. Every debt is a trait of character, and the list reads as a portrait in negative of the man Marcus feared he would be without them.

04The themes

Impermanence

The time of a human life is a point, its substance flowing, its perception dim, the whole body decaying, the soul a whirl, fame without judgement (2.17). Time is a river of passing events (4.43). Marcus makes it concrete with history: think of Vespasian's age, people marrying, raising children, falling sick, dying, feasting, flattering, and all of it gone; then Trajan's (4.32). Even names grow obsolete: Camillus and Caeso were once on every tongue, then Scipio and Cato, then Augustus, then Hadrian and Antoninus (4.33).

The view from above

Look down from above on the countless herds of men, their rites, their voyages in storm and calm, births and deaths (9.30). Hadot called the exercise the view from above: seen from the height of the whole, honours and injuries shrink to their size. Its companion is the physical definition, which strips a thing to what it is: roast meat is the corpse of a fish or a bird, the purple robe sheep's wool dyed in shellfish blood, sex a rubbing of entrails and a spasm (6.13). Neither is contempt for the world; both remove the false value judgement that turns an indifferent into a passion.

The inner citadel

People seek retreats in the country, by the sea, in the hills; but one can retreat at any hour into oneself (4.3). The mind free of passions is a citadel (akropolis), and no man has a stronger place to flee to (8.48). Things do not touch the soul; disturbance comes only from judgement about them (4.3). The image gave Hadot his title, and it is a Stoic image of control, not of withdrawal from duty.

Duty

The most quoted entries are about getting up. At dawn, when reluctant to rise, say: I am rising to do the work of a human being (5.1). At dawn, also say: today I shall meet the meddling, the ungrateful, the violent, the treacherous, the envious; they act from ignorance of good and evil, and I can be harmed by none of them, since we are made to work together like feet, hands and eyelids (2.1). The emperor's own danger is named: take care not to be made a Caesar, not to be dyed in the purple (6.30).

The cosmic city

If mind is common to us, so is reason, law and citizenship, and the cosmos is a kind of city (4.4). The fullest statement is the one module 13.2 hands here: my city and fatherland, as Antoninus, is Rome; as a human being, the cosmos; what benefits these cities is alone good for me (6.44). Seneca's two commonwealths (On Leisure 4.1, in module 13.2) allowed a man to serve the greater one in retirement. Marcus does not have that choice. He holds both citizenships at once and makes the lesser the place where the greater is served.

Death

Do not despise death but welcome it as one of the things nature wills, like teething, puberty or greying (9.3). The book ends with the actor dismissed in the third act: depart, then, serenely, for the one who releases you is serene (12.36). Beside the Stoic consolations runs another argument, the alternative "either providence or atoms" (4.3): if a providential order rules, accept it; if only atoms, still keep your post. Whether this is doubt or a device that makes the conclusion hold on either hypothesis is a central question of interpretation (see the last section).

05The debt to Epictetus

Marcus thanks Rusticus for lending him Epictetus' notes (1.7); module 13.6 sets out Epictetus' teaching, and the weight of the debt is this module's question. It is visible on the surface. Marcus quotes Epictetus by name: you are a little soul carrying a corpse (4.41). A run of entries near the end of book 11 (11.33–38) quotes or paraphrases him on seeking a fig in winter, on the robber of free will who does not exist, and on the art of assent. The vocabulary of impressions, assent and what is up to us is Epictetus'.

Hadot built the book's structure on it. Epictetus divides the student's training into three fields: desire and aversion, impulse and appropriate action, and assent (Discourses 3.2.1–2). Hadot finds the same three in Marcus as disciplines tied to the three parts of philosophy: desire to physics, accepting what universal nature brings; action to ethics, acting justly for the common good; assent to logic, admitting only true impressions. One entry gives all three at once: enough is your present judgement, if it grasps the truth, your present action, if it serves the community, and your present disposition, if it welcomes whatever comes from outside (9.6).

The differences matter as much. Epictetus is a teacher of young men, speaking through Arrian's record, and his examples are slaves, exiles and lecturers. Marcus is a pupil writing to himself, and his are courtiers, generals and the dead emperors. He gives far more room to physics, the whole and death, and much less to argument. He has the creed without the classroom.

Where sources disagree. Hadot (The Inner Citadel, 1998) reads Marcus as a disciplined follower who organised his notebook around Epictetus' three disciplines. Rachana Kamtekar, reviewing the translation (Bryn Mawr Classical Review 1998.11.35), objected that Hadot leans on Epictetus' reputed orthodoxy to play down the Stoic doctrine that all faults are equal, where the Epictetus he cites makes only a psychological observation. The debt is certain; how far Epictetus can serve as the key to Marcus' doctrine is not.

06The transmission: from Arethas to Zurich

The book survived by one thread. Arethas of Caesarea, the scholar-bishop whose commissioned Plato Greek module 7.4 records, wrote to Demetrius, archbishop of Heraclea, before 907, that he had long owned an old copy of the emperor's most profitable book, so worn it was falling to pieces, and had had it copied to hand on to posterity. His copy is lost but is usually thought to be the ancestor of the manuscripts we know. The Suda, in the late tenth century, describes a guide to his own life by the emperor Marcus in twelve books and quotes some thirty passages; John Tzetzes quoted books 4 and 5 about 1150.

The first edition came from a manuscript in the Palatine library at Heidelberg, supplied by Michael Toxites. Wilhelm Holtzmann, who wrote as Xylander, printed the Greek with a Latin translation at Zurich, at Conrad Gesner's instigation and from the press of Andreas Gesner; the preface is dated October 1558 and the title page 1559. The manuscript was then lost, probably taken apart at the printer's (Sellars, "The Early Modern Reception of the Meditations", 2025). For long stretches of the text the 1558–59 edition is therefore a witness of its own, and the one surviving manuscript of the whole, Vaticanus Graecus 1950, is corrupt and drops lines. Thomas Gataker's edition (Cambridge, 1652) fixed the section numbers still used.

07The afterlife

The book's fame lagged behind the emperor's. The Renaissance knew Marcus first as the good ruler of the Historia Augusta and of Antonio de Guevara's fictional Libro áureo (1528), and Justus Lipsius, whose On Constancy (1584) launched neo-Stoicism as a consolation for the wars of the Low Countries, built it chiefly on Seneca, whose works are module 13.5's. His Stoic handbooks of 1604 largely leave the Meditations aside, though he knew and quoted them (Sellars 2025). Montaigne, whose early essays take their Stoicism from Seneca and Cicero, does not mention Marcus at all (Sellars 2025), though a French translation appeared in 1570. The book became a classic over the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, through Casaubon's English, Gataker's edition and readers from Shaftesbury to Adam Smith, who brought Marcus into the sixth edition of The Theory of Moral Sentiments (1790) (Sellars 2025).

Frederick the Great, by the account of Kevin Kennedy ("Frederick the Great: A Stoic on the Throne?"), set busts of Marcus at Sanssouci, but first found the book too Stoic for his taste and called its ethics fit for statues, in letters to his sister Wilhelmine; in 1762, in the Seven Years' War, he admitted to his reader that Marcus and the Stoics held the upper hand only so far, since nature often overpowers philosophy. Voltaire in 1775 flattered him as a Marcus Aurelius.

Matthew Arnold's essay "Marcus Aurelius" (1863; collected in Essays in Criticism, 1865) made the emperor a Victorian figure. It answered John Stuart Mill's comparison of Stoic and Christian ethics in On Liberty (1859), the book in which Mill called Marcus' writings the highest ethical product of the ancient mind and set beside them his persecution of Christianity (chapter 2; Lee Behlman, "The Victorian Marcus Aurelius", Journal of Victorian Culture, 2011). Arnold admired the moral beauty of the book and found in it a melancholy he set against Christian joy. Later Victorian writers argued over whether Marcus linked Stoicism to Christianity or set it apart, and over his responsibility for the persecution at Lyon in 177, which module 13.7 tells (Behlman 2011).

The modern revival is popular as much as scholarly. Hadot's reading of ancient philosophy as a way of life reached Foucault in his last years (Greek module 12.5 has his course on Cynic frankness). The Stoicism Today project grew from a workshop at the University of Exeter in 2012, out of Christopher Gill's research on Stoic practical ethics, and from it came Stoic Week; Ryan Holiday and Stephen Hanselman's The Daily Stoic (2016) turned Marcus, Seneca and Epictetus into a page a day. The Meditations are read now chiefly as a manual for coping, which is close to what Hadot says they were.

08Philosopher or tired emperor?

The case for the tired emperor is in the text. The same few thoughts recur for hundreds of entries, as if they would not stay learned. The writer hates rising (5.1), forbids himself to complain of court life (8.9), and keeps rehearsing the worst people he will meet. Arnold heard melancholy; Thomas W. Africa ("The Opium Addiction of Marcus Aurelius", Journal of the History of Ideas 22, 1961) went further and read the visions of flux and the contempt for the body as effects of the opium in the theriac Galen prepared for him. And the record of the reign sits awkwardly beside the book: Mill's persecutor, and the father who made Commodus his heir (module 13.7).

The case for the philosopher is in the method. Hadot showed that the repetition is a technique, that the bleak definitions are a Stoic exercise against false value, and that the providence-or-atoms alternatives argue that duty holds on either hypothesis. Christopher Gill, in a lecture asking how Stoic the Meditations are, reads the whole as a coherent Stoic project of ethical self-development, its unorthodox-looking moments being hasty moralising inside that frame. The Historia Augusta says Marcus often quoted Plato's saying that states flourish when philosophers rule or rulers philosophise (Marcus 27.7).

The dichotomy is not one a Stoic would accept. Philosophy, for Epictetus and for Marcus, was not the invention of doctrine but the training of judgement, and talking oneself into one's duty by correct reasoning is exactly what the school asked of its members. Marcus was no original philosopher and claimed nothing of the kind; he thanks the gods that he did not waste himself on theory (1.17). He was a philosopher in the sense his school valued, a practitioner, and the tiredness is the material on which the practice works.

Where sources disagree. Africa (1961) diagnosed the book's mood as a drug's; Arnold (1863) read it as the melancholy of a noble soul without the consolations of Christianity. Hadot (1992) dismissed every such psychobiography, of opium, depression or ulcers, as a misreading of Stoic exercises as symptoms, while Kamtekar (1998) noted that Hadot then psychologises Marcus himself. Whether the gloom belongs to the man or to the method cannot be settled from a text written to change its writer.

09Self-test

  1. Why is To Himself probably not Marcus' title, and where does the English title Meditations come from?
    Answer

    The only surviving manuscript of the whole text is untitled; the title is known from the lost manuscript behind the first edition and from Arethas' usage about 900, centuries after Marcus. Meditations comes from Meric Casaubon's English translation of 1634, Meditations Concerning Himselfe.

  2. What do the place notes "among the Quadi on the Granua" and "at Carnuntum" establish, and what do they leave open?
    Answer

    That some of the writing was done in camp on the Danube during the northern wars of the 170s. They do not date the other ten books, and since it is disputed whether they close one book or head the next, they cannot say whether book 1 was written among the Quadi or separately.

  3. What evidence supports Brunt's view that Marcus wrote for himself and not for publication?
    Answer

    The entries address a "you" who is the writer, repeat and abbreviate, and break off; and no ancient author certainly quotes the book before Arethas, so it was not copied as a published work would have been (Brunt 1974; Hadot).

  4. How is book 1 ordered, and what does it leave out?
    Answer

    Family (1.1–4), teachers and friends (1.5–15), Antoninus (1.16), the gods (1.17). It leaves out victories, offices, buildings and almost all doctrine: every debt is a trait of character.

  5. What does Marcus credit to Severus in 1.14, and why is it striking from an emperor?
    Answer

    Knowledge of Thrasea, Helvidius, Cato, Dion and Brutus, and the idea of a state with one law for all, equality and free speech, and of a monarchy valuing the freedom of the governed. An emperor names as models the men who died opposing emperors.

  6. What are the view from above and the physical definition, and what are they for?
    Answer

    The first looks down on human affairs from the height of the whole (9.30); the second strips a thing to its material, as the purple robe to dyed wool (6.13). Both remove false value judgements, the source of the passions, rather than expressing contempt for the world.

  7. How does Meditations 6.44 differ from Seneca's two commonwealths in On Leisure 4.1?
    Answer

    Seneca allowed the greater commonwealth to be served in retirement. Marcus, Rome's citizen as Antoninus and the cosmos' as a human being, holds both at once and serves the greater through the lesser.

  8. What is Hadot's three-discipline thesis, and what objection did Kamtekar raise?
    Answer

    That Marcus organises his notes around Epictetus' three fields (Discourses 3.2.1–2), desire, action and assent, tied to physics, ethics and logic, as in 9.6. Kamtekar argued that Hadot leans on Epictetus' reputed orthodoxy, as in playing down the doctrine that all faults are equal, where the Epictetus he cites does not bear the weight.

  9. Why does the loss of the Palatine manuscript matter for the text?
    Answer

    It was the source of Xylander's edition of 1558–59 and was lost at or after printing, so that edition stands in for a manuscript, beside the corrupt Vaticanus Graecus 1950, the only surviving manuscript of the whole text.

  10. What did Lipsius and Montaigne take from the Meditations, and what does that show?
    Answer

    Very little: Lipsius built neo-Stoicism in On Constancy (1584) chiefly on Seneca and quoted Marcus only occasionally, and Montaigne never mentions him (Sellars 2025). The book's fame came later, in the seventeenth century and after.

10Carry forward

  1. The Meditations are a private notebook whose repetitions are a technique: Stoic doctrine already known, restated until it governs action. Read any entry as an exercise addressed to its writer, not a thesis argued to a reader.
  2. The most read book of ancient philosophy survived by one worn copy, one bishop's interest and one printed edition from a manuscript then lost; its reputation came over a millennium after it was written, and each age, from Arethas' profitable book to Arnold's melancholy and the daily page of modern Stoicism, has read its own need into a text written for no one else.
  3. Roman Stoicism ends not in doctrine but in self-address: the question of how much philosophy a Roman in power may allow himself is answered by the one Roman with the most power, who allowed himself the most and kept it to himself, and whose philosophy is judged, as he judged it, by how he did his duty.
