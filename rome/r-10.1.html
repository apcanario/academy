01A Roman poem that taught Europe Greek myth

For a thousand years the Latin West met the Greek gods mainly in one book, written in Rome by a poet who never pretended to be Greek. Greek module 0.1 treats the Metamorphoses as a trap for anyone recovering Greek myth, and Greek module 3.7 sets its best-known stories against the Greek evidence; module 6.3 gives Ovid's career, the bare plan of the poem, its closing claim and the exile. This module reads the poem as a Roman artefact, with the Fasti and the Heroides beside it.

The proem states the paradox. The gods are asked to "spin down" (deducite) a "continuous song" (perpetuum carmen) from the world's first origin to the poet's own times (1.3–4). Callimachus had refused to write one continuous song (Aetia fr. 1.3 Pfeiffer), and deductum carmen was Virgil's phrase for the slender Callimachean poem (Eclogue 6.5): Ovid promises an epic's span built from a miniaturist's episodes. If the minority reading illa is right in line 2, the gods have changed the poet's own undertakings too, from elegy to epic.

02From Chaos to Caesar's star

The poem runs to nearly 12,000 hexameters in fifteen books, ordered by time and drifting towards history. Books 1–2 run from Chaos and the flood to the loves of the gods; books 3–4 follow the house of Cadmus at Thebes; the middle books move through the heroes to Orpheus in books 10–11; book 12 reaches Troy. Ovid then compresses Virgil's epic into a "little Aeneid" (13.623–14.608) in which Dido gets four lines (14.78–81) and the space goes to Scylla, Glaucus, Circe and Polyphemus. Book 14 ends with the Alban kings, Vertumnus and Pomona, and Romulus; book 15 gives Numa, Pythagoras' long speech, the arrival of Aesculapius at Rome (15.622–744) and the death and deification of Julius Caesar (15.745–870).

The Roman books change what metamorphosis means. Early in the poem change is usually loss: a girl becomes a tree, a boy a flower. In the last books it becomes ascent. Aeneas is purged of his mortal part in the river Numicius and made the god Indiges (14.581–608); Romulus becomes Quirinus and his wife Hersilia the goddess Hora (14.805–851); Caesar becomes a star. Roman metamorphosis is apotheosis, and Pythagoras' doctrine, omnia mutantur, nihil interit, "everything changes, nothing perishes" (15.165), underwrites it. The same speech lists cities that rose and fell, Troy, Sparta, Mycenae, Thebes, Athens, and adds that Rome is now rising (15.420–435). The reader is left to finish the thought.

Where sources disagree. Brooks Otis (Ovid as an Epic Poet, 1966) divided the poem into four blocks: a divine comedy (1.1–2.875), the avenging gods (3.1–6.400), the pathos of love (6.401–11.795), and Rome and the deified ruler (books 12–15). Stephen Wheeler (Narrative Dynamics in Ovid's Metamorphoses, 2000) called such block schemes too reductive and found the design in repeated patterns and continuous linking that keep deferring closure.

03The Roman frame: a heavenly Senate and a new star

The first council of the gods is drawn as a meeting of the Senate. Jupiter summons the gods along the Milky Way to his house; the halls of the noble gods stand open on either side, the plebs live elsewhere, and the narrator would not fear, if boldness were allowed, to call the place the Palatine of the great sky (1.168–176). When the gods clamour for punishment, the narrator compares the outcry to the world's horror when an impious band raged to blot out the Roman name in Caesar's blood: Augustus' people are as loyal to him as the gods to Jupiter (1.199–205). Whether the blood is the dictator's in 44 BC or that of a plot against Augustus is unsettled, and Greek module 3.7 weighs what the parallel implies about Jupiter's power. Greek gods are given a Roman constitution, with the princeps as the term of comparison.

The ending answers the opening. Told by Jupiter that the Fates' decrees are fixed, Venus stands unseen in the middle of the Senate house, snatches Caesar's soul and carries it up, where it catches fire and becomes a star trailing its hair (15.761–850). Pliny quotes Augustus' memoirs on the real comet of July 44 BC, taken for Caesar's soul received among the gods (Natural History 2.93–94). Ovid supplies the logic with a straight face: none of Caesar's deeds was greater than fathering this man, and he had to be made a god so that Augustus would not be born of mortal seed (15.746–761). After Jupiter and Augustus are paired as rulers and fathers (15.858–860), the epilogue sets the poet above both. His work will outlast Jupiter's anger, fire, sword and time (15.871–872); his better part will be borne above the stars, higher than Caesar's new one (15.875–876); he will be read wherever Roman power reaches, and the last word is vivam, "I shall live" (15.877–879). The exile poems call Augustus Jupiter and his sentence the thunderbolt, and after AD 8 these lines read as defiance. Ovid says he burned his own copy on leaving Rome and that friends' copies lacked his final revision (Tristia 1.7).

04Invented and transformed: five stories

Greek module 3.7 gives the Greek evidence for Narcissus and Echo, Pygmalion and Arachne, and Greek module 0.1 for Medusa; the question here is what each owes to Rome.

Pyramus and Thisbe (4.55–166) is the clearest case of a story made Roman in texture. It is told by one of the daughters of Minyas, who refuse Bacchus' festival and work wool while they trade stories, and she chooses it because it is not a common tale (4.53). Two young Babylonians, forbidden to marry, whisper through a crack in a party wall and curse it as the lover of Latin elegy curses his mistress's locked door (4.73). They agree to meet at Ninus' tomb under a white mulberry; Thisbe, early, flees a lioness and drops her veil; Pyramus finds it bloodied and falls on his sword. His blood leaps up as water hisses from a split lead pipe (4.121–124), a simile from the city's plumbing, and darkens the fruit. Thisbe dies on the same blade, and their ashes rest in one urn (4.166).

Where sources disagree. No Greek text tells Ovid's story. Nicolaus' Progymnasmata, a rhetorical handbook of the fifth century AD, preserves a version in which Pyramus becomes the Cilician river of that name and Thisbe a spring, and a Roman-period mosaic in the House of Dionysus at Nea Paphos shows the river-god Pyramus with Thisbe. Many see here the Greek aetiology Ovid moved to Babylon; but both witnesses are later than Ovid, and his narrator's claim that the tale was not common (4.53) cuts either way.

Narcissus and Echo (3.339–510): the boy is Greek, the pairing Ovid's, and its stamp is the declaimer's trick of words returned with their meaning changed, as when Narcissus calls "let us meet here" and Echo returns coeamus as an offer of love. Arachne (6.1–145) is a contest between two arts. Minerva weaves the twelve gods enthroned on the Areopagus augusta gravitate, "with august dignity" (6.73), centred and hierarchical, with punished mortals in the corners, like a state relief; Arachne weaves a crowded, unframed sequence of the gods' rapes and disguises (6.103–128), which is to say the Metamorphoses, and neither Pallas nor Envy could fault it (6.129–130). After AD 8 a mortal artist destroyed by a goddess for telling the truth about the gods was hard not to read as autobiography. Pygmalion (10.243–297) courts his ivory girl with the gifts of elegy, shells, flowers and amber, so that the living statue is the elegiac lover's fantasy granted. Medusa's rape in Minerva's temple and the punishment of the victim (4.790–803) are told at Perseus' wedding feast, in answer to a guest's question, by the man who killed her: the most retold version of her story is her killer's after-dinner aetiology.

05The lost Hellenistic shelf

Ovid's quarry is listed in Greek module 3.7: Nicander's Heteroioumena, Boios' Ornithogonia, Parthenius, Callimachus. Two points add the Roman side. The genre already had a Latin voice: Aemilius Macer, an older friend, read the young Ovid his poem on birds, after Boios, and his poem on snakes and healing herbs (Tristia 4.10.43–44). And where Antoninus Liberalis' summaries of Nicander allow a test, Ovid changes the frame more than the plot. Antoninus 10, citing Nicander's fourth book and Corinna, has the daughters of Minyas, maddened by Dionysus, tear Leucippe's son Hippasus apart and become a bat, an owl and an eagle-owl. Ovid keeps the defiance and makes all three bats (4.389–415), but drops the murder and turns the sisters into storytellers, so that Pyramus and Thisbe, the Sun's loves and Salmacis and Hermaphroditus enter the poem through them. Antoninus also gives Nicander's versions of the Pierides' contest with the Muses (9; Ovid's book 5), Battus (23; book 2), Byblis (30; book 9) and a girl turned boy, Leucippus where Ovid has Iphis (17; book 9). The Hellenistic catalogues supplied changes; Ovid supplied voices.

06The Roman names, and why they stuck

Ovid's gods are Jupiter, Juno, Minerva, Venus, Mercury, Diana, Neptune, Ceres, Vulcan and Bacchus; his heroes include Ulixes and Hercules; Persephone is Proserpina. The equations were centuries older than Ovid, and module 2.2 traces how they were made and how imperfectly they fit. Where Latin had no equivalent he kept the Greek, Phoebus and Apollo, and he dressed the Latin names in Greek epithets, Saturnia for Juno, Tritonia for Minerva, Cytherea for Venus, so that a Roman name carried a whole Greek story.

Three things made the names permanent. Ovid became a school author: the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, when he was taught, glossed, imitated and moralised more than any other ancient poet, are Ludwig Traube's aetas Ovidiana. Greek was almost unread in the Latin West, so there was no rival vocabulary (Greek module 9.3, with Bulfinch's Latin-named handbook of 1855). And the vernaculars translated Ovid long before Homer: Arthur Golding's English Metamorphoses (1567) gave Shakespeare his Jove and Juno. Only in the nineteenth century, with a new Hellenism and Greek central again in the schools, did English begin to prefer Zeus to Jove.

07The Fasti: the Roman year as a poem

Modules 2.3 and 2.4 use the Fasti as a source for festivals, and module 6.3 reads its Augustan days; here it is a poem, the Metamorphoses' companion, written in the same years and in deliberate contrast. The Metamorphoses is hexameter, Greek in matter and ordered by time from the world's beginning; the Fasti is elegiac, Roman in matter and ordered by the civic calendar, a book to a month, a day at a time, with star-risings, festivals and anniversaries. The poet admits the metre is slight for it: now for the first time, elegiacs, you sail with larger sails (2.3). His voice is the antiquarian's and his method interrogation. Mars is asked why matrons keep his festival (3.167–258); Flora expounds her own cult (5.183–378); the Muses disagree over the name of May (5.1–110) and three goddesses over June (6.1–100), and the poet declines to judge. It is a poem of rival explanations, which is why module 2.3 warns against trusting its reasons.

Its Roman stories are those the Metamorphoses squeezes into its last books or leaves out: Carmenta, Evander, and Hercules and Cacus (1.461–586); the Fabii at the Cremera (2.193–242); Lucretia (2.685–852; module 1.6); Numa coaxing Jupiter into giving the charm against lightning (3.259–392); and Anna Perenna, Dido's sister, who flees to Italy, drowns in the Numicius and becomes a nymph honoured on the Ides of March (3.523–696). Some stories are told in both poems, and the doubles show the difference of key: Callisto (Metamorphoses 2.401–530; Fasti book 2), Romulus' apotheosis (Metamorphoses 14.805–828; Fasti 2.475–512) and above all the rape of Proserpina (Metamorphoses 5.341–661; Fasti 4.417–620).

Where sources disagree. Richard Heinze ("Ovids elegische Erzählung", 1919) compared the paired stories and found two consistent manners: the epic Metamorphoses grand, its gods majestic; the elegiac Fasti tender, intimate and sentimental. Stephen Hinds (The Metamorphosis of Persephone, 1987) showed Ovid playing with exactly those markers, putting elegiac matter into the epic and epic gestures into the elegy, so that genre becomes a subject of the poems rather than a rule they obey. Heinze's categories remain the starting point; his rigidity does not survive.

The poem stops at the end of June. From exile Ovid says he wrote "six and as many" books, each ending with its month, dedicated to Augustus, and that his fate broke the work off (Tristia 2.549–552); most read that as six books written and six planned, since no ancient author quotes July to December. What survives was retouched after Augustus' death: Carmenta foretells Livia's deification as Julia Augusta, a name she took only in AD 14 (1.536). The opening now addresses Germanicus (1.3), Tiberius' adopted son and himself translator of Aratus' poem on the stars, the right reader for a calendar of risings and settings. The end was not designed as one: on 30 June Clio points to the monument of Philippus and praises Marcia, Augustus' cousin and wife of Ovid's friend Paullus Fabius Maximus (6.797–812), and the poem stops. A calendar meant to fill a year fills half of one, revised by a poet writing to the dynasty that had banished him.

08The Heroides: myth in the heroine's voice

The Heroides are fifteen verse letters from women of myth to absent husbands and lovers, most of whom have left them, among them Penelope to Ulixes, Phaedra to Hippolytus, Dido to Aeneas, Ariadne to Theseus, Medea to Jason and Sappho to Phaon, with three pairs of letters and replies (16–21) added later. Ovid claimed the form as a work unknown to others (Ars Amatoria 3.345–346), though Propertius' letter of Arethusa to her soldier husband (4.3) is a Roman precedent, and novavit can mean "renewed" as well as "invented". Each letter freezes a known story at one moment and lets the abandoned woman argue it, as Ariadne does from the empty shore. Dido's letter (7) is the most Roman, Virgil's fourth book reread by its victim, in which pious Aeneas is a deserter and the Roman mission a pretext. The collection's contribution is myth told from inside, and the heroic epistle from Drayton to Pope's Eloisa to Abelard descends from it.

Where sources disagree. Sappho's letter stands outside the main manuscript tradition of the collection. Karl Lachmann (1848) and R. J. Tarrant ("The Authenticity of the Letter of Sappho to Phaon", 1981) judged it an imitation; Edward Courtney (1997) and Thea Thorsen (Ovid's Early Poetry, 2014) defended it, pointing to the Sappho letter in Ovid's own list (Amores 2.18). E. J. Kenney's commentary on 16–21 (1996) accepts the double letters as late Ovid against doubts based on their metre.

09Afterlives: from the Ovide moralisé to Bernini

Module 10.4 takes the reception of Rome at large; this is Ovid's own line. The Middle Ages kept him by moralising him. Arnulf of Orléans in the twelfth century and John of Garland in the thirteenth explained the fables as veiled truths; the anonymous Old French Ovide moralisé of the early fourteenth century turned some 12,000 Latin lines into about 72,000 French ones, following each story with expositions that read it as history, as moral warning and as a figure of Christ, the Virgin or the soul. It absorbed older French poems, among them the Philomena attributed to Chrétien de Troyes, which survives only there.

Chaucer used the Ovide moralisé beside the Latin but left its morals behind. The Book of the Duchess begins with its narrator reading Ceyx and Alcyone; Thisbe, Dido, Medea, Ariadne and Philomela are among the heroines of the Legend of Good Women; and the Wife of Bath retells Midas with his wife, not his barber, betraying the ass's ears, then sends her hearers to Ovid for the rest. Shakespeare had Golding and the Latin. The mechanicals stage Pyramus and Thisbe in A Midsummer Night's Dream, Wall included; Lavinia in Titus Andronicus turns the pages of the Metamorphoses to Philomela to name her own rape (4.1); Prospero's farewell to magic in The Tempest (5.1) reworks Medea's invocation from book 7 through Golding's words. Francis Meres wrote in 1598 that the sweet, witty soul of Ovid lived in Shakespeare.

Painters and sculptors read for the instant of change. Titian's poesie for Philip II, listed in Greek module 9.3, stage Ovid's moments: Actaeon as he sees Diana, Callisto's pregnancy exposed at the bath. Bernini's Apollo and Daphne (1622–25), carved for Cardinal Scipione Borghese, shows bark closing over Daphne's body and leaves sprouting from her fingers as Apollo reaches her, Metamorphoses 1.548–552 made marble. Its base carried a couplet by Cardinal Maffeo Barberini, the future Urban VIII, warning that whoever chases fleeting beauty fills his hands with leaves or plucks bitter berries: the method of the Ovide moralisé, three centuries on, attached to a statue that needs no moral.

10Self-test

  1. How does the proem of the Metamorphoses combine two poetic programmes that had been set against each other?
    Answer

    It asks the gods to "spin down" (deducite, Virgil's word for the slender Callimachean poem, Eclogue 6.5) a "continuous song" (perpetuum carmen, the kind Callimachus refused, Aetia fr. 1.3) from creation to Ovid's own day (1.3–4): an epic's span made of miniatures.

  2. What happens to the meaning of metamorphosis in the Roman books? Give two examples.
    Answer

    Change becomes ascent rather than loss: Aeneas is made Indiges (14.581–608), Romulus Quirinus and Hersilia Hora (14.805–851), Caesar a star (15.843–850). Pythagoras' "everything changes, nothing perishes" (15.165) gives it a doctrine.

  3. How do the first council of the gods and the deification of Caesar frame the poem as Roman?
    Answer

    The council is a Senate meeting on the "Palatine of the great sky", with noble gods and plebs, and the gods' outcry is likened to the world's horror at an attempt on Caesar (1.168–205). At the end Venus takes Caesar's soul from the middle of the Senate house and makes it a star (15.761–850), and Jupiter and Augustus are paired as rulers (15.858–860).

  4. Why is the logic of Caesar's deification double-edged, and how does the epilogue answer it?
    Answer

    Ovid says Caesar's greatest deed was fathering Augustus and that he had to be made a god so that Augustus would not be born of mortal seed (15.746–761), so the apotheosis serves the heir. The epilogue then sets the poet's fame beyond Jupiter's anger and above the stars, ending on vivam (15.871–879).

  5. What makes Pyramus and Thisbe Roman in texture, and why is its Greek background uncertain?
    Answer

    The lovers curse the wall like the elegiac lover at a locked door (4.73), the blood spurts like water from a split lead pipe (4.121–124), and their ashes share one urn (4.166). The Cilician version with river and spring survives only in Nicolaus' fifth-century handbook and a Roman-period mosaic at Nea Paphos, both later than Ovid.

  6. What does comparison with Antoninus Liberalis 10 show about Ovid's use of Nicander?
    Answer

    Antoninus' daughters of Minyas kill Hippasus and become a bat and two kinds of owl; Ovid drops the murder, makes all three bats (4.389–415) and turns them into narrators of three tales. He changed frame and voice more than plot.

  7. Why did Ovid's Latin names for the gods become Europe's names?
    Answer

    He was the central school author of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries (Traube's aetas Ovidiana), Greek was almost unread in the Latin West (Greek module 9.3), and the vernaculars translated Ovid first, as Golding did in 1567. English began to prefer Greek forms only in the nineteenth century.

  8. How do the Fasti and the Metamorphoses differ, and what did Heinze and Hinds argue about the stories they share?
    Answer

    Elegiac against hexameter, Roman against Greek, the calendar day by day against chronology from creation. Heinze (1919) found consistent elegiac and epic manners in doubles such as Proserpina; Hinds (1987) showed Ovid deliberately crossing those markers.

  9. What evidence shows that the Fasti was left unfinished and revised in exile?
    Answer

    Ovid says his fate broke off the work (Tristia 2.549–552) and no ancient author quotes July to December; Carmenta's prophecy names Livia Julia Augusta, a name from AD 14 (1.536); the opening addresses Germanicus (1.3); and the poem stops on 30 June without a designed close (6.797–812).

  10. What links Bernini's Apollo and Daphne to the Ovide moralisé?
    Answer

    Maffeo Barberini's couplet for the base turns Ovid's scene into a warning that pursuing fleeting beauty yields only leaves and bitter berries. That is the medieval habit of attaching a Christian moral to an Ovidian story, as the Ovide moralisé did for every tale.

11Carry forward

  1. Read the Metamorphoses as a Roman universal history: Greek in matter, its frame runs to Rome, and in the Roman books metamorphosis becomes apotheosis, ending with Caesar's star and the poet's own claim to outlast Jupiter's anger.
  2. Ovid changed frames, voices and textures more than plots, and his Latin names carried the result into Europe's schools; where Antoninus Liberalis allows a test, the plot is Hellenistic and the frame, voice and texture are Ovid's.
  3. The Fasti and the Metamorphoses are a pair, Roman myth in elegiac couplets by the calendar and Greek myth in hexameter by time; the Fasti is half a year, retouched in exile for the dynasty that banished its author, and its explanations are poetry before they are evidence.

Give me an in-depth report on Ovid's Metamorphoses as the book through which later Europe learned Greek myth, read as the Roman poem it is. Cover: its structure from Chaos to the apotheosis of Caesar; the Roman frame (the council of the gods as the Senate, Jupiter and Augustus, the ending on Caesar's star); which stories Ovid invented or transformed (Echo and Narcissus, Pyramus and Thisbe, Arachne, Pygmalion, Medusa) and which he took from lost Hellenistic poets; the Roman names and why they stuck; the Fasti as its counterpart for Roman myth; the Heroides; and the poem's later life in the Ovide moralisé, Chaucer, Shakespeare, Titian and Bernini. Point back to Greek modules 0.1 and 3.7, which treat Ovid as a trap for the Greek material, and concentrate on what he did as a Roman.
