01Sulla's system under attack: Lepidus and Sertorius

Sulla died in 78, where module 5.2 ends. His settlement had handed the courts to the Senate, gagged the tribunes and planted veterans on confiscated land; within eight years most of it was gone, undone by men he had raised. The narrative rests on the fragments of Sallust's Histories (78–67), Plutarch's Lives, Appian's Civil Wars and Cassius Dio, now joined by a contemporary, Cicero; module 0.1 surveys the later narratives and module 5.6 introduces Sallust and Cicero as writers.

Marcus Aemilius Lepidus, consul in 78, had been elected with Pompey's support against Sulla's wishes (Plutarch, Pompey 15). He opposed Sulla's state funeral and promised to restore the confiscated land (Appian, Civil Wars 1.105, 1.107). Sent to suppress dispossessed farmers who had killed Sullan colonists at Faesulae, he joined them, and in 77 marched on Rome demanding a second consulship. The Senate passed its final decree; Sallust gives the speeches of Lepidus and of Philippus, who moved it (Histories 1.55 and 1.77 M). Catulus beat Lepidus outside the city, and Pompey had Lepidus' legate Marcus Brutus, father of the tyrannicide, killed after he surrendered at Mutina (Pompey 16).

In Spain the Marian Quintus Sertorius had held out since 80 with Lusitanian support, wearing down Metellus Pius by guerrilla war, forming a senate of exiles and schooling chiefs' sons at Osca as hostages (Plutarch, Sertorius 14, 22). The Senate sent Pompey, twenty-nine and holding no office, with proconsular command: not as proconsul, Philippus said, but in place of both consuls (Cicero, Philippics 11.18). Sertorius beat him at Lauron in 76, and the war dragged on until his officer Perperna murdered him in 73 or 72 (Sertorius 18, 26). Pompey executed Perperna and burnt Sertorius' letters unread, sparing the senators who had written them (Pompey 20).

02Spartacus, 73–71 BC

In 73 gladiators broke out of Lentulus Batiatus' school at Capua: seventy-eight in Plutarch (Crassus 8), about seventy in Appian (Civil Wars 1.116), sixty-four in Velleius (2.30.5). Their leader Spartacus was a Thracian who, Appian says, had fought Rome and been captured and sold; Plutarch makes him intelligent and humane, more Greek than Thracian. Runaway slaves and rural poor joined them, and through 72 they beat praetors and both consuls (Appian 1.116–117).

Late in 72 Marcus Licinius Crassus, Sulla's richest lieutenant, took command and revived decimation: on five hundred men who had fled, in Plutarch (Crassus 10); on two legions, in Appian (1.118). Pushed into Bruttium, Spartacus paid Cilician pirates to ferry men to Sicily; they took the money and sailed away. He broke through Crassus' ditch across the peninsula, and in 71, with Pompey returning from Spain, Crassus forced the last battle. Spartacus died fighting his way towards Crassus (Crassus 11). The Livian epitome claims 60,000 dead (Periochae 97); Appian alone reports six thousand prisoners crucified along the road from Capua to Rome (1.120). Pompey cut down five thousand fugitives, by Plutarch's count, and claimed to have torn the war out by the roots (Pompey 21). Crassus received only an ovation.

No slave speaks in the sources. Sallust's Histories, written a generation later, were the fullest and probably lie behind Plutarch, but survive in scraps; Plutarch and Appian wrote two centuries on; the epitome, Florus and Orosius descend from Livy. Cicero, a contemporary, mentions the war only in passing, as when he mocks Verres' claim to have kept it out of Sicily (Verrines 2.5).

Where sources disagree. Plutarch's Spartacus marches north meaning to cross the Alps and send his men home, until they refuse (Crassus 9); Appian's sets out for Rome with 120,000 foot, then turns back because his men are ill-armed and no city has joined him (1.117). Velleius gives 90,000 in the last battle (2.30.6); none of these figures is likely to rest on a count. Marx, writing to Engels in 1861, saw in Spartacus a representative of the ancient proletariat; Keith Bradley (Slavery and Rebellion in the Roman World, 1989) argued that the slave revolts sought escape, not abolition. Module 9.3 places the revolt within the institution.

03The year 70: Pompey, Crassus and the end of Sulla's constitution

Crassus and Pompey kept their armies near Rome and stood together for the consulship of 70. Pompey, thirty-five, had held no magistracy and was not a senator; the rules were waived (Cicero, De imperio Cn. Pompei 62). Pressure to restore the tribunate had been constant since 78, and Sallust gives the tribune Licinius Macer a speech demanding it in 73 (Histories 3.48 M). In 70 the consuls restored the tribunes' veto and right to legislate. The praetor Lucius Aurelius Cotta divided the juries among senators, equites and tribuni aerarii. The revived censors expelled sixty-four senators (Periochae 98), among them Gaius Antonius and Lentulus Sura, both heard of again in 63. Pompey paraded before them as a knight leading his horse; Crassus feasted the people at ten thousand tables (Plutarch, Pompey 22; Crassus 12). Sulla's enlarged Senate, standing courts and colonies survived; his government without tribunes did not.

Verres and the Verrines

Gaius Verres, governor of Sicily 73–71, was prosecuted for extortion that summer by Cicero, a new man from Arpinum who had been quaestor in Sicily. His advocate Hortensius, consul-elect for 69, played for delay into that year; Cicero replaced the long opening with a short speech, called his witnesses at once, and after the first hearing Verres went into exile at Massilia (Plutarch, Cicero 7). The five books of the Actio secunda were published, never delivered.

They are unique evidence: a governor's year rebuilt from city accounts, tithe records and testimony. Verres sold verdicts (2.2), rigged the grain tithe (2.3), stripped temples of their statues (2.4) and crucified a Roman citizen, Gavius, as he cried "civis Romanus sum" (2.5.162). Cicero claimed forty million sesterces (Verrines 1.56); Plutarch says Cicero assessed the damages at 750,000 denarii and was suspected of a bribe for keeping them low (Cicero 8). This is prosecution oratory, and every figure is the prosecutor's. It made Cicero the first advocate in Rome.

04The great commands: the Gabinian and Manilian laws

By 68 pirates had destroyed a consular fleet at Ostia and kidnapped two praetors (Cicero, De imperio 32–33; Plutarch, Pompey 24). In 67 the tribune Aulus Gabinius proposed one commander for three years over the whole sea and fifty miles inland, with imperium equal to the governors' there (Velleius 2.31.2). The bill named no one; everyone knew whom it meant. When the tribune Trebellius vetoed, Gabinius began to have the tribes depose him, as Tiberius Gracchus had Octavius (module 5.1), and he gave way. Grain prices fell on the day of the appointment (Plutarch, Pompey 26). Cicero says Pompey began the war in early spring and finished it by midsummer (De imperio 35); surrendered pirates were resettled as farmers at Soli, renamed Pompeiopolis (Pompey 28).

Where sources disagree. Plutarch says Gabinius' bill offered two hundred ships and fifteen legates (Pompey 25), and that Pompey was then given five hundred ships, 120,000 infantry, 5,000 cavalry and twenty-four legates (26). Appian gives 270 ships, 120,000 foot, 4,000 horse, twenty-five legates and 6,000 talents (Mithridatic Wars 94). Plutarch has the western sea cleared in forty days, Appian forty days in the west and as many in the east (95), the Livian epitome the whole sea in forty days (Periochae 99). The totals were probably authorisations rather than ships at sea.

Lucullus had driven Mithridates from Pontus (Greek module 7.3) and in 69 taken Tigranes' capital, Tigranocerta, but his curbs on interest in Asia angered the tax-farmers, and in 68–67 his army mutinied, stirred by his brother-in-law Publius Clodius (Plutarch, Lucullus 20, 34). In 66 the tribune Gaius Manilius transferred the war to Pompey. Cicero, praetor, spoke for the law in his first speech from the Rostra: a general needs knowledge of war, courage, authority and luck (De imperio 28), Pompey alone has all four, and the revenues of Asia and the equites' money there are at stake (14–19).

05Pompey's settlement of the East

Pompey beat Mithridates in 66, and Tigranes surrendered and kept Armenia on payment of 6,000 talents (Plutarch, Pompey 33). Syria was annexed in 64 and the Seleucids ended without a battle, as Greek module 7.3 tells. In 63 Pompey took the Temple at Jerusalem after a three-month siege and entered the Holy of Holies, leaving its treasure untouched (Josephus, Jewish Antiquities 14.72); Hyrcanus II became high priest without the royal title. Pontus was joined to Bithynia as a province, Cilicia was enlarged, and client rulers bound to Pompey personally, Deiotarus in Galatia and Ariobarzanes in Cappadocia, filled the gaps.

The totals differ by source. Plutarch reports triumphal tablets claiming a thousand strongholds, nearly nine hundred cities, eight hundred pirate ships, thirty-nine cities founded, revenues raised from fifty to eighty-five million drachmas and twenty thousand talents for the treasury (Pompey 45). Pliny quotes a dedication: 12,183,000 people defeated, killed or received in surrender, 846 ships sunk or taken, 1,538 towns and forts (Natural History 7.97–98). These are claims, not audits.

06Cicero's consulship and Catiline

Lucius Sergius Catilina, a patrician and a killer in Sulla's proscriptions (Asconius, on Cicero's In a White Toga), lost the election for 63 to Cicero and Gaius Antonius, and in 63 lost again on a promise to cancel debts. Cicero, the first new man made consul in a very long time (De lege agraria 2.3), bought Antonius' loyalty with Macedonia and presided at the elections wearing a breastplate (Pro Murena 52).

In Etruria a Sullan centurion, Manlius, raised veterans and ruined farmers, and in late October the Senate passed its final decree. After a plot to kill Cicero at his morning reception was betrayed by Fulvia, mistress of a conspirator (Sallust, Catiline 28), Cicero denounced Catiline in the Senate on 8 November, and Catiline left for Manlius' camp. In Rome, Lentulus Sura, now praetor, and Cethegus approached envoys of the Allobroges, who told their patron, who told Cicero; arrested with sealed letters in the night of 2–3 December, they turned evidence (Sallust 40–41, 44–45). On 5 December Silanus, consul-elect, proposed death. Caesar, praetor-elect, argued against killing citizens untried; his speech in Sallust warns that bad precedents begin as good ones (51.27). Cato, tribune-elect, carried the house (52). Five men were strangled in the Tullianum that evening, and Cicero announced it in one word, vixerunt, they have lived (Plutarch, Cicero 22). Catiline fell at Pistoria in January 62 with an army that, Sallust says, died with its wounds in front (61).

Cicero published the speeches in 60 among his consular orations (Letters to Atticus 2.1.3), when the executions had become a liability; how far he revised them is uncertain. They argue the consul's case: a monster from whom Cicero alone saved Rome. Sallust's Catiline, written about 42–41, gives Catiline a voice (20, 35), treats the plot as a symptom of a society corrupted by wealth and ambition, and makes the climax the debate of Caesar and Cato (51–54), giving the First Catilinarian one sentence (31.6). The two agree on outline and dates and part on motive and blame. Module 5.6 reads Sallust as a writer; deep dive module 11.4 takes up Sallust's Sempronia.

Where sources disagree. Sallust (18–19) and Suetonius (Julius 9) report an earlier plot of 66–65 to murder the consuls; Robin Seager ("The First Catilinarian Conspiracy", 1964) argued that it was a fiction built from electoral slander. Sallust reports and rejects charges against Crassus (48) and Caesar (49), which Suetonius, citing Caesar's enemies, takes more seriously. Whether 63 was a coherent revolution or the desperation of bankrupt nobles and veterans, enlarged by the consul who crushed it, is still argued.
Where sources disagree. Sallust (51.43) and Cicero (Against Catiline 4.7–10) make Caesar's proposal permanent custody in Italian towns; Plutarch (Caesar 7) and Appian (Civil Wars 2.5–6) make it custody until Catiline was beaten, then trial. The difference is the legal point. Gaius Gracchus' law forbade executing a citizen without the people's verdict; Cicero replied that a public enemy is no citizen (4.10), and the final decree, like Opimius' in 121 (module 5.1), claimed that emergency suspended the rule. Clodius tested that claim in 58 (deep dive module 11.3).

07Caesar before 60, and Clodius at the Bona Dea

Gaius Julius Caesar, born in 100 to an old patrician house, was Marius' nephew by marriage and Cinna's son-in-law. He refused Sulla's order to divorce Cinna's daughter and was spared with the warning that the boy held many Mariuses (Suetonius, Julius 1). Captured by pirates, he promised to crucify them and did (Plutarch, Caesar 2). He then ran a popular line: Marius' images at his aunt's funeral in 69, 320 pairs of gladiators as aedile in 65, and Marius' trophies restored to the Capitol (Caesar 5–6). In 63 he backed a treason charge against the aged Rabirius for the killing of Saturninus in 100, a challenge to the final decree; Cicero defended Rabirius (Dio 37.26–28). The same year, deep in debt, he beat Catulus to the office of pontifex maximus (Plutarch, Caesar 7). Before he could leave for Spain in 61, Crassus stood surety for 830 talents of his debts (Caesar 11).

In December 62 the women's rites of the Bona Dea were held in the pontifex maximus' house. Publius Clodius Pulcher got in dressed as a female lute player and was caught (Plutarch, Caesar 9–10). Tried for sacrilege, he claimed to have been at Interamna; Cicero testified that he had seen him in Rome that day (Plutarch, Cicero 29). The jury acquitted by thirty-one votes to twenty-five, and Cicero blamed bribery (Letters to Atticus 1.16.5). Caesar divorced his wife Pompeia because his household must be free of suspicion (Suetonius, Julius 74). Clodius never forgave Cicero; his tribunate of 58 and Cicero's exile belong to deep dive modules 11.3 and 11.5.

08Pompey's return and the alliance of 60

Pompey landed at Brundisium in December 62 and dismissed his army (Velleius 2.40.3). He asked two things: ratification of his eastern acts as a whole, and land for his veterans. The Senate, led by Lucullus, Cato and Metellus Celer, whose half-sister Pompey had divorced, insisted on examining the acts one by one and blocked a land bill in 60 (Dio 37.49–50). Cato also opposed the Asian tax-farmers, whom Crassus backed, in their plea to cancel a third of their contract; Cicero complained that Cato spoke as if in Plato's Republic, not the dregs of Romulus (Letters to Atticus 2.1.8). Caesar, back from Spain in 60, could not both await a triumph outside the city and declare his candidacy within it; Cato talked out an exemption, and Caesar chose the consulship (Plutarch, Caesar 13; Suetonius, Julius 18). He then reconciled Pompey and Crassus in a compact that nothing should be done in the state that displeased any of the three (Julius 19). In December 60 Caesar's agent Balbus invited Cicero in (Letters to Atticus 2.3.3); he declined.

Where sources disagree. Plutarch (Caesar 13–14), Appian (Civil Wars 2.9) and the Livian epitome (Periochae 103) put the compact before Caesar's election in 60; Dio has him win both men over before it but narrates the compact after (37.54–58), Suetonius sets it after the election (Julius 19), Velleius in the consulship (2.44.1). Cicero's letter of December 60 shows an understanding with Pompey forming, Crassus not yet in it. Asinius Pollio began his history of the civil wars in 60 (Horace, Odes 2.1.1), dating the Republic's end to the compact.

The ancient accounts call it a pact or, in Velleius, a partnership in power (2.44.1); Varro called it a three-headed monster (Appian, Civil Wars 2.9). The surviving accounts do not call it a triumvirate. A triumvirate was a board created by law, like that of Antony, Lepidus and Octavian under the lex Titia of 43 (module 5.5); the arrangement of 60 had no law, title, term or office. "First triumvirate" reads the legal junta of 43 back into a private alliance of three men who distrusted one another.

09Self-test

  1. Why did Lepidus' revolt start at Faesulae, and what did it reveal about Sulla's settlement?
    Answer

    Farmers dispossessed for Sullan colonists rose and killed the settlers; Lepidus, sent to suppress them, joined them. The confiscations had created a constituency for revolt, which Lepidus courted by promising to restore the land (Appian, Civil Wars 1.107).

  2. Why can no figure for Spartacus' army be trusted, and whose figures are they?
    Answer

    No slave source survives, the near-contemporary Sallust is in fragments, and the narratives are two centuries later. Appian gives 120,000 foot, Velleius 90,000 in the last battle, the Livian epitome 60,000 dead; Appian alone gives the six thousand crucified.

  3. What did the consuls of 70 undo of Sulla's system, and what survived?
    Answer

    The tribunes' veto and right to legislate were restored, Cotta's law shared the juries among senators, equites and tribuni aerarii, and the revived censors purged sixty-four senators. The enlarged Senate, the standing courts and the veteran colonies survived.

  4. Why did Cicero shorten his opening in the Verres trial, and what limits the Verrines as evidence?
    Answer

    Hortensius aimed to delay the trial into 69, when he would be consul; Cicero called witnesses at once and Verres fled after the first hearing. The speeches rest on documents and testimony but argue a prosecutor's case, and the Actio secunda was published, never delivered.

  5. What made the Gabinian law unprecedented, and what earlier tribunician tactic did Gabinius use?
    Answer

    One commander, three years, the whole sea and fifty miles inland, imperium equal to the governors' (Velleius 2.31.2), and resources beyond any magistrate's. Faced with Trebellius' veto, he began to have the tribes depose him, as Tiberius Gracchus had Octavius.

  6. How should the figures for Pompey's eastern conquests be read?
    Answer

    As self-advertisement, attributed to their reporters: Plutarch's triumphal tablets with a thousand strongholds and thirty-nine cities founded (Pompey 45), and Pliny's dedication with 12,183,000 people and 1,538 towns and forts (Natural History 7.97–98). They are claims, not audits.

  7. How do Cicero's four speeches and Sallust's Catiline differ as evidence for 63?
    Answer

    Cicero's are contemporary but were published in 60, perhaps revised, and argue the consul's case. Sallust wrote about twenty years later, gives Catiline a voice, makes the plot a symptom of moral decay and builds the climax on the debate of Caesar and Cato, passing over the First Catilinarian in a sentence (31.6).

  8. On what grounds were the conspirators executed, and what was the legal objection?
    Answer

    On the Senate's vote under the final decree, with Cicero arguing that public enemies forfeit citizenship (Against Catiline 4.10). Gaius Gracchus' law forbade executing citizens without the people's verdict, the point of Caesar's proposal, whose terms Sallust and Plutarch report differently.

  9. Why did the Bona Dea scandal matter beyond the sacrilege?
    Answer

    Clodius' acquittal by 31 votes to 25 amid bribery (Letters to Atticus 1.16.5) showed that a court could be bought, and Cicero's testimony against his alibi made Clodius his enemy, with consequences in 58 (deep dive modules 11.3 and 11.5).

  10. Why is "first triumvirate" a misleading name for the alliance of 60?
    Answer

    A triumvirate was a board created by law, as by the lex Titia of 43. The compact of 60 was private, with no title, term or office; ancient writers called it a pact or a partnership in power (Velleius 2.44.1), and Varro a three-headed monster.

10Carry forward

  1. The extraordinary command became the normal answer to crisis: Pompey's career from 77 to 62 was a run of commands, the greatest of them voted by the people over senatorial objection, with resources and imperium no annual magistrate held, and the dynasts of the 50s and 40s built on that model.
  2. Sulla's settlement failed because it could not exclude the tribunate and the people. Once the tribunes were restored in 70 the assemblies were again the road to power, and the Senate's counter, the final decree, was itself put in question by the execution of the Catilinarians.
  3. This period has contemporary evidence for the first time, but it is advocacy: one interested witness shaped the whole tradition of 63, and every figure, from Spartacus' army to Pompey's conquests, belongs to the writer who gives it.
