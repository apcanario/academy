01The Capitoline

Greek module 9.2 walks Athens from an Acropolis where everything standing belongs to one generation. Rome is stacked: archaic cult under republican paving under imperial marble under churches, and what stands is usually the last rebuilding. Each stop gives what is there, the module that owns it and one thing to look for; the story stays in the module.

The temple platform. The foundations of Jupiter Best and Greatest, shared with Juno and Minerva (module 2.2), lie inside the Capitoline Museums: cappellaccio tufa, about 54 by 62 metres (module 1.5). The temple burned in 69 and rose again from 70 (module 7.2); Procopius has Gaiseric ship off half its gilded roof in 455 (module 8.5). Look for the size of the blocks, then decide with module 1.5's dispute whether the whole platform carried the temple.

The Tabularium. The arcaded substructure of 78 BC under the Senator's Palace closes the Forum's west end; the museum's underground gallery runs through it to a view of the Forum. Look for the arch framed by engaged half-columns, a Greek order drawn on a structure of arches, which the Theatre of Marcellus and the Colosseum later stacked in storeys (module 10.3).

Where sources disagree. The name rests on a lost inscription in which Catulus, consul in 78, records building a substructure and a tabularium (CIL VI 1314), long read as the state record office. Nicholas Purcell (1993) argued that the inscriptions do not name the building; Pier Luigi Tucci (2005) took it for one record office among many, with the temple of Juno Moneta on top; Filippo Coarelli (2010) reads it as the foundations of a large Sullan temple. The arches are certain; the name is not.

The Capitoline Museums. In the Hall of the She-wolf the bronze wolf stands among the Fasti Capitolini set into the walls. The wolf is module 1.4's: cast in one pour, radiocarbon-dated to about 1021–1153, its twins added after 1471. The Fasti, the Augustan list of consuls and triumphs found in the Forum in 1546, are modules 0.1 and 0.3. Look for the head of the triumphal list, Romulus over the Caeninenses, a record no one could have kept: two objects in one room that dress legend as record. The original equestrian Marcus Aurelius stands in the glass hall over the temple foundations. It survived at the Lateran because it was taken for Constantine (module 10.4); the reign is module 7.3, and deep dive module 13.7 uses statue and column as sources for the man. Look for the extended right hand and the absence of any weapon.

02The Forum, west end

The temple of Saturn. Eight columns on a high podium below the Capitol. It held the state treasury, and its image wore woollen bonds loosed at the Saturnalia (module 2.2, with the doubts over its foundation date); Caesar emptied the reserve treasury in 49 (module 5.4). Look for the frieze inscription, incendio consumptum restituit, "restored when consumed by fire": the columns are a late antique rebuilding.

The Rostra. The speakers' platform is named from the beaks of Antium's warships fixed to its predecessor in 338 (Livy 8.14.12; module 3.3). Before Caesar moved it here in 44 (Dio 43.49.1), the platform displayed Octavius' head in 87 (module 5.2); Cicero's head and hands followed in 43 (module 5.5). Look for the holes in its front where bronze beaks were fixed, and later compare its picture on the Arch of Constantine, where Constantine speaks from it (modules 8.3 and 10.3).

The Arch of Septimius Severus. Dedicated in 203 for restoring the state and extending the empire (module 7.4); its campaign panels are module 10.3's. Look for the fourth line of the inscription, recut after Geta's murder as "best and bravest princes", the dowel holes of the bronze letters still showing the lost name (module 7.4).

The Comitium and the Lapis Niger. Before the Curia a patch of black stone paving marks a spot the late Republic chose to keep. Beneath it Giacomo Boni found in 1899 an archaic altar, a column stump and the cippus of about 570–550 BC with a sacred law naming a rex (module 1.5). Module 0.1 weighs it as a source; module 1.4 gives the ancient guesses, Romulus' tomb or Faustulus', and Coarelli's Volcanal. Look for the paving itself: a marker laid some five centuries after the stone it covers, over a place Romans could no longer explain.

The Curia. The brick hall standing to its roof is Diocletian's rebuilding after a fire of 283 (module 8.2), saved by becoming a church in the seventh century. Its predecessor burned as Clodius' pyre in 52 (module 5.4; deep dive module 11.3), and it housed the altar of Victory that Symmachus defended in 384 (module 8.4). Look for the height: here you stand inside the Senate's room at full scale.

The basilicas. The Basilica Aemilia to the north and the Basilica Julia to the south are roofed halls of the type of Cato's Basilica Porcia of 184 (modules 4.5 and 10.3). Augustus lists the Julia, begun by Caesar, among the works he finished (Res Gestae 20.3). Look for the gaming boards scratched into the Julia's steps, and on the Aemilia's floor the stains of bronze coins melted in a fire usually laid to Alaric's sack of 410 (module 8.5).

03The Forum, east end and the Sacred Way

The temple of the deified Julius. Octavian dedicated it in 29 where the crowd had burned Caesar's body, and decked its platform with prows from Actium (modules 5.5 and 6.2; the funeral is deep dive module 11.7's). Look for the curved niche in the podium's front, whose round core is usually taken for the altar on the site of the pyre.

The temple of Castor. Vowed at Lake Regillus and dedicated, Livy says nearly five centuries later, in 484 (module 1.6); the three standing columns belong to Tiberius' rebuilding (Suetonius, Tiberius 20). Look for Juturna's basin beside it, where the twins watered their horses after the battle (module 0.2): a myth kept by keeping a place.

The Regia. A small building traced by Frank Brown from the late seventh century to the later sixth, where a bucchero sherd reads rex (module 1.5); the spears of Mars and the October Horse are modules 2.1 and 2.3. Look for the irregular plan, kept through every rebuilding: a king's cult house, too small for a palace.

The temple of Vesta and the House of the Vestals. The round temple held the fire and no image. The Vestals and their living burial are module 2.3, Augustus' gift of the rex sacrorum's house to them module 6.2, their end module 8.4. Look, in the long court of their house, for the statue bases of the chief Vestals, among them the base of 364 whose Vestal's name has been chiselled out (CIL VI 32422); no source says why.

The Arch of Titus. Walk up the Sacred Way, the triumphal road (module 9.2), past the temple of Antoninus and Faustina (module 7.3), kept standing as a church. At the top stands the arch dedicated to the deified Titus (CIL VI 945): history in module 7.2, the triumph of 71 in module 9.2, the relief as art in module 10.3. Look for the spoils panel, the Temple's lampstand and table carried through a gate seen at an angle. Then read the masonry: Giuseppe Valadier rebuilt the missing parts in travertine in the early nineteenth century; the marble is ancient.

04The Palatine

The Iron Age huts. On the Cermalus, the south-west corner, hut floors with post-holes and gutters are cut into the tufa beside the spot where Romans kept a hut of Romulus (Dionysius 1.79.11; module 1.4). Look for the ring of post-holes, and keep module 1.4's verdict: a village, not a founder. The Lupercal of 2007 is module 1.4's dispute.

The House of Augustus. Octavian lived in Hortensius' house, bought its neighbours and on ground struck by lightning vowed the temple of Apollo, dedicated in 28 beside them (module 6.2). The rooms Gianfilippo Carettoni excavated are painted in the Second style (module 10.3). Look at how small they are beside the temple platform, and test Suetonius' word "modest" (Augustus 72.1) against them.

Where sources disagree. Carettoni took the painted rooms for Augustus' house. Irene Iacopi and Giovanna Tedone argued that they were filled in after 36 BC for the Apollo complex, and Andrea Carandini reconstructed a grand residence wrapped round the temple. T. P. Wiseman (The House of Augustus, 2019) rejects the reconstruction, trusts the texts' modest house and places it under the later palace.

The House of Livia. A house of the same years, named from a lead pipe stamped with Julia Augusta, the name Livia took under Augustus' will (module 9.3; her politics, module 6.1). Look for the Second-style panel of Io guarded by Argus, with Mercury coming to free her.

The Flavian palace. Domitian's palace covers the hill's centre (module 7.2); its concrete halls lead towards the Pantheon (module 10.3), and Palatium gave the word palace (module 6.2). Look for the scale of the state rooms, with Statius' poem of thanks for a dinner there in mind (Silvae 4.2; module 10.2).

05The Imperial Fora, Trajan's Markets and the Column

The Via dei Fori Imperiali follows Mussolini's Via dell'Impero, opened in 1932 over a demolished quarter (module 10.4).

The Forum of Caesar. Dedicated in 46 with the temple of Venus Genetrix, ancestress of the Julii (module 5.4). Look for the three re-erected columns of the temple at the head of the square.

The Forum of Augustus. The temple of Mars the Avenger, vowed at Philippi and dedicated in 2 BC, closed a square whose exedrae set Aeneas and the Julii opposite Romulus and the republican great men (module 6.2). The Temple of Peace nearby received the vessels of Jerusalem (module 7.2). Look for the exedrae and their tiers of statue niches: module 6.2's argument in stone.

Trajan's Column. Dedicated in 113, it carries both Dacian wars in its spiral frieze, and its base later received Trajan's ashes (module 7.3); module 9.2 weighs the frieze as evidence for the army, module 10.3 reads it as art. Look for the inscription over the door, which says the column shows how high the hill stood that was cut away for the forum (module 7.3). St Peter has stood on top since 1587.

Trajan's Markets. A brick hemicycle and terraces cut into the Quirinal slope behind the forum's north-eastern exedra, built with it: streets, a vaulted hall and rooms with shop-like doors. No written module describes it; the reign is module 7.3, the city's supply module 9.4. Look for the vaulted hall, and ask what the rooms were for.

Where sources disagree. Corrado Ricci, who cleared the complex in 1924–34, published it as Trajan's market ("Il mercato di Traiano", 1929), and the name stuck. The Museo dei Fori Imperiali there reads the rooms as offices and record stores of a multi-functional centre tied to the forum, perhaps used by the procurator of Trajan's forum. No ancient text names the building.

06The Colosseum and the Arch of Constantine

The Colosseum. Begun by Vespasian on the drained lake of Nero's Golden House and dedicated by Titus in 80 (module 7.2, with Alföldy's reading of its lost bronze letters). The games and the flooding question are module 9.4's; the radial vaults and the Tuscan, Ionic and Corinthian storeys module 10.3's. Look for the hypogeum, the cages and lift shafts under the arena, usually dated to Domitian, that made it, in module 9.4's word, a machine.

The Arch of Constantine. Dedicated by the Senate in 315 for Constantine's tenth anniversary (module 8.3); the reused Trajanic, Hadrianic and Aurelian sculpture, the new frieze and the argument over decline are modules 8.3 and 10.3. Look for instinctu divinitatis in the attic inscription (CIL VI 1139): an unnamed divinity a pagan Senate could write and a Christian emperor accept.

Where sources disagree. A. L. Frothingham (1912) dated the arch's core as early as Domitian, and Maria Letizia Conforto (Adriano e Costantino, 2001) argued that at least its lower part is a Hadrianic arch remodelled for Constantine. Patrizio Pensabene and Clementina Panella (Arco di Costantino tra archeologia e archeometria, 1999) and Mark Wilson Jones (Journal of the Society of Architectural Historians, 2000) defend a single Constantinian design.

07The Circus Maximus, the Forum Boarium and Sant'Omobono

The Circus Maximus. The valley between Palatine and Aventine, laid out in legend by Tarquinius Priscus (module 1.5); the races, the colours and the dispute over its seating are module 9.4's, and every triumph passed through it (module 9.2). Look, at the curved south-eastern end, for the footings found in 2015 of the arch of 80–81 that claimed Titus took a Jerusalem never taken before (CIL VI 944; module 7.2): Pompey had taken it in 63 BC.

The Forum Boarium. The cattle market and harbour at the Tiber Island crossing (module 0.2), where the Ara Maxima served Hercules with a Greek rite (module 1.3) and Gauls and Greeks were buried alive after Cannae (module 4.3). Two temples stand: the round marble temple, Hercules Victor in module 2.2, and the temple of Portunus of about 100 BC (module 10.3). Look at Portunus for Ionic columns free in the porch and engaged round the cella: Greek orders on an Etruscan podium.

Sant'Omobono. Beside the church lie the twin temples of Fortuna and Mater Matuta that tradition gives to Servius Tullius (module 1.5); the site gave eighth-century Greek pottery (module 1.1) and a terracotta Hercules and Minerva of about 530 (module 1.3). Look down: the republican podia stand on the fill that buried the archaic temple about 500 BC (module 1.5).

08The Campus Martius

Largo Argentina. Four republican temples, lettered A to D because their gods are disputed; the round temple B is usually taken for the Fortune of This Day that Catulus vowed at Vercellae in 101 (Plutarch, Marius 26; module 5.2). Behind them lie remains of the hall of Pompey's theatre complex where the Senate met on 15 March 44 (module 5.4; the murder source by source, deep dive module 11.7). Look for that hall's foundation, and recall that the room was afterwards walled up (Suetonius, Julius 88).

The Pantheon. Hadrian's rebuilding under the original builder's name, probably begun under Trajan (module 7.3), its dome module 10.3's; Agrippa's building had held Caesar's statue inside and Augustus' in the porch (Dio 53.27.3; module 6.2). Look for the porch inscription, M AGRIPPA L F COS TERTIVM FECIT, and on the block behind it a second pediment above the first (module 10.3).

The column of Marcus Aurelius. In the Piazza Colonna, finished by 193 (module 7.3), its harsher frieze read against Trajan's in module 10.3. Look for the rain miracle, a winged storm god pouring rain over the army, which deep dive module 13.7 sets against the Christian versions. St Paul has stood on top since 1589.

The Ara Pacis and the Mausoleum. The altar dedicated in 9 BC and the tomb begun in 28 BC are module 6.2's, with the disputes over the sacrificer, the goddess and the sundial; the altar was reassembled away from its site in 1938, beside a Mausoleum isolated in a new square (module 10.4). Look on the long sides for the depth of carving, front figures deep and those behind shallow (module 10.3), then at the museum wall carved with the Res Gestae that once stood in bronze before the tomb (Suetonius, Augustus 101.4; module 6.2).

09The Baths of Caracalla and the Palazzo Massimo

The Baths of Caracalla. Inaugurated in 216, with 1,600 marble seats by Olympiodorus' count (module 7.4); bathing as an institution is module 9.4's, the bath plan module 10.3's, and the Farnese Hercules and Bull found here in the 1540s are now in Naples. Look for the symmetry: the same suite of rooms on either side of the axis through the great hall. Beneath lies a Mithraeum (module 2.5).

The Palazzo Massimo. The National Museum's palace faces the Baths of Diocletian, whose cold hall is Michelangelo's Santa Maria degli Angeli (module 10.3). It holds the garden room from Livia's villa at Prima Porta, where the Prima Porta statue was found (module 6.2): a painted orchard full of birds round all four walls of an underground room, the Second style dissolving the wall (module 10.3). Look for the double fence, a light lattice in front and a stone balustrade behind, that holds you at the edge of a garden you cannot enter. Here too is the bronze Boxer, a Hellenistic original found on the Quirinal (module 10.3; Greek module 9.1). Look for the cuts picked out in inlaid copper. Then find the Fasti Antiates, the one surviving pre-Julian calendar (module 2.3), to set beside the Capitoline Fasti where the walk began.

10Walking orders

One day: Capitoline, Forum, Palatine, Colosseum and arch, the fora from the street, the Pantheon. Two days: first the Capitol, Forum and Palatine, then down to the river quarter and along the Circus to the Baths of Caracalla; next the emperors' city, from the Colosseum through the fora to the Campus Martius, ending by Metro at the Palazzo Massimo.

11Self-test

  1. What do the wolf and the Fasti in the Hall of the She-wolf have in common as evidence?
    Answer

    Both dress legend as record. The wolf, long thought Etruscan, is a medieval casting of about 1021–1153 with fifteenth-century twins (module 1.4); the Augustan Fasti open their triumphs with Romulus over the Caeninenses, a record no one could have kept (modules 0.1 and 0.3).

  2. Why is the name Tabularium insecure, and what about the building is certain?
    Answer

    It rests on Catulus' lost inscription recording a substructure and a tabularium (CIL VI 1314); Purcell argues that the inscriptions do not name the building, Tucci sets the temple of Juno Moneta on it, and Coarelli makes it a temple platform. Catulus' date of 78 BC and the arches framed by half-columns (module 10.3) are not in doubt.

  3. Name three inscriptions in the Forum that change what you are looking at, and say how.
    Answer

    The temple of Saturn's incendio consumptum restituit makes its columns a late antique rebuilding; the fourth line of the Severan arch, recut after Geta's murder, shows his name in the dowel holes (module 7.4); the Vestal base of 364 (CIL VI 32422) has had its honorand's name chiselled out.

  4. What does the Lapis Niger show about how Romans treated their own archaic past?
    Answer

    The late Republic covered an archaic altar, column stump and inscribed cippus of about 570–550 BC with black paving instead of removing them. The spot was kept, though ancient writers could only guess its meaning, Romulus' tomb or Faustulus' (modules 1.4 and 1.5).

  5. Which three modules own the Arch of Titus, and what does each take from it?
    Answer

    Module 7.2 the Jewish War and the dedication to the deified Titus (CIL VI 945); module 9.2 the triumph of 71 that the spoils panel records; module 10.3 the relief's procession swinging into and out of the plane. Valadier's travertine marks what is restoration.

  6. What is disputed about the House of Augustus?
    Answer

    Carettoni identified the painted rooms as Augustus' house; Iacopi and Tedone argue they were filled in after 36 BC for the Apollo complex; Carandini reconstructs a grand residence round the temple. Wiseman (2019) rejects the reconstruction and puts the modest house of the texts under the later palace.

  7. What does Trajan's Column say about itself, and why is "Trajan's Markets" a hypothesis rather than a fact?
    Answer

    Its inscription says it shows the height of the hill cut away for the forum (module 7.3). The name comes from Corrado Ricci's clearance and his publication of 1929; the museum now reads the rooms as offices and stores of a multi-functional centre, and no ancient text names the building.

  8. What does the attic inscription of the Arch of Constantine claim, and what is argued about the arch itself?
    Answer

    That Constantine won by the prompting of the divinity, instinctu divinitatis, a formula pagan senators and a Christian emperor could share (CIL VI 1139; module 8.3). Conforto argued that at least the lower part is a Hadrianic arch remodelled; Pensabene and Panella and Wilson Jones defend a single Constantinian design.

  9. What does the temple of Portunus show, and what lies beneath the visible temples at Sant'Omobono?
    Answer

    Portunus shows the Roman synthesis: Ionic columns free in the porch and engaged round the cella, on a high Etruscan podium (module 10.3). At Sant'Omobono the republican podia stand on the fill that buried the archaic temple about 500 BC (module 1.5).

  10. What links Largo Argentina to Caesar and to the war against the Cimbri?
    Answer

    Behind the temples lies the hall of Pompey's complex where Caesar was killed on 15 March 44, afterwards walled up (Suetonius, Julius 88; module 5.4, deep dive module 11.7). The round temple B is usually identified with the Fortune of This Day that Catulus vowed at Vercellae in 101 (Plutarch, Marius 26).

12Carry forward

  1. Rome is a stack, not a set: on almost every site what stands is the last rebuilding (Diocletian's Curia, a late antique Saturn, Tiberius' Castor, Hadrian's Pantheon, Valadier's arch), so the first question at any ruin is which layer you are looking at.
  2. Monuments are claims before they are evidence: the Fasti's Romulus, the column's hill, Titus' Jerusalem, Agrippa's name and Constantine's divinity say what someone wanted said, and erasures such as Geta's line or the Vestal of 364 are part of the message.
  3. Names on the ground are hypotheses with histories: Tabularium, House of Augustus, House of Livia, Trajan's Markets, Lupercal and tomb of Romulus rest on an inscription, a pipe or an excavator's reading, and the city you walk was arranged by Michelangelo, Ricci and Mussolini as much as by emperors.
