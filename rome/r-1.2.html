Origins: what the ancients said

The Greeks called them Tyrrhenoi, the Romans Tusci or Etrusci; they called themselves Rasenna (Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Roman Antiquities 1.30.3), rasna in their inscriptions. Every surviving ancient account of their origin is Greek.

Herodotus 1.94, writing about 430 BC, reports the Lydian version: under King Atys an eighteen-year famine forced half the people to sail from Smyrna under his son Tyrrhenus to the land of the Umbrians. Herodotus sets Atys before the Heraclid kings of Lydia, some eight centuries before he wrote. The story became orthodoxy: Virgil's Etruscans are a "Lydian people" (Aeneid 8.479), and in AD 26 Sardis cited an Etruscan decree acknowledging kinship (Tacitus, Annals 4.55). Hellanicus, Herodotus' contemporary, made them Pelasgians who landed at the mouth of the Po under King Nanas and took Cortona (quoted by Dionysius 1.28.3). Dionysius 1.30, writing about 7 BC, rejects both: they are native, "a very ancient nation" unlike any other in language and customs, and the Lydian historian Xanthus never mentions Tyrrhenus (1.28.2).

Where sources disagree. Myrsilus of Lesbos reversed Hellanicus: Tyrrhenians became Pelasgians (Dionysius 1.28.4). All four views are Greek genealogising of the kind applied to every foreign people. Dionysius had the better argument from language, but also a motive: his first book argues that the Romans were Greeks (1.89–90), a case easier to make if Rome's neighbours were not.

Origins: Villanovans, Lemnos and ancient DNA

Massimo Pallottino (L'origine degli Etruschi, 1947) changed the question: not where a people came from but how a nation formed. The Villanovan culture, named after a cemetery near Bologna dug from 1853, appears in the ninth century BC on the very plateaux that became Veii, Caere, Tarquinia, Vulci and Volsinii, and develops without a break into the later cities; Etruscan inscriptions appear on the same sites from about 700 BC. No horizon of intrusion has been found.

The Lemnos stele, found at Kaminia in 1885 and dated to the later sixth century BC, shows a warrior and carries a text in a language plainly related to Etruscan: Lemnian aviš sialχviš matches Etruscan avils śealχls, "of sixty years". With Alpine Raetic they form the Tyrsenian family (Helmut Rix).

Where sources disagree. For Robert Beekes (The Origin of the Etruscans, 2003) Lemnian is the residue of a migration from north-west Anatolia around 1200 BC. Carlo de Simone and most Etruscologists reply that Lemnian is so close to sixth-century Etruscan that it is best explained by Tyrrhenian traders or pirates from Italy settled in the Aegean. Raetic, which nobody derives from Anatolia, tips the balance west.

Genetics was once cited for the east, from Near Eastern mitochondrial lineages in modern Tuscans (Achilli and colleagues, 2007), but modern samples cannot date an ancestry. Ancient genomes have settled the population question. Posth and colleagues (Science Advances, 2021), sampling 82 individuals from 800 BC to AD 1000, found the Etruscans genetically like their Latin neighbours, as Antonio and colleagues (Science, 2019) had suggested: they carried the steppe-related ancestry that reached Italy in the second millennium BC, with no recent Anatolian input, and eastern Mediterranean ancestry arrives only under the Empire. The paradox is the result: a population with ancestry usually linked to Indo-European speakers kept a pre-Indo-European language.

The twelve peoples and the Fanum Voltumnae

Etruria was never a state but a set of independent cities, which the Romans called the twelve peoples of Etruria. Their delegates met yearly at the shrine of Voltumna, the Fanum Voltumnae, for festival, games, a priestly election and debate on war (Livy 4.23, 4.25, 5.17). In 403 BC, Livy says, Veii's king, beaten in that election, withdrew his performers mid-festival, and the league left Veii to face Rome alone (5.1). Livy wrote nearly four centuries later, but the point holds: the league could coordinate, not command. The shrine is probably at Campo della Fiera below Orvieto, excavated by Simonetta Stopponi since 2000, and Constantine's rescript to Hispellum (CIL XI 5265) still mentions games at Volsinii.

No ancient text lists the twelve. Six are certain:

  • Veii, 16 km from Rome, its rival for the Tiber crossing; Pliny makes its sculptor Vulca the maker of the Capitoline Jupiter (Natural History 35.157).
  • Caere (Greek Agylla), the great port city, with a treasury at Delphi (Strabo 5.2.3).
  • Tarquinia, city of Tarchon and Tages and of the painted tombs.
  • Vulci, a bronze-working centre and the richest source of Attic vases.
  • Volsinii (probably Orvieto), destroyed by Rome in 264 BC; Metrodorus of Scepsis claimed Rome took it for its two thousand statues (Pliny, Natural History 34.34).
  • Clusium, inland and agrarian, Porsenna's city (module 1.6).

Other candidates are Perusia, Cortona, Arretium, Volaterrae, Vetulonia, Rusellae and Populonia. Etruscans also ruled Capua and founded cities beyond the Apennines (Livy 5.33).

Metals, princes and the vase trade

Etruria's wealth lay underground: copper, lead and silver in the Colline Metallifere and the Tolfa hills, and iron on Elba, smelted on the island and worked on the mainland (Diodorus 5.13). The ores drew the Euboeans to Pithekoussai on Ischia about 770 BC, and then to Cumae.

In the orientalising period, about 720–580 BC, a new elite of "princes" was buried with luxuries from the Levant, Egypt and Greece. The Regolini-Galassi tomb at Caere, found intact in 1836 and now in the Vatican, held a woman's burial of about 675–650 BC: a gold pectoral, a great disc fibula with embossed lions and rows of tiny ducks, Phoenician-style silver bowls, a bronze bed, and silver vessels inscribed mi larthia, "I am Larthia's".

Trade ran both ways. Etruscan wine and black bucchero cups reached Gaul, Carthage and Greek sanctuaries. Aristotle knew of Etruscan-Carthaginian treaties on imports and mutual protection (Politics 1280a), and around 540 BC Caere and Carthage together fought the Phocaeans off Alalia (Herodotus 1.166–167, about a century later). At Gravisca, Tarquinia's port, an anchor-stock was dedicated to Apollo by a Sostratos, perhaps the Aeginetan whom Herodotus calls the most profitable trader of all (4.152).

Attic pots came in enormous numbers. Most of the finest Attic black- and red-figure vases in museums came from Etruscan graves, Vulci above all, where Lucien Bonaparte's diggers found thousands from 1828. Athenian workshops made for this market: the "Tyrrhenian" amphorae of the mid-sixth century, and Nikosthenes' amphorae copying a bucchero shape.

Where sources disagree. Michael Vickers and David Gill (Artful Crafts, 1994) argued that painted pottery was cheap, a stand-in for silver carried as makeweight cargo; John Boardman replied that the scale of the trade and its tailoring to Etruscan taste show a valued commodity. Whether Etruscan buyers chose scenes for their stories is also debated (Nigel Spivey; Christoph Reusser).

The language: read aloud, barely understood

The Etruscans took their alphabet around 700 BC from the western Greek script of the Euboeans at Pithekoussai and Cumae; a seventh-century ivory tablet from Marsiliana d'Albegna carries a model alphabet of twenty-six letters. Etruscan had no b, d, g or o, so gamma stood for /k/. Rome took its letters through Etruria, which is why Latin C sounds /k/ and Gaius is abbreviated C.

Because the letters are Greek, every text can be pronounced. About 13,000 inscriptions survive, mostly short epitaphs and gift labels: mi "I", clan "son", avil "year", lupu "died", turce "gave". Four longer texts carry the weight:

  • The Pyrgi tablets, gold sheets found in 1964 at Caere's port, about 500 BC: two in Etruscan, one in Phoenician, recording a temple given by Thefarie Velianas, ruler of Caere, to Uni, whom the Phoenician calls Astarte. A paraphrase rather than a word-for-word bilingual, they confirm ci "three" and avil "year".
  • The Liber Linteus, the only surviving Etruscan book: a ritual calendar written about 250 BC, to judge by its letters, on linen radiocarbon-dated to about 360–210 BC, cut into strips to wrap a mummy in Ptolemaic Egypt, bought in Alexandria in 1848 and now in Zagreb. About 1,200 words give days, gods and offerings.
  • The Tabula Capuana, a terracotta slab from Capua of about 470 BC, now in Berlin: some 390 legible words of another ritual calendar.
  • The Cippus Perusinus, a boundary stone found at Perugia in 1822, of the third or second century BC: about 130 words on an agreement between two families over land and a tomb.

The difficulty is structural. With no relatives beyond Lemnian and Raetic, the etymological method, reading Etruscan through a known language, has failed every time. What works is the combinatory method, comparing how words behave across texts, and the bilingual method; but the bilinguals are short Latin-Etruscan epitaphs and the long texts are technical ritual and legal documents. The literature, tragedies and histories that Varro still knew, is wholly lost. The grammar is known in outline, the secure vocabulary in hundreds of words; even whether huth is four and śa six, or the reverse, is disputed.

Where sources disagree. A minority still seeks a kin language: Frederik Woudhuizen reads Etruscan as a colonial form of Luwian, and Beekes tied it to north-west Anatolia. Rix, Larissa Bonfante and most Etruscologists treat it as non-Indo-European and test every proposed decipherment against the combinatory evidence; none so far has passed.

A revealed religion: the disciplina Etrusca

Livy calls the Etruscans a nation "devoted beyond all others to religious rites" (5.1.6). What set them apart was revelation. At Tarquinia a ploughman drove his share deep and Tages rose from the furrow, a child in form with an old man's wisdom, and chanted the discipline to the assembled Etruscans, who wrote it down (Cicero, On Divination 2.50, scornfully). The contrast with the scriptureless religion of Greek module 8.1 is total: here were sacred books and experts to read them.

Cicero names libri haruspicini on entrails, fulgurales on lightning and rituales on rites (On Divination 1.72). The ritual books prescribed how to found cities, consecrate walls and gates and order armies (Festus, s.v. rituales); others treated fate, which allotted the Etruscan name ten saecula (Censorinus 17.5–6, citing Varro).

Haruspicy. The haruspex read a sacrificed sheep's liver as a map of the sky. The bronze liver found near Piacenza in 1877, of the late second century BC, divides its rim into sixteen regions and its surface into further fields, each with a god's name: Tin, Uni, Nethuns, Fufluns, Cel. They answer to the sixteen regions of the Etruscan sky, lucky in the east, unlucky in the west (Pliny, Natural History 2.143). The Senate had sons of leading families sent to the Etruscan peoples to learn the discipline (Cicero, On Divination 1.92).

Where sources disagree. Clay liver models from Mari, Babylon and Hattusa make hepatoscopy a Near Eastern science, and the Piacenza liver's likeness to them was long cited for Herodotus' migration. Walter Burkert (Die orientalisierende Epoche, 1984) answered that itinerant seers carried the craft west in the eighth and seventh centuries, to Greeks and Etruscans alike. The liver proves contact, not migration.

Lightning. Nine gods threw bolts of eleven kinds, Tinia three (Pliny, Natural History 2.138): a warning bolt he sends alone, a second on the advice of twelve gods, a third, destructive, only after consulting the hidden higher gods (Seneca, Natural Questions 2.41). Seneca states the difference from Greek science in a sentence: the Etruscans think "clouds collide in order to release lightning" (2.32.2).

The gods are known from the liver, dedications and engraved mirrors that label Greek myths. Their names show three layers.

Tombs, banquets and women

Etruscan cities lie under medieval towns; their cemeteries survive. Caere's Banditaccia tumuli are carved inside as houses. Tarquinia's Monterozzi necropolis holds some two hundred painted chambers, most from about 530–450 BC: the Augurs, Hunting and Fishing, the Leopards and the Triclinium, with couples at banquet and dancers among trees. From the fourth century the dead feast in the underworld instead, escorted by the demons Charun and Vanth.

Women recline beside men. The Sarcophagus of the Spouses from Caere, about 520 BC, sets husband and wife on one couch. Women bore their own first names (Larthia, Ramtha, Thanchvil), and epitaphs often name the mother. In Greece only hetairai reclined at the symposium. Aristotle's Customs of the Tyrrhenians said Etruscans dined with their wives "under the same cloak" (Athenaeus 1.23d). Theopompus, in the fourth century BC, went further: their women are held in common, exercise naked with men, dine with any man present, toast whom they please, drink hard, and raise children whose fathers no one knows (FGrHist 115 F 204, in Athenaeus 12.517d–518b).

Where sources disagree. Larissa Bonfante read the paintings and names as evidence of real aristocratic standing for women, which Greeks could interpret only through the courtesan; Theopompus found decadence everywhere, and his Etruscans belong with Posidonius' luxurious ones (Diodorus 5.40). Whether the banquets show the funeral feast, an afterlife or an ideal of family life is unsettled.

What Rome took from Etruria

Livy (1.8.3) derives the curule chair, the purple-bordered toga and the twelve lictors from Etruria, twelve because each people gave a lictor to a jointly chosen king; Dionysius (3.61) has the Etruscans send Tarquinius Priscus a crown, ivory throne, sceptre and purple robes. Both wrote six or seven centuries after the events.

For gladiators, the masked Phersu setting a dog on a hooded man in the Tomb of the Augurs is cited; but Livy has Campanians arming gladiators (9.40.17), Rome's first show came in 264 BC (Livy, Periochae 16), and Georges Ville (1981) argued for Campania.

The long decline

In 474 BC Hiero I of Syracuse, answering Cumae's appeal, destroyed an Etruscan fleet off Cumae (Diodorus 11.51, four centuries later). Pindar, within a few years, prays that the Tyrrhenian war-cry stay at home (Pythian 1.71–75); a helmet Hiero dedicated at Olympia as "Tyrrhenian spoils from Cumae" is in the British Museum. Samnites took Capua about 423 (Livy 4.37), Gauls overran the Po valley, and Dionysius I of Syracuse sacked Pyrgi in 384 (Diodorus 15.14).

Veii fell to Rome in 396 BC after a siege Livy, four centuries on, makes ten years long, ending with Camillus' tunnel and the evocatio of Veii's Juno to the Aventine (5.21–22). There followed war with Tarquinia (358–351), defeat at Sentinum (295), the destruction of Volsinii (264) and Latin colonies such as Cosa (273). Citizenship came in 90 BC; Sulla's colonists and his siege of Volaterrae broke the old families. Etruscan inscriptions end in the early Empire.

What remained was learning. A haruspex declared the comet of 44 BC the end of the ninth saeculum (Servius on Eclogues 9.46). Claudius, whose first wife came from an Etruscan family, wrote a Greek history of the Etruscans in twenty books, the Tyrrhenika, and had it read aloud yearly at Alexandria (Suetonius, Claudius 42.2); it is lost, glimpsed perhaps in his speech on the Gauls (module 1.5). In AD 47 he founded a college of haruspices lest "the oldest discipline of Italy" lapse (Tacitus, Annals 11.15), and haruspices still offered to save Rome from Alaric in AD 408 (Zosimus 5.41).

Where sources disagree. Cumae is called the beginning of the end, but decline was regional: the southern coastal cities lost imports in the fifth century while Clusium, Volsinii and the Po towns flourished. Livy's own speaker likens the siege of Veii to Troy's (5.4.11), and Cornell (The Beginnings of Rome, 1995) treats the ten years as literary; John Ward-Perkins' South Etruria survey showed Veii's countryside still farmed after 396.

Self-test

  1. Why does Dionysius reject the Lydian origin, and why should his own view also be read with care?
    Answer

    Lydian language and customs were unlike Etruscan, and Xanthus of Lydia never mentions Tyrrhenus (Roman Antiquities 1.28–30). But Dionysius was arguing that the Romans were Greeks, which made native Etruscan neighbours convenient.

  2. How did Pallottino reframe the origins question, and what archaeological pattern supports him?
    Answer

    He asked how the Etruscan nation formed rather than where it came from. Villanovan settlements of the ninth century BC occupy the same plateaux as the later cities, with continuous development and no sign of intrusion.

  3. Why does the Lemnos stele not prove an eastern homeland?
    Answer

    Lemnian is so close to sixth-century Etruscan that it may be the speech of Tyrrhenian settlers from Italy in the Aegean (de Simone), and the third member of the family, Raetic, is Alpine. Beekes' Anatolian reading remains a minority view.

  4. What did Posth and colleagues (2021) find, and why is it a paradox?
    Answer

    Iron Age Etruscans were genetically like the Latins, with steppe-related ancestry and no recent Anatolian input. A population with ancestry usually linked to Indo-European speakers nonetheless kept a non-Indo-European language.

  5. What was the league at the Fanum Voltumnae, and what does the Veii episode of Livy 5.1 show about it?
    Answer

    An annual religious gathering of the twelve peoples with festival, games and a priestly election, which also debated war. When Veii's king disrupted the festival the league left Veii to fight Rome alone: it could coordinate but not command.

  6. What does the Regolini-Galassi tomb reveal about orientalising Etruria?
    Answer

    Metal wealth let a new elite acquire Levantine, Egyptian and Greek luxuries and the craft styles that came with them; the principal burial was a woman, Larthia, named on silver vessels, evidence both of early writing and of female status in the princely class.

  7. Why can Etruscan be read aloud but not understood?
    Answer

    Its alphabet is Greek, so the sounds are known. But the language has no relatives beyond Lemnian and Raetic, the bilinguals are short (Pyrgi is a paraphrase), the long texts are technical ritual and legal documents, and the literature is lost.

  8. What does Seneca's remark on lightning (Natural Questions 2.32.2) reveal about the disciplina Etrusca?
    Answer

    That Etruscans treated natural events as signs sent on purpose: clouds collide in order to release lightning. The discipline asks what the gods intend, not what causes an event, and its books codify how to read and answer the signs.

  9. Which Roman borrowings from Etruria are well founded, and why is gladiatorial combat doubtful?
    Answer

    The fasces (Tomb of the Lictor, Vetulonia), the curule chair and praetexta, the temple form Vitruvius 4.7 calls Tuscan, and haruspicy. Gladiators rest on Nicolaus of Damascus, but the earliest funeral duels are Lucanian and Campanian, and Rome's first show (264 BC) followed its conquest of Campania.

  10. Was the defeat off Cumae in 474 BC the end of Etruscan power?
    Answer

    It ended southern Etruscan sea power, but inland and northern cities flourished in the fifth century. The real losses were Campania, the Po valley and Veii in 396, followed by slow Romanisation completed by the citizenship of 90 BC.

Carry forward

  1. The Etruscans formed in Italy: the Lydian and Pelasgian stories are Greek genealogy, and archaeology and ancient DNA point to local formation. Apply the same scepticism to Rome's own imported ancestry, the Trojan claim of module 1.3.
  2. Almost everything Etruscan reaches us through outsiders: a language we can pronounce but barely translate, a lost literature, Greek moralists and Roman antiquarians. Judge every claim of Etruscan origin for a Roman institution on its own evidence, separating attested borrowings from ancient guesses.
  3. Etruscan religion was a revealed discipline of signs read by experts; Rome imported the experts rather than the theology, and haruspices served the Roman state until AD 408. Expect them wherever Livy reports prodigies.
