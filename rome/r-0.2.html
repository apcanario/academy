The shape of Italy: a mental map

Greek module 0.2 laid out the Aegean triangle, Magna Graecia and Sicily from the Greek side; none of that is repeated here. Hold Rome's world as a peninsula pushed into the middle of one sea, a mountain seam down its length, and the city halfway down the western side, on the shin of the boot.

Read it in three vertical bands. West: a chain of volcanic plains facing the Tyrrhenian (Etruria, Latium, Campania), with Rome at the joint of the first two. Middle: the Apennine spine, its upland peoples in long parallel valleys. East: a narrow Adriatic strip, poor in harbours, that widens only into Apulia. Cap the bands with the Po plain under the Alps, Rome's "Gaul this side of the Alps". Hang Corsica and Sardinia off Etruria and Latium, and Sicily under the toe like a ball kicked towards Africa, with Cape Bon in Africa 140 kilometres from its western cape. Then widen the view. The Mediterranean is two basins joined at the Sicilian Channel, with Italy across the waist; round the rim, clockwise from Spain, run Gaul, Italy, Illyricum, Greece, Asia Minor, Syria, Egypt and Africa; beyond the rim the empire stopped at Ocean, the Rhine and Danube, the Euphrates and the Sahara.

The site of Rome: ford, salt and hills

Rome stands where the lower Tiber is crossed. About 25 kilometres from the sea the river divides round the Tiber Island, the first point upstream where it could be forded and then bridged, so the land route between Etruria and Latium passed here. Beside it lay the Forum Boarium, cattle market and landing place, where Euboean pottery arrives in the eighth century and the Ara Maxima served Hercules, a god of traders. Livy gives the first bridge, the wooden Pons Sublicius, to Ancus Marcius (1.33.6). Cicero (On the Republic 2.5–11) praised the site as wisely chosen; Strabo (5.3.2) thought it taken "more by necessity than choice".

The crossing was also a salt road. Salt beds lay at the river mouth, and the Via Salaria ran north-east into the Sabine hills, named, Pliny says, for the salt carried to the Sabines along it (Natural History 31.89). Salt made Rome a middleman between coast and Apennines and set it against Veii, which held the right bank; Plutarch has Romulus take the salt-works from the Veientines (Romulus 25).

The canonical seven hills are the Palatine, Capitoline, Aventine, Caelian, Esquiline, Viminal and Quirinal (Varro, On the Latin Language 5.41–54). Stand on the Tiber Island facing east: the Capitol is on your left, the Palatine ahead, the Aventine on your right, the Caelian behind the Palatine; the Esquiline, Viminal and Quirinal are tongues of a plateau running in from the north-east. Seven is an ideal, not a survey. Virgil's Rome "ringed seven citadels with a single wall" (Georgics 2.535), and Revelation 17:9 turned the image against the city.

Where sources disagree. Varro derived the December festival of the Septimontium from "these seven hills on which the city stands" (On the Latin Language 6.24). Festus, quoting Antistius Labeo, lists eight places, mostly spurs and districts (Palatium, Velia, Fagutal, Subura, Cermalus, Oppius, Caelius, Cispius), without the Capitol, Quirinal or Aventine. Most scholars take the festival to belong to an early league of Palatine and Esquiline communities, and the canonical seven to be a later rationalisation.

Between Capitol, Palatine and Velia lay a low valley draining through the Velabrum to the river. Ovid remembered it as swamp (Fasti 6.401), and the Lacus Curtius at its centre kept the memory. It was a cemetery from the tenth century; in the later seventh it was raised with fill and given its first floor, the moment Rome acquires a public centre. Livy, writing more than five centuries later, credits Tarquinius Priscus with draining it (1.38.6) and Tarquinius Superbus with the Cloaca Maxima (1.56.2).

Where sources disagree. Albert Ammerman (American Journal of Archaeology, 1990) argued from cores that the valley was seasonally flooded ground rather than a permanent marsh, and that the decisive work was a deliberate landfill. John Hopkins (The Genesis of Roman Architecture, 2016) accepts an archaic open channel but notes that the vaulted Cloaca visible today is largely Republican. Livy may be right about the first canal and wrong about the sewer.

North-west of the Capitol, in a loop of the river, lay the flood-plain of the Campus Martius. It was outside the pomerium, the sacred boundary within which military command could not be exercised (Tacitus traces Romulus' line in Annals 12.24). So the comitia centuriata, the people voting as an army, met there, and generals awaited their triumphs there. Livy makes it the Tarquins' land, consecrated to Mars at their fall (2.5.2), and the place where Romulus vanished, at the Goat's Marsh (1.16.1). Augustan monuments later filled it (Strabo 5.3.8).

Latium and the Alban Hills

Old Latium, Latium vetus, is the coastal plain south of the Tiber, closed by the Sabine and Aequian hills to the north-east, the Volscian Lepini to the south-east, and the promontory of Circeii, where Greeks placed Circe. Its centre is the Alban Hills, a dormant volcano some 20 kilometres south-east of Rome, with the crater lakes of Albano and Nemi on its south-western side. Tuff gave building stone and fertile soil. The summit, the Alban Mount, held the Latins' common shrine of Jupiter Latiaris, and Pliny lists the vanished populi Albenses who once shared its sacrificial meat (Natural History 3.68–70). The Latin towns ring the hills: Tibur, Praeneste, Tusculum, Aricia, Lanuvium, Lavinium, Ardea, Gabii; their cults and league belong to module 1.1. Two works show Romans handling this landscape: the tunnel that drained Lake Albano, dug, Livy says, because an oracle tied the fall of Veii to it (5.15–16, 5.19), and the Pontine Marshes, which defeated drainage from Caesar's plan (Suetonius, Julius 44) until the 1930s.

The regions of Italy and the Apennine spine

The Apennines run about 1,200 kilometres from Liguria, swing east almost to the Adriatic at Ariminum, then run south-east down the middle and into the toe. They lie nearer the Adriatic, so the west has the broad plains and long rivers (Arno, Tiber, Liris, Volturnus). Polybius describes the peninsula in 2.14–17, Strabo in Books 5 and 6, Pliny in Natural History 3.38–138.

The pattern has three tiers. Plains peoples were urban, rich and reachable. Apennine peoples lived in villages and hill-forts, moved flocks between summer mountain and winter plain, and fought Rome longest; Livy's Samnite Wars (Books 7–10), written three centuries later, are a war of plain against mountain. The Po plain was Celtic, conquered late and colonised hard.

Why Italy, unlike Greece, could be unified by land

Greek module 0.2 argued that Greek mountains and sea favoured many small states without forcing them. Italy differed in three ways. Its plains are continuous along the western side, so a power in Latium could reach Etruria and Campania without crossing water or a range, and Rome sat at the middle of that chain. The Apennine valleys run lengthwise, and the mountain peoples needed lowland winter pasture and markets, so upland and plain were bound together by drove roads and by war. And there is no inner sea: an army could march from Rhegium to the Po. Mommsen's version is that Greece faces east and Italy west. Manpower followed: Polybius counts more than 700,000 foot and 70,000 horse available to Rome in 225 (2.24), a reservoir no Greek state approached.

Where sources disagree. Ancient writers made the geography providential: Strabo (6.4.1) lists the climate, harbours and central position that fitted Italy to rule, and Pliny calls it a land "chosen by the will of the gods" (Natural History 3.39). Modern historians are warier. W. V. Harris (War and Imperialism in Republican Rome, 1979) located the drive to expand in Roman society, T. J. Cornell (The Beginnings of Rome, 1995) in the alliance system that turned defeated enemies into soldiers. Geography made unification possible; institutions made it happen.

The islands and the sea lanes

Rome came late to the sea. The Tyrrhenian was an Etruscan, Greek and Carthaginian water: off Alalia in Corsica, about 540, Etruscans and Carthaginians together fought the Phocaeans (Herodotus 1.166–167), and the first Roman treaty with Carthage (module 1.6) divides trading zones. Sicily lies three kilometres from Italy at the Straits of Messina and about 140 from Cape Bon; it is both bridge and barrier between the two basins, which is why Rome and Carthage collided there over Messana in 264 (Polybius 1.10–11). It became the first province in 241, and Cato called it the state's storehouse (Cicero, Verrines 2.2.5). Sardinia and Corsica, seized from Carthage in 238 while it fought its mercenaries, closed the Tyrrhenian triangle. The eastern lane ran from Brundisium across the Strait of Otranto, about 70 kilometres at its narrowest, to Apollonia and Dyrrhachium; the First Illyrian War (229) was fought to secure it.

The roads as the skeleton of conquest

A Roman road was first a military work: it ran from Rome to a colony, fixed the line of march, and moved an army in any season. Dionysius counts roads, with aqueducts and sewers, as Rome's three most magnificent works (3.67.5).

  • Via Appia, 312: the censor Appius Claudius Caecus, from Rome across the Pontine Marshes to Capua (Livy 9.29.6), in the middle of the Second Samnite War; extended by stages to Beneventum, Tarentum and Brundisium. Diodorus (20.36) says he exhausted the treasury on it.
  • Via Flaminia, 220: Gaius Flaminius as censor, from Rome through Umbria over the Apennines to Ariminum, the gate to the Po plain.
  • Via Aemilia, 187: the consul Marcus Aemilius Lepidus, from Ariminum along the edge of the Po plain to Placentia (Livy 39.2.10); Emilia keeps his name.
  • Via Egnatia, c. 146–120: named from the proconsul Gnaeus Egnatius on a milestone found near Thessalonica, it crossed the Balkans from Dyrrhachium and Apollonia to Thessalonica, later Byzantium (Strabo 7.7.4, from Polybius). It turned the Adriatic crossing into a land route east, the road to Philippi.

The sequence tracks the conquest: Campania and Samnium, then the Adriatic, then the Po, then Macedonia. Colonies came first or with the road (Cales 334, Venusia 291, Ariminum 268, Placentia and Cremona 218), and the road tied them to Rome.

Mare nostrum, the provinces and the frontiers

"Our sea" is a Republican phrase (Caesar, Gallic War 5.1; Sallust, Jugurtha 17). It became literally true when Pompey cleared the pirates in three months of 67 under the Gabinian law (Plutarch, Pompey 24–28). The provinces came in rough rings round the sea:

  • The western islands and Spain: Sicily 241, Sardinia and Corsica 238, the two Spains 197.
  • The settlement of 146: Macedonia and Africa, the territory of destroyed Carthage.
  • The east by bequest and annexation: Asia 133, Cilicia c. 102, Bithynia and Cyrene 74, Crete 67, Syria 64.
  • The north-west: Transalpine Gaul c. 121, Caesar's Gaul 58–50.
  • Octavian, Augustus and after: Egypt 30, Galatia 25, the Alpine and Danubian provinces, Britain AD 43, Dacia and Arabia 106.

Beyond the coasts the empire ended at rivers. Caesar reached the Rhine in 58–55; Augustus pushed to the Elbe (Res Gestae 26) and gave it up after Varus lost three legions in AD 9. The Danube was reached in the Augustan wars in Illyricum and crossed for good only by Trajan into Dacia. The Euphrates faced Parthia: Crassus died beyond it at Carrhae in 53, Augustus recovered the standards by diplomacy in 20, and Hadrian gave up Trajan's Mesopotamia in 117. Tacitus describes the empire of AD 14 as "fenced by Ocean or distant rivers" (Annals 1.9) and records Augustus' advice to keep it within its limits (1.11).

Where sources disagree. Edward Luttwak (The Grand Strategy of the Roman Empire, 1976) read the rivers and walls as a planned defensive system. Benjamin Isaac (The Limits of Empire, 1990), C. R. Whittaker (Frontiers of the Roman Empire, 1994) and Susan Mattern (Rome and the Enemy, 1999) answered that Romans had neither the maps nor the doctrine for such a plan, that rivers were highways and zones of exchange rather than lines, and that halts followed defeats and costs.

Myths pinned to places

Roman myth is topographical: the stories were told at the spots they explained, and the spots were kept to prove the stories. Most of it reaches us through Livy, Dionysius and Virgil, five to seven centuries after the events.

Two patterns recur. The city's myths cluster at the crossing and its hills, each an aetiology for something a Roman could walk past: cave, hut, fig, rock, spring. The myths of Latium cluster on the Alban Hills and the coast and tell of a Latin headship passing from Lavinium to Alba to Rome; Cumae is the Greek door through which prophecy and the alphabet came in.

Where sources disagree. Even the hills were not fixed to the twins: Ennius has Romulus, not Remus, take the auspices on the Aventine (module 1.4). The places are uncertain too: Alba Longa has been sought at Castel Gandolfo and Palazzolo without a city being found, Lake Regillus has never been securely identified, and the "Sibyl's cave" that Maiuri matched to Aeneid 6.42–44 is read by many archaeologists as a defensive gallery of the fourth or third century BC.

Self-test

  1. Why did Rome grow at the Tiber Island rather than at the river mouth?
    Answer

    The island split the river at the first point upstream where it could be forded and bridged, so the land route between Etruria and Latium crossed there and the Forum Boarium beside it became market and landing. Cicero (On the Republic 2.5–11) praises the inland site as safe from pirates; Strabo (5.3.2) thought it chosen by necessity.

  2. What does the name of the Via Salaria reveal about early Rome's economy and its first great rival?
    Answer

    It was the road by which salt from the Tiber mouth went up to the Sabines (Pliny, Natural History 31.89), making Rome a middleman between coast and Apennines. Control of the salt beds set it against Veii on the right bank; Plutarch (Romulus 25) has Romulus seize them.

  3. Why is the canonical list of seven hills unlikely to be the original sense of the Septimontium?
    Answer

    Festus, quoting Antistius Labeo, gives eight places for the festival, mostly spurs of the Palatine and Esquiline, and omits the Capitol, Quirinal and Aventine. The seven-hilled city is a later ideal, fixed in Varro (On the Latin Language 6.24) and the Augustan poets.

  4. How does archaeology modify Livy's story of the draining of the Forum?
    Answer

    The valley was a cemetery, then in the later seventh century was raised by deliberate fill and floored; Ammerman (1990) argued it was seasonally flooded ground rather than a marsh. An archaic channel may go back to the kings, but the vaulted Cloaca Maxima seen today is largely Republican.

  5. Why did the comitia centuriata meet in the Campus Martius?
    Answer

    It was the people voting as an army, and military command could not be exercised inside the pomerium; the Campus lay outside it. Generals awaiting a triumph waited there for the same reason.

  6. Give three geographical reasons why Italy, unlike Greece, could be unified by land.
    Answer

    The western plains form a continuous chain with Rome at its middle; the Apennine valleys run lengthwise and their peoples depended on lowland pasture and markets; and no inner sea divides the peninsula. Greek module 0.2 gives the contrasting Aegean pattern.

  7. Match each road to its builder, date and endpoints, and say what links them.
    Answer

    Appia: Appius Claudius Caecus, 312, Rome to Capua, later Brundisium. Flaminia: Gaius Flaminius, 220, Rome to Ariminum. Aemilia: Marcus Aemilius Lepidus, 187, Ariminum to Placentia. Egnatia: Gnaeus Egnatius, c. 146–120, Dyrrhachium to Thessalonica. Each follows the front of conquest and ties colonies to Rome.

  8. Why was Sicily Rome's first province, and what did it supply?
    Answer

    Three kilometres from Italy and about 140 from Africa, it is the hinge between the two Mediterranean basins, where Roman and Carthaginian interests met at Messana in 264; Rome took it in 241. It supplied grain: Cato called it the state's storehouse (Cicero, Verrines 2.2.5).

  9. What does Tacitus say about the empire's limits in AD 14, and how do Luttwak and his critics read the river frontiers?
    Answer

    He describes an empire fenced by Ocean or distant rivers (Annals 1.9) and Augustus' advice to keep it within its limits (1.11). Luttwak (1976) saw a planned grand strategy; Isaac, Whittaker and Mattern see rivers as zones of contact and halts as the result of defeats and costs.

  10. Which myth is pinned to Lake Regillus, and how did the Forum preserve it?
    Answer

The twins were seen watering their horses at Juturna's spring in the Forum (Dionysius 6.13), beside which stood the temple of Castor, dedicated in 484.

Carry forward

  1. Rome is a river crossing and a market before it is anything else: the Tiber Island ford, the salt road and the Forum Boarium explain why a city grew on low, flood-prone hills between Etruria and Latium, and why its oldest myths cluster round that crossing.
  2. Italy's geography made unification by land possible (continuous western plains, a lengthwise Apennine spine, no inner sea), and roads and colonies made it actual; the same method, exported round the Mediterranean and halted at the Rhine, the Danube and the Euphrates, is the shape of the empire.
  3. Roman myths were pinned to visible places by writers centuries after the events; always ask who tied the story to the spot and when, because the topography (the Lupercal, the hut of Romulus, Juturna's spring) was curated to prove the story.
