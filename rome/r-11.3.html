01The urban plebs: how many, and who

No ancient writer counts the people of Rome; module 9.4 sets out the counts that survive and the modern estimates, from about 450,000 to about a million, built on them. For politics the number that matters is the citizen men on the grain list. When Caesar reviewed it, Suetonius says, it held 320,000 names, and he cut it to 150,000 (Julius 41.3; module 5.4). These were adult male citizens living in the city. With their wives and children, and with the slaves and foreigners who had no vote, they were the bulk of the city's population.

The plebs was not a class of the idle. Its men were shopkeepers, craftsmen, porters, builders and dockers, living in rented rooms (module 9.4) and exposed to the price of grain, the rent day, debt and fire. Many were freedmen or the sons of freedmen. When a contio heckled him after Tiberius Gracchus' death, Scipio Aemilianus asked how men to whom Italy was a stepmother could frighten him (Velleius 2.4.4); Valerius Maximus adds that he had brought them to Rome in chains himself (6.2.3). Debt and rent were as explosive as grain: they drove the agitation of the praetor Caelius in 48, which ended with his death at Thurii (Caesar, Civil War 3.20–22), and of the tribune Dolabella in 47 (Dio 42.29–32).

Freedmen were registered in the four urban tribes, so in a tribal vote the city's freedmen counted for four units of thirty-five. Lily Ross Taylor (The Voting Districts of the Roman Republic, 1960) argued that many freeborn residents of the city kept their families' rural tribes, which, on her reading, gave the city crowd more weight in legislation voted in the Forum than its four urban tribes suggest.

Where sources disagree. Plutarch read the fall from 320,000 to 150,000 as a census showing what the civil wars had cost (Caesar 55), and Livy's epitomator also calls it a census of 150,000 citizens (Periochae 115). Suetonius says it was a review of those receiving public grain, carried out street by street through the owners of the apartment blocks, with vacancies later filled by lot (Julius 41.3). Suetonius' version fits the numbers: no three years of war killed more than half the citizen men of Rome. The two figures measure the grain list, not the population.

02The contio as stage

Module 11.1 describes the contio: a meeting summoned by a magistrate, without a vote, at which only those he called on spoke. In the late Republic it became the main point of contact between politicians and the urban plebs, and the tribunes, who could summon one on almost any day, were its most frequent conveners. Cicero names three places where the Roman people's judgement on public affairs could best be seen: the contio, the assemblies, and the crowds at games and gladiatorial shows (Pro Sestio 106). In the same passage he argues that contiones packed with hired men no longer showed it (104–106).

The convener owned the stage. He could bring a magistrate or a private man before the crowd and make him answer. Early in 58 Clodius produced the consul Piso and asked his opinion of the execution of the Catilinarians; Piso said he had always disapproved of cruelty (Cicero, Against Piso 14). Every speaker addressed the plebs as sovereign, and even a consul defending the Senate's interests claimed the people's side: Cicero began his consulship by telling a contio that he would be a popular consul, and turned the word against the tribune Rullus' land bill (De lege agraria 2.6–10).

The crowd answered back. In February 56, at the trial of Milo before the people, Clodius' gangs shouted Pompey down for the whole of his speech. Then Clodius, Cicero told his brother, put questions to his followers and they roared the replies: who is starving the people? Pompey. Who wants to go to Alexandria? Pompey. Whom do you want to go? Crassus (Letters to Quintus 2.3.2). A practised speaker could make a crowd speak with one voice, and a rival could be shamed before it. Cicero praised the Roman people in public; in private he wrote of the wretched, starving rabble of the meetings, the leech of the treasury (Letters to Atticus 1.16.11).

03The tribunate as an instrument

Polybius, describing Rome about 150, says that the tribunes are bound to do what the people resolve and to pay attention to their wishes (6.16.5). In practice they came from the Senate's own families and usually acted with it. From 133 the tribunate became the instrument of anyone who wanted to legislate without the Senate. It gave four tools: the right to propose laws to the plebeian council; the veto, which could stop any magistrate's act and any decree; the right to summon a contio at will; and the sacrosanctity of the tribune's person, which made violence against him sacrilege and made him the natural centre of a crowd. Sulla took away the first and limited the second (module 5.2). Their restoration in 70 (module 5.3) reopened the route round the Senate.

Christian Meier ("Populares", 1965) argued that the populares were not a party but men who chose a method, the popularis ratio, of carrying measures through the people against the Senate's will; the same man could use it one year and denounce it the next. Cicero puts both sides in his own dialogue. His brother Quintus calls the tribunate a thing born in sedition for sedition; Marcus answers that its power is excessive, but that the people's force without a leader is fiercer, and that a tribune can restrain it (On the Laws 3.19–26).

The street came with the method, and force was used on both sides. In 100 Saturninus carried his land law for Marius' veterans by bringing countrymen into the city; the urban plebs, who thought the law favoured the Italians, fought them in the assembly, and the countrymen won with clubs (Appian, Civil Wars 1.29–30). His grain law, named for its price, a semis and a triens, five-sixths of an as a modius, met force from the other side. The urban quaestor Quintus Caepio told the Senate that the treasury could not bear it; the Senate resolved that whoever carried it acted against the state; and when Saturninus pressed on, Caepio and the "good men" with him broke down the voting gangways and overturned the ballot boxes (Rhetorica ad Herennium 1.21). In 88 the tribune Sulpicius went about with some three thousand swordsmen and six hundred young equestrians he called his anti-senate (Plutarch, Sulla 8, Marius 35; module 5.2 has the outcome).

04Grain from Gaius Gracchus to Clodius

The dole as an institution is module 9.4's; here it is politics. Gaius Gracchus' law of 123 let every citizen buy a monthly ration from public granaries at 6⅓ asses a modius (Periochae 60; module 5.1). Cicero states both sides as an optimate saw them: the plebs liked it, since it gave them food in plenty without labour, and the boni opposed it because they thought it called the plebs from work to idleness and drained the treasury (Pro Sestio 103). Elsewhere he praises a later, undated law of Marcus Octavius, which cut back Gracchus' largesse, as bearable for the state and necessary for the plebs (On Duties 2.72).

Each grain law after that was a move in a contest. Saturninus' law belongs to his first tribunate of 103 or his second of 100; if it passed, it did not last, for the price Clodius abolished in 58 was still the Gracchan 6⅓ asses (Asconius, on Cicero's Against Piso). Sulla ended the distributions (module 5.2). The consul Lepidus carried, unopposed, a law giving five modii in 78 (Granius Licinianus, book 36). The consuls of 73 carried the Terentian and Cassian law (Cicero, Verrines 2.3.163), and that year the tribune Macer, in Sallust's version of his speech, sneered that the people valued their liberty at five modii, no more than a prisoner's ration (Histories 3.48.19). Late in 63 or early in 62 Cato, as tribune, persuaded the Senate to extend the distributions to the poor whom Caesar was stirring, at a cost Plutarch gives as 1,250 talents a year (Cato Minor 26) and, in another Life, as 7,500,000 drachmas, the same sum (Caesar 8).

Clodius made the grain free. His law, one of four he carried at the start of his tribunate in January 58 (Dio 38.13; Asconius, on Against Piso), abolished the price and, Cicero claimed, cost almost a fifth of the state's revenues (Pro Sestio 55); Cicero also says he put the whole supply into the hands of his henchman Sextus Cloelius (De domo sua 25). Free grain made the list worth joining. Dionysius, writing under Augustus, complains that masters freed slaves so that they could draw the monthly public grain (Roman Antiquities 4.24.5), and Dio says such manumissions troubled Pompey's distribution in 56 (39.24). The list became a constituency. Every man on it owed his ration to a law a tribune had carried, and this, more than the cost, made grain the core of popular politics.

Free grain did not guarantee grain. In September 57, with prices high, crowds gathered first at the theatre and then at the Senate and, urged on by Clodius, blamed the shortage on Cicero's return. Cicero proposed that Pompey be given charge of the supply, and a law gave him that charge for five years, with power over grain throughout the world (Letters to Atticus 4.1.6–7). Caesar's cut and the closed list of the principate are modules 5.4 and 9.4.

05The neighbourhood associations

Rome's streets were organised before any politician organised them. Each neighbourhood (vicus) had its crossroads shrine of the Lares, whose festival, the Compitalia, and its games were run by local magistri; module 2.4 describes the cult. Beside them stood collegia of trades and of worshippers, with officers, funds and meeting places, and with many freedmen and slaves among their members. Asconius explains that the masters of the colleges, like the masters of the neighbourhoods, used to give the Compitalia games in the purple-bordered toga (on Cicero's Against Piso).

In 64, the year Catiline stood for the consulship, the Senate dissolved the colleges that seemed to have been set up against the state, and the games lapsed with them (Asconius, on Against Piso). One of Clodius' laws of 58 restored them, and Cicero says Clodius added countless new ones recruited from the dregs of the city and from slaves (Against Piso 9; Dio 38.13). Cicero describes what followed as a levy: at the Aurelian tribunal in the Forum men, slaves among them, were enrolled street by street and divided into squads (Pro Sestio 34; De domo sua 54). The neighbourhood and the college gave Clodius what earlier popular leaders had lacked: a standing organisation through which a crowd could be called out on a given day.

The Senate struck at the organisation, not the cult. In February 56 it decreed that the political clubs and the squads (sodalitates decuriatique) should disband, and that a law should make those who did not liable under the law on violence (Letters to Quintus 2.3.5). In 55 the consul Crassus' law on associations (de sodaliciis) made it a crime to organise a tribe's voters for a candidate through such bodies; Cicero's Pro Plancio of 54 defends a man charged under it. Caesar dissolved all colleges except the ancient ones (Suetonius, Julius 42.3), and Augustus in 7 BC made the crossroads cult his own (module 2.4).

06Clodius and Milo, 58–53

Publius Clodius Pulcher, patrician, intruder at the Bona Dea in 62 and Cicero's enemy (module 5.3), became a plebeian by adoption in 59 with Caesar presiding as pontifex maximus (module 5.4) and was tribune for 58. His tribunate is shared with deep dive module 11.5, which follows Cicero; here it is the street. Besides the grain and the colleges, his first laws limited the censors' power to strike men from the Senate and curbed the use of the auspices to block assemblies, the weapon Bibulus had used in 59: Cicero says the Aelian and Fufian laws were overturned (Pro Sestio 33; Dio 38.13).

Then he turned on Cicero. His law that anyone who had put a Roman citizen to death without trial should be barred from fire and water (Velleius 2.45.1) named no one, but it put the final decree of 63 on trial, the test module 5.3 announced: Cicero had executed the Catilinarians on the Senate's authority, and the people now declared that authority no defence. Cicero left Rome, and his house on the Palatine was pulled down. Clodius' following then turned on Pompey, and after a slave of Clodius' was caught with a sword near him, Pompey kept to his house while Clodius remained in office (Plutarch, Pompey 49).

Against gangs there was no police, only other gangs. Rome had no force for public order beyond the magistrates' attendants, and Wilfried Nippel (Public Order in Ancient Rome, 1995) showed how far order rested on the authority of magistrates and the self-help of households and neighbourhoods. In 57 two tribunes, Titus Annius Milo and Publius Sestius, raised their own men, gladiators among them, to win back the Forum for Cicero's recall. Cicero defended the step: Milo had tried to prosecute Clodius for violence and been blocked by a consul, a praetor and tribunes, so force was the only remedy left (Pro Sestio 86–90). In late January 57 Clodius' men broke up the vote on the recall, and Cicero describes the Tiber filled with citizens' bodies, the sewers choked, and blood mopped from the Forum with sponges (Pro Sestio 75–77). Sestius was left for dead at the temple of Castor (79).

Violence became routine. In November 57 Clodius' men drove off the workmen rebuilding Cicero's house, set fire to his brother's, attacked Cicero himself on the Sacred Way and assaulted Milo's house (Letters to Atticus 4.3.2–3). Riots delayed the elections for 53 until the summer of that year, and none had been held for 52 when it began (Dio 40.45–46; Asconius, on Pro Milone). Both men spent heavily on shows and followers. Milo, a candidate for the consulship, owed seventy million sesterces, Pliny says (Natural History 36.104); Clodius was standing for the praetorship.

Where sources disagree. Dio has Caesar and Pompey back Clodius against Cicero in 58 (38.12–17), and Cicero at times blamed the alliance of 60 for his fall; yet within months Clodius was besieging Pompey. Erich Gruen ("P. Clodius: Instrument or Independent Agent?", Phoenix, 1966) argued for independence, and W. Jeffrey Tatum (The Patrician Tribune: Publius Clodius Pulcher, 1999) reads him as a noble pursuing his own standing by popular means, ruthless but no revolutionary. Whether his programme served the plebs or only himself cannot be settled, since nearly all the evidence is Cicero's.

07Bovillae, the Senate house and the Pro Milone

The fullest account of 52 is the commentary of Asconius Pedianus, written for his sons under Nero, about a century later, from the public records, the speeches and the histories. On 18 January Clodius, riding back towards Rome from Aricia with about thirty armed slaves, met Milo on the Appian Way near Bovillae. Milo was travelling to Lanuvium in a carriage with his wife Fausta and a much larger train that included gladiators. A brawl began among the slaves at the rear; the gladiator Birria wounded Clodius in the shoulder with a long blade; Clodius was carried into an inn at Bovillae; and Milo, judging that a wounded Clodius was more dangerous to him than a dead one, had him dragged out and killed.

A senator found the body on the road and sent it to Rome. That night it lay in the atrium of Clodius' house, where his wife Fulvia showed the crowd its wounds. Next morning the tribunes Munatius Plancus and Pompeius Rufus had it carried to the Rostra and harangued the crowd; then the scribe Sextus Cloelius led the crowd in carrying it into the Senate house and burning it on a pyre of benches, tables and the clerks' records. The Senate house burned down, and the Basilica Porcia beside it (Asconius; Dio 40.49). Module 5.4 gives the pyre in outline. Eight years later another crowd burned another body in the Forum, Caesar's (module 5.5).

The Senate passed the final decree, naming the interrex, the tribunes and Pompey, who held a proconsul's command outside the city, and authorised Pompey to levy troops across Italy (Asconius). After weeks of disorder Pompey was made sole consul (module 5.4) and carried laws on violence and bribery with a shortened procedure. Milo was tried in April under the law on violence, with Pompey's soldiers posted round the Forum. Cicero spoke for him and, Plutarch says, was so shaken by the soldiers that he could barely begin (Cicero 35). Milo was condemned by thirty-eight votes to thirteen, and the three orders of jurors (eighteen senators, seventeen equites, sixteen tribuni aerarii) divided alike: senators twelve to six, equites thirteen to four, tribuni aerarii thirteen to three (Asconius). Cato, Velleius says, voted openly to acquit (2.47.4). Milo went into exile at Massilia.

The speech we read is not the one Cicero gave. Asconius says the speech actually delivered was taken down and still survived, and Dio says that when Cicero sent Milo the finished version, Milo replied that it was lucky Cicero had not spoken so in court, or he would not be eating the mullets of Massilia (40.54.3–4). The published Pro Milone argues two lines. Clodius laid the ambush and was killed in lawful self-defence, under a law not written but born in us (Pro Milone 10), for silent enim leges inter arma, laws fall silent among arms (11). And, beyond the case, had Milo killed Clodius on purpose he would deserve the honours given to the killers of would-be tyrants (72–80).

Where sources disagree. Cicero's published speech makes Clodius the ambusher, waiting with picked men while Milo travelled encumbered by his wife and her maids (Pro Milone 28–29); the prosecution made Milo the ambusher. Asconius, who read the evidence, reports the meeting as chance and the killing as Milo's decision once Clodius was wounded. Cicero's narrative is an advocate's, finished after the verdict; most historians follow Asconius.

08The final decree as legalised violence

The Senate's decree that the consuls should see that the state suffered no harm (module 5.1) was passed at least six times between 121 and 49: against Gaius Gracchus in 121 (Cicero, Against Catiline 1.4), Saturninus in 100 (Pro Rabirio perduellionis reo 20), Lepidus in 77 (Sallust, Histories 1.77 M), Catiline in 63 (Sallust, Catiline 29.2), after Clodius' death in 52 (Asconius) and against Caesar in 49 (Caesar, Civil War 1.5). Sallust states its effect: by Roman custom it is the greatest power the Senate grants a magistrate, to raise an army, wage war, coerce allies and citizens by every means, and hold supreme command and jurisdiction at home and in the field, none of which a consul may otherwise do without the people's order (Catiline 29.3).

It conferred no legal power. Andrew Lintott (Violence in Republican Rome, 1968; second edition 1999) argued that it was the Senate's public backing for a magistrate's use of force, drawing on an old tradition that a would-be tyrant might be killed, and that the magistrate remained answerable for what he did. The record bears him out. Opimius was tried and acquitted (module 5.1); Rabirius was prosecuted in 63 for the killing of Saturninus thirty-seven years before (module 5.3); Cicero was driven out by Clodius' law. Each prosecution was the people's reply to the Senate's claim, and each verdict settled the case, never the principle.

The decree was legalised violence in a precise sense: the Senate could not make the killing of a citizen lawful, but it could make it respectable, and it chose the occasions. In 121 and 100 the force it licensed was turned on tribunes and former tribunes and their followers; in 77 and 49 on proconsuls with armies; in 52 it handed force to a proconsul with an army, and the order it restored was Pompey's. Lintott's argument cuts both ways. Clodius' crowds and Milo's gangs could also appeal to old traditions of popular self-help, and the Senate's violence and the street's grew together.

Where sources disagree. Sallust presents the decree as a power granted more Romano, by Roman custom (Catiline 29.3). Caesar, its target in 49, wrote that the Senate had never come to it before except when the city was almost on fire and every hope of safety gone (Civil War 1.5.3), and has himself tell his soldiers that it had been used against ruinous laws, tribunician violence or a secession, none of which had happened (1.7.5–6). Clodius' law of 58 put the question to the people, and the people answered against the Senate; the courts of 120 and 63 had answered for it, or not at all. Who could judge the decree's use afterwards was never settled.

09Italian voters after 89

The Social War made the Italians south of the Po citizens (module 5.2), and after the struggle over their tribes the Senate granted the new citizens the vote in 84 (Periochae 84). The census of 70, the first to register them in numbers, counted 900,000 citizens (Periochae 98), against 394,736 in 125/124 (Periochae 60; module 5.1). Every one of them could vote only in Rome, in person.

Elections now belonged less to the city. Candidates courted the towns: Cicero planned to go to Cisalpine Gaul as a legate in 65 because its vote seemed to count for much (Letters to Atticus 1.1.2), and the canvassing handbook ascribed to his brother tells the candidate to hold all Italy in his mind, divided by tribes, town by town (Commentariolum petitionis 30–31). Crowds came from all Italy for the elections, the games and the census of 70, and Cicero, prosecuting Verres, wanted the case heard while they were in the city (Verrines 1.54). Italy decided the vote that recalled Cicero: he heard at Brundisium that the centuriate assembly had passed the law in August 57 with an incredible concourse of Italy (Letters to Atticus 4.1.4). The country vote was the answer to Clodius' city.

Who came is another matter. The handbook's advice implies that the voters worth courting were the men of standing in each town who could bring others, and Mouritsen (Plebs and Politics in the Late Roman Republic, 2001) holds that the Italian electorate was in practice the well-off who could afford the journey. The urban crowd kept its weight in legislation, and tribunes tried to change the balance of the tribes in its favour: Manilius' law spreading freedmen through all thirty-five tribes, at the end of 67, was annulled almost at once (Dio 36.42), and in 52 Cicero claimed that laws were being engraved in Clodius' house that would hand the citizens over to their own slaves, which Asconius explains as a law giving freedmen votes in the rural tribes (Pro Milone 87).

10Actors or audience?

Module 11.1 sets out the general debate over Rome as a democracy. The evidence of the street bears on one part of it: what the urban plebs did between 133 and 52.

Fergus Millar's The Crowd in Rome in the Late Republic (1998) followed politics from Sulla's dictatorship to Caesar's crossing of the Rubicon through the crowds in the Forum and read them as the sovereign people at work; Mouritsen (2001) saw small, partial and often organised crowds; Morstein-Marx (2004) a judge offering verdicts on a narrow choice. Module 11.1 gives their cases.

The street evidence cuts across all three. On grain, the colleges and the punishment of Cicero the plebs acted for itself and got what it wanted: free grain, its associations restored and a consul exiled. On wars, provinces and commands it was an audience, applauding policies argued before it. Clodius' following was neither a mob for hire nor the people at large; it was an organised constituency, held together by the grain law and the restored colleges. Paul Vanderbroeck (Popular Leadership and Collective Behavior in the Late Roman Republic, 1987) studied how such crowds were raised through intermediate leaders, the officers of colleges and neighbourhoods among them.

Violence is the test. Lintott (1968) argued that much late-Republican violence grew from traditions the Romans thought legitimate, popular self-help and the tribune's protection on one side and the killing of would-be tyrants on the other, so that each side's force looked lawful to itself. On that reading the crowd that burned the Senate house was, by its own lights, giving a tribune's champion a funeral; to the Senate it was arson. The plebs could act, but only through the institutions it had: tribunes, colleges, contiones and the vote. When the decree of 52 put soldiers round the Forum, the arbiter of the street became the army (deep dive module 11.2), and the larger question passes to deep dive module 11.8.

Where sources disagree. Cicero divides the crowd in two: the true Roman people, seen at the games and the assemblies, and the hired men of the contio (Pro Sestio 104–106). P. A. Brunt ("The Roman Mob", Past and Present, 1966) took the riots as the protests of a populace with real grievances over grain, debt and housing; Mouritsen (2001) and Vanderbroeck (1987) stress organisation and leadership, so that what looks like the people acting is usually an organised minority. The evidence comes almost entirely from Cicero, who had every reason to call his enemies' crowds hirelings and his own the Roman people.

11Self-test

  1. What is the best evidence for the size of the citizen plebs of Rome, and why do Plutarch and Suetonius read it differently?
    Answer

    The grain list: 320,000 men, cut by Caesar to 150,000 (Suetonius, Julius 41.3). Plutarch (Caesar 55) treats the drop as a census showing war losses, and the Periochae (115) also call it a census; Suetonius describes a review of grain recipients street by street, which fits the numbers, since no three years of war halved the city's citizen men.

  2. What does Clodius' performance at Milo's trial in February 56 show about the contio?
    Answer

    Clodius' gangs shouted Pompey down, then answered Clodius' questions in chorus: who is starving the people? Pompey (Letters to Quintus 2.3.2). The convener controlled the stage, a drilled crowd could be made to speak with one voice, and a rival could be shamed before it.

  3. Which powers made the tribunate an instrument for going round the Senate, and how does Cicero's On the Laws argue about it?
    Answer

    Legislation through the plebeian council, the veto, the right to summon a contio, and sacrosanctity. Quintus calls the office born in sedition for sedition; Marcus concedes its power is excessive but argues that the people without a leader are fiercer, and a tribune can restrain them (3.19–26).

  4. How did the price of public grain change between 123 and 58, and why did Clodius' law matter beyond its cost?
    Answer

    Gaius Gracchus set 6⅓ asses a modius (Periochae 60); Saturninus proposed five-sixths of an as, and if his law passed it did not last; the price Clodius abolished in 58 was still 6⅓ asses (Asconius). Free grain made the list worth joining, prompting manumissions (Dionysius 4.24.5; Dio 39.24), and turned the recipients into a constituency bound to the tribune who gave it.

  5. What were the collegia, and how did the Senate and Clodius treat them between 64 and 55?
    Answer

    Neighbourhood and trade associations with officers and funds, including freedmen and slaves, whose masters ran the Compitalia games. The Senate dissolved those thought hostile to the state in 64; Clodius restored them and, Cicero says, added new ones (Against Piso 9), enrolling men street by street in squads; the Senate ordered the squads disbanded in 56 (Letters to Quintus 2.3.5), and Crassus' law of 55 made organising voters through associations a crime.

  6. Why was Clodius' law of 58 against those who had executed citizens without trial a test of the final decree?
    Answer

    Cicero had executed the Catilinarians on the strength of the Senate's decree of 63. The law (Velleius 2.45.1) named no one but declared that the Senate's authority was no defence for killing citizens untried, so the people, not the Senate, judged the decree's use, and Cicero went into exile.

  7. How do Cicero and Asconius differ on the death of Clodius, and why is the published Pro Milone not a record of the trial?
    Answer

    Cicero makes Clodius the ambusher and the killing self-defence (Pro Milone 10–11, 28–29); Asconius reports a chance meeting and Milo's decision to kill the wounded Clodius at Bovillae. Asconius says the delivered speech survived separately, and Dio has Milo joke that had Cicero spoken so, he would not be eating mullets at Massilia (40.54.3–4).

  8. What powers does Sallust say the final decree gave, and why does Lintott call it backing rather than law?
    Answer

    To raise an army, wage war, coerce allies and citizens by every means and hold supreme command and jurisdiction (Catiline 29.3). Lintott argued that it added no legal power and left the magistrate answerable, which the trials of Opimius and Rabirius and the exile of Cicero confirm.

  9. How did the enfranchisement of Italy change Roman elections, and where does it show in 57?
    Answer

    The citizen body grew to 900,000 by the census of 70 (Periochae 98), all voting in Rome, so candidates canvassed the towns of Italy by tribe (Commentariolum petitionis 30–31; Letters to Atticus 1.1.2). Cicero's recall in August 57 was carried in the centuriate assembly with an incredible concourse of Italy (Letters to Atticus 4.1.4), the country's answer to Clodius' city crowd.

  10. On which questions does this module's evidence show the urban plebs acting, and on which merely watching?
    Answer

    It acted on matters that touched it directly: grain, its associations and the punishment of Cicero for executing citizens. On wars, provinces and commands it was an audience. Even its action ran through organisations, tribunes, colleges and gangs, which Vanderbroeck and Mouritsen stress against Brunt's picture of protest driven by real grievances.

12Carry forward

  1. Popular politics in the late Republic ran through institutions, not mobs: the tribunate, the contio, the grain list and the neighbourhood colleges. Whoever held them could call out a crowd, and Caesar and Augustus took them over or closed them rather than leave them to others.
  2. Violence was argued inside the constitution, not outside it: the Senate's final decree and the street's self-help each claimed legitimacy, and courts and assemblies judged each case after the event. When neither side could win the argument, the man with soldiers settled it, as Pompey did in 52.
  3. Almost everything about the plebs comes from Cicero, who praised the Roman people in public and despised the crowd in private, and from Asconius reading Cicero. Ask of every crowd who organised it, who reports it, and what the reporter needed it to be.
