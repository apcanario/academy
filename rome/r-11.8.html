01Which republic, and what counts as an explanation

This deep dive has taken the Republic apart piece by piece: how it worked at its height (deep dive module 11.1), the armies (11.2), the street (11.3), the women of its great houses (11.4), Cicero's letters (11.5), Caesar's war commentaries (11.6) and the Ides (11.7). This module weighs the explanations that ancient and modern writers built from the same evidence. They answer different questions. Some name a cause (fear removed, an empire too large, soldiers without land); some a mechanism (aristocratic competition turned violent); some only a turning point (146, 133, 88, 49). An explanation also needs an object. Module 0.3 shows how many dates have been proposed for the end, and the convention of 31 or 27 BC marks the end of rival dynasts, not a moment Romans recognised.

The authors stand at different distances. Polybius wrote about 150 BC, before the crisis, and could only predict. Sallust wrote about 42–40 BC, inside it. Lucan wrote under Nero, Appian about AD 160 and Cassius Dio about AD 220, all under emperors, so each ancient answer is also a verdict on the monarchy that followed. The moderns are no freer of their own times: Theodor Mommsen wrote after the failure of 1848, Ronald Syme in 1939.

02Decline and the wheel: Sallust and Polybius

Module 5.6 sets out Sallust's theory. Fear of the enemy (metus hostilis) held Rome to concord; when Carthage was destroyed in 146, the desire for money grew and then the desire for power (Catiline 10), and nobles and people turned standing and liberty into licence (Jugurtha 41.2–5). The idea was older than Sallust. Plutarch says that Scipio Nasica answered Cato's demand to destroy Carthage by arguing that it must be kept, so that fear would keep Rome disciplined (Cato Maior 27; module 4.4). Velleius put the same turn at 146 (2.1), and Cicero, writing in the 50s, said the Romans had lost the republic in fact through their own vices, not by chance (On the Republic 5.2, preserved by Augustine, City of God 2.21).

As an explanation, the theory has two weaknesses. Its date is a choice: Livy, Polybius, Calpurnius Piso and Sallust each fixed the onset of luxury at a different moment (module 4.5), and in the Histories Sallust himself pushed discord back to the Republic's beginning (module 5.6). And it explains everything equally, Jugurtha's bribes and Catiline's debts alike, so it cannot say why the crisis took the form it did. What survives is the observation inside the moralism: once no rival threatened the state, the competition for glory that had served it (deep dive module 11.1) turned inward, with an empire's resources to spend.

Polybius gave the first systematic account of Rome's constitution, and module 4.5 sets out his cycle of constitutions and the mixed constitution that halts it. For this module what matters is a prediction. When a state has escaped great dangers and reached unchallenged supremacy, he wrote, prosperity makes life more extravagant and rivalry for office fiercer; the people, angered by some men's greed and flattered by others' ambition, refuse to obey; and the state takes the fairest names, freedom and democracy, while becoming the worst thing, mob rule (6.57). Written a generation before the Gracchi, the passage has looked prophetic ever since. It is also generic, deduced from a Greek theory rather than observed in Rome, and it ends in rule by the crowd, where Rome ended in rule by one man.

Where sources disagree. The ancient theorists agree that success corrupted Rome and disagree over where the rot worked. Polybius puts it in the people's demands, whipped up by ambitious men (6.57). Sallust puts it in the nobility's avarice and arrogance first and the people's licence second (Jugurtha 41). Tacitus, writing in the early second century AD, makes it a single passion, the old human lust for power, which grew with the size of the empire once rival cities and kings were gone, and he runs it from the tribunes and consuls through Marius and Sulla to Pompey, after whom the struggle was only for supremacy (Histories 2.38).

03Civil war as the story: Lucan and Appian

Lucan's Civil War is a poem, and module 10.2 reads it as an answer to the Aeneid. Its first book also stops to ask what drove a people to arms (1.67–69) and offers three kinds of answer. The first is cosmic: an envious fate denies long life to the highest things, and great things collapse upon themselves (1.70–82). The second is political: Rome was ruined by becoming the common property of three masters, and power shared was never lasting (1.84–93). The third is Sallust's: conquest brought wealth, wealth luxury, poverty that had bred soldiers was shunned, great estates swallowed the old farms, offices were bought and debt spread, until war became profitable to many (1.158–182). The poem then shows no restoration. It mourns a liberty lost at Pharsalus, and its Cato (deep dive module 13.4) stands for a cause that only the gods' disfavour defeated.

Appian's Civil Wars, written about two centuries after the events, makes a narrative into an explanation. His preface traces quarrels between Senate and people that stayed within the law until Tiberius Gracchus was killed, then violence that grew with each generation, through Sulla, Caesar and the triumvirs, to Octavian, the first to be called Augustus (1.1–5; module 5.1 has his thesis on 133). He states the result: from many kinds of civil commotion the Roman state passed to concord and monarchy (1.6). Appian, a Greek official under the Antonines, wrote from inside that concord. His sequence is a strong explanation of how, an escalation in which each act of violence licensed the next, and a weak one of why, since it assumes the destination it describes.

04Monarchy as necessity: Cassius Dio

Dio, a senator and twice consul who saw emperors made and murdered (module 7.4), gave the most direct ancient answer. Deep dive module 11.7 left it here. Before narrating the Ides, Dio pauses to judge (44.2). Democracy has a fair name but its results do not match it; monarchy has an unpleasant sound but is the most practical government, since it is easier to find one excellent man than many. Popular governments have flourished only briefly, until success bred insolence and ambition bred envy. For a city ruling most of the known world, with many peoples, great wealth and endless business, moderation under a democracy is impossible, and without moderation there is no concord. Had Brutus and Cassius considered this, he concludes, they would not have killed the city's protector. In book 52 he stages a debate in 29 BC in which Agrippa argues for restoring the Republic and Maecenas for monarchy, and Maecenas wins.

Dio's argument has two parts, and they fare differently. The claim that one ruler is generally better than many is a Greek commonplace, and his evidence is the reign he lived under. The claim that this empire could not be governed by annual magistrates, a Senate and assemblies meeting in one city is the strongest idea antiquity left, and every structural explanation since has been a version of it.

Where sources disagree. Was the end of the Republic a crime or a cure? Lucan makes civil war Rome's crime against itself and the loss of liberty a wound that the poem refuses to close; his gods favour the winning cause and Cato the losing one (1.128). Dio makes the murder of Caesar the error and monarchy the remedy (44.2), and Appian calls the outcome concord (1.6). Both sides wrote under emperors: Lucan was forced to die by one, and Dio was honoured by several.

05From Mommsen to Syme: the saviour and the oligarchy

Mommsen's Römische Geschichte (third volume, 1856) is the first modern answer, and module 10.4 places it in nineteenth-century reception. Its argument is that the senatorial oligarchy was incapable of governing an empire and rotten beyond reform, that the popular party from the Gracchi onward was groping towards the only workable alternative, a monarchy resting on the people and the army, and that Caesar, Rome's one creative genius, carried it through. The verdict on Cicero that deep dive module 11.5 quotes belongs to the same chapter of thought: the talker condemned beside the man of action. Mommsen never wrote the volume on the emperors that should have followed.

The thesis contains two claims. That some form of monarchy was needed is Dio's claim restated, and it survives in modern forms. That Caesar was the saviour does not. His own account of his wars is the case for the defence (deep dive module 11.6); his rule lasted five years after the Rubicon and ended in murder by his own marshals and men he had pardoned (11.7); and the settlement that worked was Augustus', built on what Caesar had refused to disguise (module 6.1).

Syme's The Roman Revolution (1939), written as Europe's dictators rose, replaced the hero with a faction. "The Roman constitution was a screen and a sham", he wrote: behind it a few noble houses competed for office, honour and wealth through alliances of family and friendship, and the liberty they defended was their own. The Republic fell when that competition produced dynasts too great to be contained, and the revolution of 60 BC to AD 14 was a transfer of power and property from the old nobility to a new governing class, the dynast's partisans and the men of the Italian towns. Module 6.1 gives his method, prosopography, and its reading of the Principate; deep dive module 11.1 gives his oligarchy behind the facade.

Syme explains who won better than why the old order broke. His method assumes that family alliances decided politics, which is what Brunt and Millar doubted when they examined clientship and the vote (deep dive module 11.1). Yet his central claim has held up: the nobility's own rivalry, not a revolt from below, brought down the Republic, and the dynasts who ended it were nobles playing the old game for higher stakes.

06Gruen: a Republic that was not dying

Erich Gruen's The Last Generation of the Roman Republic (1974) attacked the premise shared by Sallust, Mommsen and Syme: that the Republic of the 70s to 50s was already dying. Taking the years from Sulla's death to 49, he found institutions doing their work. Elections were held and contested, new men reached the Senate, courts tried and convicted, laws against bribery were passed, emergencies were met, and the soldiers followed their commanders as lawful officers of the state (deep dive module 11.2). The crisis came from a particular breakdown, the quarrel over Caesar's command in 50 and 49 (module 5.4), which turned into war. His judgement, in a sentence that became famous, was that "civil war caused the fall of the Republic, not vice versa" (p. 504).

The objections come from this deep dive's own evidence. The elections for 53 were delayed into that summer and none had been held for 52 when it began; the Senate house burned at Clodius' funeral; order returned only when Pompey, sole consul, put soldiers round the Forum (deep dive module 11.3). The consular elections of 54 broke records for bribery and produced a written bargain to forge a law and a decree (11.5). Institutions that work only under armed protection are working on borrowed authority. Gruen's lasting contribution is methodological. Nobody in 60 or 55 knew how the story ended, and a historian who reads every riot as a symptom of terminal decline is writing Appian's teleology again. The fiftieth anniversary of the book in 2024 was marked by a conference at Berkeley, where Robert Morstein-Marx took Gruen's sentence as the title of his paper.

07Brunt and Meier: conflict and a crisis without alternative

P. A. Brunt looked below the nobility. Social Conflicts in the Roman Republic (1971) and the long title essay of The Fall of the Roman Republic and Related Essays (1988) set out the grievances the oligarchy failed to meet: the land hunger of the rural poor and of veterans (module 5.1; deep dive module 11.2), the grain, debt and housing of the city (11.3), the Italians' demand for citizenship (module 5.2), and the exploitation of the provinces. None of these groups sought to overthrow the Republic, but each could be recruited by a commander or a tribune who promised what the Senate refused. Brunt's own formula for the soldiers, men of the country "with arms in their hands" (deep dive module 11.2), sums up the argument: the Republic fell because those it failed found leaders with armies.

Christian Meier's Res publica amissa (1966), "the Republic lost", explained why so many grievances never produced reform. The Republic rested on consensus, the Senate's coordinating authority and a web of personal ties; it had no organ that could will its own transformation. No group wanted a different order: populares and optimates alike claimed to defend the Republic, and even Caesar offered no new constitution. Meier called this a crisis without alternative (Krise ohne Alternative). The order could not be reformed, because no one could conceive of a replacement, and it could not be defended, because those who broke its rules did so for personal standing rather than a programme. The resources of empire allowed the governing class to satisfy the powerful and ignore the powerless, and the empire's demands outran a constitution built for a city. Autocracy came in the end not as a plan but as what was left.

Where sources disagree. Was the crisis structural or contingent? Meier (1966) holds that the city-state's order could neither reform itself nor meet the demands of a world empire, so that autocracy became unavoidable whatever individuals did. Brunt (1971, 1988) locates the cause in material grievances that a wiser oligarchy might have met, so that the fall was a failure of policy as much as of structure. Gruen (1974) denies that the Republic was failing before 49 at all and lays the fall on the war that followed a specific quarrel. The three agree on the evidence of riots, bribery and long commands and differ on whether it describes a disease or the ordinary noise of a working state.

08Flower: which republic fell?

Module 0.3 records that Harriet Flower (Roman Republics, 2010) divides the span into several republics and ends the republic of the nobles with Sulla's march in 88. Her full sequence changes the question. After a pre-republican period and a proto-republic ending with the Twelve Tables, she counts six republics. The first runs from 450 to 367/6 and the second from 366 to 300. The third, fourth and fifth are the republics of the nobles: 300 to 180, 180 to 139 and 139 to 88, divided at the law of 180 that fixed the ladder of office and the ballot law of 139 (deep dive module 11.1). From 88 to 81 republican government ceased. The sixth republic was Sulla's, from 81 to 60, a new construction rather than a restoration. After it came no republic at all: the alliance of Pompey, Crassus and Caesar from 59 to 53, a transition from 53 to 49, Caesar's dictatorship from 49 to 44 and the triumvirate from 43 to 33.

On this scheme there is no single fall to explain. The republic of the nobles, the one Polybius described and deep dive module 11.1 reconstructs, ended when a consul first led a Roman army against Rome. Sulla's republic, which held the first half of the years Gruen found working, was a different and more fragile order, imposed by a victor and partly undone within a decade of his death (module 5.3); the 50s, the rest of Gruen's span, Flower counts as no republic at all. Republican politics ended with the compact of 60. By the Ides there was nothing left to restore except names. The scheme's cost is that it multiplies breaks and can make every change of law look like a new constitution; its gain is to stop historians asking why one unchanging Republic died.

09Italy, the empire and the army

Recent work has moved the explanation away from the Senate house towards the three things the Republic acquired faster than its institutions could absorb.

Italy. The Social War made the Italians south of the Po citizens, and the census of 70 counted 900,000 citizens (Periochae 98) against 394,736 in 125/124 (Periochae 60). All of them could vote only in Rome, in person (deep dive module 11.3). Henrik Mouritsen (Italian Unification, 1998) argued that the story of a gradual unification of Italy under Rome was written after the Social War, and that the allies of 91 fought for independence (module 5.2). Either way, the Republic incorporated a nation into the institutions of a city without changing them, and its new citizens' leading men found their place only under Augustus, when Italian new men became consuls (module 6.1). Syme had seen the same men as the winners of the revolution.

The empire. Provinces gave a proconsul armies, money and years beyond the reach of the courts (deep dive module 11.2). Caesar had so much Gallic gold, Suetonius says, that he sold it in Italy and the provinces at 3,000 sesterces a pound (Julius 54.2; deep dive module 11.6), and Brutus, Cicero's letters show, could lend to a provincial city at 48 per cent with a governor's cavalry behind his agent (Letters to Atticus 5.21.10–13; 11.5). The Senate's restraints, age rules, annual office and sumptuary laws (11.1), had been designed for a competition among equals. The empire supplied prizes no equal could match. This is Dio's argument without his preference for monarchy, and Tacitus' lust for power growing with the empire's size.

The army. Deep dive module 11.2 weighs the client army and concludes that what survives is narrower than Gabba's model: soldiers were persuaded, not owned, but the Republic never made discharge its own business, so whoever could provide land and money held the army. Arthur Keaveney (The Army in the Roman Revolution, 2007) moved the turning point to Sulla, and Lukas de Blois stressed the officers through whom generals reached their men. The army explains the means of the fall far better than its motive. Soldiers marched on Rome in 88, 87, 83–82, 49 and 43 because commanders gave them reasons; they did not invent the quarrels.

Two further strands are recent. Morstein-Marx (Julius Caesar and the Roman People, 2021) reads Caesar as an exceptionally successful republican leader judged by republican standards (deep dive module 11.7), which removes the would-be king from the centre of the story. Edward Watts (Mortal Republic, 2018) told the century as an erosion of political norms, each breach licensing the next, until Romans accepted autocracy for the sake of stability: Appian's escalation set in modern terms.

10What survives

No single explanation survives intact, and the ones that fail do so for a common reason: each takes a part of the evidence for the whole. Sallust's moral decline is a description, not a cause, though the competition it moralises was real. Polybius' wheel was deduced before the events and ended in the wrong place. Appian and Lucan narrate an escalation and mourn or welcome its end, so they explain how and leave why to the reader. Mommsen's saviour fails on Caesar's own record. Gruen's working Republic fails if functioning is measured by whether institutions could decide conflicts without soldiers.

What survives is a layered account. The empire supplied the means: armies, provinces and money on a scale the nobility's self-discipline could not ration (Dio, Meier). Aristocratic competition supplied the motive: dynasts pursuing dignitas by the old rules for larger stakes (Syme, and Sallust's observation stripped of its moralism). The unmet claims of veterans, the urban poor and the Italians supplied the followers (Brunt). The constitution lacked any organ that could reform it, so the crisis had no exit except through force (Meier). And the event that turned strain into collapse was civil war, as Gruen insists, though the war was not an accident but the form these pressures took once two dynasts with armies could not both keep their standing. Flower's sequence adds that the collapse came in stages, and that the republic of the nobles was gone a generation before the Rubicon.

Deep dive module 11.7 asked whether the murder of Caesar could have restored the Republic. On this account it could not. By 44 the Republic's restraints rested on agreements among nobles that nobody could enforce, and the forces that had broken them were still in place: legions whose discharge no state paid for, veterans waiting for land, a city crowd attached to Caesar's name, and an empire whose commands were worth more than any office at Rome. Removing the man left all of these. Augustus succeeded where the conspirators failed because he supplied what the Republic had lacked, a single paymaster for the army and a single arbiter of aristocratic competition, and dressed it in republican names (modules 6.1 and 6.4).

11Self-test

  1. What does Sallust's theory of the fear of Carthage explain well, and why is it weak as an explanation?
    Answer

    It captures a real change: once no rival threatened Rome, the competition for glory turned inward with an empire's resources to spend (Catiline 10; Jugurtha 41.2–5). It is weak because its date is a choice (the ancients fixed the onset of luxury at different moments, module 4.5; Sallust himself moved it back in the Histories) and because it explains every scandal equally, so it cannot say why the crisis took the form it did.

  2. How did Polybius' prediction at 6.57 differ from what happened?
    Answer

    He foresaw prosperity and rivalry for office provoking the people, so that the state would take the names of freedom and democracy and become mob rule. Rome ended instead in the rule of one man, and the prediction was deduced from Greek theory a generation before the Gracchi, not observed.

  3. What three kinds of cause does Lucan give for the civil war in his first book?
    Answer

    A cosmic one, fate's envy and great things collapsing upon themselves (1.70–82); a political one, Rome become the common property of three masters, since shared power never lasts (1.84–93); and a moral one, wealth, luxury, bought offices and debt until war was profitable to many (1.158–182).

  4. Why is Appian's account a strong explanation of how the Republic fell and a weak one of why?
    Answer

    His preface traces violence escalating from Tiberius Gracchus' death through Sulla, Caesar and the triumvirs, each act licensing the next (1.1–5). But he writes from the outcome, the passage from civil commotion to concord and monarchy (1.6), and assumes the destination his sequence describes.

  5. What are the two parts of Dio's argument at 44.2, and which survives?
    Answer

    That one ruler is generally better than many, since one good man is easier to find, a Greek commonplace supported by the reign he lived under; and that an empire of many peoples and great wealth could not keep the moderation democracy needs. The second survives: every structural explanation since is a version of it.

  6. Why does Mommsen's thesis split into one claim that survives and one that does not?
    Answer

    That some form of monarchy was needed restates Dio and survives in modern forms such as Meier's. That Caesar was the saviour fails on his record: his own Commentaries are the case for his defence (11.6), his rule lasted five years after the Rubicon and ended in murder by his own marshals and men he had pardoned (11.7), and the settlement that worked was Augustus'.

  7. How did Syme's explanation replace Mommsen's, and what part of it has held up?
    Answer

    He replaced the hero with a faction: a few noble houses competing behind "a screen and a sham" of a constitution, and a revolution that transferred power and property to the dynast's partisans and the men of the Italian towns (The Roman Revolution, 1939). His prosopographical method is doubted, but his claim that the nobility's own rivalry, not a revolt from below, brought the Republic down has held.

  8. What did Gruen argue, and what evidence from this deep dive tells against him?
    Answer

    That from Sulla's death to 49 elections, courts and the Senate worked, and that civil war caused the fall of the Republic, not the reverse (1974). Against him: the elections for 53 and 52 delayed by riots and order restored by Pompey's soldiers in 52 (11.3), and the record bribery of 54 (11.5), which suggest institutions working only under armed protection.

  9. How do Meier and Brunt differ on why the grievances of the late Republic were never resolved?
    Answer

    Brunt makes the cause the oligarchy's failure to meet the needs of veterans, the poor, the Italians and the provinces, a failure of policy that commanders exploited (1971, 1988). Meier argues that the order had no organ to transform itself and that no group wanted a different constitution, a crisis without alternative from which only autocracy could emerge (Res publica amissa, 1966).

  10. On Flower's sequence, why could the murder of Caesar not restore the Republic?
    Answer

    Because the republic of the nobles had ended in 88, Sulla's republic in 60, and the years after were an alliance, a transition and a dictatorship, not a republic (Roman Republics, 2010). By 44 there was nothing to restore but names, and the armies, veterans and crowd that had broken the old restraints remained.

12Carry forward

  1. Every explanation of the Republic's fall is also a verdict on what replaced it: Lucan mourned, Appian and Dio approved, Mommsen wrote after 1848 and Syme in 1939. Ask of any theory of political collapse what its author thought of the regime that followed.
  2. Separate the levels of an explanation: conditions (empire, Italy, unpaid veterans), motive (aristocratic competition), mechanism (escalating violence with no constitutional exit) and trigger (the civil war of 49). Most quarrels between historians of the Republic are quarrels over which level counts.
  3. The Republic could be ended but not restored, because its restraints rested on agreements that nobody could enforce once soldiers had learned their price; the Principate lasted by supplying one paymaster and one arbiter under republican names, and the empire's later crises turn on who controls those two functions.
