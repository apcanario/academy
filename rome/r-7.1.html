The sources reign by reign

Module 0.1 introduces Tacitus, Suetonius and Cassius Dio. For the four reigns after Augustus none is complete, and each emperor's reputation shifts with whichever survives. Tacitus wrote the Annals early in the second century, year by year and from the Senate's benches; Suetonius arranged each life by topic, virtues before vices; Dio, a senator of the early third century, survives in his own words, with gaps, to AD 46 and then only in Byzantine epitome.

Tiberius: accession, mutiny and Germanicus

Power came before the vote. On Augustus' death Tiberius gave the watchword to the praetorians as imperator and wrote to the armies as princeps (Tacitus, Annals 1.7.5); only then did he hesitate before the Senate over accepting the whole (1.11–13), holding, Suetonius has him say, a wolf by the ears (Tiberius 25.1). Velleius, who had served under him, makes the reluctance sincere (2.124.2); Tacitus reads it as a mask, and Dio opens his book on a man who never said what he wanted (57.1).

The armies tested the reign at once. The Pannonian legions mutinied over pay and service (module 6.4 quotes their complaints, 1.17) and were calmed by Tiberius' son Drusus (1.16–30). The four legions of the lower Rhine offered the empire to their commander Germanicus, Tiberius' nephew and adopted son, who threatened to kill himself rather than break faith, bought peace with a letter written as if from Tiberius, and let the loyal soldiers butcher the ringleaders (1.31–49). His small son Gaius, the soldiers' pet, was nicknamed Caligula for his boots (1.41).

Germanicus then took the army across the Rhine for three seasons, in 16 with a fleet Tacitus puts at a thousand ships (2.6). He recovered two of Varus' three eagles (1.60, 2.25), buried the bones of Varus' legions, which Tiberius thought improper for an augur (1.61–62), and beat Arminius at Idistaviso (2.16–18). But Caecina's army was nearly lost at the pontes longi in 15 (1.63–68; module 6.4 asks whether Kalkriese is that battle), and a North Sea storm wrecked the fleet (2.23–24). Tiberius recalled him, writing that Germany could be left to its own quarrels (2.26), and gave him a triumph in 17. Sent east with authority over every governor there (2.43), Germanicus quarrelled with Gnaeus Piso, governor of Syria, and died at Antioch in 19, sure he had been poisoned; lead curse tablets bearing his name were found in his house (2.69–73; module 2.4). Piso, tried before the Senate, killed himself before the verdict (3.15).

Where sources disagree. Suetonius reports the belief that Germanicus died by Tiberius' design and Piso's agency (Caligula 2); Tacitus lets the rumour stand while admitting that the poisoning charge failed (Annals 3.14.1). The Senate's own decree, dated 10 December 20 and found near Seville, names Piso as the cause of the death only in Germanicus' own testimony, condemns Piso for leaving his province and trying to retake it by force, and spares his wife Plancina at Livia's request, as Tacitus reports (3.17).

Tiberius: Sejanus, Capri and the treason trials

Tacitus makes AD 23 the turn, when fortune began to make trouble and Tiberius to rage or lend his strength to others' rage (4.1). The agent was Sejanus, an equestrian from Volsinii and prefect of the guard, who gathered its cohorts into a single camp (4.2). Drusus, Tiberius' heir, died that year; in 31 Sejanus' divorced wife revealed that Sejanus and Drusus' wife Livilla had poisoned him (4.3, 4.11). Sejanus persuaded Tiberius to live away from Rome (4.41), and from 27 the emperor stayed on Capri (4.67). After Livia's death in 29 Germanicus' widow Agrippina and her two elder sons were destroyed. In 31 Sejanus was consul with Tiberius; on 18 October a long letter from Capri was read in the Senate (Juvenal 10.71), the guard having passed to Macro, and Sejanus was executed that day (Dio 58.9–11). Velleius, publishing in 30, had praised him as Tiberius' tireless helper (2.127–128); Valerius Maximus, soon after, cursed an unnamed would-be parricide (9.11.ext.4; module 3.2).

Where sources disagree. Tiberius wrote that he punished Sejanus for raging against Germanicus' children (Suetonius, Tiberius 61.1); Josephus has Antonia warn him of a plot (Jewish Antiquities 18.181–182); Dio's Sejanus reaches for the throne. Edward Champlin (Tiberius and His Age, 2024) draws an equestrian who modelled himself on Agrippa and may have been seen as a serious candidate for the succession. Whether there was a conspiracy is uncertain.

The instrument of fear was the law of maiestas, which Augustus had stretched from deeds to words and writings (1.72.3). With no public prosecutor, informers brought charges for a share of the estate, "a breed of men invented for the public ruin" (4.30.3). In 25 the historian Cremutius Cordus, accused of praising Brutus and Cassius, starved himself and his books were burned; punishing talent, Tacitus answers, increases its authority (4.34–35). Tacitus also shows Tiberius quashing early charges (1.73) and muttering at the senators, "men ready for slavery" (3.65.3). After 31 the trials became a massacre (6.19).

Capri is Suetonius' showpiece of sexual vice (Tiberius 43–45); Tacitus is vaguer (6.1). Champlin calls these stories incoherent fictions, probably by one skilled writer who hated Tiberius, and finds behind them a learned emperor absorbed in astrology and myth. Tiberius died at Misenum in March 37: smothered by Macro in Tacitus (6.50), by Gaius and Macro in Dio (58.28), by one of several means in Suetonius (Tiberius 73). Tacitus' obituary traces stages of declining concealment until, fear and shame gone, he followed only his own nature (6.51).

Gaius Caligula: the first years and the turn

Gaius, Germanicus' last surviving son, was twenty-four, and the Senate set aside Tiberius' will, which had made Tiberius' grandson Gemellus joint heir (Suetonius, Caligula 14.1). Suetonius counts more than 160,000 victims sacrificed for joy in under three months (14.1); exiles were recalled, accounts published, Cordus' books allowed back (15–16). Philo remembers seven golden months ended by a grave illness in autumn 37 (Embassy to Gaius 8–21). Within a year Gemellus had been killed and Macro forced to suicide (Dio 59.8–10).

Dio dates the open break to 39, when Gaius praised Tiberius in the Senate, read out the senators' own attacks on Tiberius' victims from papers he had claimed to burn, and restored treason charges (59.16); a plot by the commander in Upper Germany and Gaius' brother-in-law followed (59.22). Then come the stories of madness: the seashells gathered as spoils of Ocean (Suetonius, Caligula 46), incest with his sisters (24), the horse he is said to have meant to make consul (55.3; Dio 59.14.7), a temple to his own divinity (Caligula 22). Suetonius marks the turn in his own text: so much for the princeps, the rest must be told of a monster (22.1). He supplies causes too, epilepsy, insomnia and a love potion (50); Seneca uses the cruelties as moral examples (On Anger 3.18–19).

Where sources disagree. Philo places the change in the illness of 37, Dio in the quarrel of 39. Arther Ferrill (1991) took the madness as real. Anthony Barrett (Caligula: The Corruption of Power, 1989; revised as Caligula: The Abuse of Power, 2015) denies insanity and finds a capable young man so absorbed in his own importance that he lost all sense of moral responsibility. Aloys Winterling (Caligula, 2003) argues that from 39 Gaius refused the double-speak by which senators treated a monarch as a fellow citizen while flattering him as a god: he took the flattery literally, and the horse insulted the Senate.

The Jews of Alexandria and Philo's embassy

In 38 the Greek crowd of Alexandria mocked the Jewish king Agrippa I, set up images of Gaius in the synagogues, and the prefect Flaccus declared the Jews foreigners and aliens and penned them into one quarter, where they were looted and killed (Philo, Against Flaccus 25–72). Both communities sent embassies, the Jewish one led by the aged Philo (Josephus, Jewish Antiquities 18.257–260). Gaius heard the Jews while touring the gardens of Maecenas and Lamia, ordering refurbishments, asked why they did not eat pork, and dismissed them as more unfortunate than wicked for not believing him a god (Embassy to Gaius 349–367). Meanwhile he had ordered his statue set up in the Temple at Jerusalem; the legate of Syria delayed, Agrippa interceded, and Gaius' death ended the danger (Embassy 188–348; Jewish Antiquities 18.261–309). Claudius' letter of 41, preserved on papyrus (P.Lond. 1912), told the Greeks to leave Jewish worship alone and the Jews not to seek more than they had in a city not their own.

Where sources disagree. Josephus quotes an edict of Claudius granting Alexandria's Jews civic rights equal to the Greeks' from the earliest times (Jewish Antiquities 19.280–285). The papyrus, untouched by copyists, says a city not their own and bars them from the gymnasium's games. How far the Jews sought full citizenship, rather than security as a community, is still argued.

Gaius was killed in a palace passage on 24 January 41 by guard officers led by Cassius Chaerea, whom he had mocked with obscene watchwords (Suetonius, Caligula 56–58; Dio 59.29). Josephus gives the fullest narrative (Jewish Antiquities 19.1–273).

Claudius: the guard's emperor

While the consuls held the Capitol and the Senate debated liberty (Suetonius, Claudius 10.3; Josephus, Jewish Antiquities 19.158–184), a soldier found Germanicus' brother Claudius hiding behind a curtain in the palace and the guard took him to its camp (Claudius 10.1–2). He promised each guardsman 15,000 sesterces in Suetonius' figure, the first Caesar to buy the soldiers' loyalty (10.4), 5,000 drachmas in Josephus' (19.247). The Senate, without troops, voted the powers. The guard chose; the Senate ratified.

Claudius was fifty, lame and stammering, an antiquarian who had written Etruscan and Carthaginian histories (Claudius 30, 42), and for Suetonius the servant of his wives and freedmen (29.1). Freedmen ran the secretariats, Narcissus the correspondence and Pallas the accounts (28); module 7.5 asks how much of a bureaucracy this was. In 43 Aulus Plautius invaded Britain, and Claudius came for sixteen days, with elephants, to take the surrender of Camulodunum (Dio 60.19–23). His arch claimed, on the usual restoration, eleven kings surrendered without loss (CIL VI 920), and his son became Britannicus.

Tacitus' Claudius, from book 11, is a court drama of wives. In 48 Messalina went through a public marriage with the consul designate Gaius Silius; Narcissus stampeded Claudius into having her killed, and the emperor, told at dinner, called for more wine (11.26–38). Tacitus concedes the tale will seem fabulous (11.27). In 49 Claudius married his niece Agrippina (12.5–7), who had Seneca recalled (12.8), her son adopted as Nero (12.25) and Burrus put over the guard (12.42). Claudius died in October 54.

Where sources disagree. Tacitus has Agrippina poison a dish of mushrooms (12.66–67); Pliny the Elder names Agrippina's mushroom (Natural History 22.92); Suetonius says all agree on poison but not on where or by whom (Claudius 44.2); Josephus says only that some said so (Jewish Antiquities 20.148). Seneca's satire has him die listening to comic actors. A sick man of sixty-three could die naturally; the case rests on motive, with Britannicus near manhood.

The Lyon tablet

In 48, as censor, Claudius proposed that the nobles of Gallia Comata be allowed to hold magistracies and so enter the Senate. The bronze from Lyon (CIL XIII 1668) preserves part of the speech; module 1.5 uses it for Mastarna, and module 0.1 sets it beside Tacitus' version. The argument is an antiquarian's. Outsiders have always ruled Rome: the Sabine Numa, Tarquinius Priscus, son of a Corinthian, Servius the captive's son or Etruscan Mastarna. The constitution has always changed, from kings to consuls, dictators, tribunes and decemvirs. Augustus and Tiberius admitted the best men of the colonies and towns, and Vienne already sends senators; against Caesar's ten years of war Gaul sets a hundred years of loyalty. Tacitus reports the objection that these were heirs of the Gauls who had besieged Caesar at Alesia (11.23); his Claudius answers more sharply that Athens and Sparta fell because they treated the conquered as aliens, Romulus made enemies citizens on the same day, and what is now defended by precedent will itself become precedent (11.24).

Seneca's satire on the deification

Claudius was made a god, and the senators laughed when Nero's funeral speech, written by Seneca, praised his foresight (Annals 13.3). Seneca also wrote the satire Dio calls the Apocolocyntosis, the "pumpkinification" (60.35.3). It sends Claudius limping to heaven, where Augustus lists his murders to the gods (10–11), then to the underworld, where he is convicted without a hearing, as he had convicted others, and handed to a freedman as a law clerk (14–15). Every joke is the senatorial case: rule by freedmen and wives, trials unheard, senators killed. Seneca's life is deep dive module 13.5.

Nero: the good years and the murder of Agrippina

Nero was sixteen at his accession. His first speech to the Senate, written by Seneca according to Dio (61.3.1), promised no more trials behind closed doors and a household kept apart from the state (Annals 13.4). Asked to sign a death warrant, he wished he had never learned to write (Suetonius, Nero 10.2); in 58 he proposed abolishing the indirect taxes (Annals 13.50). Trajan was later said to have rated five years of Nero above any emperor's (Aurelius Victor, Caesares 5.2). Yet Britannicus was poisoned in 55 (13.15–17; module 1.4).

The coins chart Agrippina's fall: in 54 her head faces her son's, in 55 it sits behind his. In March 59 Nero sent her home from Baiae in a boat built to collapse; she swam ashore, and the fleet prefect came to her villa to kill her (14.1–8). Nero's letter to the Senate accused her of plotting; Thrasea Paetus walked out (14.10–12), the first gesture of a resistance that deep dive module 13.4 follows to his death in 66.

Where sources disagree. Tacitus makes Poppaea's taunts the motive (14.1) and names his authorities on the rumoured incest, Cluvius Rufus blaming Agrippina and Fabius Rusticus Nero (14.2). Barrett (Agrippina, 1996) finds the Poppaea motive weak, since Nero married her only in 62, and argues that Agrippina remained a force to the end and that the good years coincided with her influence. Champlin (Nero, 2003) shows Nero later singing Orestes on stage (Suetonius, Nero 21.3), turning the crime into tragedy.

Nero: Boudica, the fire and the Golden House

In Britain in 60 or 61 Prasutagus, king of the Iceni, died leaving Nero co-heir with his two daughters. The procurator's men plundered the kingdom, flogged his widow Boudica and raped the daughters, and the Trinovantes, dispossessed by the veterans of Camulodunum, joined the rising (Annals 14.31). Camulodunum, Londinium and Verulamium were burned, about 70,000 citizens and allies dying by Tacitus' figure (14.33), 80,000 by Dio's (62.1.1). Suetonius Paullinus won the final battle; Tacitus gives a little under 80,000 British dead against about 400 Roman, and has Boudica take poison (14.37). Module 7.5 takes the revolt as a limit of the system.

Where sources disagree. Tacitus' Agricola makes the rising a general revolt against occupation (15–16), the Annals a reaction to particular outrages (14.31). Dio, in epitome, adds that Seneca had called in forty million sesterces of loans at once (62.2.1), gives Boudica a long speech and an omen from a released hare, and has her die of illness (62.12.6).

On 19 July 64 fire broke out by the Circus Maximus and burned for six days; of Rome's fourteen regions three were levelled and seven gutted (Annals 15.38–40). Nero opened his gardens to the homeless, cut the price of grain (15.39), and rebuilt with wider streets, lower blocks and fireproof stone (15.43). To end the rumour of his guilt he punished the Christians, which module 2.5 examines with Annals 15.44.

Where sources disagree. Tacitus leaves open whether the fire was chance or the emperor's malice, since writers gave both (15.38.1), and reports only a rumour that Nero sang of Troy's fall (15.39.3). Suetonius (Nero 38) and Dio (62.16–18) make him the arsonist, and Pliny the Elder, who lived through it, mentions the fires with which Nero burned the city (Natural History 17.5). Champlin argues for Nero's guilt; Barrett (Rome Is Burning, 2020) judges the origin undetermined and the guilt mostly legend.

On the cleared ground rose the Golden House. Suetonius describes a colossal statue of Nero 120 feet high, a mile-long portico, a lake like a sea, fields and woods, and Nero saying he could at last live like a human being (Nero 31); Tacitus describes landscapes contrived by its architects Severus and Celer (15.42); Martial, under the Flavians who built over it, says one house had filled the city (On the Spectacles 2). A wing survives under Trajan's baths; Roman painting is module 10.3. Champlin reads it as public in spirit, a seaside villa brought into the city for the people.

Nero: conspiracy, the Greek tour and the end

In 62 Burrus died, Tigellinus took the guard, Seneca withdrew, Octavia was divorced and killed, and the treason law returned (Annals 14.48–64). In 65 a conspiracy around Gaius Calpurnius Piso was betrayed; the guard tribune Subrius Flavus told Nero he had begun to hate him when he became the murderer of his mother and wife, a charioteer, an actor and an arsonist (15.67). Seneca was ordered to die (15.60–64; deep dive module 13.5), and the poet Lucan too (15.70; module 10.2). In 66 Thrasea followed (16.21–35), and there the Annals break off.

In 66–67 Nero toured Greece, competing at festivals crowded into one year; at Olympia he fell from his chariot and was crowned anyway (Suetonius, Nero 23–24). At the Isthmus he proclaimed the province free (IG VII 2713), a gift Vespasian revoked (Greek module 7.4). The general Corbulo, summoned to Greece and ordered to die, said only that he deserved it (Dio 63.17.5–6).

In March 68 Vindex, governor of Gallia Lugdunensis, rose, and Galba in Spain answered his call (Suetonius, Galba 9–10); module 7.2 begins with Galba's reign. The guard went over to Galba, and the Senate declared Nero a public enemy (Nero 49.2) and recognised Galba (Plutarch, Galba 7.1). Nero fled to a freedman's villa, lamented what an artist was dying in him, and with his secretary's help stabbed himself in June 68 (49). Flowers were laid on his tomb for years, and false Neros appeared in the East (57). Champlin's Nero starts from that afterlife: a showman whose popularity with the plebs and the Greeks the senatorial tradition buried.

When the princeps is bad

The Principate had no way to remove a bad emperor. His powers were voted for life, and the Senate could act only when armed men had decided, as it did against Nero once the guard deserted him. Gaius died by his guard officers, Claudius perhaps by his wife, Nero when governors and guard abandoned him; none of it touched the system. Government continued: Britain was conquered under Claudius, Corbulo fought for Armenia under Nero, and Tacitus wrote that there can be great men even under bad emperors (Agricola 42.4). "Bad" was also a senator's measure, applied to emperors who killed senators, while the plebs mourned Nero. Tacitus gives a Roman general the provincial lesson: bear the greed of rulers as you bear barren years and floods (Histories 4.74). He gives Galba the dynastic one, adoption of the best man in place of inheritance (Histories 1.16), the remedy module 7.3 follows. Winterling's Gaius suggests a structural answer: the Principate ran on the pretence that the monarch was a citizen, and the emperors remembered as monsters were those who stopped pretending.

Self-test

  1. What had Tiberius done before the Senate debated his position in AD 14, and how do Velleius and Tacitus read his hesitation?
    Answer

    He had given the watchword to the praetorians as imperator and written to the armies as princeps (Annals 1.7.5), so the vote ratified power already held. Velleius, his officer and admirer, takes the reluctance as sincere (2.124.2); Tacitus reads it as a mask (1.11–13).

  2. Why did Tiberius recall Germanicus in 16, and what does the Senate's decree on Piso add to the story of his death?
    Answer

    Tiberius wrote that Germany could be left to its quarrels (Annals 2.26), after heavy losses at the pontes longi and in the North Sea storm. The decree of December 20 condemns Piso for leaving his province and trying to retake it by force, names Piso as the cause of the death only in Germanicus' own testimony, and records Livia's protection of Plancina, matching Tacitus' admission that the poisoning charge failed (3.14.1, 3.17).

  3. How did Sejanus rise and fall, and why is his motive uncertain?
    Answer

    As prefect he concentrated the guard in one camp (Annals 4.2), induced Tiberius to withdraw to Capri, and was destroyed by a letter read in the Senate on 18 October 31 after the guard had passed to Macro (Dio 58.9–11). Tacitus' account is lost; Tiberius' memoir (Suetonius, Tiberius 61.1), Josephus' story of Antonia's warning and Dio give different motives, and Champlin sees a credible successor rather than a usurper.

  4. How did a treason trial work under Tiberius, and what does the case of Cremutius Cordus show?
    Answer

    Maiestas now covered words and writings (Annals 1.72.3), and with no public prosecutor informers brought charges for a share of the estate (4.30). Cordus, accused in 25 of praising Brutus and Cassius, starved himself and his books were burned; Tacitus comments that punishing talent increases its authority (4.34–35).

  5. Where do Philo, Dio and Suetonius place the change in Gaius, and how do Barrett and Winterling explain his conduct?
    Answer

    Philo in the illness of autumn 37 (Embassy to Gaius 14–21), Dio in the quarrel with the Senate in 39 (59.16); Suetonius gives no date but turns to the "monster" at Caligula 22.1 and blames disease and a potion (50). Barrett finds a sane autocrat without moral responsibility; Winterling a rational ruler who exposed the Senate's double-speak by taking its flattery literally.

  6. What happened to the Jews of Alexandria in 38, and what did Claudius' letter of 41 decide?
    Answer

    Images of Gaius were set up in the synagogues and Flaccus declared the Jews aliens, confining them to one quarter amid killing and looting (Philo, Against Flaccus 25–72). Claudius' letter (P.Lond. 1912) told the Greeks to respect Jewish worship and the Jews to seek no more in a city not their own, which contradicts Josephus' version of his edict (Jewish Antiquities 19.280–285).

  7. How did Claudius become emperor, and what precedent did it set?
    Answer

    Soldiers found him in the palace after Gaius' murder and took him to the praetorian camp, where he promised a donative (15,000 sesterces a man in Suetonius, Claudius 10.4; 5,000 drachmas in Josephus, Jewish Antiquities 19.247) while the Senate debated liberty. The Senate then voted him the powers: the guard could make an emperor and the Senate would ratify.

  8. What were the arguments of Claudius' speech on the Gallic nobles, and how did Tacitus rework them?
    Answer

    The bronze argues from history: Rome's kings were outsiders, its constitution always changed, Augustus and Tiberius admitted provincial elites, and Gaul had been loyal a hundred years (CIL XIII 1668). Tacitus compresses this, adds Athens and Sparta, ruined by excluding the conquered, and ends that today's innovation will become precedent (Annals 11.24).

  9. What does Seneca's Apocolocyntosis accuse Claudius of, and why is its authorship awkward?
    Answer

    Of rule by freedmen and wives, of judging without a hearing, and of killing senators and kin, which Augustus lists in the gods' debate (10–11); he is convicted unheard and made a freedman's law clerk (14–15). Seneca also wrote the funeral speech in Claudius' praise that made the Senate laugh (Annals 13.3).

  10. Who blames Nero for the fire of 64, who does not, and where do Champlin and Barrett stand?
    Answer

    Suetonius (Nero 38), Dio (62.16–18) and Pliny the Elder (Natural History 17.5) blame him; Tacitus says writers gave both chance and malice (Annals 15.38.1). Champlin (2003) argues for Nero's guilt; Barrett (2020) thinks the origin undetermined and the guilt mostly legend.

Carry forward

  1. The Senate voted the powers, but the guard, the armies and the family chose the man: Claudius was made in the praetorian camp and Nero unmade by governors and the guard, and every succession after 68 turns on the same fact.
  2. Tacitus, Suetonius and Dio wrote from the Senate's injuries, generations later; documents (the Piso decree, the Lyon tablet, Claudius' letter), contemporaries (Velleius, Philo, Seneca) and the modern revisions (Barrett, Champlin, Winterling) show competence, popularity or calculation behind the monsters, so read every portrait for whom the emperor harmed.
  3. A bad princeps could be removed only by murder or rebellion, yet the empire kept being governed; the age's answers, Seneca's education of the prince, Tacitus' endurance as of bad weather and Galba's choice by adoption, frame the politics of the next century.
