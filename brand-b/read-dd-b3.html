1 · Karpathy's documented practice

1.0 · "The Karpathy method" is a label others made

Karpathy has no method by that name. The phrase appears in titles by third parties, among them "Stop Prompting Claude. Use Karpathy's Method Instead." (SozAI transcript page) and "The Karpathy Loop: AI Agents Running 700 Experiments Autonomously" (Noqta); titles as listed in search results, 29 October 2026. The most-shared artefact under his name, a CLAUDE.md of four coding rules, was written by the developer Forrest Chang, who distilled it from a Karpathy post of 26 January 2026; the reporting article says Karpathy has not explicitly endorsed it, though it adds that he has shared the project (Pillitteri, 4 May 2026). One blog cites an "AISN 2026" talk; a search for that name on 29 October 2026 returned no record of any such talk, so nothing attributed to it is used here.

The rule for the rest of the section: a practice is attributed to him only where his own post, talk, repository or interview states it.

1.1 · Vibe coding (February 2025), and what he later separated from it

The post. On 2 February 2025 (date from the status number; Willison's article gives "February 6th" without a year) Karpathy posted: "There's a new kind of coding I call 'vibe coding', where you fully give in to the vibes, embrace exponentials, and forget that the code even exists." He described the practice in his own terms: "I 'Accept All' always, I don't read the diffs anymore"; error messages pasted back "with no comment"; "The code grows beyond my usual comprehension"; and the scope: "It's not too bad for throwaway weekend projects" (post quoted in full by Simon Willison, 19 Mar 2025; X original at status 1886192184808149383, not opened). Grade: stated opinion about his own weekend practice.

His own test of it. On 27 April 2025 he wrote up building a small web app this way: "I did not write any code directly; 100% of the code was written by Cursor+Claude." The model "kept hallucinating deprecated APIs, model names, and input/output conventions", most of his time went on configuration and deployment, and his verdict was that "vibe coding full web apps today is kind of messy and not a good idea for anything of actual importance" (Karpathy, Vibe coding MenuGen, 27 Apr 2025). Grade: documented practice, one project.

The later distinction. The LLM-generated summary of his Sequoia AI Ascent talk that he posted on 30 April 2026 separates two things. Vibe coding "raises the floor" by letting almost anyone build software, and he says "Vibe coding is fine for prototypes and personal tools." Agentic engineering is the professional discipline of coordinating fallible agents while preserving "correctness, security, taste, and maintainability"; it "raises the ceiling" (Karpathy, Sequoia Ascent 2026 summary, 30 Apr 2026). Several outlets date a renaming to a post on the term's first anniversary, around 8 February 2026 (SD Times, 6 May 2026); none quotes or links his words, and the post could not be found, so the distinction is sourced here to the April blog post only.

Transfer to his work. Vibe coding transfers to throwaway scripts: a one-off parser, a test of an idea. It does not transfer to anything filed in the vault or published on Academy, by Karpathy's own verdict ("not a good idea for anything of actual importance") and by the sourcing standard, which requires every claim checked before filing.

1.2 · "How I use LLMs" (February 2025)

A two-hour video on his own channel, published 27 February 2025 (YouTube, How I use LLMs). Its chapter list, from the video's description, covers choosing a model and pricing tier, thinking models and when to use them, search, deep research, file uploads, the Python interpreter, Claude Artifacts, Cursor, voice, images, memory and custom instructions, and custom GPTs. A transcript could not be retrieved, so nothing he says inside the video is used here. It stays out.

What the chapter list does show is the order he teaches in: pick the model and the tier, then decide whether the task needs thinking, then add tools (search, files, code) one at a time. That is B2's routing logic, a year earlier and for a general audience. Grade: documented practice (a recorded demonstration), content unverified here. Transfer: nothing new beyond B2.

1.3 · Software 3.0 at YC AI Startup School (June 2025)

Talk given in San Francisco on 17 June 2025, published by Y Combinator on 18 June (YouTube, Andrej Karpathy: Software Is Changing (Again)). Quotations below come from a third-party automatic transcription of that video (billmill.org transcription), labelled as such.

  • The autonomy slider. In Cursor the user can go from tab completion to whole-repository changes, and "you are in charge of the autonomy slider"; Perplexity runs from quick search to deep research; products should let users "slide that autonomy slider" over time, a lesson he draws from Tesla Autopilot. His summary: "less Iron Man robots and more Iron Man suits."
  • The generation–verification loop. "usually they are doing the generation and we as humans are doing the verification", so it is in the user's interest to make that loop fast; visual review (a GUI) is faster than reading text.
  • The leash. "we have to keep the AI on the leash": oversized changes defeat verification, and narrower, concrete prompts make it more likely to succeed. His own habit: "I always go in small incremental chunks."
  • The context window as the lever. LLMs lack the consolidation by which a human colleague builds expertise, so "context windows are really kind of like working memory", and the developer programs that memory directly.

Grade: stated opinion from long engineering experience; the Autopilot analogy is documented practice from his earlier job, applied by analogy. Transfer: all four transfer to his work. The slider is a decision he makes per task (B2 routes model and effort; this adds how much to delegate). The verification loop is his audit system. The leash is B3's one-artefact-per-prompt. The working-memory point is B1 §1.

1.4 · Context engineering over prompt engineering (June 2025)

On 25 June 2025 he posted: "+1 for 'context engineering' over 'prompt engineering'." People associate prompts with short task descriptions; in "every industrial-strength LLM app, context engineering is the delicate art and science of filling the context window with just the right information for the next step", which involves "task descriptions and explanations, few shot examples, RAG, related (possibly multimodal) data, tools, state and history, compacting" (post as quoted by Simon Willison, 27 Jun 2025; X original at status 1937902205765607626, not opened). He was agreeing with Tobi Lütke, who had written six days earlier that the term "describes the core skill better" (same page). Grade: stated opinion. Transfer: complete; B4 is built on it.

1.5 · Dwarkesh Patel (October 2025), then the turn to agents (January 2026)

October 2025: agents "not net useful" on his own repository. In the interview published 17 October 2025, Karpathy described three ways people work with code: writing from scratch, autocomplete, and agents. Autocomplete was "my sweet spot", because typing the start of what you want is "a very high information bandwidth to specify what you want." On nanochat, agents failed: it is "a fairly unique repository", the models "kept misunderstanding the code because they have too much memory" of common internet patterns, kept inserting a library wrapper he had deliberately avoided, and were "way too over-defensive"; his verdict was "It's just not net useful." Agents did well on boilerplate and on porting his tokenizer to Rust, where he had a Python version he understood and tests, which made him feel "safer." He framed the period as "the decade of agents" (Dwarkesh Podcast, Andrej Karpathy, 17 Oct 2025). Grade: documented practice, one codebase, his own account.

December 2025: the threshold. In his year-end review he called Claude Code "the first convincing demonstration of what an LLM Agent looks like" (Karpathy, 2025 LLM Year in Review, 19 Dec 2025).

January 2026: about 80% agent-driven. His post of 26 January 2026 (X status 2015883857489522876, not opened; quoted by The Decoder, 27 Jan 2026) reported that he went from roughly 80% manual coding with 20% agent use in November to about 80% agent use with 20% manual edits in December, calling it, in the article's standfirst quotation, the "biggest change to my basic coding workflow in ~2 decades" and saying "I really am mostly programming in English now." He told readers to "watch them like a hawk", because agents make "subtle conceptual errors that a slightly sloppy, hasty junior dev might do." The failure modes, as The Decoder lists them from the post: wrong assumptions built on without checking; not managing confusion; not seeking clarification; not surfacing inconsistencies; not presenting trade-offs; not pushing back; sycophancy; bloated abstractions; dead code left behind; and constructions of about 1,000 lines that shrink to about 100 when challenged. A second reporter quotes his remedy: "Don't tell it what to do, give it success criteria and watch it go" (Code with Andrea newsletter, 28 Jan 2026). The same newsletter reports, in its own words, that he noticed his manual coding ability declining. On 25 February 2026 he added: "coding agents basically didn't work before December and basically work since" (quoted by Simon Willison, 26 Feb 2026). The 80/20 figures are his own report about his own work. Grade: stated opinion with a self-reported number.

Reviewing as a separate skill. The brief asks for his point that reviewing is a different skill from writing. The Decoder's report of the post carries it only as paraphrase, without quotation: being good at reviewing code does not mean you can still write it, "since generation and discrimination are different abilities in the brain" (The Decoder's wording, not marked as his). An earlier post (4 June 2025, by status number) makes the point in GAN terms: creation has two modes, generation and discrimination; LLMs "have collapsed (1) to ~instant, but have done very little to address (2)", and "A person still has to stare at the results and discriminate if they are good" (reproduced in a Telegram channel; X status 1930305209747812559, not opened). This is the weakest-sourced quotation in the unit and is listed as unsure.

Transfer. The failure list transfers to prose with one change of vocabulary: "wrong assumptions built on without checking" is a figure taken from memory in a unit; "bloated abstractions" is padding; "dead code" is a superseded paragraph left in place. "Give it success criteria" is B3's DONE WHEN clause. The 80/20 shift transfers to code only: his course units are already 100% model-written and checked by sampling.

1.6 · nanochat (October 2025) and autoresearch (March 2026)

nanochat. First committed 13 October 2025; "a full-stack implementation of an LLM like ChatGPT in a single, clean, minimal, hackable, dependency-lite codebase", about 8,000 lines in 45 files, small enough that he recommends packaging the whole repository into one file and pasting it into an LLM to ask questions (nanochat, initial README, commit 3a5e0bc). The current README states its contribution policy: "When submitting a PR, please declare any parts that had substantial LLM contribution and that you have not written or that you do not fully understand" (nanochat README, read at the commit of 3 July 2026). Grade: documented practice. Transfer: two habits. A corpus sized to fit one window can be asked questions whole; disclosure of what one did not write or does not understand is a rule he could adopt for anything published.

autoresearch. Repository last committed 25 March 2026 (karpathy/autoresearch); launched on X on 7 March 2026 by status number. From its README and program.md:

  • The loop. An agent gets a small, real training setup, "modifies the code, trains for 5 minutes, checks if the result improved, keeps or discards, and repeats."
  • One file the agent may edit. train.py is "the single file the agent edits"; prepare.py, which holds the evaluation, is read-only; no new packages. The human edits only program.md, which the README calls "essentially a super lightweight 'skill'." The stated reason for the single file: "This keeps the scope manageable and diffs reviewable."
  • The five-minute budget. Every run trains for exactly five minutes of wall clock, so results are comparable whatever the agent changes; that gives about 12 experiments an hour and about 100 overnight. The downside, in the README's words: results "become not comparable to other people running on other compute platforms."
  • The keep-or-revert rule. One metric, validation bits per byte. If it improved, the commit stays; "If val_bpb is equal or worse, you git reset back to where you started." A simplicity criterion weighs gains against added complexity: an improvement from deleting code is kept; "A 0.001 val_bpb improvement that adds 20 lines of hacky code? Probably not worth it." A run over ten minutes is killed and reverted.
  • Never stop. The agent is told not to ask whether to continue: "The loop runs until the human interrupts you, period."

The run and its gain. The press reports that his two-day run made about 700 changes and found about 20 improvements that carried over to a larger model (VentureBeat, paraphrasing his X post of 9 March 2026, status 2031135152349524125, not opened). The 700 and the 20 are therefore unverified here. The gain is in his own repository: nanochat's "time to GPT-2" leaderboard records run 5 on 9 March 2026 at 1.80 hours, down from 2.02, and says "all of the improvements that went into this commit came from fully autonomous 'research' done by a private version of autoresearch run on a d12 model"; run 6, from a second round, reached 1.65 hours on 14 March (nanochat, dev/LEADERBOARD.md). That is about 11% faster after round one and 18% after round two (my arithmetic on his figures). Grade: documented practice with a self-reported, inspectable result; one task, one metric, his own leaderboard.

Transfer. The loop needs three things his writing does not have: a scalar metric computed by a script, a run cheap enough to repeat a hundred times, and a single file whose changes can be reverted. Course units fail all three: their quality is judged by a sampled audit run by another model, at Fable prices (B2 §2). His build scripts pass: kit/verify.py and kit/index_check.py give pass or fail, run in seconds, and live in files that git can revert. The loop transfers to the kit; it does not transfer to the writing.

1.7 · LLM Knowledge Bases (April 2026)

The X post is dated 2 April 2026 by status number; VentureBeat dates its report 3 April (VentureBeat, linking status 2039805659525644595, not opened). Two days later he published the method as a gist (created 4 April by the gist page), which was opened (Karpathy, llm-wiki.md, gist created 4 Apr 2026). From the gist:

  • Three layers. Raw sources, "immutable": the LLM reads them and never changes them. The wiki, LLM-written Markdown (summaries, entity and concept pages, comparisons): "The LLM owns this layer entirely." A schema file such as CLAUDE.md or AGENTS.md that sets structure and conventions.
  • Three operations. Ingest: a new source is summarised and the index, related pages and log are updated; "a single source might touch 10-15 wiki pages." Query: the LLM answers from the wiki with citations, and good answers can be filed back as pages. Lint: periodic checks for contradictions, stale claims, orphan pages, missing cross-references and gaps.
  • Navigation by index. An index.md lists every page with a one-line summary; the LLM reads it first. An append-only log.md records ingests, queries and lint passes. At "~100 sources, ~hundreds of pages" this "avoids the need for embedding-based RAG infrastructure."
  • The human's role. The human sources material, directs and asks; the gist says the user "never (or rarely)" writes the wiki directly. It contrasts the approach with retrieval, where "the LLM is rediscovering knowledge from scratch on every question."

The figures the brief names, about 100 articles and 400,000 words, come from the X post as VentureBeat paraphrases it (VentureBeat: "at a scale of ~100 articles and ~400,000 words, the LLM's ability to navigate via summaries and index files" suffices); VentureBeat also quotes him: "You rarely ever write or edit the wiki manually; it's the domain of the LLM." The gist, opened, gives "~100 sources" and "hundreds of pages" and no word count. Grade: documented practice (a shared method file), size self-reported.

Transfer. This is the closest of all his methods to the Learning Project, and the match is partial. The vault is Markdown and canonical; courses-index.md is an index of identifiers; the changelog is a log. The differences are in §4.

1.8 · Verifiability, and Sequoia AI Ascent 2026

Verifiability (November 2025). A task is verifiable when practice on it is "resettable (you can start a new attempt)", "efficient (a lot attempts can be made)" and "rewardable (there is some automated process to reward any specific attempt that was made)". "Software 1.0 easily automates what you can specify. Software 2.0 easily automates what you can verify"; progress is therefore "jagged": fast in math, code and puzzles, slow in creative, strategic and common-sense work (Karpathy, Verifiability, 17 Nov 2025).

Sequoia AI Ascent 2026. The talk video was not opened; the summary he posted was, which he labels "AI generated content below for this talk follows", produced by an LLM from the transcript and his earlier posts, and published under his name with a cleaned-up transcript (Karpathy, Sequoia Ascent 2026 summary, 30 Apr 2026). It repeats that traditional software automates what you can specify while LLMs automate what you can verify, with capability spiking where tasks are verifiable and labs train on them. It gives the line he says he keeps returning to: "You can outsource your thinking, but you can't outsource your understanding." Humans still decide what is worth building, which results are suspicious, and how to direct agents. He describes his own work as delegating "macro actions" (features, refactors, tests), writing specs and plans, reviewing diffs and supplying judgement: "The agents fill in the blanks."

Grade: stated opinion, built on an argument about how models are trained. Transfer: the verifiability test is the most useful single idea in this unit for him, because it says in advance which of his tasks a model will do well and which it will do plausibly. A citation can be checked against its source; a positioning sentence cannot be checked by any script. The first can be delegated with sampling; the second needs his judgement, with the model as drafter.

2 · The other methods with a primary source and a named author

2.1 · Anthropic

Building effective agents (19 December 2024). Erik Schluntz and Barry Zhang separate workflows, "systems where LLMs and tools are orchestrated through predefined code paths", from agents, where "LLMs dynamically direct their own processes and tool usage." The advice is "finding the simplest solution possible, and only increasing complexity when needed"; agents suit "open-ended problems where it's difficult or impossible to predict the required number of steps." Five workflow patterns: prompt chaining, routing, parallelisation, orchestrator-workers, evaluator-optimizer. Three principles: simplicity; transparency, "explicitly showing the agent's planning steps"; and careful tool design (Anthropic Engineering). Written for developers building on the API. Grade: documented practice (from customer work), with no measurement published. Transfer: the patterns transfer by name to his setup. His course is a prompt chain (brief, unit, fresh check, audit); the fresh check is an evaluator-optimizer with him as the loop. The simplicity rule transfers whole.

Effective context engineering for AI agents (29 September 2025). Defines context engineering as "the set of strategies for curating and maintaining the optimal set of tokens" and sets the target as the "smallest possible set of high-signal tokens"; recommends just-in-time loading by "lightweight identifiers (file paths, stored queries, web links, etc.)", compaction, structured note-taking, and sub-agents with "clean context windows" returning condensed summaries of about 1,000 to 2,000 tokens (Anthropic Engineering). The post cites no benchmark; its multi-agent claim ("substantial improvement over single-agent systems") has no figure. Grade: documented practice, vendor. B1, B3 and B4 already cite and apply it; nothing new to transfer.

Claude Code best practices (April 2025, and now). The original post, "Claude Code: Best practices for agentic coding", appeared on 18 April 2025 (date as later reports give it; Willison noted it the next day) (Simon Willison's note, 19 Apr 2025); its URL now redirects to the current Claude Code documentation page, so the April 2025 text could not be opened. Willison's note records one recommendation from it: the words "think", "think hard", "think harder" and "ultrathink" escalated the thinking budget. The current page, read on 29 October 2026, retires most of that advice in a table headed "What you can stop doing": "think hard", "think harder" and "be thorough" "are ordinary prompt text that nudges Claude within its effort setting rather than keywords that raise it", while "ultrathink still works: it asks for deeper reasoning on that single turn without touching your effort setting", and the old rule "The context window is your main constraint, manage it aggressively" is retired because "Most current models have a 1M-token window" (Claude Code docs, Best practices). What the current page keeps:

  • "Give Claude something it can run that comes back pass or fail. Once it has that, it can keep going on its own and you don't have to watch."
  • An independent reviewer: a second session "reviews … without having watched it being written."
  • A brainstorm-to-spec flow: ask Claude to interview you until it can write SPEC.md, then "start a fresh session to build from it."
  • After two failed corrections, "/clear plus one new prompt that folds in what you learned works better almost every time."
  • Five questions before an unattended run: does it know what done looks like; can it check its own work; what may it touch; what stops it; where does the result go and who signs off.

Written for developers in Claude Code. Grade: documented practice, vendor; no measurements. Transfer: the reviewer, the pass/fail check, the fresh session after two misses and the five questions all transfer; the 1M-window retirement does not, because his claude.ai window is 200K (B1 §1).

2.2 · OpenAI

A practical guide to building agents (April 2025). A 32-page PDF reported on 17 April 2025 (Maginative); the PDF shows no date (OpenAI PDF). It reserves agents for workflows with complex decisions, rules that are hard to maintain, or heavy unstructured data; recommends to "maximize a single agent's capabilities first"; to "build your agent prototype with the most capable model for every task to establish a performance baseline", then try smaller models; to "Set up evals to establish a performance baseline"; to layer guardrails because "a single one is unlikely to provide sufficient protection"; and calls "Human intervention … a critical safeguard" for high-risk actions. Written for product teams. Grade: documented practice, vendor. Transfer: "most capable model first, then step down" is the opposite order to B2's cost-first routing for small tasks and the same order for units; it belongs to DD-B1.

Model prompting guides. The GPT-4.1 guide (14 April 2025, Noah MacCallum and Julian Lee) says the model "is trained to follow instructions more closely and more literally than its predecessors"; three reminders in agent prompts (persistence, tool use, planning) "increased our internal SWE-bench Verified score by close to 20%", and "inducing explicit planning increased the pass rate by 4%"; for long inputs, "place your instructions at both the beginning and end of the provided context" (OpenAI Cookbook, page marked archived). Vendor data, internal benchmark, no method published. B3 §2 and §5 already set OpenAI's later reasoning-model advice against Anthropic's; the one new transferable item is literalness, which B3 §4 already covers for Claude ("The model takes the verb literally").

2.3 · Simon Willison

Vibe coding against AI-assisted programming (19 March 2025). Vibe coding is "building software with an LLM without reviewing the code it writes", and "Vibe coding is not the same thing as writing code with the help of LLMs!" His rule: "I won't commit any code to my repository if I couldn't explain exactly what it does to somebody else." Vibe coding is fine where stakes are low; ask "how much harm the code you are writing could cause if it has bugs or security vulnerabilities" (Willison). Grade: stated opinion. Transfer: the commit rule transfers to filing: a unit is filed only if he could explain each claim's source.

How he uses LLMs to write code (11 March 2025). Headed points include: account for training cut-off dates; "Most of the craft of getting good results out of an LLM comes down to managing its context"; ask for options in research; for production code, "I treat it like a digital intern, hired to type code for me based on my detailed instructions"; and "the one thing you absolutely cannot outsource to the machine is testing that the code actually works" (Willison). Grade: documented practice (his public projects), stated as opinion. Transfer: cut-off dates are B1 §4; "testing" maps to his audits.

The lethal trifecta (16 June 2025). An agent is exposed to data theft when it combines "Access to your private data", "Exposure to untrusted content" and "The ability to externally communicate"; the safe course is to "avoid that lethal trifecta combination entirely", because guardrail products that catch "95% of attacks" are "very much a failing grade" in security (Willison). Grade: stated opinion. Transfer: direct, and it collides with his setup (§4).

2.4 · Ethan Mollick

Four principles (Co-Intelligence, Portfolio, 2 April 2024). Publisher and date from Penguin Random House. The book was not opened; the principles are quoted from a reader's notes (Arango, reading notes): "Always invite it to the table"; "Be the human in the loop: assume AI will need supervision and guidance; provide it"; "Treat AI like a person, but tell it what kind of person it is"; "Assume this is the worst AI you'll ever use." Written for a general business audience. Grade: stated opinion. Transfer: principle 2 transfers and is already his method. Principle 3 meets B3 §1's evidence: personas in system prompts did not improve factual accuracy across 2,410 questions (Zheng et al., EMNLP Findings 2024, cited there); its documented effect is on register. Principle 1, applied to a 5–7 hour week, collides with B2's cost routing; he leaves it.

The jagged frontier field experiment (Dell'Acqua et al., September 2023). A preregistered experiment with 758 Boston Consulting Group consultants, assigned to no AI, GPT-4, or GPT-4 with a prompt-engineering overview. On tasks inside the frontier, AI users completed "12.2% more tasks" and finished "25.1% more quickly", with higher quality; on a task chosen to sit outside it, "consultants using AI were 19 percentage points less likely to produce correct solutions" (HBS Working Paper 24-013; percentage-point wording from the SSRN abstract, posted 18 Sep 2023). Mollick's own write-up gives the quality gain as 40% and the outside-frontier correctness as 84% without AI against 60–70% with it (One Useful Thing, 16 Sep 2023). The paper names two working styles: "Centaurs", with a strategic division of labour between human and AI tasks, and "Cyborgs", "completely integrating their task flow with the AI" (SSRN abstract). The HBS page lists the paper as forthcoming in Organization Science. Grade: measured, GPT-4 in 2023, consulting tasks. Transfer: the mechanism transfers; the size does not. Where a task sits outside the frontier, the model's answer is persuasive and wrong, and using it makes a skilled person less accurate. That is Karpathy's jaggedness measured on people.

2.5 · Spec-driven development, as practised

Harper Reed (16 February 2025). Three steps: hone the idea with a chat model that asks "one question at a time" until it can write spec.md; have a reasoning model turn the spec into a plan of small, test-driven prompts (prompt_plan.md) and a checklist (todo.md) that keeps state across sessions; then execute prompt by prompt. "The success really depends on how well step 2 went." His caveats: "This is working well NOW, it will probably not work in 2 weeks, or it will work twice as well"; the workflow is "largely a solo endeavor" (Reed, My LLM codegen workflow atm). Grade: documented practice, anecdote.

GitHub Spec Kit (2 September 2025). 2.6 · The prompting literature, and its limits

  • Chain-of-thought (Wei et al., arXiv 2201.11903, first posted 28 January 2022). Including worked reasoning steps as examples improved large models on arithmetic, commonsense and symbolic reasoning; a 540B-parameter model with eight such examples reached state of the art on GSM8K (arXiv). Measured, 2022 models.
  • ReAct (Yao et al., ICLR 2023, first posted 6 October 2022). Interleaving reasoning with actions (here, Wikipedia lookups) reduced hallucination against chain-of-thought on HotpotQA and FEVER, and beat imitation and reinforcement-learning baselines on ALFWorld and WebShop by 34 and 10 absolute points of success rate (arXiv). Measured.
  • Self-consistency (Wang et al., ICLR 2023, first posted 21 March 2022). Sample many reasoning paths and keep the most consistent answer; gains over chain-of-thought of 17.9 points on GSM8K, 11.0 on SVAMP, 12.2 on AQuA (arXiv). Measured.
  • Where chain-of-thought does not help (Sprague et al., arXiv 2409.12183, v3 May 2025). A meta-analysis of over 100 papers and an evaluation of 20 datasets on 14 models: chain-of-thought helps "primarily on tasks involving math or logic"; on MMLU, answering directly is nearly as accurate unless the question or answer contains an equals sign (arXiv). Measured.

What survives for him. Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.1 think adaptively and always (B2 §7), so writing "think step by step" into a prompt asks for what the model already does, and Anthropic's current Claude Code page says such phrases do not raise effort. ReAct is how a search-enabled chat already works. Self-consistency survives as a verification habit: for a figure or a verdict, two independent runs that agree are stronger than one (B1 §5, "one good answer is one draw"). Sprague et al. place his main work, long-form synthesis and judgement, outside the tasks where explicit reasoning prompts measurably help.

  • The fresh checker in a chat that did not see the writing (method.md §5). Claude Code's independent second session; Anthropic's evaluator-optimizer; Karpathy's generation–verification split.

  • The vault, the index and the changelog. Karpathy's wiki, index.md and log.md; Anthropic's structured note-taking; Harper Reed's todo.md.

Collisions with his setup as B1 to B4 and A1 describe it

  1. Retrieval against index navigation. Karpathy's pattern navigates by index and avoids embedding retrieval at about a hundred sources. B4 F1 measured his Project knowledge at 907,260 characters, above the 200K window, so in retrieval mode by the documented rule: the arrangement the pattern exists to avoid. B4's fix (five files in the knowledge, everything else read by path after cloning) moves him onto the pattern.
  2. "The LLM owns the wiki" against "failures stay in the record" and exact-replacement errata. In the gist, lint may rewrite pages. His method forbids it: auditors "never edit a unit"; they report and errata are exact replacements checked one by one (method.md §6). A lint pass in his vault reports and never edits a filed unit.
  3. Model-written memory against Markdown he can read. Karpathy's knowledge lives in files the human can open. claude.ai memory is notes a model writes and re-inserts, whose amount per chat is undocumented (B1 §7, B4 §1). The durable record of facts about the course belongs in the vault; memory is a convenience layer.
  4. "NEVER STOP" against the audit gate and 5–7 hours a week. autoresearch runs until interrupted. His method stops at every fourth unit for an audit (method.md §3), and Fable runs cost credits at API rates on Pro (B2 §2). An unattended loop that writes units would breach the gate and the budget.
  5. The retired 1M-window advice. Claude Code's page retires context-window management because current models have 1M tokens. On claude.ai he has 200K (B1 §1). The retirement applies to his Claude Code sessions, not to his Project chats.
  6. The lethal trifecta in one window. His account has memory and a Gmail connector (private data), web search (untrusted content), and connectors that can send mail or publish (B4 F14 lists Gmail, Google Drive, Vercel and Claude in Chrome). B4 recommended loading connectors on demand to save tokens; on Willison's argument the stronger reason is that a research chat that reads web pages should not also hold a tool that can send.
  7. "Always invite it to the table" against the routing table. B2 routes small tasks to small models in light chats to protect his limits; Mollick's principle, written for exploration, would spend them. He leaves it.
  8. Persona prompting. Mollick's principle 3 and B3 §1: personas set register and showed no accuracy gain in the one large test cited there. His templates keep a READER line and no persona.
  9. Interview-me specs against one question per response. Harper Reed and Claude Code both start with the model asking questions until a spec exists. His saved preferences allow one question per response, only when blocked (B3 §4). An interview has to be requested explicitly in the opening line, which then makes it the task.

B4 (written 29 October 2026). What it should take: the knowledge-base pattern (raw sources, compiled Markdown, lint, query by index) as the model for his vault and Project memory. DD-B1 (Agents; DD-B1 owns agents). B · Operating rules

Ten rules, each traced to its card. Written to be pasted into his way-of-working file.

How I work with AI (from DD-B3, 29 Oct 2026)

  1. Delegate what can be checked. Before handing a task to a model, I ask whether something other than my own reading can check the result: a script, a source, or a fresh model. If yes, the model does it and I sample. If no (positioning, strategy, what to cut), the model drafts and I decide. (K10, M2)
  2. Every brief ends with how to tell it is done. Success criteria a stranger could check, never a script of steps. (K6, A3, S2)
  3. The writer never checks its own work. Checks run in a chat or agent that did not see the writing. (A3, A1, K4)
  4. I file only what I could explain. For every claim in a unit or page I publish, I could say where it comes from; anything a model wrote that I do not understand is disclosed or cut. (W1, K7, K10)
  5. Vibe coding is for scripts I will delete. Nothing produced without reading goes into the vault, the kit or Academy. (K1, K2)
  6. Small pieces, one at a time. One artefact per chat; after two failed corrections, a new chat with a rewritten brief. (K4, A3)
  7. Unattended runs have four guards. One file or folder they may change, a fixed time or spend cap, a pass/fail check, and keep-or-revert; I approve the result. Never on course units. (K8, A3, O1)
  8. The vault is the memory. Markdown in the repo is the source of truth; indexes carry one line per unit; lint reports and never edits a filed unit; an answer worth keeping becomes a file. (K9)
  9. Never the trifecta. No chat holds my private data, untrusted web content and a tool that can send or publish at the same time. (W3)
  10. No "think harder" in prompts. Effort is set in the model menu; for a figure or verdict that matters, I run it twice independently and compare. (P1, P3)
