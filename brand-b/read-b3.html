01The four parts of a brief that survives

A brief survives when someone with no context can act on it without coming back with a question. Anthropic's prompting guide states the test in those terms. It asks the user to think of Claude as "a brilliant but new employee who lacks context on your norms and workflows", and gives a check to run before sending: "Show your prompt to a colleague with minimal context on the task and ask them to follow it. If they'd be confused, Claude will be too." (Anthropic, Prompting best practices; vendor guidance). Between them, the three vendors' guides read for this unit cover all four parts below, each in its own words and with its own emphasis; none of them presents the four as a set.

Who the reader is

Two different things travel under the word "role". One is a persona given to the model ("You are an expert editor"). Anthropic says a role set in the system prompt "focuses Claude's behavior and tone for your use case" and that a single sentence makes a difference (same page; vendor guidance). Measured on accuracy, personas did nothing: Zheng and colleagues gave four families of models 162 roles across 2,410 factual questions and found that a persona in the system prompt did not improve performance against a control with none; choosing the best persona per question helped, but picking it automatically did little better than chance (Zheng et al., Findings of EMNLP 2024). The two sources measured different things, tone and factual accuracy, so they do not contradict each other. Together they place a persona's documented effect in register. Neither shows it making answers more correct.

The other meaning is the reader of the output: who will read it, what they already know, and what they will do with it. Anthropic's blog lists "Explaining the purpose or audience for the output" among the cases where context improves results (Claude blog, Best practices for prompt engineering for 2026, 10 Nov 2025; vendor guidance). OpenAI's help-centre article asks the user to "Be specific, descriptive and as detailed as possible about the desired context, outcome, length, format, style, etc" (OpenAI Help Center, Best practices for prompt engineering with the OpenAI API; vendor guidance). Anthropic gives the mechanism: an instruction that carries its reason lets the model apply the reason to cases the instruction did not name. Its example replaces "NEVER use ellipses" with the reason (the output will be read by a text-to-speech engine that cannot pronounce them) and adds that "Claude can generalize from the explanation" (Prompting best practices). A reader line is that kind of reason, given once for the whole output. His course briefs already carry one: every Brand + AI brief has a Reader line drawn from reader.md (method.md §4, Brief template).

What the output is

The second part names the artefact, its form and where it will live. Anthropic: "Be specific about the output format and constraints you want" (Prompting best practices). OpenAI: "Articulate the desired output format through examples" (OpenAI Help Center). §3 turns this part into a contract.

Constraints

Constraints are the rules the output must satisfy: what it must contain, what it must leave out, its tone and its limits. Two pieces of vendor guidance shape how they are written.

State the positive. Anthropic: "Tell Claude what to do instead of what not to do", replacing "Do not use markdown in your response" with "Your response should be composed of smoothly flowing prose paragraphs." (Prompting best practices). OpenAI says the same: "Instead of just saying what not to do, say what to do instead" (OpenAI Help Center).

When a prohibition is needed, name the pattern. Anthropic's guide to Opus 5.5 says that a general instruction such as "avoid a generic AI look" "mostly swaps one default for another", and that the model "responds well to instructions that name specific patterns to avoid" (Anthropic, Prompting Claude Opus 5.5; vendor guidance, written about frontend design). Applying it to prose ("no 'I hope this finds you well'" in place of "avoid corporate tone") is my extension.

Older research gives a reason to be wary of bare negatives. Jang, Ye and Seo found that on tasks phrased with negation, language models performed worse as they grew larger, the reverse of the usual scaling trend (Jang et al., Transfer Learning for NLP Workshop, PMLR 203, 2023). The paper was first posted in September 2022 and tested models of that period; it says nothing measured about current Claude models. It supports one habit only: pair every "do not" with the "do" that replaces it.

Examples, including negative ones

The vendors agree that examples help and disagree on how many.

  • Anthropic's documentation: "Include 3–5 examples for best results", each relevant, diverse and wrapped in <example> tags so the model can tell them from instructions (Prompting best practices).
  • Anthropic's blog: "Start with one example (one-shot)", adding more only if the output still misses (Claude blog, 10 Nov 2025).
  • Google: "We recommend to always include few-shot examples in your prompts", with two warnings: "if you include too many examples, the model may start to overfit the response to the examples", and the examples should share one structure and format (Google, Prompt design strategies, updated 13 Oct 2026).
  • Anthropic's engineering team warns against stuffing "a laundry list of edge cases" into a prompt and recommends instead to "curate a set of diverse, canonical examples" (Anthropic, Effective context engineering for AI agents, 29 Sep 2025).

All four are vendor guidance. The range runs from one example to five, and the deciding variable in all of them is whether the output matches.

Research says what examples teach. Min and colleagues found that randomly replacing the labels in demonstrations "barely hurts performance" on classification and multiple-choice tasks, consistently over 12 models including GPT-3; what drove the effect was the label space, the distribution of the input text and the format of the sequence (Min et al., EMNLP 2022). On those tasks a demonstration worked mainly by showing the shape of an answer. My reading for his use: choose an example for its shape (length, structure, register), and expect the shape to be copied, which is Google's overfitting warning seen from the other side.

Negative examples have thin evidence. None of the vendor pages read for this unit advises putting a labelled bad example into a prompt. Anthropic's Fable 5.1 guide recommends the positive form with a reason attached: add "one complete example of a correct response" with "a sentence explaining why the response is correct" (Anthropic, Prompting Claude Fable 5.1; vendor guidance, written about quoting retrieved sources). One preprint tested the negative form directly: contrastive chain-of-thought gave models valid and invalid reasoning demonstrations side by side and reported gains on reasoning benchmarks; the abstract gives no figure and no venue was found (Chia et al., arXiv 2311.09277, 2023; preprint). The rule the library follows is my synthesis of these: a bad example appears only beside a good one, labelled as wrong, with the specific pattern named and the reason it fails.

02Why specificity beats length

Length is a cost, and specificity is what the cost buys. Anthropic's blog lists among common mistakes: "Longer, more complex prompts are NOT always better." (Claude blog, 10 Nov 2025). Its engineering team frames the goal as "finding the smallest possible set of high-signal tokens" and adds that "minimal does not necessarily mean short" (Effective context engineering). The same post describes two ways to miss: engineers "hardcoding complex, brittle logic" at one extreme, "vague, high-level guidance" at the other (same post). All vendor guidance.

Why extra text costs. B1 §2 sets out the attention budget and the evidence that recall falls as the window fills. Three studies bear on prompts specifically:

  • Levy, Jacoby and Goldberg padded the same reasoning questions with extra text of different lengths, types and positions; reasoning performance degraded "at much shorter input lengths than their technical maximum", in every version of their dataset (Levy et al., ACL 2024).
  • Shi and colleagues built GSM-IC, school arithmetic problems with irrelevant sentences added, and found that performance "is dramatically decreased when irrelevant information is included"; telling the model to ignore irrelevant information was one of the mitigations that helped (Shi et al., ICML 2023; arXiv 2302.00093).
  • Jaroslawicz and colleagues gave 20 models from seven providers up to 500 keyword-inclusion instructions in one report-writing task; the best frontier models reached 68% accuracy at 500, and models showed a bias towards earlier instructions (Jaroslawicz et al., arXiv 2507.11538, 2025; a workshop poster at NeurIPS 2025, not the main track).

These tested models of 2022 to 2025 on tasks unlike his. The direction is the same in all three: material that does not serve the task competes with material that does.

What specificity means. Claude Code's documentation gives the working definition: write instructions "concrete enough to verify", such as "Use 2-space indentation" instead of "Format code properly", and it states that the more specific and concise the instructions, the more consistently Claude follows them (Claude Code docs, Memory; vendor guidance, written for CLAUDE.md files). Anthropic's evaluation guide applies the same test to success criteria: "Instead of 'good performance,' specify 'accurate sentiment classification.'" (Anthropic, Define success criteria and build evaluations). A specific instruction is one the output can be checked against. It can be short. "Keep it concise" cannot be checked; "up to three reasons of twelve words or fewer", the decision format in his saved preferences, can.

Where sources disagree. OpenAI's API guide says that "reasoning models will provide better results on tasks with only high-level guidance", and that this "differs from GPT models, which benefit from very precise instructions" (OpenAI, Prompt engineering). Anthropic's general guide points the other way: "The more precisely you explain what you want, the better the result." (Prompting best practices). Anthropic's Opus 5.5 guide, though, agrees with OpenAI on one point: in chat applications, instructions telling the model to think carefully before answering can be removed, because the model sets its own amount of thinking and effort is the main control (Prompting Claude Opus 5.5). Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.1 think adaptively, always on (B2 §7), which makes them reasoning models in OpenAI's sense; that mapping is mine. My reconciliation: be exact about the output (what it is, who reads it, when it is done), which every source endorses, and leave the route to the model, which is where the reasoning-model guidance points. The templates follow that split: they specify artefacts and checks, and none of them scripts the model's reasoning.

03Output contracts

An output contract is the part of a brief that says what will be handed back. It has three clauses.

Format. Anthropic's guide gives three levers: say what to do; use XML tags as format indicators; and match the prompt's own style to the output wanted, since "removing markdown from your prompt can reduce the volume of markdown in the output" (Prompting best practices; vendor guidance). A format clause names the container as well as the layout: a Markdown file in the vault, a message ready to paste, a table in the chat. §4 shows why the container matters in his setup.

Length rule. Length targets hold for short outputs and fail for long ones. LIFEBench tested 26 widely used models on length instructions from 16 to 8,192 words in English and Chinese and found that "most models reasonably follow short-length instructions but deteriorate sharply beyond a certain threshold"; the preprint adds that almost all fell short of the maximum output lengths their vendors claim (Zhang et al., NeurIPS 2025 Datasets and Benchmarks Track; arXiv 2505.16234). Anthropic adds that its latest models are less verbose and "Might skip detailed summaries for efficiency unless prompted otherwise" (Prompting best practices). So a word limit is a usable contract for a caption, a message or an email. For a long piece the contract that holds is structural: one section per item of the brief, each answered, none padded. His method already says this, in its own words: the topic decides, never pad, never cut substance (method.md §4, Length rule). The structural form is my synthesis of the method and the LIFEBench result.

What done looks like. Anthropic's prompt-engineering overview assumes three things before any prompting starts: "A clear definition of the success criteria for your use case", "Some ways to empirically test against those criteria", and a first draft (Anthropic, Prompt engineering overview). For unattended agents, the Opus 5.5 guide offers the option of stating "the completion condition up front" (Prompting Claude Opus 5.5); the Fable 5.1 guide's suggested system prompt says the request sets the scope and "the scope is the deliverable: don't quietly narrow, widen, or swap it" (Prompting Claude Fable 5.1). All vendor guidance, written for API and agent use; carrying it into chat is my inference. In the library, each template ends with a DONE WHEN list: checks he, or Fable, can run on the output line by line.

One more clause belongs in any contract that touches facts. Anthropic's hallucination guide recommends giving the model explicit permission to say it does not know, asking it to cite a quote or source for each claim, and having it retract any claim it cannot support with a quote (Anthropic, Reduce hallucinations; vendor guidance, no figure given). His method already works this way: every unit hands in an unsure list and a Sources list (B1, filed before the rule was enforced, has none), so unverified claims have a sanctioned place to go, and the checker starts from that list (method.md §4, Before handing in).

04Why his preferences file works, and why one-line asks sometimes do not

The preferences file

His saved preferences are account-wide; Anthropic's help centre says instructions saved there are "applied to all of your conversations with Claude" (Claude Help Center, Understanding Claude's personalization features). B1 §6 places them in the window: text present in every chat, with no documented order of precedence against project instructions. Four features of his file match the guidance in §1 to §3 (the Learning Project's setup as of 28 October 2026):

  1. It is always there. It does not depend on memory writing it down or on retrieval finding it (B1 §3).
  2. Its strongest rules can be checked. Decisions come as a single pick, up to three reasons of twelve words or fewer, and one override line; one question per response, and only when blocked. Each can be verified against the output, which is the Claude Code standard of "concrete enough to verify" (Claude Code docs, Memory), and each sets a short length, the range LIFEBench found models follow (Zhang et al., NeurIPS 2025).
  3. Its prohibitions name patterns. It bans specific openers and praise for ordinary work, by example, which is the form the Opus 5.5 guide says works (Prompting Claude Opus 5.5).
  4. Some rules carry their reason. The format rule says outputs are mobile-first because he reads on a phone, so the model can apply the reason to cases the rule does not list (Prompting best practices, the ellipsis example).

Its weak lines are the ones that cannot be checked: "concise", or sarcasm "when earned", leave the judgement to the model. And it cannot carry the task. No standing file knows who will read today's output, what it is or when it is done.

It also contains one live conflict. The preferences send anything with three or more sections to a dark-theme HTML artifact. A course unit has many sections and must be a Markdown file in the vault (method.md §7), and the Project instructions name its .md path. Claude Code's documentation states that when two instructions contradict each other, "Claude may pick one arbitrarily" (Claude Code docs, Memory); it is written about CLAUDE.md files, and applying it to claude.ai is my analogy, since the help centre states no precedence at all (B1 §6). Until the layers are reconciled, which is B4's job, every template whose output collides with a preference says so in its OUTPUT line.

One-line asks

A one-line ask still arrives in a window holding his preferences and Project instructions. What it lacks is the task's own three parts: the reader, the output and the definition of done. Three documented mechanisms explain what happens next.

  • The model takes the verb literally. Anthropic: "If you say 'can you suggest some changes,' Claude will sometimes provide suggestions rather than implementing them, even if making changes might be what you intended." (Prompting best practices; vendor guidance). The Sonnet 5.5 guide gives the reverse case: an open-ended request "can start building a presentation, report, or video when you only wanted ideas" (Anthropic, Prompting Claude Sonnet 5.5).
  • Gaps get filled with defaults. The Opus 5.5 guide says the model "tends to get to work quickly", and that on loosely specified tasks it helps to tell it to look through the relevant sources before acting (Prompting Claude Opus 5.5; written about multi-app workflows).
  • A brief that arrives in pieces does worse than one complete brief. Laban and colleagues compared the same tasks given fully specified in a single turn against a simulated conversation in which the same instruction arrives in pieces, one per turn, with no corrections from the user. Across more than 200,000 simulated conversations, every top open- and closed-weight model they tested did worse in the multi-turn setting, "an average drop of 39% across six generation tasks"; the models "make assumptions in early turns and prematurely attempt to generate final solutions, on which they overly rely", and when they take a wrong turn "they get lost and do not recover" (Laban et al., ICLR 2026; arXiv 2505.06120). The paper (posted May 2025) tested 15 models, Claude 3.7 Sonnet, GPT-4.1, o3 and Gemini 2.5 Pro among them; none is a current model.

The practical rule from the third finding, which the paper itself gives its users (§7.4: consolidate the requirements into one instruction and start a new conversation): when a first answer misses, rewrite the brief with what the miss revealed and start a new chat, instead of correcting in place. His method's one-chat-per-unit rule (method.md §4) applies the same logic at the scale of a course.

A one-liner is enough when the input fully determines the output and the standing layers already carry tone and format: reformatting, triage, a single lookup. B2 §6 and §8 give the evidence that these are the tasks where model differences stop mattering; that the same tasks tolerate a short prompt is my reading.

05Common failure patterns

Asking for a plan when he wants the thing

The verb of the request sets the artefact (§4, first mechanism). "How should I structure the episode?" asks for advice; "Write the script" asks for the script. His preferences push the model towards acting (no menus, deliver first), but they cannot overturn a request that literally asks for options. Anthropic's guide adds the other half: "If you want Claude to do more than the task strictly requires, explicitly request it rather than relying on the model to infer this from vague prompts." (Prompting best practices). Fix: the first line of every template is a TASK line that opens with the verb of the artefact and names it.

Burying the ask

The ask is buried when it sits in the middle of a message, after background and before pasted material. Sources disagree on where it belongs.

  • OpenAI's help article: "Put instructions at the beginning of the prompt and use ### or """ to separate the instruction and context" (OpenAI Help Center).
  • Anthropic, for inputs of 20,000 tokens or more: place long documents at the top and the query at the end; "Queries at the end can improve response quality by up to 30 percent in tests, especially with complex, multidocument inputs." (Prompting best practices). That figure is vendor data; the page publishes no method, task set or model list behind it.
  • OpenAI's API guide: context is "usually best positioned near the end of your prompt" (OpenAI, Prompt engineering).

The research agrees on one point only: the middle is the weak position. Liu and colleagues found recall worst in the middle of long inputs (B1 §2), and IFScale found a bias towards earlier instructions (Jaroslawicz et al., 2025). The library's layout is my synthesis: a one-line TASK first, material inside tags, and, when the material is long, the TASK repeated as the last line.

Stacking unrelated tasks

"Write the email, fix the calendar, and draft three captions" is three tasks in one window. Google: "Instead of having many instructions in one prompt, create one prompt per instruction", and for multi-step work, "make each step a prompt" (Google, Prompt design strategies; vendor guidance). Anthropic says explicit chaining stays useful "when you need to inspect intermediate outputs or enforce a specific pipeline structure" (Prompting best practices). Three findings above explain the cost: each task's material is irrelevant context for the others (Shi et al.), instruction count degrades adherence (IFScale), and a long mixed window degrades reasoning (Levy et al.). B2 adds a fourth from his own routing table: the three tasks want different models and efforts, and one chat runs them all on one. Fix: one artefact per prompt. Where tasks form a pipeline (journal synthesis, then script, then titles, then email), each step is its own prompt and its output is read before it feeds the next; B5 and B7 build that chain.

06DeliverablePrompt library

Ten templates for his recurring tasks, as of 28 October 2026. Each is usable on its own.

How the templates are built. Every template has the same parts in the same order, each traced to the unit: TASK (one line, the artefact's verb: §5), READER (§1), OUTPUT (the contract: §3), CONSTRAINTS (positive first, prohibitions as named patterns: §1), EXAMPLES (good, and bad only beside good, labelled with why: §1), MATERIAL (pasted inside tags: §5), DONE WHEN (checks on the output: §3), and TASK again as the last line when the material is long (§5).

Conventions.

  • <FILL: …> is a slot filled every time the template is used.
  • <UNKNOWN: …> in a worked example marks a real value this unit does not have. Replace it before sending; never let the model guess it.
  • Tags such as <takes> and </takes> wrap pasted material, so instructions and material cannot be confused (Prompting best practices).
  • The Model line comes from B2's routing table; where B2 has no row, the line says so and the pick is my analogy.
  • One template per chat. If the first output misses, change the brief and open a new chat (§4).
  • The patterns in each avoid-list are starting choices of mine. Extend them from the first outputs, as Anthropic's Opus 5.5 guide advises for named patterns: "check which styles the first result used instead, and extend the list if needed" (Prompting Claude Opus 5.5).
  • Private details stay generic in the templates; they go in only at the moment of use.

The writer's opening note

The evidence in this unit is of three kinds, labelled where it appears. Vendor guidance is a model maker's own documentation on how to prompt its models (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google): it says what the vendor recommends and almost never shows the measurement behind it. Research is peer-reviewed work, or a preprint labelled as one; most of it tested models from 2022 to 2025, and none of it tested Opus 5.5. His setup is the Learning Project as it stood on 28 October 2026: his saved preferences, the Project's method files and his voice-drafting skill. The unit was written with search off; searches were used only to confirm sources and their URLs, checked 28–29 October 2026. What a context window is, which layers put text into it, and why every instruction raises a probability without guaranteeing anything are in B1 (§1, §6). Which model and effort each task gets is B2's routing table. This unit assumes both.
