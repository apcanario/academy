2 · What his plan measures

claude.ai shows him no prices. It meters him against limits, and Anthropic publishes the rules but almost no rates.

Plans and prices (USD; the pricing page shows no euro prices). Pro is $20 a month billed monthly, or $17 a month billed annually ($200 up front). Max is $100 a month for five times Pro's usage per session and $200 for twenty times (Claude, Plans & pricing; Claude Help Center, About Claude's Max plan usage).

How usage is counted. The session limit resets every five hours; paid plans also carry weekly limits, and Anthropic reserves the right to add other caps (Claude Help Center, About Claude's Pro plan usage; Claude, Plans & pricing). Claude Code draws on the same limit as chat (both pages). The only numbers Anthropic gives: about 45 messages per five hours on Pro, defined as about 200 English sentences of 15 to 20 words on a less compute-intensive model; at least 225 on Max 5x and at least 900 on Max 20x for "relatively short" conversations on such a model. The window stays at 200K tokens on every plan (Max usage; Claude Help Center, usage and length limits).

What draws it down. Anthropic's list, dated 22 September 2026: message length, attachment size, current conversation length, tool use (Research, web search), model choice, effort level, artifacts, and multi-step tasks such as running code or browsing. Project content is cached and counts less when reused (Claude Help Center, Usage limit best practices). Higher effort spends more tokens and reaches the limit sooner (Claude Help Center, Change the model, effort and extended thinking settings).

How much each model costs him inside the plan is undocumented. None of these articles gives a per-model rate. The Pro article says only that a less compute-intensive model allows more messages. Fable is the one model with rules of its own (article dated 2 September 2026):

  • On Pro, Fable 5.1 sits outside the plan's limits and runs on usage credits.
  • On Max, up to half of the weekly limit may go to Fable at no extra cost; this is a cap inside the same pool, and Fable uses the pool faster than other Claude models.
  • On Team and seat-based Enterprise, premium seats include Fable; standard seats pay with credits (Claude Help Center, Claude Fable models on your plan).

Usage credits are prepaid and billed at standard API rates once an included limit is used up (Claude Help Center, Manage usage credits for paid Claude plans). Anthropic also raised five-hour limits on Pro, Max, Team and seat-based Enterprise when Opus 5.5 launched, without saying by how much (Anthropic, Introducing Claude Opus 5.5).

The consequence for routing: he pays in two currencies. Inside his limits the currency is a share of a five-hour and a weekly allowance, at an exchange rate per model that Anthropic does not publish. Past his limits, and for every Fable run on Pro, the currency is credits at API prices. That second rule is why this unit uses the API price list as the common measure: it is the only published price that applies to him, and, with credits enabled, it is his literal marginal price whenever he runs over.

Four rules on the same page change what a task costs:

  • Haiku 5.5 has a cliff at 100K. A request whose prompt exceeds 100,000 tokens pays five times the per-token rate (arithmetic on the table), and the prompt count includes cache reads and writes. Every other current model bills its full 1M window at one rate.
  • Batch is half price for every model, for work that can wait.
  • Web search costs $10 per 1,000 searches plus the tokens the results add.
  • Newer tokenizer. Claude 4.7 and later models produce about 30% more tokens for the same text; all four current models are in that group (B1 §2).

Thinking is billed as output. The tokens Claude spends thinking are charged at the output rate, and in full, even when the app shows only a summary or nothing; thinking blocks kept from earlier turns are billed again as input (Anthropic, Steering thinking). The pricing page itself does not mention thinking.

On the price list alone, an output token on Haiku, Sonnet, Opus and Fable costs in the ratio 1 : 20 : 40 : 100 (arithmetic on the table). §5 shows why that ratio misleads.

5 · Cost per task

The cost of a task is input tokens times the input price plus output tokens, thinking included, times the output price, summed over every turn, with the whole history re-sent on each turn (B1 §2; §3 above). The price list fixes one factor. The other, how many tokens a model spends to finish the task, varies by model and by effort, and it can swamp the first.

A small task, by arithmetic. Assume a Shōmen post takes 4,000 input tokens (instructions, brand notes, the brief) and 1,500 output tokens including thinking. Those token counts are my assumption; nobody measured them. At API prices:

Model One post A hundred posts
Haiku 5.5 $0.0012 $0.12
Sonnet 5.5 $0.023 $2.30
Opus 5.5 $0.046 $4.60
Fable 5.1 $0.115 $11.50

On equal token counts the ratios follow the price list. In practice the counts are unequal.

Four readings, all arithmetic on that table:

  1. Price per token predicts little at the top of the effort range. Opus 5.5 costs twice Sonnet 5.5 per token. At medium effort it costs 2.8 times as much per task; at max, Sonnet costs 91% of what Opus costs and scores two points lower. Pushing the cheaper model to maximum effort spends its price advantage.
  2. A small model at full effort can match a large one at low effort. Haiku 5.5 at max scores 43 against Opus 5.5 at low's 42, for 38% of the cost.
  3. A cheaper token can make a dearer task. At current prices, Gemini 3.8 Flash is 2.7 times cheaper per token than Argon and costs 2.2 times more per task, scoring 12 points lower.
  4. Across vendors, the same score has very different prices. Around an index of 50 to 51: GPT-6.1 Sol at high costs $0.32, Opus 5.5 at medium $1.34 (4.2 times Sol), Fable 5.1 at high $3.91 (12 times Sol).

Where sources disagree. Artificial Analysis's model page for Opus 5.5 gives $10.04 per task at max effort and names index v5.0 with nine evaluations (model page); its leaderboard gives $5.98 for the same configuration (leaderboard); its methodology page names v4.3.2 with ten evaluations (methodology). This unit uses the leaderboard because it reports every model and effort level on one page, so the ratios above are at least internally consistent. The absolute dollar figures should be read as approximate.

Anthropic's own cost-per-task claims point the same way and are vendor data: at its default (medium) effort, Opus 5.5 beat Opus 5 at max effort on Terminal-Bench 4.0 for about a fifth of the cost, and beat GPT-6 Astra at max effort on GDPval-AA for about a fifth of the cost per task (Anthropic, Introducing Claude Opus 5.5).

None of these tasks is a course unit, a caption or a calendar entry. What transfers is the mechanism: the bill is set by tokens spent per finished job, and effort moves that number as much as the model does.

One cost no table captures is his own time. A cheap first output that needs a second run, or a correction he has to spot and make, is paid out of 5 to 7 hours a week. No study measures that for him. The deliverable handles it with escalation triggers, so that a model that keeps costing him rework is replaced.

6 · When the cheap model is the right one

Three bodies of evidence say the cheap model is often enough, and they agree on when.

Routing research. FrugalGPT (Chen, Zaharia and Zou, TMLR 2024; first posted on arXiv 2023) found API prices varying by up to two orders of magnitude and reported that a cascade, which learns which models to query for each request, matched GPT-4's performance with up to 98% lower cost (arXiv 2305.05176). RouteLLM (Ong et al., ICLR 2025) trained routers that send each query to a strong or a weak model and cut costs by more than half in some cases without loss of response quality on the benchmarks they tested (ICLR 2025 proceedings). The mechanism in both: for a large share of queries the weak model's answer is already as good as the strong model's, and the saving comes from identifying those queries. Both tested 2023–24 models.

Compute against size. Snell and colleagues compared spending more inference compute on a smaller model against using a larger one at equal total compute. On problems where the smaller model already had a non-trivial success rate, the smaller model with more compute beat a model 14 times its size (Snell et al., arXiv 2408.03314; published at ICLR 2025 under a slightly changed title). Effort can stand in for model size on tasks within the small model's reach.

Vendor guidance. Anthropic positions Haiku 5.5 for classification, extraction and routing (Models overview) and for summaries, compactions and classification (Haiku 5.5 launch). Its effort documentation says to run Haiku 5.5 at low for chat, short tool tasks and simple high-volume requests, and at high for knowledge work, longer agent tasks and strict instruction following (Anthropic, Effort). A Claude Code team post (Lydia Hallie, 7 Jul 2026, before the 5.5 family) gives the general rule: smaller models for more routine tasks; larger models for more complex or ambiguous ones (Claude blog, Choosing a Claude model and effort level in Claude Code).

Where the cheap model falls off. Anthropic's own table shows the gap widening with task length. On GDPval-AA v2.1, a knowledge-work test, Haiku 5.5 scores 1620 against Sonnet 5.5's 1840; on Terminal-Bench 4.0, long agentic terminal tasks, 39.2% against 70.6% (Haiku 5.5 launch; vendor).

Put together, Haiku is the right model when the task is fully specified by its input, its correct output can be recognised at a glance, volume or speed matters, and an error is cheap to catch: classification, extraction to a fixed schema, reformatting, short tool calls. It is the wrong model when the task is open-ended, long or multi-step, or when a mistake hides well (a plausible wrong figure inside a long text). The combination of conditions is my synthesis of the sources above; each condition traces to one of them.

Two cautions specific to his setup. On the API, Haiku's price quintuples once a prompt passes 100K tokens (§3), so a cheap task loaded with a heavy context loses most of its advantage. On claude.ai, the same applies to his limits in kind: conversation length and attachments draw usage whatever the model (§2), so a small task belongs in a light chat, outside a Project stuffed with course documents.

7 · What thinking and effort settings do

Thinking is adaptive. On each request the model judges whether reasoning would improve the answer and how much; a simple factual question may get no thinking at all, a multistep problem gets more (Anthropic, Steering thinking). On Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.1 thinking is always on and cannot be switched off; on Haiku 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5 it cannot be switched off in the apps either (Help Center, Change the model, effort and extended thinking settings).

Effort sets how many tokens the whole response may spend. Anthropic describes it as a behavioural signal, not a strict token budget, covering how often and how deeply Claude thinks, how many tool calls it makes, and how much it explains. The levels are low, medium, high, xhigh and max, with availability varying by model; xhigh is aimed at long-horizon work of more than 30 minutes. At lower effort Claude can skip thinking on easy problems and still thinks, less, on hard ones. On Opus 5.5, effort is the main lever on reasoning because thinking cannot be disabled (Anthropic, Effort).

Defaults. On the API, Opus 5.5 and Haiku 5.5 default to medium; Sonnet 5.5 and Fable 5.1 to high (Effort; Models overview). Opus 5 defaulted to high, so the same request on Opus 5.5 with no effort set runs one level lower than it used to (Effort). In the apps, Sonnet 5.5 defaults to medium (Sonnet 5.5 launch) and Fable 5.1 to medium on claude.ai and in Cowork, high in Claude Code (Fable 5.1 launch). The claude.ai help article marks each model's default in the menu without listing them (Help Center).

On claude.ai the effort setting sits inside the model menu next to the send button. Anthropic's labels: low and medium suit routine tasks and stretch his limits; high is the best overall balance; extra high is for long-running coding and agentic work (Opus 4.7 and newer); max is the most thorough. Higher effort means more thorough responses that take longer, use more tokens and reach limits sooner (Help Center).

What a step of effort costs, measured. On Artificial Analysis's index (third-party vendor data, §5), Opus 5.5 from low to max costs 10.9 times as much per task for 16 more points; medium to high costs 36% more for 3 points; high to xhigh costs 1.9 times more for 2 points; xhigh to max 1.7 times more for 2 points. Sonnet 5.5 from low to max costs 15.6 times more (leaderboard; arithmetic on its figures). The Claude Code post's diagram shows a high-effort path using roughly seven times the tokens of a low-effort one, but the post labels its curves illustrative (Claude blog), so that figure is not evidence.

Extra thinking on easy problems is waste. Chen and colleagues documented reasoning models spending large amounts of compute on trivially easy problems (the title's example is 2 + 3) and showed the excess could be cut while keeping accuracy (Chen et al., ICML 2025; arXiv 2412.21187). Anthropic's own guidance says the same in product terms: lower effort before trying to talk the model out of thinking (Steering thinking).

Model or effort: the diagnostic. The Claude Code post separates the two: "the model setting is roughly how capable; the effort setting is roughly how thorough." If Claude had full context, clearly tried, and still got it wrong, pick a more capable model; if it skipped files, skipped tests or did not double-check, raise effort (Claude blog). The same post recommends the default effort for most tasks and treating effort as a standing preference more than a per-task dial (same post).

Keep effort fixed within a chat. On the API, changing thinking or effort between requests invalidates the prompt cache, with two exceptions the page lists (Steering thinking). Whether the same happens on claude.ai is undocumented, as is the effect of caching on his limits (B1 §2). Setting effort once, before the first message, costs nothing and avoids the question.

8 · Where the quality differences show, and where they do not

Long-form synthesis

On Anthropic's knowledge-work tests the gap between Opus and Sonnet nearly vanishes. GDPval-AA v2.1: Opus 5.5 1846, Sonnet 5.5 1844; AA-Briefcase v1.1: 1822 against 1811 (Sonnet 5.5 launch; vendor). The Haiku launch reports Sonnet at 1840 and 1824 on the same two tests and Haiku 5.5 at 1620 and 1578 (Haiku 5.5 launch), so Anthropic's own figures for Sonnet move by a few points between pages; the Sonnet page notes those two runs used a pre-release deployment with a since-fixed bug and may understate Sonnet slightly (Sonnet 5.5 launch). Anthropic ran Opus 5.5 at max effort for its table unless noted (Opus 5.5 launch).

On reasoning with tools (Humanity's Last Exam), Anthropic reports Opus 5.5 at 67.7%, Sonnet 5.5 at 64.5% and Haiku 5.5 at 57.4% (Sonnet 5.5 launch; Haiku 5.5 launch; vendor).

At the efforts he actually gets by default, the third-party index separates Opus and Sonnet: Opus 5.5 at medium scores 51, Sonnet 5.5 at medium 41, and Sonnet needs max effort (56, at $5.46 a task) to come within two points of Opus at max (leaderboard).

A course unit is long, open-ended, searched and cited synthesis. The case for Opus there rests on that default-effort gap and on Anthropic's unquantified statement that Opus is stronger on open-ended work (§1). No source here measured anything like a course unit. His own record adds one data point about process: in the Greek review, reports written without a fresh checker scored 4.28 on his rubric, and syllabus unit 12, written with one, scored 4.8 (method.md §1, citing audits/greece-13.4-cohorts.md). That is a small internal sample, and it measures a checking step, which is the subject of B6.

Code

On agentic coding the top two are level and Haiku falls behind on long tasks. Terminal-Bench 4.0: Sonnet 5.5 70.6%, Opus 5.5 66.4% (at xhigh), Haiku 5.5 39.2%. FrontierCode 1.1: Opus 54.4%, Sonnet 52.1% (at xhigh), Haiku 46.4% (Sonnet 5.5 launch; Haiku 5.5 launch; vendor). Anthropic still recommends Sonnet or Opus over Haiku for complex agentic coding (Haiku launch). For his generator scripts and parsers, Sonnet is enough on this evidence; the Claude Code diagnostic (§7) says when to move up.

Audit

No benchmark measures auditing a course unit against its sources. Three pieces of evidence bear on it.

  • Knowledge reliability. On AA-Omniscience v1.1 (3,000 questions across six domains; it rewards correct answers, penalises wrong ones and leaves abstentions unpenalised), Fable 5.1 has the highest accuracy, 68%; Opus 5.5 has the highest overall index, 48, against 46 for Fable 5.1 and Gemini 4 Argon; Gemini 4 Argon has the lowest hallucination rate, 4% (Artificial Analysis, AA-Omniscience; third-party vendor data).
  • Model judges. Strong model judges agreed with human preferences more than 80% of the time, the same rate at which humans agree with each other, and showed position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases (Zheng et al., NeurIPS 2023, arXiv 2306.05685). The judges tested were 2023 models.
  • Self-preference. Model evaluators score their own outputs above others', and the bias grows with the model's ability to recognise its own text (Panickssery, Bowman and Feng, NeurIPS 2024; arXiv 2404.13076).

So the best-evidenced part of his method is that a different model audits from the one that wrote. Choosing Fable specifically rests on that independence and on a narrow lead in factual accuracy; general capability benchmarks favour Opus 5.5 (§1). Fable and Opus are both Anthropic models, and none of the studies above tested whether self-preference carries across sibling models from one maker. That question stays open.

His own record is consistent with this. Audit 1 failed 23 of 91 cited claims in the A2/A6/A9 research document and none of the 7 sampled in B1 (Audit 1, 28 Oct 2026); the research document was written by Fable 5.1 research agents and B1 by Opus 5.5 (courses-index.md; the audit notes that its own prompt named Opus 5.5 for all four and that the index record should stand). Two documents on different tasks prove nothing about the models. They do show that the dearer model's output was the one that failed, and that the audit, whatever wrote the text, is what caught it.

Reformatting and triage

No benchmark isolates reformatting or triage, so the evidence that model size stops mattering here is indirect: the routing results and the compute-against-size result in §6, and Anthropic's positioning of Haiku for exactly these jobs. The reasoning that ties them together is mine: reformatting and triage are tasks whose correct output is fully determined by the input, so a model has nothing to add beyond following the instruction, and capability differences show up where a task needs something the input does not contain (judgement, synthesis, search). The check is cheap, because the output can be compared with the input at a glance; sample it before trusting it (B6).

One exception. Where the task is arithmetic over numbers, such as totals or averages from an export, the model should write and run code, and the code should produce the numbers. The same input can give a model different outputs even at fixed settings (B1 §5); a script gives the same output every time.

Rules for using the table

  • Escalate by cause. Wrong with full context and a real attempt: move the model up one step. Skipped steps, missed files, no double-check: raise effort one step (§7).
  • Run small tasks in a light chat. Captions, calendar and reformatting go outside the Learning Project, where the window carries less (§2, §6; B1 §2).
  • Never trust a single run on anything that will be published or filed. Sample its claims against the sources first (§8; B6).
  • Keep a one-line log of every escalation (task, from, to, trigger). Two escalations on the same row in a month change the row.
  • Re-read the prices before relying on them. Two change on dates already announced: Gemini 4 Argon on 1 December 2026, Gemini 3.8 Flash on 1 January 2027 (§4). The whole table is due for the staleness check on 28 April 2027.
