01From two dictated takes to a script

What the brief describes, and what the filed units record

The brief says vlog episode 2 was scripted from two dictated takes. The filed units record the episode and say nothing of how it was made. A1 §6 read it on 29 October 2026 as published on 13 October, 5 minutes 54 seconds long, recorded the day before he flew to Poland, an episode about the cost of training to the people around him that "asks which matters more, being better physically or worse emotionally" and ends on a bereavement. A1's reading of it against the format: the miss is named, the record has nothing taken out, and "the method is absent again": no session, no syllabus, no stage. The route from takes to script is therefore taken from the brief as his account, and the filed units say what the route lacked.

What it lacked is what the chain now adds. B3 template 4 cuts a script from two or more takes and marks every line with its origin ([T1], [T2] with timestamp, or [NEW]); B5 step 4 turns that template into a skill step with A3's numbers: a hook in the first 30 seconds, the season label said once, "week <n> of <total>", at least three chapters, exactly one miss, the ending from the takes, about 10 minutes and 12 at most (B5 §A, step 4; A3 §2). Episode 2 at 5:54 sits below A3's floor; A3 set that length after the episode was published, so the gap says nothing about the episode.

The two inputs the script needs, and why a record sits between them

Two dictated takes are enough for a short episode about one evening. For a fortnight's episode they are not the only input. A3 §1 puts a weekly journal entry and a fortnightly record between the takes and the script, and B5 step 4 reads "the record and the takes". The reason is A3's evidence on recall: Shiffman, Stone and Hufford's review argues that real-time sampling exists to "minimize recall bias" (A3 §1), and the record is the place where every take's "what did not work" survives to the script (A4 §4: "the record holds everything the takes hold"). His rule from this: the script is cut from the record and the takes together, never from takes alone, unless the episode covers one session.

For a retrospective episode the timing matters more. The Athens finale is cut from material captured in October (A3 §5). A take dictated in November about October is the "take recorded on Sunday" that A3 §1 warns about, one that "holds the version he has already edited in his head". A4's five-question test adds a hard rule for personal material: it must be "in a take or entry made at the time" (A4, disclosure policy clause 4, question 2). So a retrospective take can supply the frame (what he thinks now, said as now), and cannot supply what Athens felt like. His rule, from those two units: a take recorded after the event is dated and marked as a later account in the script, and any line about how something felt in Athens comes from a take or entry made in Athens or is cut.

What the script step outputs

The output contract is B5 step 4's, unchanged: hook, where this sits, chapters with one-line purposes, the miss, the ending, gaps, every line marked. A5 §5 builds the edit on it ("The edit assembles a script that already exists"), and A5's runtime map gives the chapter times (A5, Deliverable B1). The one thing this unit adds is a second check after check_quotes.py: he reads every [NEW] line and rewrites or cuts it (§9).

02Titles that are not clickbait

A5 §4 owns the rules and this unit applies them; it repeats none of the evidence. In short: YouTube's three statements on clickbait (Analytics help 7628154, the spam policy 2801973, the India enforcement post of 18 December 2024) all describe a title or thumbnail whose promise the video does not keep, and A5 turns that into a list of excluded titles: the result before it happens, a question the episode does not answer, a hidden truth, a number the script does not state, capitals for volume (A5 §4, "Titles the rule excludes"). B5 step 5 adds the mechanical checks: every title ends with the season label, is 100 characters or fewer counted in code, and quotes the line from the first 30 seconds that makes it true. A4 clause 5 adds one more exclusion: a title never leads with "a diagnosis, a crisis, a loss or a verdict on myself".

Where the brief and the filed units differ. The brief's deliverable asks for five title options. YouTube's Test & Compare takes "up to three" titles, thumbnails or pairs and picks on watch time (A6 §1.2, from YouTube Help 16391400), and B5 step 5 writes three. The two are compatible: the model drafts five, and he picks the three that go into the test. There is a reason in the evidence to ask for more than he will use. In Doshi and Hauser's experiment, 300 writers wrote eight-sentence stories with no AI, one AI idea, or a choice of up to five; stories by "writers with the most access to AI" scored 8.1% higher for novelty and 9% higher for usefulness than those written without AI, as rated by 600 evaluators (Doshi and Hauser, Science Advances 10(28), 2024, doi:10.1126/sciadv.adn5290, as reported by the University of Exeter, 15 Jul 2024 and ScienceDaily, Jul 2024; the PubMed Central copy refused the fetch). That is fiction, judged by strangers, with ChatGPT ideas; the transfer to title options is mine. It supports asking for options and choosing among them, and §8 gives the same study's warning about what options cost.

The suffix sets the budget. Both published titles end with " | Karate Vlog: Road to WIKF Global Cup 2026, Ep. n" (A1 §6). That suffix is 51 characters, so a season-one title has 49 characters before it under B5's 100-character limit (counted by script, 2 November 2026). The Athens finale keeps the suffix with "Ep. 3" (A3 §5, item 1).

03Thumbnail copy in the Shōmen stylesheet

A5 §4 set the thumbnail rules; this unit uses them as written and does not restate the contrast arithmetic. What the copy step needs from them:

  • Text: one line of at most four words, plus at most one number, which comes from the script and A4's publish list (A5 §4, rule 2; A4 clause 3). This fills the slot B3 template 5 left open as "<UNKNOWN: word limit; A5 covers thumbnails>".
  • Styles: the Shōmen system B3 records: the Tomorrow typeface, "red #FF2A50 and light blue #54C8FF highlights on near-black", "Swiss-grid: facts in aligned rows, no decoration. HUD/glitch: the same facts as readouts" (B3, templates 5 and 6). In Swiss-grid, the season label and the syllabus position sit in aligned rows, the number on its own row (A5 §4, rule 3).
  • Colour: text light blue, or red on near-black; red and light blue never on each other (A5 §4, contrast: red on light blue is 1.94:1).
  • Image: a frame from the footage, never shot for the thumbnail (A5 §4, rule 1; A3 §1).
  • Test: two of the three Test & Compare variants are the same frame and text in the two styles; the third changes the frame (A5 §4, rule 4).

The model's part is the copy only: the line, the number and which style row each sits in. Choosing the frame is his, at the edit, from the two or three frames the edit hands on (A5, Deliverable B3). A finale has a number that needs no publish list: its place in the season. A season of three episodes ending at episode 3 is "3 / 3", which is the syllabus position A5 rule 3 puts on its own row.

04A Shorts cut list

One Short per episode, chosen from the final cut, 30 to 60 seconds, with the episode as its related video; never two from one episode (A3 §3, from A2 §4's reading of AIR Media-Tech and Metricool, vendor and correlational; B5 step 6; YouTube Help 14075157 via A3). The brief's "cut list" is B5 step 6's output: one pick and at most two reserves, each with start and end timestamps in the final cut, its first and last line quoted, one line on why it stands alone, and a vertical-crop check (is the subject in the centre third in every shot).

Two filed units shape the choice. A5 §3 puts the episode's strongest moment in the hook as a few seconds and in full in its chapter, and says "That moment is usually also the Short (B5 step 6), so one selection serves both." A4's pre-mortem names the risk specific to Shorts: a Short "must make sense to someone who has not seen the episode", so a line that is first-person in the episode can travel as a general claim; its guard is that "the Short is checked for first-person wording before it goes out" (A4, pre-mortem 4). The timestamps exist only after the edit, which is why B5 runs this step on Wednesday of week A, after the Saturday edit has handed on "The Short's start and end timestamps" (A5, Deliverable B3).

05Instagram variants in the two locked styles

The channel's Instagram posts are cut from the episode's footage and stills, four a fortnight, two a week, Reels for strangers and carousels for followers, exported from his own editor, nothing shot separately (A3 §3 and checklist D; A2, Deliverable 2; A6, Instagram rules 1–3). The two locked styles are Shōmen's, and B3 template 6 is the only template written for them; it asks, per style, for on-image text for the 1:1 feed post, on-image text for the 9:16 story, a caption and alt text. B5 §6 kept it a template outside the skill, because "the check is his eye on two locked styles". This unit keeps that decision and adapts the template from a Shōmen subject to an episode (Deliverable A, step 7).

Two of the template's open slots can now be filled, one from Instagram's Help Center and one from a report of an Instagram announcement, both checked 2 November 2026:

  • Hashtags: five at most. Instagram's @creators account announced that it "will gradually update the number of hashtags that you can include in a caption for a reel or post to five" (Social Media Today, 18 Dec 2025, reporting a Threads post; the Threads post itself was not opened). Whether the rollout has reached his account was not checked.
  • Carousel size: up to 20. "You can upload up to 20 photos and videos and share them as a single post in Instagram Feed" (Instagram Help Center, Share a post with multiple photos or videos). The template's data-line count stays his choice, under that ceiling of 20.

A6's rules apply to each post as they stand: the first three seconds of a Reel are its opener, judged on skip rate and sends per reach (A6, Instagram rule 3); no other app's watermark (rule 2). A4 clause 7 applies to the styles themselves: the channel using Shōmen's colours is a style, but a Shōmen product in a frame is a mention, and every mention says it is his brand (A5 §4, rule 6).

06The weekly email

The email is the last step of the chain, and every filed unit that touches it agrees on its shape. Weekly, on Thursday; 300 words or fewer (his rule, A3 §3); in an episode week the lead journey in his words with one lesson he stated in the record, one line on each other live journey only if something moved, then the episode's link; British English; no Shōmen promotion unless he asks; no request for shares (B5 step 7; B3 template 10; A2, Deliverable 2). A4 and B5 add two lines it must carry: a failure or missed week stated plainly (B5 step 7; A4 §4), and, in the footer, that "a model drafts scripts and emails from my dictated words and that every quotation is checked against the source" (A4, disclosure policy clause 8). The check is mechanical: check_quotes.py --max-words 300 passes (B5 §9).

The email is also where the journeys that do not lead the season stay in the record (A3 §3). In the Athens finale's week the lead journey is season one, closing; the others are the first half-marathon sessions, the weight, the history course and coaching (A3 §4).

07Which model handles each step, and why

B2's routing table sets the model for every step that has a row; B3 and B5 filled the gaps by analogy and said so. B5's unsure list left two of those analogies for this unit to confirm or change: titles and the Short, both Sonnet 5.5 at low effort (B5, unsure item 9). This unit keeps both, for the reasons in the table: in each, the input is a finished script, the output is short, and its correctness can be checked by code and by him in minutes, which is B2 §6's description of a task where a smaller model is enough, without being so mechanical that Haiku's documented use (classification, extraction, routing) fits it.

Three of B2's rules govern every row. B5 adds a fourth: one step per chat, each reading the file the step before it wrote (B5, skill §0). Nothing in the chain is checked by the model that wrote it: the checks are code and him, which is DD-B3's operating rule 3 ("The writer never checks its own work") in its cheapest form.

What the routing saves, and what it does not. The only measured saving from writing with a model in the evidence found is Noy and Zhang's: 453 college-educated professionals given occupation-specific writing tasks, half with ChatGPT; "The average time taken decreased by 40% and output quality rose by 18%" (Noy and Zhang, Science, 2023, doi:10.1126/science.adh2586). Those were occupation-specific writing tasks done with a 2023 model. No source found measures time saved on a script, a title or an email for a creator, and A3's slots (Friday outline hour, Tuesday packaging hour, Wednesday's half-hour Short and hour of Instagram, Thursday 0.75 hours) already assume the drafts exist. The hours log is what tests them (A9, read 2).

08Where AI helps his voice, and where it flattens it

The brief names three helps (structure, cuts, options) and three places where AI flattens the voice (first-person prose, jokes, the ending). The studies found bear on each, at different strengths, and none tested his material or a current model.

Where it helps: structure, cuts and options

Structure and cuts are rearrangement of his words. B3 template 4 and B5 step 4 ask the model to reorder and cut his sentences and to rewrite none; the result is checked against the takes word for word (B5 §9). In that arrangement every sentence on screen is his, and the model's contribution is the order and the selection. That is the division DD-B3's operating rule 1 draws: where something other than his reading can check the result (here, a script that compares quotations), "the model does it and I sample".

Options help most where the writer starts from least. In Doshi and Hauser's experiment, less creative writers (measured by a divergent-thinking test) gained most from five AI ideas: novelty up 10.7% and usefulness up 11.5% against no AI, which put them "on a par with those with high DAT scores" (DAT is the divergent-thinking test), while "AI was not judged to enhance the work produced by more creative writers" (University of Exeter, 15 Jul 2024; peer-reviewed study, reported by the university). For him the transfer is to the parts where he has least practice: titles and thumbnail copy, where the course has two published titles to go on (A1 §6). That reading is mine.

Where it flattens: first-person prose

Four studies point the same way: text the model writes pulls a writer's work toward the model's average.

  • The reduction comes from the model's text. Padmakumar and He had people write essays with GPT-3 and with InstructGPT. Writing with InstructGPT "(but not the GPT3) results in a statistically significant reduction in diversity", it "increases the similarity between the writings of different authors", and the effect "is mainly attributable to InstructGPT contributing less diverse text to co-written essays"; "the user-contributed text remains unaffected by model collaboration" (Padmakumar and He, ICLR 2024, pp. 642–669). Peer-reviewed; GPT-3 and InstructGPT.
  • Options converge too. In Doshi and Hauser's experiment, stories written with one AI idea were more similar to each other than stories written with none, by 10.7% as the release puts it (the paper gives it as a share of the similarity score's range) (University of Exeter, 15 Jul 2024). The authors call it a social dilemma: each writer is better off using the ideas, and "the collective novelty of stories may be reduced further" (same release).
  • Professional writers rewrite model prose heavily. Chakrabarty, Laban and Wu had 18 MFA-trained writers edit 1,057 paragraphs from three models, gathering 8,035 edits, "about 8 edits per paragraph", sorted into seven kinds of fault, the commonest being awkward word choice and phrasing (28% of edits), poor sentence structure (20%), unnecessary or redundant exposition (18%) and cliché (17%) (§5.1); in a preference test, writer-edited paragraphs were preferred "65% of the time" and unedited model paragraphs were "least preferred 60% of the time". The authors warn of "a reduction in linguistic diversity and individual voice" (§7.4) (Chakrabarty, Laban and Wu, Can AI writing be salvaged?, arXiv 2409.14509; preprint, venue not confirmed on the copy read).
  • The model's style wins against a writer's own. Agarwal, Naaman and Vashistha ran 118 participants from India and the United States through culturally grounded writing tasks with and without a Western-centric model's suggestions; "AI suggestions led Indian participants to adopt Western writing styles", "altering not just what is written but also how it is written" (Agarwal, Naaman and Vashistha, arXiv 2409.11360 v3, 12 Mar 2025, doi:10.1145/3706598.3713564; the abstract page names no venue, and the v3 paper's ACM reference line names CHI '25, Yokohama, 2025).

What the four have in common, read together: the model's own sentences carry the flattening, and a writer's sentences used as they are do not (Padmakumar and He's result stated directly). That is the mechanism behind B5's first rule, "His words stay his", and behind the limit on [NEW] lines: one sentence, marked, bridging two of his lines, and rewritten by him every time (B3 template 4; B5 step 4). The transfer to a vlog script is mine; none of the studies used spoken scripts. For Portuguese lines, B5's rule to quote in the language he spoke and mark any translation [TR] keeps the model's English out of them; Agarwal and colleagues' result is about Indian participants writing in English and drifting toward Western, American-centred styles, and its relevance to Portuguese is my inference.

Where it flattens: jokes

Where sources disagree. Jentzsch and Kersting: asked repeatedly for a joke, ChatGPT gave mostly the same few: "Over 90% of 1008 generated jokes were the same 25 Jokes" (Jentzsch and Kersting, WASSA 2023, pp. 325–340). Gorenz and Schwarz: on fixed comedic prompts, ChatGPT 3.5's jokes "were rated as equally funny or funnier than human-produced jokes", scoring above about 73%, 63% and 87% of lay human writers on three tasks, and its satirical headlines did not differ significantly from The Onion's (means 2.37 against 2.49 on a 0–6 scale) (Gorenz and Schwarz, PLOS ONE 19(7), e0305364, 3 Jul 2024). Read together: the second study measures whether strangers laugh at a joke on a set prompt against laypeople; the first measures whether the model's jokes vary. Neither measures whether a joke sounds like a particular person. This unit: funniness is not the test for his channel; a joke on camera is a claim about how he talks, and a joke he did not make is invented, which B3 template 4 and B5's rules for every step already forbid ("jokes he did not make"). The model writes no jokes; it may mark a line in the takes that he meant as one.

Where it flattens: the ending

The ending is where a model's text would do most to his meaning, and the filed units already close it. B5 step 4: "Ending: the last line, from the takes." A5 §3: the episode ends on that line with "no wind-down", no recap and no subscribe request. B3 template 3: "Add no lesson to it"; B5's rules for every step: "Add no lesson, no reframing, no encouragement." The evidence for keeping the model out of the ending concerns opinion. Jakesch and colleagues had more than 1,500 participants write about whether social media is good for society, with an assistant steered for or against it; those using a biased assistant were twice as likely to write a paragraph agreeing with it, were more likely to state its opinion afterwards, and a majority did not notice the bias (Cornell Chronicle, 15 May 2023, reporting Jakesch, Bhat, Buschek, Zalmanson and Naaman, CHI 2023, doi:10.1145/3544548.3581196; the paper itself was not opened). An ending is where an episode states what the season meant. A model that drafts it supplies the conclusion, and that study suggests he would not notice. That application is mine.

09The rule that the final pass is his

The rule: nothing in the pipeline is published until he has made the last pass over it, and the last pass is a set of checks he can answer yes or no. It rests on four things in the filed units and one in the evidence above.

  1. The chain is built to stop at him. B5's skill publishes nothing: "every output is a draft he reads, corrects and publishes himself"; its scheduled task gets no connectors, so it cannot post (B5 §4, §D).
  2. He files only what he could explain. DD-B3's operating rule 4: "anything a model wrote that I do not understand is disclosed or cut". Applied to a script, every [NEW] line is either his rewrite or gone.
  3. Where nothing but his reading can check, he decides. DD-B3's operating rule 1: positioning, strategy and "what to cut" go to him. The ending, the jokes and the pick among titles are that kind of judgement (§8).
  4. The record rules bind the published version. Once out, an episode changes only in the open, with a dated note (A4, record rule 2), so the final pass is the last silent edit he gets.
  5. The writers' own edits were preferred. In Chakrabarty and colleagues' test, paragraphs edited by the writers were preferred 65% of the time and unedited model paragraphs were least preferred 60% of the time (§8). Their writers edited model prose; his final pass mostly edits his own reordered sentences, so the transfer is partial and mine.

What the pass contains, his rule from the units above; each line is in Deliverable A, step 9:

  • every [NEW] and [C] line rewritten in his words or cut;
  • every joke on screen is one he made;
  • the ending is a line from the takes, chosen by him;
  • every title and thumbnail word is true of the first 30 seconds (A5 §4);
  • every number is on the publish list and appears whichever way it moved (A4 clause 3; record rule 5);
  • every personal line passes the five-question test (A4 clause 4);
  • the AI line is in the description and the email footer (A4 clause 8).

What it costs. It sits inside the slots A3 already has: the script pass in Friday's outline hour, the package pass in Tuesday's hour, the Short and Instagram in Wednesday's, the email in Thursday's 0.75 hours (A3, checklists C and D). No new hours are added; whether the slots hold with the pass inside them is for the hours log (A9, read 2).

A · The pipeline as a runnable procedure

Step 0 · Before the first step of a season

  • [ ] season.md exists with the label, the target, publish_numbers from A4 clause 3, and leave_out (B5, skill §8).
  • [ ] The journey-chain skill is installed (B5, Deliverable B) and check_quotes.py runs.
  • [ ] Each step below runs in its own new chat, outside the Learning Project, with the model and effort set before the first message (B2, rules for using the table; B5 skill §0).

Step 1 · Takes, after each session

  • Input: one dictated take, transcribed; at least two for any episode (B3 template 4).
  • Model: Haiku 5.5, low (B2, Journal entries, tidy-up override).
  • Prompt: "Take." followed by the transcript; the skill runs B5 step 1.
  • Output: takes/<date>-<session>.md with three headings and the transcript unchanged.
  • His check: the date is the day of the session. A take recorded later is dated the day it was recorded and starts "Later account:". (his rule; basis A3 §1, Shiffman et al. via A3; A4 clause 4, question 2)

Step 2 · Record, Friday of week A (a season record for a finale)

  • Input: the fortnight's entries (or, for a finale, every entry and take of the season).
  • Model: Opus 5.5, medium (B2, override for synthesis across weeks).
  • Prompt: B5 step 3, or B3 template 3 with its worked example for the competition season.
  • Output: records/<Friday date>.md, five sections plus the syllabus step.
  • Code check: python3 scripts/check_quotes.py <record> <entries…> passes.
  • His check: every "what did not work" in the takes is in section 3 (B5 step 3, DONE WHEN).

Step 3 · Script, Friday of week A

  • Input: the record and the takes.
  • Model: Opus 5.5, medium (B2, Video scripts, original).
  • Prompt: B5 step 4, which is B3 template 4 with A3's numbers. For a finale add: Ending: in a finale, the last line from the takes, then the handover: <next season>, <target>, <opener date>. Mark any take recorded after the event as a later account.
  • Output: episodes/ep<nn>/script.md, every line marked [T<n> mm:ss], [R] or [NEW], with a Gaps list.
  • Code check: check_quotes.py against the takes and the record passes.
  • His pass (the first half of the final pass): rewrite or cut every [NEW] line; write any joke himself; choose the ending line; record the Gaps lines in Saturday's filming window (A3 §6). (his rule)

Step 4 · Frame and edit (no model)

Filming window Saturday of week A, editing window Saturday of week B, by A5's checklist and edit template. The edit hands on: the chapter list, the Short's timestamps and two or three thumbnail frames (A5, Deliverable B3).

Step 5 · Titles and thumbnail copy, Tuesday of the next week A

  • Input: the final script, hook first.
  • Model: Sonnet 5.5, low (no B2 row; analogy with B2's Shōmen-posts row, as B3 and B5 set it; confirmed here, §7).
  • Prompt: B5 step 5 with three changes:
TASK: Write five title options for episode <nn>; I will choose up to three for one Test & Compare run.
OUTPUT: a table, five rows: Title | Angle | The line from the first 30 seconds that makes it true (quoted, with its mark) | Thumbnail text | Style row.
Thumbnail text: one line of at most four words, plus at most one number, taken from the script and the publish list in season.md. Style row: where each element sits in Swiss-grid (season label row; text row; number on its own row) and in HUD/glitch (the same facts as readouts).
  • Output: episodes/ep<nn>/titles.md.
  • Code check: every title 100 characters or fewer including the label, counted in code (B5 step 5).
  • His check: A5's clickbait list on every row; no title or thumbnail leads with a diagnosis, crisis, loss or verdict on himself (A4 clause 5); he picks up to three for Test & Compare and pairs the thumbnail styles by A5 rule 4.
