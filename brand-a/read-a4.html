1 · Honesty as register, confession as content

The two terms

Register, in the sense used here, is the level and manner in which something is said: who it is addressed to, how much weight it carries, where it sits in the piece. Honesty as register means the true account is the normal way the work talks. A miss is stated at the weight it had, inside the episode about the journey, and the episode goes on. Confession as content means the disclosure is the product: it is the subject of the piece, it sits in the title or the thumbnail, and the piece would have nothing left if it were cut. The distinction is mine; no source found defines these two terms. The cases below are documented and linked, and each is a creator anecdote in the sourcing standard's sense: chosen because it is known, and proof of nothing about frequency.

Confession as content: Elle Mills, 2018

In May 2018 Elle Mills, then past a million YouTube subscribers, posted a video about being "burnt out at 19" and announced a break from social media; Tubefilter quotes her: "I will be putting my mental health first for a bit" (Tubefilter, Sam Gutelle, 21 May 2018). NPR reported that she had "more than 1.3 million YouTube followers", that each video could take more than 60 hours to shoot, produce and edit, and that she felt drained trying to post every week or two (NPR, Laura Sydell, 13 Aug 2018). The following year she described the direction of the channel: "It's definitely been getting more raw on my channel, which is ironic because I hate being raw and vulnerable in person", and "Making videos now is almost like a diary entry for me" (Tubefilter, James Hale, 27 Mar 2019).

Her later account, in an interview with Jack Conte for Patreon's Creator Hub (undated), is the evidence that matters here. She says she was "very impulsive" in releasing the burnout video; that "I took advantage of people's longings to be seen"; that, in the narration of her later video What Happened To Elle Mills?, which the article quotes, "I don't want my life to be the product anymore"; and that "the biggest thing I learned from my YouTube experience is that not everyone deserves your vulnerability" (Patreon Creator Hub, A YouTube star's reflections on burnout, boundaries, and changing directions). The classification as confession as content is mine; the description of her life as "the product" is hers.

Honesty as register: Tom Scott, 2023–24

Tom Scott published his first video on 1 January 2014 and a video a week for the ten years after (Kottke, 2 Jan 2024); in July 2023 Kottke reported he was "stopping his channel at the end of this year to take a break" (Kottke, 6 Jul 2023). Tubefilter quotes the reason he gave: the job "keeps getting bigger and more complicated, and I am so tired"; "There's nothing in my life right now except work"; "This is my dream job and I have a lot of fun doing it", and "a dream job is still a job"; and "I don't know when or if I come back, but when I do, it won't be weekly" (Tubefilter, James Hale, 2 Jan 2024). His own newsletter for 1 January 2024 says only "After ten years, it's time to stop making videos" and that he was "finally off on sabbatical" (tomscott.com, 1 Jan 2024).

What makes this register is its shape: the state is named once, in plain words, attached to a decision about cadence, and the channel's subject does not change to the state. He did not make a series about being tired. The word "burnout" does not appear in the quotations found; it should not be attributed to him.

The record of failures, and the record becoming the content: Haushofer, 2016

In 2016 Johannes Haushofer, then at Princeton, published a "CV of Failures": degree programmes he did not get into, positions, awards and funding he did not get, paper rejections. His stated reason: "Most of what I try fails, but these failures are often invisible, while the successes are visible", and the document is "an attempt to balance the record and provide some perspective". He credits the idea to Melanie Stefan's piece in Nature (Stefan, "A CV of failures", Nature 468, 467, 18 Nov 2010). He later added a section headed "Meta-Failures" with one line: "This darn CV of Failures has received way more attention than my entire body of academic work" (Haushofer, CV of Failures, copy hosted by the University of Liverpool; Times Higher Education, 3 May 2016).

The case shows both halves of his rule "failures stay in the record". The document itself is register: dated entries, no commentary, a list beside a real body of work. The meta-failure is the risk: the record of failure drew more attention than the work it was meant to sit beside. That is the mechanism by which register turns into confession, through the audience's response, without the author changing a word.

A boundary case: Hank Green, 2023

On 19 May 2023 Hank Green published a video titled "So, I've got cancer." Dexerto quotes him in it: "I wanna be fun, goofy science guy, not struggling with anxiety, cancer guy … You can be both. I think I just have to let that go" (Dexerto, 19 May 2023). NPR's report says he did not know how the diagnosis would affect his projects, pointed viewers to his newsletter, asked them not to send medical advice, and asked for light entertainment recommendations (GPB/NPR, Tilda Wilson, 19 May 2023). The disclosure was the video's subject, so by the definition above it is content. It was also stated as information with consequences (work may slow, no advice wanted). The case is here for one line: a disclosure can take over how an audience sees the person, and the person may have to accept that it has. That is the risk §5 names for "ADHD is public".

Why a failure in the record can help, and when it cannot

One experiment is usually cited for the claim that a visible mistake makes a person more likeable. Aronson, Willerman and Floyd played 48 male University of Minnesota students a recorded quiz tryout in which the contestant answered either 92% or 30% of 50 questions correctly, and in some versions spilled coffee on himself. The blunder raised the attractiveness ratings of the superior contestant and lowered those of the average one; the blunder alone had no significant effect, and the ability by blunder interaction was significant (Aronson, Willerman and Floyd, Psychonomic Science 4(6), 227–228, 1966; doi:10.3758/BF03342263). That is one small 1966 study, all men, with a spilled drink as the failure; its replication was not checked for this unit. What it suggests, at that strength: a failure helps the person who has already shown competence, and costs the one who has not. My transfer: the miss works in the record when the same episode shows the syllabus, the sessions and the work; a miss with nothing around it is only a miss.

His two episodes, read against the distinction

A1 §6 read both published episodes. Episode 1 ("Sometimes You Win, Sometimes You Learn To Lose") is register: the loss is the episode, the miss is named on camera, and the description places it in a life with "a job, a partner, a family". Episode 2 ("Being a Better Athlete Made Me a Worse Person") is closer to the line: its subject, by A1's reading, is the person, not a step in a journey; it names his snapping at people around him and ends on a bereavement. A1 §6 left "how far the channel goes with that kind of material" to this unit. Both stay published (A6, playbook YouTube 5; record rule 3 below). The policy below governs what comes after them.

2 · The evidence on trust

The study, as published

The brief names a Frontiers in Communication 2026 study. It is Muhmin, Syah and Anggraeni (the journal's citation line reads "Muhmin AH, Syah TYR and Anggraeni A"; A2 §3 and Audit 1 name them by their given names as "Hidayat, Rahmat and Adilla"), "Parasocial relationship as a social-cognitive pathway in influencer marketing: evidence from Indonesian beauty followers", Frontiers in Communication 11, published 5 February 2026 (Frontiers, full text; doi:10.3389/fcomm.2026.1723759). A2 §3 summarised it in one sentence; this is the detail.

  • Sample. 407 valid responses from women aged 18 to 35 who follow at least one beauty influencer in Indonesia (54.8% aged 18–24, 45.2% aged 25–35), recruited by purposive sampling through an online survey shared in social-media and beauty communities. Each respondent rated one influencer.
  • Method. A cross-sectional survey on five-point scales, analysed with PLS-SEM and 5,000 bootstrap resamples.
  • Measures. Trustworthiness items include "appears genuine and authentic in every piece of content" and "shows honesty when recommending beauty products"; expertise items include "has real experience using the beauty products featured in his/her content". The parasocial-relationship scale kept four items, one of which is trust in the information shared; trust is not measured as a separate construct.
  • Findings (standardised path coefficients). Expertise to parasocial relationship 0.128 (p = 0.005) and trustworthiness 0.225 (p < 0.001), both supported; attractiveness 0.075 (p = 0.135), not supported. Parasocial relationship to purchase intention 0.432 (p < 0.001). Direct paths to purchase intention: expertise 0.127, trustworthiness 0.129, attractiveness 0.124, all supported. The model explains 24.7% of the variance in the parasocial relationship and 35.5% in purchase intention.
  • Limits the authors state. The cross-sectional design limits causal inference; the purposive sample of highly engaged followers may over-represent engaged, urban respondents; self-report measures risk common-method variance and social desirability; results may not generalise across cultures, product categories or platforms.

What it does and does not say

The brief's version, that expertise and trustworthiness "not attractiveness, predict parasocial trust and purchase intention", is half right. Attractiveness did not predict the parasocial bond. It did predict purchase intention directly, with a coefficient about the size of expertise's and trustworthiness's own direct paths. The study's distinction is between two routes: the bond runs through competence and honesty, and attractiveness reaches purchase intention without the bond. The largest single path in the model is from the bond to purchase intention.

What transfers to him, all my inference, from a correlational survey of young Indonesian women about beauty influencers, which is far from a 35-year-old man's running and karate channel:

  1. The two cues that went with the bond are the two his format produces. "Real experience using the products" is what a recorded attempt is; "shows honesty" and "genuine and authentic" are what a named miss is. The journey record is a long, dated demonstration of both.
  2. The honesty items are about recommendations. Trustworthiness was measured partly as honesty "when recommending" products. For a channel that also carries Shōmen (reader profile), the trust the record builds is spent or kept at the moment he recommends something he owns. The policy below labels every such moment.
  3. Nothing here says disclosure of private matters builds trust. The items measure honesty about the subject and the products, and the study did not measure personal disclosure at all. The evidence supports honesty as register. It says nothing in favour of confession as content.

3 · Audience entanglement and creator burnout

A9 §8 introduced both studies as evidence on why creators quit. Here they bear on what a disclosure does to the person who makes it.

ASQ: audience entanglement

Pillemer, Harrison, Murphy and Park, "Audience Entanglement: How Independent Creative Workers Experience the Pressures of Widespread Appeal on Digital Platforms", Administrative Science Quarterly 71(1), 215–260 (published online 18 December 2025; the issue is dated 2026) (SAGE, article page; doi:10.1177/00018392251399652). The full paper was not opened; the details below come from the publisher's abstract and from PsyPost's report (PsyPost, Eric W. Dolan, 20 Feb 2026).

  • Sample and method. 54 independent creators who had already succeeded: visual artists on Instagram averaging over 500,000 followers and musicians on YouTube averaging nearly 280,000 subscribers; 74 in-depth interviews, with follow-ups for some; an inductive qualitative study that builds theory and tests no hypothesis.
  • Findings. After a creator reaches a large audience, "audience entanglement" sets in: a deep interrelatedness with the audience that shapes how the creator works. It usually begins as dysfunctional entanglement: oppressive dependence on audience reactions, metrics as the main source of validation, distress at platform volatility, and a view of the work as unsustainable, with many wanting to quit. Some creators reach functional entanglement, a balanced dependence in which they draw meaning from the audience and see the work as sustainable. Creators cycle between the two; a platform change or a wave of harassment can push them back.
  • The guardrails. Three management strategies: distancing from audience input (PsyPost's examples: not reading comments for 24 hours after posting; a trusted friend or partner filtering messages); depersonalising critique (reading it as being about one piece of work, or about the commenter's day, and not as a verdict on the person); and distilling personal standards (returning to one's own ideals and why one started, ahead of trends).
  • Limits (PsyPost's list). Self-report; visual artists and musicians only, so creators "in the fitness, beauty, or gaming sectors" may differ; two platforms, Instagram and YouTube, whose feedback mechanisms may differ from TikTok's or Twitch's.

New Media & Society: burnout as "unspeakable"

Rosie Nguyen and Brooke Erin Duffy, New Media & Society, published online 7 July 2026, doi:10.1177/14614448261459667. The title registered with the DOI is "'Creator burnout is real': Risk, responsibility, and un/speakability in the creator economy" (Crossref record, read 29 October 2026, https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/14614448261459667; the same wording as the journal-contents index metatoc); Cornell's press release gives the subtitle as "… in Platform-Dependent Creative Labor" (Cornell Chronicle, Tom Fleischman, 8 Jul 2026). The journal page could not be opened; the findings below are the press release's.

  • Data. 58 pieces of creators' own content about burnout on TikTok and YouTube, 62 news accounts and 78 in-depth interviews with creators.
  • Findings. Burnout is normalised as part of the job and partly "unspeakable"; the authors tie that to the informal structure of platform labour, the "dream job" status of creative work, and power and identity markers, gender among them. Creators told the researchers about harms they would not share publicly. Gratitude toward the community, audience and platforms makes them less likely to speak; speaking publicly carries risks, audience backlash among them. A recurring fear: "If I take a break, I'm going to get punished by the algorithm." Coping split by gender in their data: men tended toward a "grinding" ethos, women toward self-care or restorative practices.
  • The guardrails. The authors' remedy is structural: Duffy argues that framing burnout as individual failure puts the burden on creators, and the release names the proposed Creator Bill of Rights (Rep. Ro Khanna), Creators 4 Mental Health and the American Influencer Council as positive steps. The study offers no individual technique comparable to ASQ's three.

What the two studies mean for disclosure

Read together, and with A9 §8's sequence (metrics read as a verdict on the person, then a collapse or a pivot), they give three consequences. Each is my inference; neither study measured a creator's disclosure policy.

  1. Confession feeds entanglement. When the episode is the person, every metric on it is a metric on the person, which is ASQ's definition of the dysfunctional state. When the episode is the journey, a weak number reads as a weak episode. That is ASQ's depersonalising strategy built into the format, before any comment is read.
  2. The pressure runs against honesty at the moment honesty matters most. Nguyen and Duffy found burnout kept quiet out of gratitude and fear of backlash. A brand that promises "failures stay in the record" is exposed exactly there: the one failure a creator is least likely to publish is his own exhaustion. The policy below settles in advance how a pause is announced, so the decision is not taken in the state the study describes.
  3. The fear about breaks has an answer for his platform. YouTube's growth team rejects the idea that breaks are punished (A9 §1, §8); AIR's correlational data links repeated gaps of 14 days or more to fewer views per video (A9 §8). The cost of an announced pause is uncertain and unmeasured; a silent one breaks the one promise the brand makes about its record.

The guardrails are already in his system. ASQ's distancing is A2's two fixed 30-minute comment slots a fortnight (A2, Deliverable 2; A2 §6) and A9's rule that Studio is read on checkpoint dates only, which A9 marks as its own untested extension of the study (A9, Deliverable 2). Distilling personal standards is the syllabus written before the season (A1 §3; A3 §2). The ASQ sample were creators with hundreds of thousands of followers; at his size (A9's expectation sheet puts him under 1,000 subscribers at month twelve) the pressure will be smaller, and the guardrails are set before it exists.

4 · How a public record stays truthful

Failures in

A1 §3 makes "the miss, named" a fixed element of every episode. A3 §1 makes the second prompt of every dictated take "what did not work", so the miss exists in the record before anyone decides what the episode is. B3 template 3 carries it through the synthesis: "Keep every failure at the weight the entries give it. Add no lesson to it."; B5 step 3 keeps the first sentence, and B5's rules for every step add "Add no lesson, no reframing, no encouragement." The chain already produces failures; what this unit adds is the rule that they survive selection.

No highlight reel

A highlight reel is a record with its denominator removed. A9 §1 made the same point about data: survivor samples "overstate what a typical new channel sees", and a channel that publishes only the sessions that went well is a survivor sample of itself. The risk sits at the cut. The episode is about ten minutes from a fortnight of sessions (A3 §2), so most of the record never reaches the screen. The rule that keeps the reel honest applies to the record: the record holds everything the takes hold (B5 step 3: "nothing appears that the entries do not contain", and nothing in them is dropped), and the episode is cut from a complete record. Numbers are the hardest case. A number that is published when it moves the right way and dropped when it does not is the highlight reel in its purest form, and record rule 5 closes it.

No retroactive editing of personal entries

Three facts make this a rule and not a hope.

  1. Edits after publication are easy and invisible on YouTube. YouTube lets a creator trim the beginning, middle or end of a published video without re-uploading, and "the URL, view count, and comments stay the same"; only unedited videos with over 100,000 views may be limited to blurring faces, and that limit does not apply to Partner Program channels (YouTube Help, Trim your videos, answer 9057455). Nothing on the page shows the viewer that a video was changed.
  2. Professions that depend on a trusted record solved this already. The ICMJE recommendations for medical journals: publish a correction notice "as soon as possible"; post a new version "with details of the changes from the original version" and the dates; "archive all prior versions"; earlier versions should "prominently note that there are more recent versions" (ICMJE, Corrections and version control). His own method already works this way: "The auditor never edits a unit; it reports", and errata are applied as exact, checked replacements (method.md §6), which DD-B3 §4 (collision 2) set against the knowledge-base pattern in which an LLM rewrites its pages.
  3. His journey files have a history by construction. B5 §5 put the takes, entries and records in a git repository, where every change is a commit with a date. B5's skill already forbids the edits that would falsify the record: step 1 "MAY NOT … correct a number he said"; step 2 "MAY NOT: edit a take". A retroactive edit is therefore detectable in principle; the rule makes it forbidden in practice.

Personal entries are the strictest case, because they are the source of everything else. A3 §1, citing Shiffman, Stone and Hufford on recall bias: "a take recorded at the kerb holds the miss as it felt; a take recorded on Sunday holds the version he has already edited in his head." A take rewritten a month later is further from the event still. A3 §1 extended "nothing is filmed twice" to the record: a re-staged miss "replaces what happened with a staged version of it". An edited entry does the same in text.

Published episodes are governed separately. A6's playbook (YouTube 5) keeps every episode public, on watch-hour grounds. The record adds a second reason: an episode deleted or quietly trimmed is a revision of the record that the viewer cannot see. Record rules 2 and 3 set what may change, and how it is shown.

5 · What "ADHD is public" commits him to, and what it does not

The brief states the fact: "ADHD is public under his name." A1's deliverable already publishes it twice: the channel description says "I have ADHD and a full-time job. All of this runs on 5–7 hours a week, and that constraint is part of the record", and the Instagram bio ends "ADHD · Porto". A1 §1 sets its place: ADHD, the job and the hours "make the method harder and the record more honest"; they belong in the expansion and the description, and "cannot carry the sentence alone". This section turns that into commitments.

What the evidence bears on

  • ADHD content is crowded and often wrong. Yeung, Ng and Abi-Jaoude rated the top 100 TikTok videos under #adhd (collected 18 July 2021) with a psychiatrist and a psychiatry resident: 52% misleading, 27% personal experience, 21% scientifically correct; 11% were uploaded by health-care professionals, whose videos were more likely to be accurate; each video averaged almost 3 million views (The Canadian Journal of Psychiatry 67(12), 899–906, 2022, doi:10.1177/07067437221082854, volume and pages from the Crossref record; reported by PsyPost, 10 Mar 2022; the journal page was not opened). The category "personal experience" is the honest one open to him; "misleading" is where an unhedged general claim lands.
  • A health fact, once he publishes it, is his to lose control of. The GDPR treats "data concerning health" as a special category whose processing "shall be prohibited", and lifts that prohibition where the processing "relates to personal data which are manifestly made public by the data subject" (Regulation (EU) 2016/679, Art. 9(1) and 9(2)(e); text as reproduced by gdpr-info.eu, an unofficial copy). On the plain text, a published diagnosis loses part of the protection it had. What that means in practice, and whether body weight counts as health data, is a legal question for A8; this unit treats it as a reason to publish health facts narrowly.
  • A disclosure can become the identity. Hank Green's line in §1 is one creator's account of it. The brand's register is plurality, "each piece leads with one journey" (brief); A1 §2's working claim is that a fixed, visible method is the best-supported way to keep returning viewers across a change of topic; a channel that came to be about ADHD would trade its method for a topic.

What it commits him to, in writing (his rules, from the above)

  1. The fact stays stated. The channel description and bio keep the line; it is never quietly removed (record rule 3). A season opener's description repeats it once.
  2. ADHD appears as a constraint on the method. It is named where it shaped a decision: why the windows are fixed (A3 §6), why an overrun happened, why a week was cut. It is never the subject of an episode, a title or a thumbnail.
  3. Every ADHD statement is first-person. "I", "for me", "this week". A general claim about ADHD appears only with a peer-reviewed source on screen or in the description, as A3 §6 cites the Safren and Solanto trials and the Gollwitzer and Sheeran meta-analysis, and labelled as what the study found.
  4. No advice. No treatment, medication or diagnostic suggestion, to anyone, in any reply. A comment asking for it gets the same line every time: what he does is in the episodes, and the question belongs with a clinician.
  5. A miss is attributed to ADHD only when the take attributes it. The record's words decide (B5 rules: his words stay his), so ADHD does not become the explanation added later for every failure.

What it does not commit him to

  • any detail of diagnosis, assessment, medication, dosage, treatment or clinicians;
  • any health matter other than ADHD;
  • symptom tracking as content, or numbers about attention, sleep or mood;
  • being an ADHD channel, an advocate or a spokesman;
  • answering messages about anyone else's ADHD;
  • ever revisiting the subject on request.

A · Disclosure policy

Disclosure policy, version 1, 29 October 2026. Applies to everything published under my name: episodes, Shorts, Instagram, the weekly email, Academy pages.

1. Public by default. Stated wherever it is relevant, at the weight the record gives it: - the method and the season's syllabus; - every miss the record holds for the journey an episode covers; - the hours, and the job as a constraint ("a full-time job"); never the employer's business; - ADHD, as a fact about me and a constraint on the method (§5); - the numbers on the publish list (clause 3); - how the work is made: a model cuts scripts and emails from my dictated words, and each is checked against them; - that Shōmen is mine, every time it appears.

2. Never published. - My absolute body weight, and any clinical measure: resting heart rate, heart-rate variability, sleep, blood results. - Diagnosis details, medication, dosage, treatment, clinicians; any health matter other than ADHD. - Where I live or start my runs: route maps go out with the start and end hidden (Strava's map visibility hides the first and last 200 metres by default and can hide the part of any activity that starts or ends within a mile of an address: Strava Help, map visibility). - Other people's private facts: health, relationships, conflicts, deaths. Other people appear by their role in a session (B5, rules for every step), and on camera only with their agreement. - My employer's internal matters and my colleagues. - Anything someone told me or sent me privately.

3. The publish list (publish_numbers in season.md): distance, duration, pace, race result, weekly running volume, weight change from the season's first weigh-in (kg, one decimal). A kind of number on the list is published every time it is measured in an episode's fortnight, whichever way it moved (record rule 5). (his rule; basis: the −15 kg journey is public as a goal in the brief; the change is what the journey measures; absolute weight is body data the journey does not need, and a published health fact loses part of its protection, §5)

4. Decided case by case: the five-question test. For feelings, setbacks outside sessions, conflict, grief, and anything about my life that bears on a journey. All five must be yes. 1. It happened inside the journey, or changed how the journey runs. 2. It is in a take or entry made at the time (it reached the record before it reached an episode). 3. The episode would keep its subject if this line were cut. If the disclosure is the subject, it stays in the record and off the channel. 4. It names no one else, or the person named has agreed. 5. It reaches the public first in an episode, which the pipeline puts at least ten days after its take: the record and outline are made on Friday of week A, the episode is edited on Saturday of week B and published the following Tuesday (A3 §6). The email and Instagram carry it only once the episode is out. Nothing under this clause is posted the same day it happened.

(his rule; basis: §1's definitions; Mills's own "very impulsive"; ASQ's distancing strategy; A3's pipeline)

5. Titles and thumbnails lead with the journey. Never with a diagnosis, a crisis, a loss or a verdict on myself. The two published titles stay as they are (record rule 3). (his rule; basis: §1, Haushofer's meta-failure; §3, confession feeds entanglement)

6. Pauses. If I stop or pause, I say so in one plain paragraph in the email and on the channel within the week I decide: that I am pausing, the expected length if I know it, and the reason at the level I would give a colleague. No episode about it while it is happening. (his rule; basis: Tom Scott's announcement, §1; Nguyen and Duffy on burnout kept quiet, §3; A9's stop rule, which records a stop "with its reason and the numbers at the time")

7. Commercial interest. Every mention of Shōmen says it is my brand. A sponsorship or gifted product gets YouTube's paid-promotion setting, which requires disclosure of "branded content, sponsorships, endorsements, or other commercial relationships" and adds a label at the start of the video (YouTube Help, paid product placements, answer 154235), and a spoken line. The UK advertising regulators' guide, written with the Competition and Markets Authority, says that under consumer law, whether "promoting your own products/services" or endorsing a brand's, "a clear and upfront disclosure is always necessary" (CAP/ASA with the CMA, Influencers' guide to making clear that ads are ads); that is UK guidance, and the Portuguese and EU rules are A8's to check. (his rule for the spoken line; basis: §2, trustworthiness measured as honesty "when recommending")

8. AI. The channel description and the email footer state that a model drafts scripts and emails from my dictated words and that every quotation is checked against the source (B5, check_quotes.py). YouTube requires disclosure of "AI-generated or altered content that seems realistic" and does not require it for a script or outline (YouTube Help, Disclosing use of GenAI content, answer 14328491); I disclose the script anyway. (his rule; basis: DD-B3, card K7 and operating rule 4)

B · Pre-mortem: it is the end of 2028, and a disclosure went badly

Each cause is hypothetical and built from the unit's evidence; none is a prediction. The order is mine. For each: what happened, why, the evidence, and the clause that guards it.

  1. The miss became the product. An episode built round a failure outperformed the rest; later titles leaned on failure; by 2028 the channel was known for him failing. Why: attention rewards the confession more than the work (Haushofer's meta-failure, §1); metrics became validation (ASQ's dysfunctional entanglement, §3). Guard: clause 5; Studio read on checkpoint dates only (A9, Deliverable 2).
  2. Posted the same evening. A bad race or a loss went to Instagram that night, in words he would not have chosen a week later. Why: Mills's own account of releasing her burnout video, "very impulsive" (§1); ASQ's distancing strategy exists for this (§3). Guard: clause 4, question 5.
  3. Someone else's story. An episode carried a private fact about a partner, family member or training partner, who objected. Why: his record is about a life with other people in it (A1 §6: "a job, a partner, a family"; episode 2's bereavement). Guard: clause 2; clause 4, question 4; B5's role-only rule.
  4. A line about ADHD travelled as advice. A Short cut from an episode, which by design "must make sense to someone who has not seen the episode" (B5, step 6), carried a general claim about ADHD and circulated without its context. Why: 52% of top #adhd TikTok videos were rated misleading (Yeung et al., §5). Guard: §5 commitments 3 and 4; the Short is checked for first-person wording before it goes out.
  5. The number that went the wrong way disappeared. Weight change stalled or reversed in a season; it stopped appearing; a viewer noticed. Why: the highlight reel in its purest form (§4); trust in this study's terms is honesty (§2). Guard: clause 3; record rule 5.
  6. A silent edit was found. An episode was trimmed after publication to remove an awkward line, and someone compared it with a download. Why: YouTube keeps the URL, views and comments after a trim, so nothing shows the change (§4). Guard: record rules 2 and 3.
  7. Burnout was hidden, then the channel stopped without a word. Why: burnout is partly "unspeakable", held back by gratitude and fear of backlash (Nguyen and Duffy, §3). Guard: clause 6.
  8. Work leaked into the record. A line about a bad week at work was read by a colleague. Why: A2 §2 records Sonia Kang's view that a side project is more acceptable to employers than "This is me, and here's my view"; contract terms are A8's. Guard: clause 2.

What to check at each quarterly checkpoint (A9, Deliverable 2), one line each: did any title or thumbnail lead with a disclosure; did any personal material go out within ten days of its take; is every listed number present in every episode it was measured for; does any published episode differ from its first version without a dated note.

C · Five record rules

  1. Filed is fixed. A take, entry or record is never edited after it is filed. A correction is a new dated line beneath it: what was wrong, what is right, why. In the journey repository the history shows every change (B5 §5; B5 steps 1 and 2, "MAY NOT"). Basis: §4; ICMJE on archived prior versions.
  2. Published work changes only in the open. A trim, a blur, a corrected figure or a changed title after publication is listed in the description with its date and reason. Test & Compare runs (A6, playbook YouTube 3) are packaging tests, recorded in the metrics sheet (A6 row Y2), and need no note. Basis: §4; YouTube's trim page; ICMJE on correction notices.
  3. Nothing published is withdrawn. No deleting, no unlisting (A6, playbook YouTube 5). One exception: material that exposes a third party's private facts or anyone's location is blurred or cut, with a dated note in the description saying that something was removed and the general reason. Basis: §4; disclosure policy clause 2.
  4. The miss is always in. Every episode names one (A1 §3); the record keeps every miss the takes contain (B5 step 3); a season's finale names the season's misses alongside its artefact (A3 §5, extended). The record is complete before anything is selected from it. Basis: §4, no highlight reel; §1, Aronson et al. on failure beside competence.
  5. A listed number is always published. Once a kind of number is on the publish list, it appears in every episode whose fortnight measured it, whichever way it moved, with its date and source (B5: numbers only from a take, an entry or a numbers/ export). A kind leaves the list only by a dated policy change announced in the email. Basis: §4; disclosure policy clauses 3 and 10.
