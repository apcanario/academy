01The choice: five of nine, and why

The brief lists nine. The test applied to each was two-part. First, evidence: at least one dated number from the first three years, a start date from a source opened in this context, and at least one statement in the creator's own words. Second, fit: one person on camera (or one host), a premise that resembles his method (A1 §3) or one of his journeys, and something in the history that speaks to a 5–7 hour week, a job held alongside, ADHD in public, or running.

Chosen.

  1. Ali Abdaal. The best-documented early curve of the nine, most of it in his own words, and the best-documented case of a channel built alongside a full-time job (he was a junior doctor from 2018 to 2020). The cost is distance from his format: a productivity channel with a team by 2020.
  2. Mike Boyd. The closest premise to the method: one skill learned from zero, on camera, again and again, for eleven years. Also the only creator in the set whose cadence is at or below bi-weekly. A1 §5 already holds his counts, so this unit can check them.
  3. Hellah Sidibe. A journey held as the format: a run streak begun on 15 May 2017 and still running in September 2026. Running is his next journey; Sidibe has several dated subscriber counts from the press.
  4. Mark Lewis. A one-person fitness channel by a man who describes himself as out of shape and unmotivated in his thirties, and who came from financial services. His early numbers are unpublished, but vidIQ shows his month-by-month curve from October 2023 to September 2026, which is the only multi-year monthly series found for any of the nine and the clearest plateau in the set.
  5. Alex Partridge (ADHD Chatter). ADHD in public under his own name, a weekly interview show begun on 20 March 2023, with figures at roughly one, two and three years from his own nomination and speaker texts and the press. The cost: he built it after founding two large media companies, and nothing found documents his hours.

Left out, by name.

  • Goal Guys. Two brothers, which is a different hours budget from one person (A1 §5 says the same). The one interview page found, a ThoughtLeaders podcast page of 11 May 2020, names them (Cam and Brenden) and gives no start date, number or statement (ThoughtLeaders, 11 May 2020); no tracker page for the channel was returned by the searches run. A1 §5's Goal Guys figures (562,000 · 236 · 87,724,487, vidIQ, 28 Oct 2026) are therefore neither confirmed nor corrected here.
  • Ben Felton. The best fit by subject (a runner who started filming his training) and the weakest by evidence. Zwift Insider dates the start to "the 2020 lockdown" (Zwift Insider, 21 Sep 2026), and the Gazette profile, which carries no date, gives "more than 165,000 followers on YouTube and Instagram" combined (Gazette, undated). No tracker page and no dated YouTube count was found, so his 12-, 24- and 36-month numbers would all be blank.
  • Jesse Enkamp. vidIQ dates his channel to 11 September 2008 and gives 1.67 million subscribers and 789 videos on 1 November 2026 (vidIQ, data 1 Nov 2026), but no page opened dates when he began posting regularly or gives an early count; Black Belt's interview page gives "more than 800,000 subscribers on YouTube" with no date (Black Belt, updated 31 Jan 2024). A7 §3 already uses him for what he documents (a gi brand beside a channel).
  • Nick Bare. A 2019 profile says he began YouTube on arriving at Fort Hood and that his supplement business "took about 4-5 years to really start making money" (G.I. Jobs, 25 Apr 2019), but it gives no date or subscriber number, and no tracker page was returned. His business is a supplement company, a different order of operation from Shōmen.

On A1 §5's question (Mike Boyd and Goal Guys). Mike Boyd is chosen; his counts are checked in §3. Goal Guys is not, for the two reasons above: two people, and no dated growth source found.

02Ali Abdaal

Start date. His own about page dates the start of his YouTube channel to 2017, while he was a Cambridge medical student (aliabdaal.com, about page, read 2 Nov 2026). Noah Kagan's profile adds that the first upload was a guitar cover in January 2017 and that medical content began in June 2017, filmed on a phone (Noah Kagan, 25 Jan 2022; author's account, not Ali's words). vidIQ dates the channel itself to 20 November 2007, about nine years before the first upload Kagan gives (January 2017) (vidIQ, data 1 Nov 2026). The 12-, 24- and 36-month points below count from June 2017.

Platform mix. YouTube first; then a cohort course (Part-Time YouTuber Academy, first cohort November 2020), a podcast (Deep Dive) and a larger team (his about page, above); Skillshare classes by 2020 (Summify summary, below); an email list (Kagan, below); and, by 2023, an expensive London studio, since given up (Mark Ellis, below). Kagan gives more than 130,000 email subscribers in January 2022, and among the tactics that worked lists starting narrow and widening the topics later, and an email list kept as a backup to YouTube (author's account).

Cadence. On Creator Science (15 December 2020) he says he set himself at least one video a week at first, then one to three a week, which he treated as non-negotiable, and that he made about two a week over a nine-month stretch (Creator Science #37, 15 Dec 2020; his words, paraphrased). In September 2020 he told Every that over his last three months as a full-time doctor he made three videos a week, and one or two after leaving (Every, Superorganizers, 24 Sep 2020). vidIQ counts 1,503 videos on 1 November 2026.

Hours a week. The only creator of the five with any figure, and it is not in his own words. Creator Science's show notes say the channel earned over £100,000 a month on 5 to 10 hours of his effort a week as of October 2020 (the show's summary). In the transcript he puts his clinical week at about 40 to 44 hours and describes editing in hour-long blocks before hospital shifts. By October 2020 he had editors and a small team, so the 5–10 hours is his share of a team's output; by his account to Every in September 2020 he was then making one or two videos a week, down from three in his last three months as a doctor. Nothing documents his hours in 2017 and 2018, when he edited alone.

12, 24 and 36 months (from June 2017).

  • At about 9 months (March 2018): about 4,000 subscribers, after what he calls slow uptake (his words, paraphrased, Creator Science, 15 Dec 2020). He also says his first-year goal was roughly 10,000, and that he had expected his first hundred videos to be weak (Creator Science).
  • At 12 months (June 2018): unpublished. His about page says "I hit 100,000 subscribers on YouTube in 2018", without a month. Build, a newsletter with no named author, gives 50,000 subscribers within one year and 1,000 after six months and 52 videos, but dates the start of vlogging to 1 August 2018 and 5,000 subscribers to March 2018, which cannot both hold (Build, 24 Feb 2023); its figures are not used.
  • At 24 months (June 2019): unpublished.
  • At 36 months (June 2020): unpublished. One million came in September 2020, 39 months from June 2017 (his about page; the months are my arithmetic).
  • After: 2 million in July 2021, 3 million in June 2022, 4 million in April 2023 (his about page); 6.7 million on 1 November 2026, having gained 10,000 to 30,000 a month through 2026 and nothing net in the 28 days to 1 November (vidIQ).

Breakout piece. The iPad note-taking video of June 2018, after which, he says, revenue changed gradient (Creator Science). Build adds a desk-setup video and "study with me" videos; it gives no dates.

What he stopped. Medicine: he worked two years as a junior doctor (2018 to 2020) and later describes leaving medicine for good (his about page). In 2023, by the account of Mark Ellis, who heard Ali's interview on The Colin and Samir Show, he gave up an expensive central London studio, reduced headcount and ended the then-current version of the Part-Time YouTuber Academy (Mark Ellis, 18 Aug 2023; secondary; Podwise lists the episode as "How Ali Abdaal makes $5,741,266.32 a year on YouTube", 14 Aug 2023, without notes: Podwise). A third-party summary of his video "My Take on Quitting YouTube" has him stopping videos for a break "after nearly 5 years"; the summary gives no date (Recall, summary; third-party summary).

How he monetised. Ads first: monetisation was switched on 17 April 2018, at £5 to £15 a day by his account (Creator Science). Then affiliate links, sponsorships, Skillshare, cohort courses. A third-party summary of his video of 28 December 2020 gives AdSense of about £1 in 2017, £12,329 in 2018, £33,186 in 2019 and £106,950 in 2020, and a 2020 total of about £1,013,000, of which Skillshare £350,000 and the first Academy cohort £220,000; the summary's own figures disagree in places (Summify, video of 28 Dec 2020; third-party summary). A summary of his video of 5 January 2023 gives 2022 revenue of $4,600,593, costs of $2,527,356 and profit of $2,073,237, down from $2,525,714 in 2021 and says Skillshare revenue fell because of pricing changes (Summify, video of 5 Jan 2023; third-party summary). Ads were about a tenth of 2020 income on those figures (my division: £106,950 of about £1,013,000).

What he says he got wrong. On repurposing videos into other formats: "I kind of wish I'd done that from day one" (Creator Science, 15 Dec 2020). On his sabbatical in 2020: "I thought I'd be way more productive than I've been so far" (Every, 24 Sep 2020). The 2023 cuts are reported as acknowledged setbacks by Ellis, not quoted as Ali's verdict, so they are not entered here as his words.

03Mike Boyd

Start date. vidIQ dates the channel to 23 October 2008 (vidIQ, data 28 Oct 2026). The format began with a kickflip: a MetaFilter post of 11 November 2015 links the video of him learning one, which took "five hours and forty-seven minutes" (MetaFilter, 11 Nov 2015). Net Influencer's profile (4 March 2026) says he filmed the kickflip partly to learn editing software while writing his master's thesis, and reports him saying that people responded to the very first video he posted (Net Influencer, 4 Mar 2026). The points below count from November 2015.

Platform mix. YouTube, with a second channel, Mike Boyd Climbs, launched about three years before the profile (so around 2023, my arithmetic) for longer climbing projects; he does not post on TikTok, which he says he struggles with (Net Influencer). A Skillshare class (A1 §5). OutlierKit puts 86% of the main channel's views on long-form and 14% on Shorts, and classes 90% of its content as evergreen (OutlierKit, data 11 Jul 2026).

Cadence. The About text promises a challenge "every month" (A1 §5). OutlierKit estimates about 0.3 uploads a week, roughly 1.2 a month, with the last upload 31 days before its read of 11 July 2026. vidIQ counts 197 videos on 28 October 2026; across the eleven years since the kickflip that is about 18 a year (my arithmetic, which overstates the rate slightly, since some videos predate 2015).

Hours a week. Unpublished. The profile describes two full-time people (Mike and his wife Kim, his "creative partner" in the profile's words; she films, buys props, makes thumbnails) plus an editor, after an early period in which Kim worked full time elsewhere (Net Influencer).

12, 24 and 36 months (from November 2015). All three unpublished. In the profile he says there was no grind to 10,000 subscribers. The first dated count found is The Courier's 2.7 million followers on 3 November 2021, six years in (The Courier, 3 Nov 2021); the article does not say which platform the figure counts. vidIQ gives 2.93 million subscribers, 197 videos and 476,918,067 views on 28 October 2026, with no net change between 30 September and 27 October 2026. OutlierKit gives 2.9 million, 196 videos and 475.7 million views on 11 July 2026, an average of 2.4 million views a video, and reports a significant fall in recent views, especially on climbing content.

Breakout piece. The kickflip (2015), by his own account the first video, and one that found an audience at once. The most-viewed video on 11 July 2026 was "Learn to Draw Dotted Lines on a Chalkboard || Learn Quick", 15.0 million views (OutlierKit); its date was not given.

What he stopped. Growth past a size he and Kim could run: Kim says that "Deciding not to become a huge production company is what has made it sustainable" (Net Influencer, her words). He stops projects he is not enjoying (mood over work, Kim's rule as the profile gives it) and moved longer training projects to the climbing channel when the quick-learn format felt stale.

How he monetised. Long-term brand partnerships through an agent, with gambling and alcohol refused (Net Influencer); a Skillshare class with 24,394 students on 28 October 2026 (A1 §5). Ad revenue is not discussed in any source opened.

What he says he got wrong. No statement found. The profile's only self-criticism is that he was not a good photographer before video, and that he is "really bad at" the business side (paying people, getting paid, working with management at Ziggurat XYZ), which Kim handles.

Against A1 §5. A1 §5 gives 2.93M · 197 · 476,842,692 (Social Blade, 17 Oct 2026); on 28 October 2026 vidIQ gives 2.93M · 197 · 476,918,067, which agrees (the views are 75,375 higher eleven days later, my subtraction, and match the vidIQ figure in A1's own unsure list). A1 §5's cadence (about 0.3 uploads a week, OutlierKit, 11 July 2026) agrees with the OutlierKit page read here. A1 §5 does not report the downward trend in recent views; OutlierKit does.

04Hellah Sidibe

Start date. Platform mix. YouTube first; Instagram, where his Shorty Awards entry gives more than 20,000 followers (Shorty Awards, 12th annual). One brand tie is documented: Gymshark called him "Gymshark Athlete, Hellah Sidibe" in March 2021 (Gymshark Central, 1 Mar 2021).

Cadence. The run is daily: outdoors, at least two miles (CNN, 2022). The uploads are not documented as a schedule. vidIQ counts 479 videos on 1 November 2026, about 48 a year since December 2016 (my arithmetic); the latest was posted on 25 October 2026.

Hours a week. Unpublished. InsideHook (10 August 2021) says he worked as a soccer personal trainer and coached youth athletes (InsideHook, 10 Aug 2021), so in 2021, about 46 months after the day-163 video (my arithmetic), the channel ran alongside other paid work; whether it did earlier, and how many hours it took, is not stated.

12, 24 and 36 months (from October 2017).

  • At 12 months (October 2018): unpublished.
  • At about 19 to 27 months: more than 125,000 YouTube subscribers, in his entry for the 12th Shorty Awards. The entry says he passed 730 consecutive days in May, which is May 2019; nominations for those awards were announced on 21 January 2020 (Wikipedia, 12th Shorty Awards). So the count was taken between May 2019 and January 2020 (my inference from the two dates); the entry itself carries none.
  • At 36 months (October 2020): unpublished.
  • After: 264,000 on 10 August 2021 (InsideHook); 276,000 on 22 September 2022 (CNN); more than 286,000 in an undated interview from about late 2022 or early 2023 (NAZ Elite, undated); 294,000 on 1 November 2026, with no net change in the 28 days to that date and a loss of 1,000 in September 2026 (vidIQ).

Breakout piece. The day-163 video. What he stopped. Not the streak: it was past 3,350 days in September 2026, with a tenth-anniversary celebration planned for May (2027, my arithmetic) (BoxLife, 21 Sep 2026). What he cut was load and freedom inside it. In 2022 he cut to 14 miles a week because of shin damage and kept the streak (CNN). How he monetised. The Gymshark Athlete title (2021, above) is the only brand tie documented; coaching work ran alongside (InsideHook, 2021). No source opened gives income, ad revenue or a sponsor's terms.

What he says he got wrong. No statement found about the channel. The nearest is a rule for the streak: "I give myself permission to quit every day" (CNN, 2022).

7.2 Against A1

A1 §5's numbers. Mike Boyd's counts agree (§3). Goal Guys is not covered (§1). A1 §5's claim about the five method channels (that each runs on a sentence a stranger could repeat after one video) holds for Boyd here: the premise has not changed since the 2015 kickflip, and the climbing channel took the projects that did not fit it, so the main channel's sentence stayed intact (§3).

A1 §2's working claim (a fixed, visible method is the best-supported way to keep returning viewers across a change of topic). Two of the five bear on it. Boyd changes topic every video under one procedure for eleven years, which is the case A1 already cites. Lewis is the second: his topics range from sprint power to race-walking to Kipchoge's pace, and what holds them, on my reading, is the frame of an older generalist testing himself, which the Kipchoge series made repeatable (§5). Neither case measures returning viewers, so both stay anecdote, as A1 §2 says of its own cases.

A1 §3's season label. Sidibe's streak is a single journey with a counter in it (day 163, 730 days, 3,350 days), and the counter is what the press repeats each time it covers him (§4). That is evidence that a number inside a running journey travels; it is not evidence about seasons that end and hand over, which is what A1's format does. No case among the five ends one journey and starts another under the same channel name, except Boyd's climbing, which he moved to a second channel.

7.3 Against A9

At 12 months. A9's Deliverable 1 sets the expectation for a bi-weekly channel at the end of year one as fewer than 1,000 subscribers, the outcome for more than nine channels in ten in the 12–51 upload band (rows 5 and 18). None of the five is a usable comparison at twelve months, for three reasons the sections above document:

  • Four of the five posted faster than bi-weekly. Abdaal posted at least weekly from 2017 and two to three a week later (§2), which is A9's 52-plus band (30.77% crossed 1,000 within the year). Lewis averages about 63 videos a year and Sidibe about 48, and ADHD Chatter publishes weekly (my arithmetic from the tracker video counts, which include Shorts). Only Boyd sits at or below bi-weekly, at about 18 a year.
  • Three of the five broke out on or near their first piece: Boyd's kickflip (the first video, by his account), Sidibe's day-163 video (an immediate hit in CNN's account) and, within a year, Abdaal's iPad video. A9 row 1 gives the median best-of-first-ten at 1,948 views; these are the upper tail by construction.
  • Partridge began with a public profile as the founder of two large media companies (§6), which no new channel has.

Abdaal's own figure is the closest to A9's base rates and still far above them: about 4,000 subscribers nine months in (§2), against A9 row 7's median monthly gains of 0.5 to 3.7 subscribers below 1,000. These five are the survivor cases A9 §1 warns about; their twelve-month numbers set no expectation for his channel.

At 24 and 36 months. A9 says no sourced figure gives months from 1,000 to 10,000 or from 10,000 to 50,000 (§4, §5, rows 8 and 10). This unit does not change that: of the ten 24- and 36-month points across the five, no YouTube subscriber count is dated at either point; the nearest is Sidibe's 125,000-plus, taken between May 2019 and January 2020 by inference, a self-reported awards figure. Partridge's points are listener and follower claims in other measures, Abdaal's one million came at 39 months, and the rest are unpublished. A9's gap stands.

At maturity, A9 §8 is supported. A9 §8 reports that growth slows with size and that mature channels stall. Three of the five showed no net subscriber change in the latest 28-day window their trackers give: Boyd (30 Sep to 27 Oct 2026), Sidibe (to 1 Nov 2026) and Abdaal (5 Oct to 1 Nov 2026). Lewis lost 1,000 in October 2026 after gains fell from 11,000 a month in January 2024 to nothing in mid-2026 (§5). Abdaal's 2026 gains of 10,000 to 30,000 a month on about 6.6 million are about 0.3% a month (my division: 20,000 / 6.6 million), close to A9's median of 0.24% for channels above a million (A9 §8). OutlierKit reports a downward trend in Boyd's recent views (§3).

Corrections to A9 §5 by name.

  • A9 §5 says Sidibe's first video "was an instant hit", from CNN. The Skydio post (24 Feb 2021) has him describe it catching on some months later (§4). The two accounts disagree; A9's line should carry both.
  • A9 §5 says Ben Felton's Gazette article "gives no year" for his start. Zwift Insider (21 Sep 2026) dates it to the 2020 lockdown (§1).
  • A9 §5's Mark Lewis figures (created 11 September 2018, 399,000 subscribers, 514 videos, a loss of 1,000 in the four weeks to 27 October 2026) agree with vidIQ's data of 31 October 2026 (§5), which gives the window as 3 to 30 October 2026.
  • A9 §5's HellahGood figure (294,000, FeedSpot, 12 October 2026) agrees with vidIQ's 294,000 on 1 November 2026 (§4). A9 says the channel "took roughly five years to reach 276,000"; on the dates found here, 276,000 (September 2022) was 59 months after the day-163 video (my arithmetic), and 264,000 had already been reached by August 2021.

Hours. The course's budget is 5–7 hours a week. Only Abdaal has an hours figure, 5–10 a week in October 2020, and it is the show's summary of a man with editors producing one or two videos a week (§2). For the other four, hours are unpublished. No case in this set documents a channel grown on 5–7 hours of one person's own work.

7.4 Against A6

Each rule is named by its number in A6's Deliverable 1, or by its section.

  • Long-form compounds (A6 §1.3). Boyd supports the mechanism: 86% of lifetime views on long-form and 90% of the catalogue classed as evergreen by OutlierKit, with an average of 2.4 million views on 196 videos at an upload rate of about one a month (§3). Lewis also supports it: all five of his most-viewed videos date from 2022 and 2023 and were still his top videos in October 2026 (§5). Neither source shows when the views arrived, so the size of the effect stays unmeasured, as A6 §1.3 says.
  • YouTube rule 6 (hold one recognisable format; build series from episodes that already worked). Lewis supports it directly: after the Kipchoge video of February 2022 he made two more on the same premise, 4.93 million and 2.32 million views (§5). Boyd supports the first half: one format for eleven years, with off-format projects moved to a second channel (§3).
