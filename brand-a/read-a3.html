01The capture layer: what is recorded during a journey, and the rule that nothing is filmed twice

The capture layer is everything recorded while the journey happens, before anyone decides what an episode is about. It has four streams. The brief names them; the evidence for how each is captured follows.

Dictated takes after each session. A take is a voice note recorded on the phone within minutes of finishing a session: a run, a long run, a test effort, a weigh-in. Two pieces of evidence support doing it straight after, by voice.

  • Straight after. Shiffman, Stone and Hufford's review of ecological momentary assessment argues that assessment built on "global retrospective self-reports" is open to recall bias, and that sampling experience in real time, in the place it happens, is designed to "minimize recall bias, maximize ecological validity" (Shiffman, Stone and Hufford, Annual Review of Clinical Psychology 4, 1–32, 2008; abstract read on CiteDrive). That is a methods review from clinical psychology, written for research diaries. The transfer to a creator's notes is mine: a take recorded at the kerb holds the miss as it felt; a take recorded on Sunday holds the version he has already edited in his head. The thesis says he records the process "so he does not forget it"; this is the sourced reason the recording has to be close to the event.
  • By voice. Ruan, Wobbrock, Liou, Ng and Landay measured English text entry by speech recognition at 3.0 times the rate of a smartphone keyboard, with a 20.4% lower error rate, by the project page's summary (the paper itself, arXiv 1608.07323, reports 2.93 times for English, fewer errors corrected during entry, 5.30% against 11.22%, and more left in the final text, 1.30% against 0.79%, pooled across both languages) (Ruan et al., "Speech Is 3x Faster than Typing for English and Mandarin Text Entry on Mobile Devices", Stanford HCI, 2016). That is a lab study of transcription speed with 2016 recognisers; it shows that speaking is the cheapest way to get words onto a phone, and nothing about the quality of what is said.

A take has three fixed prompts, his rule: what I did; what did not work; what changes next session. The second prompt is the one that matters to the brand. A1 §3 makes "the miss, named" a fixed element of every episode; a miss that is never recorded cannot be named a fortnight later. The three prompts also map onto the sections B3's journal-synthesis template asks for (what happened, what went wrong, what changed), so the synthesis has material in the shape it needs.

Journal entries. Once a week the takes become one dated entry. B2's routing table gives this job to Sonnet 5.5 at medium effort ("his notes or a voice transcript into a dated entry in his words"), with Haiku 5.5 for a pure tidy-up of a transcript (B2, Deliverable, row "Journal entries"). Every fortnight the entries become one record with B3's template 3, journal synthesis, on Opus 5.5 at medium (B3, Deliverable, template 3; B2's override for synthesis across several weeks). That record is what the episode script and the emails are cut from. B3's template 4 cuts the script from the takes and marks every line with its origin; B3's template 10 writes the email from the record. The chain from take to email is the workflow B5's skill automates; this unit fixes what goes into it and when.

Biometric screenshots. After a session he screenshots the watch app's summary of that session. The screenshot is for the screen: a split, a heart-rate trace, a weight on a date. The numbers that go into the record or a script come from the app's export, parsed by a script, because B2 assigns biometrics parsing to a script that Haiku 5.5 writes and maintains, on the ground that model outputs vary from run to run while code gives the same answer every time (B2 §8 and Deliverable, row "Biometrics parsing"; B1 §5). How much of his weight and health data is published is a disclosure question for A4; until A4 is filed, screenshots are captured and filed, and published only where the episode already names the number.

Phone footage. Short clips filmed inside a session: the start, a moment that went wrong, the finish, a view of the watch. How to film without breaking the session (tripod, microphone, the six-shot b-roll list) is A5's job. Two platform rules shape capture now:

  • YouTube counts a video as a Short only if it has a "square or vertical aspect ratio", up to three minutes long for uploads after 15 October 2024 (YouTube Help, Understand three-minute YouTube Shorts). A clip that will feed a Short has to allow a vertical frame. How to frame one shot for a 16:9 episode and a vertical Short is A5's question; until then, his rule is to keep the subject in the centre third of a horizontal frame so a vertical crop holds.
  • Instagram can take an account out of recommendations to new audiences if it mostly posts unoriginal content, which includes reposts, screenshots and minor edits such as watermarks, and its ranking page lists watermarked Reels and Reels already posted on Instagram among the causes of reduced distribution (Instagram's original content guidelines and Instagram Ranking Explained, as A6 §2.2 reports them). His own footage is original material. A Short downloaded back from YouTube or another app carries that app's marks; the Instagram version is exported from his own editor.

The rule that nothing is filmed twice. Every clip, take and screenshot is captured once, filed once, and is the only source for every format that uses it: the episode, its Short, the Instagram posts, the email. Nothing is shot separately for a platform. Three reasons, each sourced:

  1. Hours. A2's allocation budgets Instagram at four quarter-hour posts a fortnight "from the episode's footage and stills; nothing shot separately" and the Short at half an hour "cut from its footage" (A2, Deliverable 2). Neither line has time for a second shoot. Filming a Reel separately would cost a session's attention or an extra outing, which the 5–7 hour week does not contain.
  2. Originality. Instagram's recommendation eligibility rests on posting his own original material (above). Footage he shot himself and cut for Instagram meets that rule; reposting another app's export does not.
  3. The record. The thesis says failures stay in the record. A scene re-filmed because the first take looked bad replaces what happened with a staged version of it. Re-shooting a miss is the specific thing the brand promises not to do (A1 §3, "the miss, named"). How far this goes for personal entries is A4's record rules.

The one thing filmed on purpose, outside a session, is the episode's own frame (the opening, the syllabus on screen, the bridges between scenes, the ending). That happens in the fortnight's single filming window (§6), and it is filmed once.

Filing. One folder per session, named by date and session type, holding that session's take, clips and screenshots; one folder per season. That convention is his, untested; its purpose is that the fortnightly synthesis and the edit can find every piece by date without a search. Where the folders live, and how the chain reads them, is B5's.

02The recap layer: one long-form episode per milestone

The recap layer is the long-form episode: the home format on YouTube, the journey's record, and the only asset in the system that compounds (A2 §4; A6 §1.3). The brief sets one episode per milestone at 10 to 20 minutes. Both numbers need to meet the filed units.

Length: 10 to 12 minutes, planned at 10. The brief's range runs to 20 minutes. A2 §6 prices editing with a vendor rule of thumb of "30–60 minutes per finished minute" (Tasty Edits, vendor, no stated method, as A2 reports it): a 10-minute episode is 5 to 10 hours of editing, a 20-minute one 10 to 20. At the low end, a 20-minute episode alone would fill the fortnight's 10 to 14 hours (my arithmetic). A2's allocation therefore sets the episode at about 10 minutes and plans on 5 hours of editing; A9's watch-hour arithmetic uses 12 minutes. This unit keeps the brief's floor and A2's plan: about 10 minutes, 12 at most, until A5's editing workflow shows a lower cost per minute in the hours log. A 15- or 20-minute finale is possible only by taking hours from somewhere else, and the calendar in the deliverable does not do it.

Milestone and cadence. "One episode per milestone" and "one episode every two weeks" are the same rule only if the milestones fall every two weeks. The course goal fixes the cadence at bi-weekly for twelve months, and the filed units hold the date fixed: A6's rule 7 publishes the next episode on its normal date after a missed fortnight, with no catch-up doubles, and A2's bad-fortnight order shrinks the episode before the date moves (A2, Deliverable 2). The reasons are vendor data: AIR Media-Tech's 18,000-channel study associates going dark for 14 or more days three or more times a year with fewer views per video, and a predictable schedule with more views per subscriber than the same volume on random days (correlational, larger channels; AIR Media-Tech, 8 Jun 2026, as A6 §1.3 and A9 §8 report it). So the milestone is defined by the syllabus at the grain of the fortnight: the syllabus written before the season marks one step per fortnight (a block finished, a test run, a weigh-in, a first long run at a set distance), and the episode is that step. That definition is his rule. A journey whose real milestones come every six weeks still gets an episode every two, and the episode in between is the step the syllabus named for it, misses included.

Structure. Four of A1's fixed elements live in the episode (A1 §3): the season label in the title, the playlist and the first ten seconds; the syllabus on screen in the season's first episode, and the episode's place in it in every later one ("week 6 of 20"); one miss named; and, at the season's end, the artefact. The first 30 seconds deliver what the title promised, which is the window Studio's Intro metric measures (A6, playbook YouTube 2). Chapters make the syllabus position visible in the player: YouTube requires the first timestamp at 00:00, at least three timestamps in ascending order, and chapters of at least 10 seconds (YouTube Help, Video chapters).

What the episode costs, per fortnight (A2's figures, Deliverable 2): outline 1.0 h, filming 1.0 h, editing 5.0 h, title, thumbnail and upload 1.0 h; 8.0 hours in all. This unit puts the fortnightly synthesis inside the outline hour, since the synthesis is run by template and read, not written.

03The derivative layer: Shorts, Instagram, the weekly email

Everything in this layer is cut from the capture layer and the episode. None of it has material of its own (§1).

Shorts: one per episode, where the brief says two or three. This is the explicit departure from the brief. A2 §4 sets out the two vendor datasets. AIR Media-Tech's Shorts study (18,000 channels, March 2025 to March 2026) put "the optimal Shorts ratio" at 25% to 40% of uploads and found Shorts "crowding out" long-form above 55%; Metricool's 799,718 videos found long-form views per video highest at around five Shorts a month and back near the no-Shorts level at 35 or more (AIR Media-Tech, 28 May 2026; Metricool, 29 Jul 2026; both vendor and correlational, as A2 reports them). At one episode a fortnight, two Shorts per episode make Shorts 67% of uploads and three make 75%, both above AIR's crowding line; one Short per episode is 50%, under it (A2 §4, my arithmetic there). A2 concludes that "A3 should take the one-Short figure from here", and this unit does. A9 adds a reason to hold the ratio once set: channels that shifted their Shorts share by more than 30 points had lower median subscribers (A9 §8, AIR, correlational, cause unresolved), so the share is fixed at the start of the season and not changed mid-season.

The Short is the episode's strongest 30 to 60 seconds, recut vertical, with the episode set as its related video. YouTube's related-video option "allows you to showcase your Videos, Shorts, and Live in the Shorts player"; it is chosen from the creator's own channel, needs advanced feature access, and the linked video must be public or unlisted (YouTube Help, Add a related video to your YouTube Shorts). No source found measures how many Short viewers follow that link (A2 §2 found none for TikTok to YouTube either), so the Short's views are recorded and no decision rests on them in year one (A2, Deliverable 2).

Instagram: two posts a week, the floor of the brief's two to three. A2 sets two a week from the episode's footage and stills, at a quarter-hour each. The evidence for frequency is Buffer's fixed-effects analysis: weekly follower growth of 0.12% in weeks with 1–2 posts and 0.26% with 3–5, against weeks with none (Buffer, 13 Aug 2025, vendor, feed posts only, as A2 §6 and A6 §2.3 report it). A third post would move him into the higher band and cost another half-hour a fortnight that the calendar below does not have; A2's allocation stands. What each post is follows A6's playbook: a Reel for strangers, a carousel for followers (A6, Instagram rules 1 and 3). Instagram lets professional accounts schedule posts, Reels and carousels in the app up to 75 days ahead, as TechCrunch reported at launch; the Help Center page (help.instagram.com/iphone-app/439971288310029) exists but its body could not be read on 29 October 2026, so the current limit is unconfirmed (TechCrunch, 8 Nov 2022); that is what lets four posts be made in one sitting a fortnight and go out on their days.

The weekly email: one, short, on Substack. A2 puts the list and the written archive on Substack and sets the email weekly: the episode week carries the video and one lesson, the other week a short note from the journey, 0.75 hours each (A2, Deliverable 2). The 45-minute figure is an anecdote, Justin Welsh's account of his own newsletter (Justin Welsh, 6 Apr 2024, as A2 §6 reports it); here the fortnight's record supplies the material, and B3's template 10 writes the draft on Sonnet 5.5 at low effort for him to correct. The template's order holds the season's rule (§4): the lead journey first, one line on each other live journey only if something moved, then the link. "Short" is set at 300 words or fewer; that number is his rule, set so that the email fits its 0.75 hours. The email is where the journeys that do not lead the season (the weight, the history course, karate coaching) stay in the record between their seasons.

Comments. Two fixed 30-minute slots a fortnight, closed outside them (A2, Deliverable 2): responding to comments is on YouTube's own list of ways to build casual and regular viewers (YouTube Help, answer 13615784), and the fixed slots are the distancing practice A9 §8 takes from the ASQ study of large creators. The calendar puts both slots in the publishing week, when the episode is new.

04What a season is, and why one journey per season

A season is one journey, from the syllabus to the artefact, published as a numbered series: one season label, one playlist, one syllabus shown in the first episode, a fortnightly episode for each step, and a finale that ships the artefact and hands over to the next season. That definition assembles A1 §3's fixed elements (karate was season one, per the course's Frame). A season starts when the syllabus is written and ends when the artefact exists: for the half marathon, a race result; for the history course, the course itself; for karate, the Athens tournament. Its length is set by the journey. A half marathon run in spring or summer 2027 makes season two roughly 13 to 20 episodes at bi-weekly cadence (my arithmetic on 26 to 40 weeks from late November 2026, assuming a race between late May and the end of August; a race in late March would give about 17 weeks and 8 episodes); the race date is not set in any filed unit, and the syllabus fixes it.

Why one journey per season. Four lines of evidence, each at its own strength:

  1. Platform-official. YouTube's tips for growing casual and regular viewers advise content "more consistent and similar in topic or format", and building series from topics that already worked (YouTube Help, answer 13615784). A season holds both for its length: one topic, one format. A6's rule 6 is "hold one recognisable format per season". No effect size is given.
  2. The positioning. A1 §2's working claim is that a fixed, visible method is the best-supported way to keep returning viewers across a change of topic. The season is the unit in which the topic stays still long enough for a stranger to learn the method; across seasons, only the method stays. If every episode led with a different journey, the format would carry the channel with no help from the topic, and A1's only evidence that a channel can carry that is five survivors with millions of subscribers (A1 §5), with no dataset behind them (A1 §2).
  3. The brief's own register. "Each piece leads with one journey" (the brand thesis). A season extends that from the piece to the series.
  4. Hours. One lead journey means one stream of footage to capture, log and edit. Filming two journeys to episode standard in the same fortnight would double the capture layer's filing and the edit's selects without adding an hour to the week (A2's allocation has none spare).

What the season does not do is stop the other journeys. The weight loss, the history course and his karate training and coaching continue; they are captured in the takes and the journal (§1) and appear in the weekly email's second line (§3). They lead no episode until they lead a season. Weight is the case to watch: in the half-marathon season it is likely to bear on the running (my inference, unsourced), so it may appear inside an episode as a number on screen, subject to A4's disclosure policy.

What the season change tests. A1 §2 names the change from karate to the half marathon as the first point at which the channel can show whether viewers return across journeys, read from the Regular-viewer segment, which YouTube defines as viewers who watched in more than six months of the past year and says has no effect on reach (YouTube Help, answer 10246996, as A6 §1.2 and A9 §2 report it). The segment is empty before month six by construction, so the test is read at A9's third checkpoint, as a baseline with no benchmark (A9, Deliverable 2, quarter 3).

05How the Athens finale hands over to the half-marathon season

Season one is "Road to WIKF Global Cup 2026": two episodes published on 11 and 13 October 2026, both titled with the suffix "Karate Vlog: Road to WIKF Global Cup 2026, Ep. n" (A1 §6). The tournament in Athens closed it, and with it his competitive karate (reader profile). What it lacks, by A1 §6's reading, is the syllabus and the artefact. The finale cannot add a syllabus after the fact; it can ship the artefact, and it is the last chance to.

The Athens edit is season one's third and last episode. Its material was captured in October: the footage, whatever takes and entries exist, the result. What happened in Athens is not in any filed unit, and this unit states none of it. Built from B3's worked example for template 3 (the season's journal entries synthesised into one dated record, for "the retrospective episode and its weekly email"), the finale carries, in this order (his structure, from A1 §3's elements):

  1. The season label in the first ten seconds and the title suffix "Ep. 3", matching the two published episodes.
  2. What the season set out to do, as he stated it at the time (episode one's stated goal was to have fun and "get the rust out"; A1 §6).
  3. Athens, from the footage.
  4. The miss, named, at the weight the record gives it (B3 template 3: "Add no lesson to it").
  5. The artefact: the result and what the season produced. Competitive karate has ended; the episode says so once.
  6. The handover, in the last minute: season two named, its target (sub-1:45 in a half marathon in spring or summer 2027), and the date of its first episode.

Mechanics of the handover, each on a documented feature:

  • Playlist. A public playlist "Road to WIKF Global Cup 2026" holds episodes 1 to 3 in order, so a viewer who arrives from season two can find season one as a unit (A1 §6, his rule from YouTube's series tip).
  • End screen. End screens go in "the last 5–20 seconds of a video", need a video of at least 25 seconds, can feature "your most recent upload, best for viewer, or a specific video", and are edited on a published video from Studio's Content page (YouTube Help, Add end screens to videos). The finale goes out with the season-one playlist on its end screen; the day the season-two opener is published, the finale's end screen is changed to that specific video. The handover then works in both directions: the opener's description links the season-one playlist.
  • Channel description. A1's deliverable copy already reads "Season 1 was karate … Season 2 is the half marathon". It goes up the week before the finale is published.
  • The email. The finale's week carries the email that closes season one; the opener's week carries the first email of season two, built from B3's worked example for template 10 ("the first email of the half-marathon season"). On a list that starts near zero (A9 row 13), the email reaches few people, and it is the only channel where everyone on it chose to receive the handover.
  • The Short. The finale's Short has the finale as its related video; the opener's Short has the opener.

The season-two opener is the first episode with the syllabus on screen in full (A1 §3). The syllabus is the half-marathon plan he writes: the target, the race once chosen, the blocks by fortnight, how progress is measured and when the season ends. Writing the training plan is part of the journey, outside the 5–7 publishing hours; putting it on screen is inside them, in the opener's outline hour. The opener is also the first season-two capture test: its footage comes from the first sessions of the plan, captured by §1's rules.

What A9's dates become. A9 dates its checkpoints from upload one in the week of 3 November 2026 and says to shift every date by the same number of days if upload one comes later (A9, Deliverable 2). In the calendar below, the first upload of the bi-weekly cadence is the Athens finale on Tuesday 17 November 2026, fourteen days after 3 November. The quarterly reviews therefore move to Tuesday 16 February, Tuesday 18 May, Tuesday 17 August and Tuesday 16 November 2027 (my arithmetic, 91 days per quarter from 17 November). The quarter-3 review on 17 August 2027 is where the season change is first read.

06Batching for an ADHD brain: one filming window, one editing window per fortnight

The brief asks for two fixed windows a fortnight: one to film, one to edit. No study found tests how adults with ADHD should batch creative production, so the windows are his rule. Four bodies of evidence bear on the design, none of them on content work, and each transfer below is marked as mine.

Planning skills delivered as fixed routines help adults with ADHD in trials. Safren and colleagues randomised 86 medication-treated adults with ADHD and persistent symptoms to cognitive behavioural therapy or to relaxation with educational support; the CBT's first module trained organisation and planning, including the use of a calendar and task lists, and a second taught reducing distractibility, including timing attention spans. Responder rates were 53% against 23% on the Clinical Global Impression scale and 67% against 33% on the ADHD Rating Scale, with gains maintained at 6 and 12 months among responders (Safren et al., JAMA 304(8), 2010). Solanto and colleagues randomised 88 adults to a 12-week group meta-cognitive therapy teaching time management, organisation and planning skills, or to supportive therapy, and found a group effect on response with an odds ratio of 5.41 (95% CI 1.77–16.55) (Solanto et al., American Journal of Psychiatry 167(8), 2010). Both trials test therapy packages on symptoms; neither isolates a calendar, and neither measures output. What they support is the general approach: external structure, fixed in a calendar, is an evidence-based tool for adults with ADHD.

A plan that names the when and where gets acted on more often. Gollwitzer and Sheeran's meta-analysis of 94 independent tests found that implementation intentions, if-then plans that specify when, where and how to act, had a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment, d = .65 (Gollwitzer and Sheeran, Advances in Experimental Social Psychology 38, 2006). In children with ADHD, the same kind of plan improved response inhibition on a Go/NoGo task to the level of children without ADHD (Gawrilow and Gollwitzer, Cognitive Therapy and Research 32, 2008). The transfer is mine: a window written as "Saturday, 09:00, the desk, the edit opens" is an implementation intention; "edit this week sometime" is a goal intention, the weaker form those studies compare against.

Switching between kinds of work has a cost, paid at the switch. In Monsell, Sumner and Waters's task-switching experiments, with predictable switching "the cost was limited to the first trial of a run" (Monsell, Sumner and Waters, Memory & Cognition 31(3), 2003). That is a laboratory digit-classification task, measured in milliseconds, whose participants the cited record does not describe; nothing in it concerns ADHD. My inference: a fortnight that contains one editing run pays the cost of getting into the edit once, where four evening sessions pay it four times. The size of that cost for video editing is unmeasured.

Long windows meet hyperfocus, and that cuts both ways. Hupfeld, Abagis and Shah's online self-report study (a pilot of 251 adults and a preregistered replication of 372) found that higher ADHD symptoms were associated with more frequent hyperfocus, including on hobbies and screen time (Hupfeld, Abagis and Shah, ADHD Attention Deficit and Hyperactivity Disorders, 2018). A five-hour editing window is the kind of task that can absorb him; it can also run to eight hours and take the evening and the next day with it. The study measures frequency, not consequences; the risk is my reading. The window therefore has a hard end, and an overrun is logged rather than absorbed (A9's read 2 counts weeks over 7 hours; three in a quarter trigger "cut the load").

The design, from those four. His rules:

  • Two windows a fortnight, on fixed days, written in the calendar as if-then plans. Filming: Saturday of the first week, one hour. Editing: Saturday of the second week, five hours with a hard stop, breaks inside it. Nothing about the episode is edited outside the window.
  • Everything else in fixed short slots. Packaging and publishing on Tuesday, the Short and the Instagram batch on Wednesday, the email on Thursday, the outline on Friday before the filming window, the two comment slots in the publishing week. Each is under 1.5 hours, so each starts and ends in one sitting.
  • Capture is in the session, never a separate job. The take is recorded before leaving the place the session happened, which is the only capture cue that does not depend on remembering later (§1).
  • The pipeline overlaps by one fortnight. An episode is filmed in week A, edited in week B and published on the Tuesday that starts the next week A, while the next episode is being outlined and filmed. The episode that publishes is always already edited; a bad editing Saturday delays nothing that week.
  • Scheduling removes the publishing moment from the edit. YouTube lets a private video be scheduled to go public at a set date and time from the upload page (YouTube Help, Schedule video publish time); Instagram schedules posts up to 75 days ahead (§3). Publication happens at the planned hour whether or not he is at a desk.
  • Studio stays closed between checkpoints (A9, Deliverable 2, "How to run them"; A6's metrics sheet). The windows are for making things; numbers are read four times a year.
  • The worst week is planned for. Amy Suto's rule is a cadence you can hit "on your worst week, not your best one" (anecdote, as A2 §6 reports it); A2's cut order (Instagram, then the Short, then the off-week email shrinks, then the episode shortens) keeps the date. The calendar applies the fourth cut in advance to the Christmas fortnight.

What this costs, per fortnight, against A2. A2's allocation is 12.0 planned hours a fortnight and gives no hours to capture. Capture and the weekly journal entry are real work: about a quarter-hour of takes and screenshots across a week's sessions and a quarter-hour to read and correct the week's entry (my estimate). This unit adds 1.0 hour a fortnight for them, so the planned fortnight is 13.0 hours, 6.5 a week, inside 5–7 with less slack than A2 had. Every other line is A2's. The hours log at A9's checkpoints tests every estimate.

07Deliverable 1The journey template, as a reusable checklist

For any season, from the first week of a journey to the handover. Each line names its basis; (his rule) marks a convention no source sets. Hours are per fortnight and come from A2's allocation, plus the capture line from §6.

A · Before the season starts

  • [ ] Name the season and number it: "Season n: <journey>". The label goes in every title, the playlist name and the first ten seconds of every episode (A1 §3).
  • [ ] Write the syllabus: the target, the end event or artefact and its date, one step per fortnight, how progress is measured (A1 §3; §2 above). Writing it is journey time, outside the publishing hours.
  • [ ] Mark the milestone for each fortnight in the syllabus; each episode is the step its fortnight names (§2, his rule).
  • [ ] Count the episodes: weeks from opener to finale divided by two (§4).
  • [ ] Create the season's public playlist, empty (A1 §6).
  • [ ] Update the YouTube channel description and the Instagram bio to name the current season (A1, Deliverable).
  • [ ] Fix the fortnight in the calendar as if-then plans: filming window Saturday of week A, 1 h; editing window Saturday of week B, 5 h with a hard stop; publish Tuesday of week A; Short and Instagram batch Wednesday of week A; email Thursday every week; outline Friday of week A; two 30-minute comment slots in week A (§6; A2, Deliverable 2).
  • [ ] Set the Shorts share for the season: one Short per episode, 50% of uploads, and leave it there until the season ends (A2 §4; A9 §8).
  • [ ] Create the season folder; one sub-folder per session, named by date and session type (§1, his rule).
  • [ ] Check the disclosure policy for anything the season will show: weight, health data, other people (A4).
  • [ ] Shift A9's checkpoint dates to the day of the season's first upload if this is the channel's first season on the cadence (A9, Deliverable 2).

B · Every session (capture)
