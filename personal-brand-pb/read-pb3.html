01An audience described by what it does and needs

Every source in this unit was opened on 12 November 2026, and every platform rule, feature, survey figure and benchmark carries that date unless another is given. The evidence labels are the ones PB1 §1 defines (platform-official, vendor, correlational, survivor sample, peer-reviewed, anecdote, derived), plus four this unit needs:

  • Large survey: a survey of a probability or weighted national sample by a research organisation or regulator, with its sample size and fieldwork dates.
  • Official statistics: figures published by a national or EU statistics body.
  • Sponsored survey: a survey run by a polling firm for a company that sells something related to its findings.
  • Own data: the analytics an account's platforms show its owner. It describes that account's audience and nobody else's.

A trait with none of these behind it is labelled unsourced, and the profile says so.

The running example is Mara Venn (Mara, fictional): 38, a full-time accountant who restores mid-century furniture at weekends and wants to teach restoration to people who have no workshop, with no YouTube subscribers, 140 Instagram followers, a phone and 6 hours a week. Her numbers are illustrations; nothing she does is evidence about anyone else.

Why behaviour comes before demographics. Both platforms say they choose what to show a viewer from that viewer's activity. Instagram lists the signals for Reels "roughly in order of importance", starting with "Your activity" and then "Your history of interacting with the person who posted" (Instagram, Instagram Ranking Explained, Adam Mosseri, 31 May 2023). YouTube's help centre says "The algorithm aims to understand a viewer's interest across Shorts, long videos, live streams, and posts", and that "individual preferences vary, not just by topic but also by format" (YouTube Help, Good to know about recommendations, answer 16559651). Platform-official, both. Instagram's four headings for Reels signals do not include the viewer's age or gender, and the YouTube passage names interest and format. So the trait that decides whether a stranger is shown a video is what that stranger already watches and does, and the trait that decides whether they stay is whether the video serves something they need. Age, gender and country still matter for checking that a platform holds enough people of the kind you want, which is what §2 does. They do not tell you what to make.

A useful audience description therefore answers four questions, each with its own evidence:

  1. What are they trying to do? (Restore a chair without a workshop; learn a language before a move; get faster at a 10 km race.)
  2. What stands in their way? (No space, no tools, no time, no one to ask.)
  3. Where do they already look for help, and in what format? (YouTube how-to videos, Instagram, forums, books.)
  4. How many of them are there, and on which platform?

How each trait can be evidenced, and the label it carries.

Kind of evidence What it can show What it cannot show Label

What your own data shows, and when. Instagram's account insights break down by "age, city, country, gender", but follower demographics are "Not returned if the IG User has less than 100 followers" (Meta for Developers, Instagram account insights reference; platform-official; the reference describes the data available to apps, and the app's own Insights screen was not checked). YouTube's Audience tab has charts for "Your audience by age", "Your audience by gender" and "Your audience by country or region" (YouTube Help, Tips to learn who your viewers are, answer 13615375), and reports including "Channels that your audience watches", "What your audience watches" and "Formats your viewers watch on YouTube" (YouTube Help, Understand your YouTube audience, answer 9314416). YouTube says some data is limited "when metrics do not meet a certain threshold", that the thresholds are not published, and that a channel may see "Not enough demographic data" (YouTube Help, Understand limited data in YouTube Analytics, answer 9101241). Platform-official. A new channel will see little of its audience for months; the profile below starts from outside evidence and is replaced trait by trait as the platforms report.

02Who uses YouTube and who uses Instagram

The largest current survey that splits use of both platforms by age, gender, education and income is Pew Research Center's fact sheet of 19 October 2026: its National Public Opinion Reference Survey of 5,511 US adults, fieldwork 30 January to 18 June 2026, margin of error ±1.8 percentage points (Pew Research Center, Americans' Use of Social Media in 2026, fact sheet, 19 Oct 2026). The measure is whether an adult says they ever use the platform. Large survey; American.

What the table says for a small account:

  • YouTube reaches most adults in every group Pew reports, from 65% of those 65 and over to 95% of those 18 to 29, and the gap between men (85%) and women (83%) is two points. An audience defined by a need rather than by age is likely to be on YouTube whatever its age (inference from ever-use; how often they watch is not in this table).
  • Instagram is smaller in every group and leans young and female: 79% of 18–29s against 21% of those 65 and over, 56% of women against 45% of men. At 50–64, Instagram reaches about half as many adults as YouTube (derived: 41% against 85%).
  • Both lean towards higher education and income, YouTube by 13 points and Instagram by 17 points between the lowest and highest education groups shown (derived).

Outside the US. Ofcom, the UK regulator, reported on 10 December 2025 that YouTube is "used by 94% of adults" in the UK, and that "Time on YouTube is increasing, reaching an average of 51 minutes a day, not including the TV set" (Ofcom, From apps to AI search: how the UK goes online in 2025, 10 Dec 2025). Regulator data; the page names no panel or fieldwork dates and gives no Instagram figure. For the EU, this unit found no survey that splits use of YouTube and Instagram by age across member states; the figures that circulate are platforms' advertising-audience counts, which count accounts reachable by ads rather than people, and the one global page checked gave no date for them. The EU therefore has no row in the profile below.

Two limits on all of this. Ever-use is a low bar: someone who opened Instagram once in a year counts. And platform population says nothing about whether the people you want are findable there. That depends on what each platform does with a new account's posts, which is §3.

03What each platform does for a small account

Discovery: both platforms show a new account to strangers

YouTube. Todd Beaupré, YouTube's senior director of growth and discovery, said in an interview published by Creator Insider on 1 September 2026 that inside a video's first hour "videos are reaching people who've never watched the channel before", and "it's not only your subscribers who will see it" (PPC Land, 2 Sep 2026, quoting the interview; PPC Land's text rests on the interview's machine transcript, which it notes misspells the participants' names). Platform statement via trade press. The same interview gives the other half: "probably all your videos have about maybe a 10% or less click-through rate" among subscribers, and "even your best videos, 90% of the time, your subscribed audience isn't deciding to watch" (same source). YouTube's help centre says "Viewers, on average, are subscribed to dozens of channels and may not return for every new upload" (YouTube Help, performance FAQ, answer 141805). Platform-official.

Instagram. Instagram's own account of its surfaces separates them by who they serve. In Feed, a viewer sees "a mix of content from the accounts you've chosen to follow" and "recommended content from accounts we think you'll enjoy"; "The stories you see are from people you've chosen to follow, as well as ads"; in Explore, "Most of the content you'll see in Explore is from people you don't follow"; and in Reels, "the majority of what you see is from accounts you don't follow" (Instagram, Instagram Ranking Explained, 31 May 2023). Platform-official; three and a half years old, and PB7 reads Instagram's current statements. In January 2025 Adam Mosseri, head of Instagram, said "The top three signals that matter most for ranking are watch time, likes and sends", and "Likes are slightly more important for connected content, and sends are slightly more important for unconnected content" (Social Media Today, 22 Jan 2025, quoting Mosseri's video). Platform statement via trade press. Instagram also offers trial reels, which "will be shown to non-followers first", with views, likes, comments and shares shown about 24 hours after posting (Meta, Test content with non-followers using trial reels, 10 Dec 2024, updated 26 Jun 2025); Meta said in December 2024 that they would be "globally available to all eligible creators" within weeks and leaves eligibility to its Help Centre, which was not checked. Instagram said on 30 April 2024 that each eligible piece of content "is shown to a small audience that we think will enjoy it", and that "the top performing set of reels are shown to a slightly wider audience", then to a wider one again (Instagram for Creators, Helping Creators Find New Audiences, 30 Apr 2024). Platform-official.

What reaches strangers on Instagram, by format. Fanpage Karma, a social-media analytics vendor, analysed "more than 3 million" Reels, carousels and image posts published between January and April 2026, and reports reach per post as a share of the account's followers. For accounts under 10,000 followers, its smallest band, median reach was 4.9% of followers for Reels, 3.5% for image posts and 2.6% for carousels; image posts had the higher interaction rate (0.51% against 0.34% for Reels); and Reels reached "nearly three times as many non-followers as carousels" with no exact figure given (Fanpage Karma, Reel, carousel, or image post, 7 Aug 2026, modified 28 Aug 2026). Vendor data; the number of accounts is not stated, and the band holds every account under 10,000 followers. Read at Mara's 140 followers, 4.9% is about 7 accounts a Reel and 2.6% about 4 a carousel (derived). That arithmetic is an illustration of scale, nothing more: the median of a band that wide does not describe an account at its bottom.

Reach to followers against reach to strangers

On both platforms, following an account does not deliver its posts to most followers. YouTube's subscriber click-through is "maybe a 10% or less" by its own director's estimate, with no sample given (above). On Instagram, Fanpage Karma's "reach within the existing community" for accounts under 10,000 followers was 2.1% for image posts and 2.0% for Reels (same source; vendor; the article does not define the measure further). These two figures measure different things (a click on an impression against a reach) and cannot be set side by side as rates. What they share is the direction: for a small account the follower count is a weak promise of delivery on either platform, and each new post is judged largely on its own with people who do not follow the account yet.

Depth: what each platform lets one viewer go further into

YouTube's help centre says, of new channels, "Viewers are naturally drawn to channels that have already demonstrated expertise or a clear niche", "For new channels, gaining this credibility and building a community takes time", and "A key factor here is developing a critical mass of content", since "having a substantial library of other high-quality content allows them to delve deeper into your channel" (YouTube Help, answer 16559651). Platform-official. It also names, among reasons old videos gain views, "New viewers are discovering your channel and 'binging' your old videos" and "You released a new video in a series, prompting viewers to go back and watch older episodes" (YouTube Help, answer 141805). Platform-official. A ten-minute video asks for about a fifth of the 51 minutes a UK adult spends on YouTube a day (derived; Ofcom, above).

The Instagram pages checked for this unit make no comparable statement about older posts or about viewers working back through an account; PB7 reads Instagram's documentation in full.

How long a post keeps working

YouTube long-form. YouTube says "Many viewers don't watch videos in chronological order or decide what to watch based on when a video was published" and that its system "aims to deliver the right videos to the right viewers, regardless of when that video was uploaded" (answer 141805). Metricool, from 799,718 videos on 71,177 accounts, says a video usually peaks in its first three days, that roughly 83% of interactions arrive within the first ten days, and that "YouTube content keeps earning views for months, long after most social posts fade" (Metricool, YouTube Trends 2026, 29 Jul 2026, modified 4 Aug 2026). Vendor data; interactions, which are not views, and no share of views after month one is given.

YouTube Shorts. OverseerOS followed 9,982 Shorts from 676 channels, each at least 30 days old when first observed, for about eight days: 90.5% gained views, the median gain was 57, and "Among 1,546 Shorts starting below 1,000 views, none gained another 1,000 views during that interval" (OverseerOS, Do YouTube Shorts get views after 30 days?, 8 Nov 2026). Vendor data; the authors say "The research corpus is selected, not random."

Instagram. No Instagram page found states how long a post keeps being distributed. Socialinsider, from 25,503,389 Reels of brand accounts with 1,000 to 1 million followers in 2025, calls the first five days "the golden window" and says that "After 10 days from being published, Reels views decline more sharply, decreasing by 45%" (Socialinsider, How long does it take for Reels to get views, 21 Jan 2026, updated 6 Nov 2026). Vendor data, and internally unclear: the same page gives a second sample (40 million Reels from 450,000 accounts), and its averages by day are not defined as cumulative or daily. A second vendor's lifespan figures rest on "literally hundreds of thousands of posts" with no sample or period (Measure Studio, 3 Jul 2025) and are not used. On the evidence found, an Instagram post is planned as doing its work in its first week or two; a YouTube long-form video is planned as an entry that can keep drawing viewers for as long as it satisfies them.

04Short-form viewers becoming long-form viewers: what is measured

A common plan is to let short pieces find strangers and send them to long ones. This section sorts what is measured from what is assumed, platform by platform.

Inside YouTube, Shorts to long-form.

  • What YouTube says. "For instance, a viewer that often engages with your long-form videos might see your Shorts recommended", but "it is ultimately up to the viewers to engage with the content across formats", and posting both "will not inherently confuse the algorithm or negatively impact a channel's overall performance" (YouTube Help, answer 16559651). Platform-official. Net Influencer reported that Todd Sherman, YouTube's Shorts product lead, said on Creator Insider that the company had not observed Shorts harming long-form performance; the article gives no direct quote and no data (Net Influencer, 23 May 2025).
  • The mechanism exists. A creator can add "a clickable link below your channel handle" in the Shorts player that points to one of the channel's videos; it "is only available with advanced feature access" (YouTube Help, Add a related video to your YouTube Shorts, answer 14075157). YouTube's own guide to the feature, dated 14 July 2026, gives no figure for how many viewers follow the link (YouTube Blog, related videos traffic guide). Platform-official; the rate is unpublished.
  • What vendors measured. In vidIQ's study of new channels with at least ten public uploads of one format in their first 90 days, median 30-day views rose from about 180 on the first long-form upload to about 272 on the tenth (137,355 channels), while Shorts went from about 1,362 to 1,344 (367,243 channels), which vidIQ calls "no incremental growth, in fact a slight decline over time" (vidIQ, YouTube First 10 Videos Study, 28 Jul 2026, updated 30 Jul 2026). Vendor data; survivor sample; the page reports no finding on Shorts leading to subscribers or long-form views. AIR Media-Tech, from 18,000 English-language channels with at least 40 videos between March 2025 and March 2026, found mixed channels had more median subscribers than long-form-only channels in Fitness (41%), Business (39%) and Education (37% at smaller sizes), but "Home and DIY shows near-identical performance for Long-Only and Long+Short Mix across most segments": 95,100 against 94,600 at 100,000 to 1 million, 251,500 against 256,000 at 1 to 10 million (AIR Media-Tech, 28 May 2026). Vendor data; correlational, which the page says; channels far larger than a new one. The page also states a "0.04% to 0.5% Shorts-to-long-form conversion" for channels that set up the link well, with no method or sample, and says its dataset "measured subscriber counts, not cross-format viewing rates directly"; that figure is not used here. Metricool's channels posting around five Shorts a month averaged 8,789 views per long-form video against a global average of 5,985 (correlation, by its own account; Metricool, 29 Jul 2026). OverseerOS, comparing 370 long-form videos from 37 channels before and after their first Short, found "no consistent evidence that simply adding Shorts automatically damages long-form YouTube performance" and says it "cannot observe competitor impression allocation", because impressions are private Analytics data; it reports no cross-format viewing rate (OverseerOS, 1 Sep 2026). Vendor data; small sample.

Across platforms, Instagram to YouTube. No source found measures how many people who watch a creator's Reel go on to watch the same creator's YouTube video. The mechanics of sending them (the profile link PB2 set, the caption, Stories) are PB7's.

What this adds up to. Measured: Shorts' own views do not grow across a new channel's first ten (vendor, survivors); mixing Shorts goes with more subscribers in some niches and with no difference in Home and DIY (vendor, correlational, large channels); YouTube says Shorts do not harm a channel. Not measured anywhere found: the share of Shorts viewers who watch a long-form video by the same creator, and the share of Instagram viewers who move to YouTube. A plan that depends on either rate rests on an unsourced number. The allocation in the deliverable gives Shorts no hours at 6 hours a week for that reason, and treats Instagram as valuable in itself (reach to strangers, contact with followers), with any traffic it sends to YouTube counted as a bonus that Mara's own data may show.

05The email list: the channel you own

An email goes to every address on the list. No ranking feed sits between the sender and the subscriber, although inbox providers' filters still decide which folder it lands in, and this unit found no large dataset on that for small senders. A list held with an email service does not depend on either platform account.

What is measured.

  • Opens. beehiiv reports an average open rate of 41.24% in 2025 across 28 billion emails sent on its platform, against 37.98% for 2024 in its text (37.67% in its own graphic), and says Apple's Mail Privacy Protection "auto-loads images", making the open rate less reliable; beehiiv says it "proactively filters out most bot activity" (beehiiv, The State of Newsletters 2026, 6 Jan 2026). Platform data. GetResponse, from more than 4.4 billion messages its customers with at least 500 contacts sent in 2023, reports 39.64% and adds that open rates "have become less reliable since Apple's introduction of Mail Privacy Protection in 2021" (GetResponse, Email Marketing Benchmarks 2024). Vendor data. These are opens of an email a subscriber chose to receive; they cannot be set against a platform's reach as like for like.
  • Sign-ups from a landing page. GetResponse reports an average landing-page conversion rate of 10.76% (subscriptions per visit, all industries, 2023), from 0.7% in retail to 39.93% in restaurants and food (same source). Vendor data; its customers' pages, whatever brought the visitors.
  • Sign-ups supplied by a newsletter platform's network. Substack said on 22 February 2024 that "Recommendations, along with the Substack app, powers a network that drives 50% of all new subscriptions and 25% of new paid subscriptions on Substack" (On Substack, 22 Feb 2024). beehiiv says newsletters using its Recommendations grew "2.75x faster on average", without the method or period (beehiiv, above). Platform claims, platform-wide; neither says what a new list gets.

What is not measured. No source found gives the share of a creator's YouTube viewers or Instagram followers who join the creator's email list, on either platform. The 10.76% applies to people who reach a sign-up page; how many of a small account's viewers ever reach one is the missing number. For Mara that means no forecast of her list size is possible from published data, and PB9, which prices what a list could earn, must work from her own counts.

What it costs to start. beehiiv's Free plan is $0 for "Up to 2,500" subscribers and includes landing pages and "Unlimited email sends" (beehiiv, pricing, checked 12 Nov 2026). Platform-official. No other tool's prices were checked for this unit; PB9 compares them.

The rule that comes first. Collecting email addresses is processing personal data. Which rules apply in the EU, the UK and the US, and what a sign-up form must say, is a question PB8 answers with the primary sources: for the EU, the General Data Protection Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2016/679, EUR-Lex); for the UK, the UK GDPR (legislation.gov.uk); for the US, the CAN-SPAM Act (FTC, CAN-SPAM Act: A Compliance Guide for Business, Aug 2023, updated 22 Jul 2025). Until it is answered, no address is collected. This unit is information, not legal advice.

06Splitting the hours at 3, 6 and 10 a week

What has a measured relation to output, and for which platform.

  • YouTube. In vidIQ's study of 32,926,088 channels under 1,000 subscribers compared with themselves 360 days later, 8.65% of those that added 12 to 51 videos crossed 1,000, against 30.77% of those that added 52 or more and 1.86% of those that added 1 to 11; vidIQ says "This is an observational association, not proof that uploading caused subscriber growth" and that totals "do not separate Shorts, long-form videos, or livestreams" (vidIQ, Uploads and reaching 1,000 subscribers, 11 Sep 2026). Vendor data; correlational. AIR Media-Tech's 18,000 channels (US, UK, Canada, Australia; main analysis at 100,000 to 1 million monthly views) found in Home and DIY a median of 114,000 subscribers for channels posting 2 to 5 videos a month (178 channels) against 90,700 for 10 to 20 a month (87 channels); it found channels on a predictable schedule got more views per subscriber than channels posting as many videos on random days, and that channels which "went dark for 14+ days three or more times a year got fewer views per video", and says "This is correlational data, not causal" (AIR Media-Tech, 8 Jun 2026). Vendor data; correlational; large channels. YouTube's own performance FAQ reports no such link for breaks: "We studied thousands of channels that took a break and found no correlation between break length and changes in views" (YouTube Help, answer 141805, checked 12 Nov 2026; platform-official, no sample size, period or method given).
  • Instagram. Buffer's fixed-effects analysis of 2.1 million feed posts from 102,000 accounts (photos, Reels and carousels; Stories excluded) compared each account with itself: against weeks with no posts, the weekly follower growth rate was higher by 0.12% at 1–2 posts a week, 0.26% at 3–5, 0.44% at 6–9 and 0.66% at 10 or more, and reach per post was about 12% higher at 3–5 posts a week than at 1–2 (Buffer, How often should you post on Instagram, 13 Aug 2025). Vendor data; within-account association. At 140 followers, the step from 1–2 to 3–5 posts a week is worth about 0.2 followers a week (derived: 140 × 0.0014).
  • Hours per piece. No dataset found gives the hours a one-person creator spends per long-form video or per Instagram post (PB1 §8 found the same). Every hour figure in the allocation is a planning estimate, replaced by the logged hours PB1 set up.

Three rules the allocation follows, each tied to the evidence above.

  1. The long-form video is the fixed point. It is the only piece with a platform statement that it keeps working (§3), the one the YouTube datasets count, and the one the stop rule in PB1 counts. Its date is protected before anything else.
  2. Instagram takes the hours that are left, inside Buffer's bands. At a small follower count the measured difference between bands is fractions of a follower a week; the reason to post is reach to strangers and contact with followers, which §3 sets as Instagram's role.
  3. Nothing gets hours on an unmeasured conversion. Shorts and Instagram posts are not given extra hours on the assumption that they send viewers to YouTube (§4), and the email list is given the minimum that keeps it running (§5).

What is cut first in a bad week. The order follows the same evidence. Instagram posts go first: on Buffer's within-account estimates, dropping a week from 3–5 posts to 1–2 lowers that week's growth rate by 0.14 percentage points (derived), and an Instagram post has the shortest shelf life on the evidence found (§3). Replies move to the following week: they are delayed, and none is lost. The video is edited shorter before its date moves, because gaps of 14 days or more go with fewer views per video in AIR's data (correlational) and because a fixed schedule is what PB1's stop rule counts. The hours log is never cut: the quarterly verdicts in PB1 read it. PB4 turns this order into the calendar's rule for a lost week.

The deliverable applies all of this to Mara.

07Deliverable

D1 · Mara's audience profile

Built 12 November 2026 from §1 to §5. Mara Venn: 38, a full-time accountant who restores mid-century furniture at weekends and wants to teach restoration to people who have no workshop; no YouTube subscribers and 140 Instagram followers at the start; films on a phone; 6 hours a week. From PB2: she restores indoors, on a table, with hand tools that fit in one box, and her account, One Box Restoration, is for "people who own, or want to buy, a mid-century chair, side table or small cabinet and have no workshop, garage or shed" (PB2, D1).

The test of PB2's audience. Each part of PB2's audience was checked against outside evidence. The evidence supports keeping it as written: it sizes the "no workshop" and "buys second-hand" parts through proxies, it finds nothing that contradicts them, and it finds nothing that sizes the mid-century part. The profile therefore keeps PB2's audience unchanged and adds two traits with evidence behind them (they learn practical jobs from video; they are reachable on YouTube at any age, on Instagram mainly under 50). PB2's D1, D3 and D4 stand.

The sentence. People who own, or want to buy, a mid-century chair, side table or small cabinet, have no workshop, garage or shed, and learn practical jobs from video; most of them can be reached on YouTube at any age, and on Instagram mainly under 50.

Why each budget is built this way.

  • At 6 hours (her plan), the posting rates are PB1's, unchanged, so PB1's expectation sheet, stop rule and checkpoints stand as written. The one change is inside PB1's publishing-and-replies line: 0.25 hours a fortnight become the email.
  • At 3 hours, a video every two weeks no longer fits: one video's capture, edit and packaging alone take 7.0 hours, more than the 6 a fortnight. The video moves to every four weeks at the same length and format, which keeps it a full restoration (PB2's format) and keeps her at 3 or 4 uploads a quarter, at or above the floor of 3 in PB1's stop rule; a quarter with 3 has no margin. The cost, on correlational data, is a rate below AIR's best band and a gap of 28 days between every upload, longer than the 14 days AIR associates with fewer views when it recurs. Instagram drops to Buffer's 1–2 band.
  • At 10 hours, the extra hours go to a second long-form video each fortnight, the move with the largest difference in the largest YouTube dataset (8.65% to 30.77%, vidIQ; correlational, and the groups may differ in time, money and audience), still inside AIR's 2–5 band. Instagram stays at 3 a week. Override if she does not finish a piece every week: then the video stays fortnightly and the spare hours go to Instagram at 5 posts a week (the top of Buffer's recommended band) and to the edit of each video.

A move between budgets changes posting rates, so PB1's rows Y1, Y4, Y5 and I4 and its stop rule are re-read on the day she moves (PB1 §1).

The cut order in a bad week (6 hours, her plan). A bad week is any week in which fewer than 6 hours are available. Cuts are made in this order, and each step is taken only if the one before did not free enough:

  1. Instagram: that week's three posts become one (frees 1.0 hour). The week's growth rate falls from Buffer's 3–5 band to its 1–2 band, a difference of 0.14 points, about 0.2 followers at 140 (derived).
  2. Replies move to the next week (frees up to 0.6 hours, derived: the week's whole publishing-and-replies line, 2.5 ÷ 4, so replies alone free less; nothing is lost).
  3. The video is edited shorter, to about 8 minutes, and goes out on its day (frees an estimated 1.0 hour; her estimate, unsourced).
  4. The upload date moves by up to 7 days, once; the next video returns to the fortnightly date. Two moved dates in a quarter are logged and read at the checkpoint against PB1's stop rule and load verdict.

Never cut: the log, which PB1's verdicts read. The email is kept, at 15 minutes; in a bad week it carries only the link.

Cut order at the other budgets. At 3 hours: the week's Instagram post goes first, then the edit is shortened, then the upload moves by up to 7 days (a gap of five weeks). At 10 hours: the second video of the fortnight goes first, which returns her to the 6-hour plan for that fortnight, then the 6-hour order applies.
