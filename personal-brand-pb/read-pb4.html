01The capture layer: record the work once, while it happens

Search was off for this unit. It was used on 12 November 2026 to re-open, at its source, every claim carried over from earlier units and every source this unit adds; each platform rule below carries that date. The labels are PB1 §1's (platform-official, vendor, correlational, peer-reviewed, anecdote, derived), plus one: her rule marks a convention Mara sets for herself that no source sets.

The running example is Mara Venn (Mara, fictional): 38, a full-time accountant who restores mid-century chairs, side tables and small cabinets at weekends, indoors on a table, with hand tools that fit in one box, and wants to teach restoration to people with no workshop; she publishes as One Box Restoration. She started with no YouTube subscribers and 140 Instagram followers, films on a phone and has 6 hours a week: 12 a fortnight, split by PB1 and PB3 into capture 2.0, long-form edit 4.0, packaging 1.0, Instagram derivatives 3.0, publishing and replies 1.25, email 0.25 and log 0.5. Her posting rates are one 10-minute YouTube video a fortnight and three Instagram feed posts a week; her day one is Monday 4 January 2027 (PB1, PB3). Her numbers are illustrations; nothing she does is evidence about anyone else.

The capture layer is everything recorded during the work itself, before anyone decides what a video or a post is about. Its rule is that nothing is staged and nothing is filmed twice: every clip, still, voice note and receipt is captured once, filed once, and is the only source for every format that uses it.

Why the record has to be made during the work. Three reasons, each with its source.

  1. Hours. PB1's capture line is 2.0 hours a fortnight for filming "during the weekend restoration work she is doing anyway" (PB1, D2). No line in the 12 hours pays for a second shoot. A Reel filmed separately would come out of the edit or the Instagram line, and the fortnight has no slack hour (PB3, D2: 24.0 of 24 per four weeks).
  2. Originality on Instagram. Instagram's original content guidelines say that content judged unoriginal "may be ineligible to appear in recommendations to people who don't already follow you", that an account which mostly posts it "may not be seen in recommendations to new audiences", and that low-effort edits such as borders, watermarks and speed changes do not make content original; original work is "work that you wholly created or reflects your unique perspective" (Instagram, Original content guidelines, undated page, read 12 Nov 2026; platform-official). On 30 April 2026 Instagram extended the loss of recommendation eligibility for accounts that mainly repost others' work from Reels to photos and carousels (TechCrunch, 30 Apr 2026; platform announcement via the press). Footage Mara shot herself, cut in her own editor, is original on that definition. A file downloaded back from YouTube or another app carries that app's marks; the Instagram versions are exported from her own edit.
  3. The promise. Every video is "one piece, start to finish: what was wrong with it, what I did, what went wrong and what it cost" (PB2, D4). A mistake re-filmed because the first take looked bad replaces what happened with a staged version of it, and "what went wrong" is the one part of the promise that cannot be re-created honestly.

Why straight after, and why by voice. At the end of each session Mara records a voice note on the phone before she packs the box. Two pieces of evidence bear on that, neither about creators:

  • Shiffman, Stone and Hufford's review of ecological momentary assessment notes that assessment "typically relies on global retrospective self-reports", which are limited by recall bias, and describes sampling experience in real time to minimise recall bias and "maximize ecological validity" (Shiffman, Stone and Hufford, "Ecological Momentary Assessment", Annual Review of Clinical Psychology, 2008, doi 10.1146/annurev.clinpsy.3.022806.091415; abstract read on CiteDrive, which dates it 2007). Peer-reviewed, a methods review from clinical psychology. The transfer to a restorer's notes is this unit's inference: a note made at the table holds the mistake as it happened; a note made a week later holds the version already tidied in memory.
  • Ruan and colleagues measured English text entry by speech recognition at 2.93 times the rate of a phone keyboard (153 against 52 words a minute) and, across English and Mandarin together, fewer errors corrected during entry (5.30% against 11.22%) and slightly more left in the final text (1.30% against 0.79%) (Ruan, Wobbrock, Liou, Ng and Landay, "Comparing Speech and Keyboard Text Entry for Short Messages in Two Languages on Touchscreen Phones", arXiv 1608.07323; Proceedings of the ACM on Interactive, Mobile, Wearable and Ubiquitous Technologies 1(4), Dec 2017). Peer-reviewed; a laboratory study with 2016 recognisers. It shows speech is the cheaper way to get words into a phone, and nothing about what is said.

The note has three fixed prompts (her rule): what I did; what went wrong; what comes next. The second answers PB2's "what went wrong"; the third feeds the idea backlog (§6).

That is the 1.0 hour a Saturday that makes up PB1's 2.0 capture hours a fortnight. The restoration itself is outside the 6 hours: PB1 set the capture line as the cost of filming work she does anyway, and this unit keeps that boundary.

Does one piece a fortnight fit two Saturdays? The capture hours fit: two sessions of an hour each film one piece. Whether the restoration fits two weekends depends on the piece, and no source found gives restoration times for these pieces; Mara's estimate per piece goes into her backlog and her log records the real figure. The system never splits a piece across two videos, so the cadence is protected by three rules (her rules): the season's list holds only pieces she estimates at two weekends; a larger piece waits until she has a bank, one finished and fully captured piece that has not been edited yet, which covers the fortnight the larger piece overruns; and the bank is built from her own pace, whenever a piece finishes in one weekend and the next starts a week early. If her log shows pieces averaging more than two weekends, the promise holds and the cadence changes: at a checkpoint she moves to one video every four weeks, the cadence PB3 set for 3 hours a week, and re-reads PB1's posting-rate rows the same day.

02The anchor piece and its cadence

The anchor piece is the one everything else is cut from: Mara's 10-minute YouTube video of one piece, start to finish, every two weeks. PB3 §3 set YouTube long-form as the home on the evidence of what each platform does for a small account; this section fixes how the anchor is made on time.

The cadence, and what the evidence says about holding it. YouTube's own tips for building casual and regular viewers say "To grow regular viewers, publish content regularly and maintain a strong connection with your audience", and "Be sure to keep your channel fresh with uploads and playlists" (YouTube Help, New, casual, & regular viewers tips, answer 13615784, checked 12 Nov 2026; platform-official, no effect size). Two sources then disagree about gaps:

  • AIR Media-Tech, from 18,000 English-language channels in the US, UK, Canada and Australia, with its primary analysis on channels at 100,000 to 1 million monthly views, found that "Channels that went dark for 14+ days three or more times a year got fewer views per video", and that channels on a predictable schedule got more views per subscriber than channels posting the same number of videos on random days; it says "This is correlational data, not causal" (AIR Media-Tech, 8 Jun 2026). Vendor data; correlational; channels far larger than a new one.
  • YouTube's performance FAQ says "We studied thousands of channels that took a break and found no correlation between break length and changes in views", that "it may take some time to 'warm up' your audience again", and that "growth in views across uploads is not correlated with time between uploads" (YouTube Help, YouTube performance FAQ & troubleshooting, answer 141805, checked 12 Nov 2026). Platform-official; no sample size, period or method is given.

PB1 and PB3 cited only the first. Read together, neither shows that a missed fortnight costs a small channel views. The fortnightly date is still held, for a reason that does not depend on either: PB1's stop rule counts uploads per quarter (fewer than 3 in each of two consecutive quarters), and the upload count is the output PB1's YouTube base rates are indexed by (vidIQ, Uploads and reaching 1,000 subscribers, 11 Sep 2026, 12–51 band; PB1 §2). The cadence is held because the year is judged on it.

The edit is the line most likely to overrun. PB1 gives the edit 4.0 hours for 10 finished minutes, and no dataset gives hours per video for one-person accounts (PB1 §8, PB3 §6). The only published figure found is a video-editing company's rule of thumb: "As a rule of thumb, expect 30 minutes to an hour of editing time per minute of finished video" (Tasty Edits, How long does it take to edit a video?, published 19 May 2021, updated 17 Sep 2026). Vendor; no method or sample. On that figure a 10-minute video takes 5 to 10 hours (derived), above Mara's 4.0. The figure is too weak to replace her plan; it is enough to say where the plan will break first. Her edit window therefore has a hard stop and a defined fallback (§5, §7), and PB5's timed edit template has to show 10 minutes in 4 hours or say what changes.

The anchor's fixed points (her rules): the video goes public on Tuesday of every other week, scheduled in advance; YouTube lets a creator set a video as scheduled or private on the upload page and choose a date, time and time zone for it to go public, from Studio or the YouTube app (YouTube Help, Schedule video publish time, answer 1270709, checked 12 Nov 2026; platform-official). The same page says a scheduled video does not publish while a Community Guidelines strike's penalty period runs. The publishing moment is then separate from the editing: a Tuesday arrives whether or not she is at a desk.

The pipeline. A piece is restored and captured on two Saturdays; edited the following fortnight on the Sunday of its first week; cut for packaging and Instagram on the Sunday of its second week; and published on the Tuesday that opens the fortnight after. At any moment three pieces are in the system: one on the table, one in the edit, one published and feeding Instagram. The published video was finished a week earlier, so a bad Sunday never puts that Tuesday at risk.

03The derivative layer on each platform

Everything in this layer is cut from the capture layer and the finished edit. None of it has material of its own.

YouTube: no derivatives at 6 hours. PB3 gave Shorts no hours at 6 hours a week, because no source measures how many Shorts viewers go on to watch the same creator's long-form, and because AIR Media-Tech's study of 18,000 English-language channels (March 2025 to March 2026) found that "Home and DIY shows near-identical performance for Long-Only and Long+Short Mix across most segments" (AIR Media-Tech, 28 May 2026; vendor, correlational, channels far larger than a new one) (PB3 §4, D2). The calendar keeps that. PB6 may revisit it with its own evidence; until then nothing on YouTube is made but the anchor.

Instagram: three feed posts a week, six a fortnight, made in one sitting. The rate is PB3's, set from the one Instagram dataset that compares an account with itself: Buffer's fixed-effects analysis of 2.1 million feed posts (photos, Reels and carousels; Stories excluded) from 102,000 accounts found an account's weekly follower growth rate higher by 0.12% in weeks with 1–2 posts and 0.26% in weeks with 3–5, against weeks with none, and reach per post about 12% higher at 3–5 posts a week than at 1–2 (Buffer, How often should you post on Instagram, 13 Aug 2025). Vendor data; within-account association. At 140 followers the step between the two bands is a fraction of a follower a week (about 0.2 followers a week, derived in PB3 §6 from Buffer's 0.14-point step: 140 × 0.0014; PB1, I4, puts 3 posts against none at about 0.36); the reason to post three is the reach to strangers that PB3 §3 set as Instagram's role, and the band is where Buffer's data puts the next measurable step.

The mix follows PB3's reading of format reach: Fanpage Karma, from more than 3 million Reels, carousels and image posts published January to April 2026, found median reach for accounts under 10,000 followers of 4.9% of followers for Reels, 3.5% for image posts and 2.6% for carousels, and that "Reels reach nearly three times as many non-followers as carousels" (Fanpage Karma, Reel, carousel, or image post, 7 Aug 2026, modified 28 Aug 2026). Vendor data; the number of accounts is not stated. Mara's provisional mix is four Reels and two carousels a fortnight (Mara, fictional); PB7 tests the mix and may change it, and nothing in this unit depends on the split as long as each post stays at 30 minutes.

Each points to the full video through the profile link PB2 set; how well that sends viewers to YouTube is unmeasured (PB3 §4) and PB7's to examine. Post 1 goes out with the video on the Tuesday; the others on Thursdays and Saturdays across the fortnight. An Instagram post is planned as doing its work in its first week or two on the vendor evidence PB3 §3 found, which is weak (Socialinsider, How long does it take for Reels to get views?, 21 Jan 2026, updated 6 Nov 2026, whose page gives two different samples and day-by-day figures that do not match its own 45% headline); spreading the six across the fortnight keeps one new post in that window each week.

Scheduling. TechCrunch reported at launch that Instagram lets all professional accounts schedule posts, Reels and carousels in the app up to 75 days ahead (TechCrunch, 8 Nov 2022). Instagram's help page on scheduling describes how to "schedule Reels, photos, and carousel posts" in its summary, but its body could not be read on 12 November 2026 (Instagram Help, How to create and manage scheduled posts and Reels on Instagram), so the current limit is unconfirmed. Mara schedules at most 13 days ahead, so the limit does not bind her if it still exists in any form near that figure.

Email: one per video, written in 15 minutes. PB3 set one short email per video on beehiiv's Free plan, with nothing collected until PB8 answers the data-protection question (PB3 §5, D2). Until then the 15 minutes on publishing Tuesday still write the email (the link, what went wrong, what it cost) and keep it as a draft, so that the archive exists from upload 1 and the slot exists when collection starts (her rule).

04Series and seasons

The series is PB2's: One Box Restoration, one piece per video, start to finish, one box of tools. YouTube names series as a route for returning viewers: "Create a series of videos from topics that have already proven popular with your viewers", and "Try creating content that's more consistent and similar in topic or format" (YouTube Help, answer 13615784); among the reasons old videos gain views it lists "You released a new video in a series, prompting viewers to go back and watch older episodes" (YouTube Help, answer 141805). Platform-official; no effect sizes.

A season is a bounded run of the series with a stated number of pieces, a start date and an end date. Mara's seasons match PB1's quarters (her rule): Season 1 is seven pieces, uploads 1 to 7, from Tuesday 5 January to Tuesday 30 March 2027, and ends at the Q1 review on Monday 5 April 2027. Each season has its own YouTube playlist, and each video carries an on-screen line with its place in the season ("piece 3 of 7"); how that appears in titles and thumbnails is PB5's. A format change from a review verdict (PB1, D3, verdict 3, which applies from the Q2 review) takes effect at the first video edited after the review. The calendar runs two weeks ahead, so the next season's first video is already edited when the review sits (D2); the change starts at that season's second video, and from there the season keeps one format, consistent in YouTube's sense.

Why a run with an end is easier to hold than an open one. No study found compares creators who publish in seasons with creators who publish without end, on either platform. What exists is evidence about goals with a visible end and a fresh start, from other settings. Each transfer below is this unit's inference.

  • Effort rises as the end comes into view. Kivetz, Urminsky and Zheng found that members of a café reward programme "purchase coffees more frequently the closer they are to earning a free coffee", that customers given a card on which they appeared to have a head start completed "the 10 required purchases faster than customers who receive a 'regular' 10-stamp card", and that "a stronger tendency to accelerate toward the reward predicts greater retention and faster reengagement in the program" (Kivetz, Urminsky and Zheng, "The Goal-Gradient Hypothesis Resurrected", Journal of Marketing Research 43, 2006, 39–58). Peer-reviewed; consumers and coffee. The inference: "piece 5 of 7" is a distance to a finish line; an open channel has none.
  • A new start resets the record. Dai, Milkman and Riis found that searches for "diet", gym visits and commitments to goals rise after temporal landmarks such as "the outset of a new week, month, year, or semester; a birthday; a holiday", and propose that landmarks open new mental accounting periods that "relegate past imperfections to a previous period" (Dai, Milkman and Riis, "The Fresh Start Effect", Management Science 60(10), 2014, 2563–2582). Peer-reviewed; archival field data. The landmarks studied are calendar ones; whether a self-declared season start works the same way is untested. Mara's seasons start on quarter boundaries, which are calendar landmarks in their own right.
  • The end is where the decision lives. PB1 sets four dated reviews and four verdicts. A season that ends at the review puts the decision to continue, change one thing, change the format or stop at a planned point; a bad week in the middle of a season is not that point (PB1 §7).

What the evidence does not support: that viewers prefer seasons, that seasons grow a channel, or that a season end justifies a break. On breaks, YouTube says it found no correlation between break length and changes in views and AIR associates repeated gaps of 14 days or more with fewer views per video (§2); with that disagreement unresolved, Mara's seasons run back to back with no planned gap in year one (her rule), and PB10 decides whether a break between seasons enters year two.

05Batching in two windows a fortnight

Mara's fortnight has two working sessions, both on Sundays (her rule): the edit window (4.0 hours, Sunday of week A) and the cut window (4.5 hours, Sunday of week B: packaging 1.0, six Instagram posts 3.0, scheduling the video and the posts 0.5). Capture happens inside the Saturday restoration, and three short fixed slots hold the rest: the email on publishing Tuesday (0.25), replies on Wednesday and Friday of week A and Wednesday of week B (0.25 each), and the hours log on Sunday evening (0.25 a week). Every post of the fortnight, the video and its six Instagram posts, comes out of the two windows.

What the task-switching evidence supports.

  • Switching costs time, more for complex and unfamiliar tasks. Rubinstein, Meyer and Evans measured how fast young adults performed when successive tasks switched, and found that "time costs increased with the complexity of the tasks" and with their unfamiliarity (APA press release on Rubinstein, Meyer and Evans, Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance 27(4), Aug 2001; APA, Multitasking: switching costs, 20 Mar 2006). Peer-reviewed, reported by its publisher; laboratory tasks such as arithmetic and classifying shapes.
  • With a predictable switch, the cost sits at the start of the run. In Monsell, Sumner and Waters's experiments, in which participants switched between classifying a digit as high or low and as odd or even, "With predictable switching, the cost was limited to the first trial of a run" (Monsell, Sumner and Waters, "Task-set reconfiguration with predictable and unpredictable task switches", Memory & Cognition 31(3), 2003, 327–342). Peer-reviewed; a laboratory task, measured in milliseconds.
  • An unfinished task follows you into the next one. Leroy's two experiments found that "People need to stop thinking about one task in order to fully transition their attention and perform well on another", that leaving a task unfinished made the shift harder and hurt performance on the next task, and that finishing was not sufficient by itself: time pressure on the first task helped people let go of it (Leroy, "Why is it so hard to do my work? The challenge of attention residue when switching between work tasks", Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes 109(2), 2009, 168–181). Peer-reviewed; short laboratory work tasks.
  • A window written as when and where gets kept more often. Gollwitzer and Sheeran's meta-analysis of 94 independent tests found that implementation intentions, plans that spell out "the when, where, and how of goal striving in advance", had a positive effect of medium-to-large size on goal attainment, d = .65 (Gollwitzer and Sheeran, Advances in Experimental Social Psychology 38, 2006, 69–119). Peer-reviewed meta-analysis.

From these, this unit's inferences, none tested on video work: one edit window a fortnight pays the cost of getting into the edit once, where four evening sessions would pay it four times; each window should end on a finished thing (an exported video; six scheduled posts), with the hard stop doing the job Leroy's time pressure did; and each window is written in the calendar as an if-then plan ("Sunday 10:00, at the table, the edit opens"); "edit this week" is the weaker goal intention those studies compare against.

What the evidence does not support.

  • Any size for the saving. The experiments measure milliseconds on digit and shape tasks, or minutes on short office tasks. Nothing found measures how much batching saves on editing a video or making six posts.
  • The "40 percent" figure. The American Psychological Association's page says brief mental blocks from shifting between tasks "can cost as much as 40 percent of someone's productive time", attributing the statement to David Meyer without citing a study for the number (APA, 20 Mar 2006, above). It is widely repeated as a finding. No source found measures it, and this unit does not use it.
  • That batching makes better posts. No source found compares posts made in one sitting with posts made one at a time.
  • That batching is safe. A batch concentrates risk: a lost Sunday loses a whole window. §7 is the price of the two-window design.

06The idea backlog

The backlog is one list, kept on the phone (her rule), with two parts.

Pieces. Every candidate piece, with: what it is; where it came from; what is wrong with it; her estimate of weekends; whether any step needs a workshop (PB2 D4: she says so and shows a workaround or refers it to a professional); the date bought; and its status (seen, bought, on the table, captured, edited, published). The cost is not estimated here; it is logged from receipts as it is spent, because "what it cost" is part of the promise (PB2).

Posts. Ideas for Instagram posts and for later pieces, mostly from the third prompt of the voice note ("what comes next") and from comments and messages read in the reply slots. Each idea is written with the piece or fortnight it belongs to, or marked "unplaced".

Why write it down with a slot. Masicampo and Baumeister found across several studies that unfinished goals caused intrusive thoughts during an unrelated reading task and hurt performance on an unrelated anagram task, and that letting participants "formulate specific plans for their unfulfilled goals" eliminated those effects; "Once a plan is made, the drive to attain a goal is suspended" (Masicampo and Baumeister, "Consider it done! Plan making can eliminate the cognitive effects of unfulfilled goals", Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 101(4), 2011, 667–683, doi 10.1037/a0024192). Peer-reviewed; laboratory studies. The transfer is this unit's: an idea written into the backlog with the fortnight it belongs to stops competing for attention inside the edit window; an idea kept in the head does not.

How it is kept current (her rules, no extra hours): ideas go in during the voice note and the reply slots; a piece's status changes when it is bought, at each Saturday session and at each window. The next season's first two pieces are picked from it by week 10 of the season before, because they are restored and captured before the review; the rest are picked at the quarterly review (her rule).

07What is dropped first when a week is lost

PB3 set the cut order for a bad week, any week with fewer than 6 hours: Instagram posts from three to one (frees 1.0 hour); replies moved to the next week; the video edited to about 8 minutes (frees an estimated 1.0 hour); the upload moved by up to 7 days, once; the log never cut (PB3, D2). PB1's verdict 2 applies a similar rule across a quarter: the video shorter and Instagram from six posts a fortnight to four before the cadence slips (PB1, D3). This section schedules it at the scale of a week. The reasons for the order are PB3's: Instagram goes first because a post's working life is the shortest on the vendor evidence found (§3) and the measured difference between Buffer's bands is fractions of a follower a week at her size; replies are delayed, never lost; the video is shortened before its date moves because the year is judged on the upload count (§2). Two additions from this unit's design:

  • Capture is never cut while the restoration goes ahead. A session that happens and is not filmed cannot be filmed again (§1). If a Saturday is lost, the restoration is lost with it and the piece moves a week; the bank (§1), when she has one, covers the fortnight.
  • The email is kept, at 15 minutes with only the link (PB3, D2).

A lost week, in hours. The plan has no slack (24.0 of 24 hours per four weeks; PB3, D2), so the cut order is the only margin, and a lost week always costs something visible. The two cases:

  • Week A lost (the edit window). The next Sunday holds the edit at about 8 minutes (3.0), then a shortened cut: packaging 1.0, two Instagram posts in place of six (1.0; one a week for the two weeks the batch covers), scheduling 0.25. With capture 1.0 and the log 0.25 that week is 6.5 hours. Replies wait for the next slots, which are not lengthened. The video goes out on its date.
  • Week B lost (the cut window). The upload moves 7 days to the Tuesday of the following week B. The next Sunday holds the shortened cut of the delayed piece (2.25: packaging, two posts, scheduling) and the edit of the next piece at about 8 minutes (3.0); with capture and the log, 6.5 hours. The next video returns to its fortnightly date, one week after the delayed one. The week after the recovery week carries the moved email (0.25) on top of its 6.0, so it runs at 6.25, with replies kept to their slots as after a lost week A; it stays under PB1's 7-hour line. That uses the one date move PB3 allows; a second lost cut window in the same quarter skips that fortnight's upload (her rule; PB3 instead logs a second moved date, and the skip keeps the next video on its date), and the gap is logged for the review.

Both recovery weeks run at 6.5 hours, over the 6-hour plan and under PB1's over-budget line of 7, so neither counts towards PB1's "cut the load" verdict (three weeks over 7 in a quarter). A week over 7 can come only from an overrun on top of the plan, and the place it will most likely come from is the edit window (§2). The hard stop is the defence: at 4 hours the edit window ends; if the video is not exported, it is finished in the first part of the next cut window, and that fortnight's Instagram drops from six posts to two to pay for it (step 1). An overrun is logged at its real length.
