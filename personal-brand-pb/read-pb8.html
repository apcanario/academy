01Honesty as register, confession as content

Two terms. Register is the level at which something is said: where it sits in the piece, how much weight it carries, who it is addressed to. Honesty as register means the true account is the normal way the work talks: a mistake is named at the weight it had, inside the video about the work, and the video moves on. Confession as content means the disclosure is the product: it is the subject of the piece, it sits in the title or the thumbnail, and the piece has nothing left if it is cut. The distinction is this course's; no source found defines the two terms. The cases below are documented and linked, and each is a creator anecdote: chosen because it is known, and proof of nothing about how often anything happens.

Honesty as register: Tom Scott, 2024. After ten years of videos, Tom Scott stopped his weekly schedule; Tubefilter's report quotes his reason: the job "keeps getting bigger and more complicated, and I am so tired"; "There's nothing in my life right now except work"; "a dream job is still a job"; and "I don't know when or if I come back, but when I do, it won't be weekly" (Tubefilter, James Hale, 2 Jan 2024). The word "burnout" does not appear in the report, and it is not attributed to him here. The shape is what makes this register: the state is named once, in plain words, attached to a decision about cadence, and the channel's subject does not change to the state. Anecdote.

Confession as content: Elle Mills, 2018 and after. Elle Mills released a video titled "Burnt out at 19" in 2018. In a later interview with Patreon's Creator Hub (undated), she says she was "very impulsive with even releasing" it, and that "I took advantage of people's longings to be seen"; the article quotes the narration of her later video What Happened To Elle Mills?: "I don't want my life to be the product anymore"; and she gives as her biggest lesson that "not everyone deserves your vulnerability", a line her video credits to her friend Tomska (Patreon Creator Hub, A YouTube star's reflections on burnout, boundaries, and changing directions). Calling it confession as content is this course's reading; the words "the product" are hers. Anecdote.

How register turns into confession without a word changing: Johannes Haushofer, 2016. Haushofer, then a Princeton professor, published a "CV of Failures": programmes he did not get into, funding he did not get, rejected papers. His reason: "Most of what I try fails, but these failures are often invisible, while the successes are visible", and the document is meant to "balance the record and provide some perspective". He later added a "Meta-Failures" line: "This darn CV of Failures has received way more attention than my entire body of academic work" (Haushofer, CV of Failures, copy hosted by the University of Liverpool; CNBC, URL dated 27 Apr 2016). The document is register: dated entries without commentary, beside a body of work. The audience made it the headline. That is the mechanism to plan for: a record of mistakes kept honestly can still become what an account is known for, through what the audience responds to.

What this means for an account like Mara's. Mara Venn (Mara, fictional) is 38, a full-time accountant who restores mid-century furniture at weekends, and each of her YouTube videos is one piece, start to finish, with what was wrong, what she did, what went wrong and what it cost (PB2, D1). Her format already contains the honest part: the mistake is a fixed element of every video (on piece 1, sanding through one corner, PB5). The disclosure policy in the deliverable keeps it register: the mistake is named inside the piece's video, never in a title or a thumbnail, and the piece stays the subject.

02What the studies on trust found, and what they did not

One study of creators and trust. Muhmin, Syah and Anggraeni surveyed 407 Indonesian women aged 18 to 35 who follow beauty influencers (54.8% aged 18–24, 45.2% aged 25–35), each rating one influencer, and analysed the answers with PLS-SEM (Muhmin, Syah and Anggraeni, Frontiers in Communication 11, published 5 Feb 2026; doi:10.3389/fcomm.2026.1723759). Standardised paths to the parasocial relationship (the one-sided bond a follower feels): trustworthiness 0.225 (p < 0.001), expertise 0.128 (p = 0.005), attractiveness 0.075 (p = 0.135, not supported). The bond to purchase intention: 0.432. Direct paths to purchase intention: expertise 0.127, trustworthiness 0.129, attractiveness 0.124. The model explains 24.7% of the variance in the bond and 35.5% in purchase intention, and the authors state that "the cross-sectional survey design limits causal inference". Peer-reviewed; correlational; one country, one product category, women only.

What it found. The bond went with honesty and competence, and did not go with looks; looks still went with purchase intention directly, at about the same size as the other two direct paths. What it did not find. It did not measure personal disclosure at all, so it says nothing in favour of telling an audience about your private life. Its trust items concern the subject and the products (the trustworthiness items are about appearing genuine and being honest when recommending products). Nothing in it shows that an account grows faster because it is honest; it shows an association, in one survey, between rated honesty and the bond.

One meta-analysis of disclosure itself. Eisend, van Reijmersdal, Boerman and Tarrahi pooled 61 papers using 57 distinct data sets on the effects of disclosing sponsored content and found that "disclosing sponsored content reduced brand attitudes, credibility, and source evaluation" and "increased recognition, persuasion knowledge, and resistance" (Eisend et al., Journal of Advertising 49(3), 344–366, 2020; doi:10.1080/00913367.2020.1765909). Peer-reviewed; only the abstract was read, so the designs and media of the pooled studies are not described here.

What the two together mean, and where they stop. A disclosure label costs the sponsored piece some credibility in the pooled studies; the law in §6 requires the label anyway, so the cost is fixed once you take a deal. The lever left to you is how many sponsored pieces there are, which PB9 takes up. The survey's honesty items are the ones a correct label and a named mistake speak to. Neither source measures what happens to an account's trust over years, and neither involves an account of under 1,000 followers. Those are this unit's inferences, stated as inferences.

03Audience entanglement and creator burnout: what the studies report

Audience entanglement. Pillemer, Harrison, Murphy and Park, "Audience Entanglement: How Independent Creative Workers Experience the Pressures of Widespread Appeal on Digital Platforms", Administrative Science Quarterly 71(1), 215–260 (online 18 December 2025, issue March 2026; metatoc record). The full paper was not opened; the detail comes from PsyPost's report (PsyPost, Eric W. Dolan, 20 Feb 2026). The researchers interviewed 54 creators who had already succeeded, 74 interviews in all: visual artists on Instagram who "had an average of over 500,000 followers" and musicians on YouTube who "averaged nearly 280,000 subscribers". After a large audience arrives, the creators described a dysfunctional entanglement, with metrics as the main source of validation, which some moved out of into a functional entanglement; creators moved between the two. The study names three strategies: distancing from audience input (PsyPost's examples: not reading comments for a full day after posting; a trusted person filtering messages), depersonalising critique (reading it as a comment on one piece of work), and distilling personal standards (returning to one's own ideals ahead of trends). Limits PsyPost lists: self-report; visual artists and musicians only, so creators "in the fitness, beauty, or gaming sectors" may differ; Instagram and YouTube only. Peer-reviewed; qualitative, from interviews.

Burnout as partly unspeakable. Rosie Nguyen and Brooke Erin Duffy, in New Media & Society (published 7 July 2026), analysed "58 examples of creators' self-authored content on TikTok and YouTube; 62 news media accounts; and 78 in-depth interviews" (Cornell Chronicle, Tom Fleischman, 8 Jul 2026; the paper itself was not opened, and the findings are the press release's). Burnout was in part "unspeakable": creators "felt a sense of gratitude toward the community that they built, and the audience and the platforms they depend on", speaking about the job's hazards risked "blowback from audiences", and a recurrent theme was "If I take a break, I'm going to get punished by the algorithm". In their data "The male creators tend to resort to the 'grinding' ethos" and "The women tend to resort to self-care or restorative practices". The guardrail the authors argue for is structural: Duffy asks "To what extent can they rely on structural solutions for problems when they are cast as individual failures?", and the release names a proposed Creator Bill of Rights introduced by Rep. Ro Khanna (its status is not given). Peer-reviewed, qualitative, reported through a university press release.

What the two studies mean at your size. Both samples are far larger than an account under 1,000 followers, and neither measured a disclosure policy; the transfer is this unit's. Three consequences follow. When the video is the person, every metric on it reads as a verdict on the person, which is the dysfunctional state; when the video is the piece, a weak number reads as a weak piece, which is depersonalising built into the format. The one thing a creator is least likely to say, by Nguyen and Duffy's account, is that they are exhausted, so the policy settles in advance how a pause is announced, before the state arrives. And the fear of breaks has a platform answer: PB3 §6 sets out YouTube's own statement on breaks (platform-official) beside one vendor's correlational data, and the deliverable's pause clause uses both. The guardrails already exist in this course's system: PB4 fixes replies to three 15-minute slots a fortnight (distancing), PB2's positioning is the personal standard written before the first upload. Mara's numbers are judged at her checkpoint reviews (PB1, D3).

04What a disclosure policy decides

A disclosure policy is a written list of what you publish about yourself and others, decided before the moment when you would be tempted. It covers five areas. For each, here is what the evidence or the law bears on, and what stays a choice.

Yourself. Your face, voice and name are the person half of PB2's positioning (topic words for discovery, a method for returning viewers, the person for trust). Everything beyond them is a choice: where you live, your age, your history. A location is the hardest to take back once a viewer can match a window, a street or a shop sign; no source found measures how often that happens, so the policy treats it as a risk with no number attached.

Your health. Under the GDPR, "data concerning health" is a special category whose processing is prohibited unless an exception applies, and one exception is that "processing relates to personal data which are manifestly made public by the data subject" (Regulation (EU) 2016/679, art. 9(1) and 9(2)(e), as adopted, legislation.gov.uk; primary law). The Court of Justice has read "manifestly made public" as requiring that the person "explicitly made the choice beforehand", "with full knowledge of the facts", to make the data "publicly accessible to an unlimited number of persons" (CJEU, C-252/21 Meta Platforms v Bundeskartellamt, 4 Jul 2023, FRA case summary). A health fact you state in a public video meets those words. Others still need a lawful basis to process it, and the rest of the Regulation still applies to them, but the special-category prohibition no longer protects that fact (this unit's reading of the text). The UK GDPR's version of the article was not re-read for this unit, and no US equivalent was searched. Publishing a health fact is therefore close to irreversible, and the policy publishes none that the work does not need.

Your family and household. They did not choose the account. Anyone who appears is a person whose image and data you process (§6.2). Children add a higher bar in every jurisdiction found (§6.2).

Your employer. Two separate questions: what your contract lets you do outside work (§6.4), and what you say about the job. Naming your profession or employer ties your account to them in search and gives colleagues a way in; the job as a constraint ("around a full-time job") carries the information the audience needs about your hours without either. For Mara the profession matters for a further reason: an accountant who mentions costs can be read as giving financial advice. That is a judgment with no source behind it, and the policy treats it as her choice.

Other people in the frame. Helpers, sellers, neighbours, people in the street. §6.2 sets the legal question; the policy's default is that no one appears who has not agreed, and that no child appears.

The test for everything else. The policy cannot list every case, so it ends in a test applied before anything personal goes out: did it bear on the work, is it in the notes made at the time, would the video keep its subject without it, does it name no one else, and does it reach the public first in a video edited days later. The last question uses the pipeline as a delay: in Mara's calendar a piece's last session is on a Saturday and its video goes public on the Tuesday ten days later (PB4, D2: piece 2, reveal Saturday 9 January, public Tuesday 19 January 2027; derived). Elle Mills's word for the opposite was "impulsive" (§1).

05How a public record stays truthful, with corrections

Changes after publication are invisible unless you show them. YouTube lets a creator trim the beginning, middle or end of a published video "on a computer", without re-uploading, and "The video's URL, view count, and comments will stay the same"; for "unedited video with over 100,000 views, you may not be able to save changes", a limit that "does not apply to channels who are in the YouTube Partner Program" (YouTube Help, Trim your videos, answer 9057455). Platform-official. Nothing on the page says the viewer is shown that a video changed. Instagram lets captions be edited after posting; whether it marks an edited caption was not checked, because Instagram's Help Centre article bodies did not load for the tools used here (as PB7 found on 12 Nov 2026).

A model from a field that depends on a trusted record. The ICMJE recommendations for medical journals: "publish a correction notice as soon as possible detailing changes from and citing the original publication"; "post a new article version with details of the changes from the original version"; "archive all prior versions of the article"; and "Previous electronic versions should prominently note that there are more recent versions of the article" (ICMJE, Corrections and Version Control). These are rules for journals; they are used here as a tested pattern.

What follows for a creator, as rules (this course's; basis: the two sources above):

  1. A correction is visible where the error was. On YouTube: a dated line at the top of the description ("Correction, 3 Feb 2027: …") and a pinned comment saying the same. On Instagram: a dated correction line added to the caption. In the email: a line at the top of the next one.
  2. A trim is a correction. A video cut after publication carries the same dated line saying what was removed and why, in general terms if the reason is someone else's privacy.
  3. Nothing is quietly withdrawn. A video is deleted or made private only to protect someone else's privacy or safety, or on a legal demand, and the next video or post says that something was removed and the general reason.
  4. The notes behind each video are kept as they were made. The session voice notes PB4 sets up are the record; a later correction is added beneath, dated, and the original is not rewritten.
  5. Numbers stay in when they go the wrong way. If a kind of number appears in your videos (cost per piece, hours), it appears every time, whichever way it moved. A record that shows only the good numbers is a highlight reel.

06The rules, as questions with their primary sources

Each subsection gives the question to answer, what the EU, UK and US sources say, and what the platforms add. Where a source is a regulator's guidance or a secondary report, it is labelled. The table at the end of the section draws the sources by area and jurisdiction.

6.1 · Advertising and paid-partnership disclosure

The question: when a post or video promotes something you have been paid for, given, or own, what must it say, where, and which platform tool must you use?

United States. The FTC's Endorsement Guides require that where there is a connection between the endorser and the seller "that might materially affect the weight or credibility of the endorsement" and that "is not reasonably expected by the audience", it "must be disclosed clearly and conspicuously"; on social media a disclosure "should be unavoidable" (16 CFR §255.5 and §255.0(f), eCFR; primary law, revised July 2023 per the Federal Register notice of 26 Jul 2023). The FTC's plain-language guide for influencers says a material connection includes "a personal, family, or employment relationship" and free or discounted products; the disclosure goes "with the endorsement message itself", in the video itself for video, not only in a description or profile; "ad", "advertisement" and "sponsored" are acceptable, "sp", "spon" and "collab" are not; "Don't assume that a platform's disclosure tool is good enough"; and "U.S. law applies if it's reasonably foreseeable that the post will affect U.S. consumers", including posts made from abroad (FTC, Disclosures 101 for Social Media Influencers, Nov 2019, modified 3 Dec 2024). Regulator guidance.

United Kingdom. Since 6 April 2025 the unfair commercial practices rules are in the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024; the banned list includes "hidden advertising on social media", and the CMA and courts can fine traders "up to 10% of their worldwide turnover" (GOV.UK, What businesses need to know about unfair commercial practices, updated 3 Dec 2025; regulator guidance). The CMA's guidance for content creators counts as payment "Any form of incentive or reward – including money, commissions, discounts", covers gifts sent "without asking for anything in return", and applies where you "own (in full, co-own or in part), or are employed by or otherwise connected with a brand"; labels such as "ad", "advert" or "advertising" are clear, "#gifted", "#aff" or "just naming the brand" are not; a platform tool such as "paid partnership" may be used, but check that it "clearly conveys that the content is an ad", and if not "include the label #Ad in a clear, upfront position" (CMA, Social media endorsements: guidance for content creators, updated 3 Sep 2025). The advertising regulators' guide, written with the CMA, reached its fourth edition on 23 October 2026 (ASA, Influencers' guide); the full PDF was not opened, and a law firm's summary reports that it now rests on the 2024 Act, lists #Ad, #Advert, #Advertising, #Advertisement and #AdvertisementFeature, singles out "Sponsored", "Spon" and "Sp" as insufficient, and keeps disclosure for 12 months after a relationship ends (Lewis Silkin, 23 Oct 2026; secondary).

European Union. The Unfair Commercial Practices Directive bans in all circumstances "Using editorial content in the media to promote a product where a trader has paid for the promotion without making that clear in the content or by images or sounds clearly identifiable by the consumer (advertorial)" and "Falsely claiming or creating the impression that the trader is not acting for purposes relating to his trade" (Directive 2005/29/EC, Annex I, points 11 and 22, as adopted, legislation.gov.uk); and a practice is a misleading omission where a trader "fails to identify the commercial intent of the commercial practice if not already apparent from the context" (art. 7(2), as adopted). Primary law, read in the UK National Archives' copy of the text as adopted; later amendments were not checked there. The Commission's Influencer Legal Hub says influencers who regularly advertise or sell are "categorised as traders", and that each member state has a consumer protection authority that "applies European and national consumer protection rules in practice" (European Commission, Influencer Legal Hub, legal briefs dated 11 Oct 2023 and 13 Sep 2024); the national authority where you live is the one to read. In a coordinated check of the posts of 572 influencers (SCL's count; other reports of the same sweep give 576), 97% posted commercial content and "only one in five systematically indicated that their content was advertising" (SCL, 20 Feb 2024; secondary report of the Commission's sweep). A Digital Fairness Act that would address influencer marketing was announced as a fourth-quarter 2026 initiative; on 22 July 2026 no proposal had been adopted (PinMeTo, 22 Jul 2026; secondary). On 11 November 2026 the Commission presented its Digital Fairness Act proposal, which press reports say includes rules on paid influencer promotion; it still has to pass the European Parliament and the Council before it is law (ecommercenews.eu, 12 Nov 2026; CEA Digilaw, 11 Nov 2026; secondary). Its text was not read for this unit.

The platforms. YouTube: if you feature "branded content, sponsorships, endorsements, or other commercial relationships" you "have to let YouTube know by selecting the paid promotion button", which "adds a disclosure label that appears at the beginning of your video", and you remain "responsible for understanding and complying with all applicable legal requirements" (YouTube Help, answer 154235). Its branded content policy defines branded content as "any content on YouTube influenced by a brand partner in exchange for something of value", lists goods that may not be promoted at all, and says YouTube "may automatically apply a branded content label to your video and notify you" (YouTube Branded Content Policies, answer 17596007, undated); a trade report dates the announcement of automated detection to 3 September 2026 (PPC Land; secondary). Instagram: "When you create organic branded content on Instagram, our branded content policies require that you use the paid partnership label to tag the brand" (Instagram Help, paid partnership label); Instagram names the paid partnership label, with collab and remix, as tools that "make it easy to include potential third-party content in posts you had a role in creating" (Instagram, Rewarding original creators on Instagram, 30 Apr 2026). Platform-official. Both regulators above say the tool alone may not be enough, so the working rule is tool plus words.

6.2 · Other people's image, and children's

The question: when someone else appears in your video or post, do you need their agreement, in what form, and what changes if they are a child?

European Union. The GDPR does not apply to processing "in the course of a purely personal or household activity" (art. 2(2)(c)), but the Court of Justice held that posting a video on a video site "without restricting access to that video, thereby permitting access to personal data to an indefinite number of people", falls outside that exception (CJEU, C-345/17 Buivids, 14 Feb 2019, para. 43, copy of the judgment). Ireland's regulator puts the consequence plainly: if a photo "was posted publicly on a social media account", "you are likely to be considered a data controller", and you need "your lawful basis for the processing of such personal data under Article 6(1) of the GDPR" (Data Protection Commission, photos in public places; regulator guidance). Where the basis is consent, it must be one you can demonstrate, withdrawal must be possible "at any time", and "It shall be as easy to withdraw as to give consent" (GDPR art. 7(1) and 7(3), as adopted). National law may add rules of its own on a person's image; this unit did not survey them.

United Kingdom. For adults the question is the same under the UK GDPR, the Regulation as kept in UK law (UK GDPR art. 3); its household exemption was not re-read for this unit. For children, the ICO says "Children merit specific protection because they may be less aware of the risks", and for online services relying on consent, "Only users aged 13 and over can give their own consent" (ICO, Using children's information: a guide, updated 15 May 2026). That rule concerns a child signing up to a service and does not address a child appearing in your video; no UK source found settles who consents for a child shown on a creator's channel, which is a question for a lawyer if it ever arises.

United States. Image rights are state law. California's statute, as one example, makes liable "Any person who knowingly uses another's name, voice, signature, photograph, or likeness" for "purposes of advertising or selling" "without that person's prior consent", and for a minor requires "the prior consent of their parent or legal guardian" (California Civil Code §3344, public.law copy; primary law, unofficial copy). Illinois has gone further for children in creator content: from 1 July 2024, a minor under 16 is "considered engaged in the work of vlogging" where "at least 30% of the vlogger's compensated video content produced within a 30-day period" includes the minor, and the vlogger must set aside earnings in a trust (Illinois Public Act 103-0556; primary law). France did the same for children under 16 in 2020 (the EU's Better Internet for Kids summary of the law of 19 October 2020; secondary).

The platforms. YouTube treats anyone "under 18 years old" as a minor; content featuring minors "may have some features disabled", comments among them; it advises "Make sure the minor is supervised by an adult" and forbids content that reveals "a minor's personal information" (YouTube Help, Child safety policy, answer 2801999). Platform-official.

Strangers who answer your questions. PB2's repeat-back test asks strangers to describe the account (its five people, its delay and its pass mark are conventions, PB2 §3). Their answers are their words. A note that holds no name and no face, kept only to score the test, identifies no one; a recording, a name or a quotation in public is a different matter, and the rule above applies to it. The working rule (this unit's): say what the answers are for, write them down without names, publish none.

6.3 · Music and other people's footage

The question: what may you put in a video that you did not make yourself: music, clips, photographs, scans?

YouTube. "A copyright claim may be automatically generated when an uploaded video matches another video"; depending on the owner's settings the video can be blocked, monetised by the owner, or tracked; claims "usually don't impact your channel or account"; the fixes are to mute the claimed audio, trim the segment, or replace the track from the Audio Library (YouTube Help, Learn about Content ID claims, answer 6013276). A strike is different: it "means your content was removed due to a legal copyright removal request", and "Channels that get 3 copyright strikes in 90 days are subject to termination" (YouTube Help, Copyright strike basics, answer 2814000). Audio Library tracks "won't be claimed", may be used in monetised videos, and Creative Commons tracks require you to "credit the artist in your video's description" (YouTube Help, Audio Library, answer 3376882). On using other people's clips, YouTube explains that fair use is a US concept; in the EU "the use must fit into specific categories, such as quotation, criticism, review, caricature, parody, and pastiche"; other countries have "fair dealing"; and "Giving credit to the owner of a copyrighted work won't by itself turn a non-transformative copy of their material into fair use" (YouTube Help, Fair use on YouTube, answer 9783148). PB5 §5 adds the Shorts rule: a Short over one minute with an active Content ID claim is blocked globally. All platform-official.

Instagram. Instagram's 2020 music guidelines say "The greater the number of full-length recorded tracks in a video, the more likely it may be limited", "shorter clips of music are recommended", and "There should always be a visual component to your video" (Instagram, Updates and guidelines for including music in video, 20 May 2020). Instagram's Help Centre page titled "Access to the licensed music library on Instagram", which mentions Meta's Sound Collection, loaded without its body text, so what business and creator accounts may use was not read. Instagram's original content rules say "you are responsible for obtaining any necessary permissions before using someone else's work"; reposts and shares to Stories "highlight other people's work while ensuring the original creator receives attribution"; and collab, remix and the paid partnership label are the tools for including other people's work in a post you helped make (Instagram, Original content guidelines; Instagram, 30 Apr 2026). PB7 §5 covers what unoriginal posts lose in reach. Platform-official.

The law behind the platforms. The copyright statutes behind YouTube's summary were not opened for this unit, beyond the ownership section in §6.4. For a creator who films her own work, the practical position is simpler than the law: use your own sound and footage, or a track whose licence you can show.

6.4 · What to check in your employment contract

The question: does your contract, or a policy it points to, limit what you may do or publish outside work, and who owns what you make?

European Union. The Transparent and Predictable Working Conditions Directive requires member states to ensure "that an employer neither prohibits a worker from taking up employment with other employers, outside the work schedule established with that employer, nor subjects a worker to adverse treatment for doing so", while allowing "incompatibility restrictions" on "objective grounds, such as health and safety, the protection of business confidentiality, the integrity of the public service or the avoidance of conflicts of interests" (Directive (EU) 2019/1152, art. 9, LawPlayer copy; primary law, unofficial copy). The national law that implements it is the one that binds you, and a creator account may or may not count as "employment with other employers"; that reading is for an employment lawyer.

United Kingdom. Ownership: "Where a literary, dramatic, musical or artistic work or a film, is made by an employee in the course of his employment, his employer is the first owner of any copyright in the work subject to any agreement to the contrary" (Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988, s. 11(2); primary law). What a contract says about outside work is not among the items Acas lists for the written statement of particulars, which covers pay, hours, leave and the like; Acas adds that employers should record "the standards of behaviour expected from employees" (Acas, What the written statement must include, updated 24 Jul 2025). Outside-work rules therefore tend to sit in a separate clause or policy, which is why the checklist below asks for every document the contract points to.

United States. Ownership: a work made for hire includes "A work prepared by an employee within the scope of his or her employment", and then "the employer … is the author" (US Copyright Office, Circular 30, Works Made for Hire, revised Aug 2024; regulator guidance on 17 U.S.C. §101). Outside work is state law. New York, as one example, protects "legal recreational activities" (defined as leisure-time activity "for which the employee receives no compensation", so an account that earns may fall outside it) "outside work hours, off of the employer's premises and without use of the employer's equipment or other property", except where the activity "creates a material conflict of interest related to the employer's trade secrets, proprietary information or other proprietary or business interest" (N.Y. Labor Law §201-d; primary law). Other states' rules were not researched for this unit.

The checklist (this unit's, from the sources above):

  1. Any clause on outside work, second jobs or "accumulation" of activities: does it require notice or written permission, and for what?
  2. Any conflict-of-interest clause, and any duty to declare outside income.
  3. Any confidentiality clause, and every document it points to (code of conduct, handbook).
  4. Any social-media or public-communication policy: does it cover personal accounts, naming the employer, or using a job title?
  5. Any clause on who owns what you create, and whether it reaches work made outside working hours, on your own equipment, on a subject unrelated to the job.
  6. Any restriction that applies after you leave.
  7. Working hours, on-call or availability terms that collide with your fixed windows (PB4).

If any of 1, 2 or 4 requires notice or permission, give it in writing before your first public post and keep the reply; if the wording is unclear, an employment lawyer reads it.

6.5 · Registering and declaring income

The question: in the country where you are taxed, when does income from the account (platform payouts, brand payments, gifted products, sales) have to be registered or declared, and what can be set against it? PB1 §8 raised it; the answer depends on where you are taxed, so this unit states no rate or threshold as yours. Amounts below are in their source's currency, with their date.

European Union. Income tax is national: "Your status for tax purposes depends on the national laws of countries A and B, and any double-taxation agreement between them" (Your Europe, Income taxes abroad FAQ, last checked 18 Jun 2026). United Kingdom. HMRC's page for content creators says you need to tell HMRC if you earn over £1,000 from side activities, "even if you just think of it as a hobby", that the £1,000 allowance "adds up" across all of them, that you "must include the value of any gifts or services you received from promoting products online", and that registration for Self Assessment is due "by 5 October" after the tax year (HMRC, Tax help for hustles: content creators, modified 22 Sep 2026); the trading allowance page says "The trading allowance is a tax exemption of up to £1,000 a year" (GOV.UK, Tax-free allowances on property and trading income). Regulator guidance; amounts as published on those dates.

United States. Hobby income is still reported: taxpayers "must report the income they receive" on Schedule 1, and the IRS lists the factors that separate a hobby from a business (IRS, FS-2022-38, Oct 2022). 6.6 · Data protection for an email list

The question: before your first sign-up form goes live, which law applies to the addresses you collect, on what basis may you send to them, and what must every form and every email say? PB3 §5 made collection wait for this answer.

Which law applies. The GDPR reaches a controller outside the EU where processing relates to "the offering of goods or services, irrespective of whether a payment of the data subject is required, to such data subjects in the Union" (art. 3(2)(a), as adopted); the UK GDPR has the same rule for "data subjects in the United Kingdom" (UK GDPR art. 3(2)(a)). Whether a free newsletter offered in English to anyone "targets" EU or UK readers is a question the text does not settle by itself; a list open to the world will hold addresses from all three jurisdictions, so the working rule is to meet the stricter rules.

European Union. Email for direct marketing "may be allowed only in respect of subscribers or users who have given their prior consent", with an exception for a seller's own customers for "its own similar products or services", provided they can object "free of charge and in an easy manner" at collection and in each message (ePrivacy Directive 2002/58/EC, art. 13(1)–(2), as amended in 2009, legislation.gov.uk copy; each member state transposes it, so the national regulator's page is the one to read). The form must give, among other things, "the identity and the contact details of the controller", "the purposes of the processing", "the legal basis for the processing", "the period for which the personal data will be stored", "the existence of the right to withdraw consent at any time" and "the right to lodge a complaint with a supervisory authority" (GDPR art. 13, as adopted); and you must "be able to demonstrate that the data subject has consented" (art. 7(1)). Primary law.

United Kingdom. The Privacy and Electronic Communications Regulations forbid sending "unsolicited communications for the purposes of direct marketing by means of electronic mail" unless the recipient "has previously notified the sender that he consents", except for the soft opt-in for your own customers (PECR reg. 22(2)–(3); primary law; amended from 5 Feb 2026 by the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025, which inserted a new paragraph (3A), a soft opt-in for charities' marketing that does not reach a creator's own list (Withers, "Soft opt-in now in force for UK charities"; secondary)). The ICO's page: "You must not send marketing emails or texts to individuals without specific consent", and you must "provide a valid contact address so they can opt out or unsubscribe"; the page notes it "is under review" because of the 2025 Act (ICO, Electronic mail marketing, dated 19 Nov 2024; regulator guidance).

United States. The CAN-SPAM Act sets rules for commercial email, among them: no false header information; "Your message must include your valid physical postal address", which may be "a post office box you've registered with the U.S. Postal Service, or a private mailbox you've registered with a commercial mail receiving agency"; opt-outs honoured "within 10 business days"; and penalties "of up to $53,088" per email (FTC, CAN-SPAM Act: A Compliance Guide for Business, Aug 2023, modified 22 Jul 2025; regulator guidance). Whether an email that only links to a free video is "commercial" under the Act depends on its primary purpose, which the FTC guide explains; the address requirement is cheap to meet either way.

What that means for a creator's list. Consent at sign-up, recorded; a notice on the form with the article 13 items; an unsubscribe link and your identity in every email; a postal address you are willing to print, which for someone working from home means one of the FTC's two other options, a PO box or a registered private mailbox. That last line is also a disclosure decision: a home address in an email footer publishes where you live.

6.7 · The trademark check PB2 left open

The question: is the name you chose already registered, or applied for, by someone else for the same or similar goods or services, in a place where you publish?

Where to look. The EU's IP helpdesk calls TMview and EUIPO's eSearch plus the "two main online free-of-charge databases", TMview covering records from "more than 50 participating trade mark offices", including EU trade marks and international registrations; a mark "should not be similar or identical to any trade mark already existing" in "the classes of products or services to which the new trade mark applies" (European IP Helpdesk, Trade mark and design searches, 2018). The UK IPO's search lets you check "if a similar trade mark to your brand already exists in the UK" by keyword, owner or number (GOV.UK, Search for a trade mark). The USPTO replaced its old search system (TESS) on 30 November 2023 with a "new trademark search system" at tmsearch.uspto.gov (USPTO, 18 Oct 2023). All official.

What a search can and cannot tell you. It shows registered marks and applications in the registers searched; it does not show rights that were never registered, and it does not tell you whether a similar mark in a nearby class would be held to conflict. A clean search is a reason to keep going; a hit in a class near what you do is the point to ask a trade mark attorney. Registering your own name is a later decision with a fee, which no unit of this course prices.
