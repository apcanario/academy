SourcesHow this unit was made

01What the numbers below are

Every figure in this unit was checked at its source on 12 November 2026. Platform rules, thresholds, prices and benchmarks carry that date unless another is given. Each figure carries one or more of these labels:

  • Platform-official: YouTube's or Instagram's own help pages, or a statement the company made to the press.
  • Vendor: a dataset published by a company that sells tools or services to creators or brands, drawn from its own users or its own index. None of these samples is random. The companies say so themselves where they say anything.
  • Correlational: the data shows two things moving together. It cannot show that one causes the other.
  • Survivor sample: the dataset only contains accounts that kept going, or only accounts that reached a milestone. Its numbers overstate what a typical new account sees.
  • Peer-reviewed: published in an academic journal after review. Preprint: academic work not shown to have been reviewed.
  • Anecdote or documented creator case: one creator's own account of their numbers.
  • Derived: arithmetic done for this unit on a source's figures, shown where it is done.

The running example is Mara Venn (Mara, fictional): 38, a full-time accountant who restores mid-century furniture at weekends and wants to teach restoration to people who have no workshop. She starts with no YouTube subscribers and 140 Instagram followers, films on a phone and has 6 hours a week. Her numbers are illustrations. Every benchmark she is set against is sourced; nothing she does is evidence about anyone else.

One planning assumption is needed before any benchmark can be applied to her, because most datasets split accounts by how often they post. For this unit, Mara publishes one long-form YouTube video every two weeks (26 a year) and three Instagram feed posts a week cut from the same material (Mara, fictional; PB3 sets the split between the platforms and PB4 the calendar, and if either changes these rates, the rows of her sheet that depend on posting rate are re-read). Her day one is Monday 4 January 2027 (Mara, fictional), the week of her first upload.

02YouTube: the first 12, 24 and 36 months

Views per video in the first ten uploads. vidIQ studied channels created between 1 January and 30 June 2025 that published at least ten videos of the same format in their first 90 days. Its long-form cohort, 137,355 channels with live streams excluded, had median 30-day views of about 180 on the first upload, 211 on the fifth and 272 on the tenth. The median of each channel's best video among its first ten was 1,948. 57.8% of these channels had at least one of the first ten past 1,000 views and 30.8% had one past 10,000 (vidIQ, YouTube First 10 Videos Study, 28 Jul 2026, updated 30 Jul 2026). The same study's Shorts cohort (367,243 channels) had median 30-day views of about 1,362 on the first Short and 1,344 on the tenth. Two corrections apply before using these numbers. The study says "Channels that gave up before publishing ten same-format videos in their first 90 days are excluded entirely", so it is a survivor sample and the true medians for all new channels are lower. And ten videos in 90 days is faster than one every two weeks: at Mara's rate her tenth upload goes out in week 19 (derived: upload 1 in week 1, then one every two weeks). Vendor data; survivor sample.

The odds of reaching 1,000 subscribers within a year. The largest dataset found is vidIQ's study of 32,926,088 channels that had fewer than 1,000 subscribers and at least one video, compared with themselves 360 days later. 500,409 crossed 1,000, about 1.52% (derived). The share depended on how much the channel's video count grew (vidIQ, Uploads and reaching 1,000 subscribers, 11 Sep 2026):

Net change in video count over 360 days Channels Crossed 1,000
None 27,360,211 0.20%
1 to 11 3,423,276 1.86%
12 to 51 1,259,198 8.65%
52 or more 883,403 30.77%

vidIQ states the limits itself: "This is not a random sample or a census of all YouTube channels"; the groups were not matched on starting subscribers, channel age, resources or quality; the result is "an observational association, not proof that uploading caused subscriber growth"; the count is a net change in total videos, so it does not count individual uploads; and channel totals "do not separate Shorts, long-form videos, or livestreams" (same source). A channel adding 26 videos a year sits in the 12–51 band: about one in twelve channels in that band crossed 1,000 within the year, and 91.35% did not (derived: 100 minus 8.65). The 52-plus band's 30.77% belongs to channels that may also have had more time, money or audience. Vendor data; correlational.

How many videos the channels that got there had. A second vidIQ dataset counts the videos 5,330,249 channels had at the moment they reached 1,000 subscribers. All were vidIQ users' channels created in the previous three years. 5.77% had 0–5 videos, 4.17% had 6–9, 8.69% had 10–19, 13.36% had 20–39, 14.54% had 40–69, 22.58% had 70–149 and 30.89% had 150 or more (vidIQ, How many videos does it take to get 1,000 subscribers, 9 Jun 2025). So 68% of the channels that crossed had 40 or more videos and 53.5% had 70 or more (derived). This dataset holds only channels that got there. It has no denominator, so it cannot give anyone's chance of getting there at any video count. Vendor data; survivor sample.

How fast the median channel grows. Among channels that had at least one subscriber and added videos in the 90 days to 20 July 2026, the median monthly gain was 0.5 subscribers in the 1–99 band (1,047,481 channels), 1.4 in the 100–499 band (1,430,714) and 3.7 in the 500–999 band (853,903) (vidIQ, YouTube Subscriber Benchmarks 2026, 20 Jul 2026). At the median of the smallest band a channel gains about six subscribers a year (derived). The same page counts 61,186,246 channels with at least one public subscriber; 40.6% have 1,000 or more and 7.90% have 10,000 or more. That is a snapshot of channels of every age at one moment; it gives no rate for new channels. Vendor data; medians, which describe no single channel's path.

What the numbers say together. Median growth does not reach 1,000 in any time a person would plan for: at the band medians a channel takes about 200 months to go from 1 to 100 subscribers at +0.5 a month, about 290 more to reach 500 at +1.4, and about 135 more to reach 1,000 at +3.7 (derived). An inference, which no dataset found tests: the channels that reach 1,000 are likely to be ones where a few uploads do far better than the rest. The first-ten study shows that kind of concentration, with the median best video at about seven times the views of the median tenth upload (derived: 1,948 against 272).

Why subscribers move last. Todd Beaupré, YouTube's senior director of growth and discovery, said in an interview published 1 September 2026 that "probably all your videos have about maybe a 10% or less click-through rate" among subscribers, and that "even your best videos, 90% of the time, your subscribed audience isn't deciding to watch" (PPC Land, 2 Sep 2026). That is a secondary report of an interview, with no sample given for the 10%. Read with the vidIQ figures, it means a subscriber count summarises past reach, and the per-video numbers (views, click-through rate, percentage viewed) move first. The checkpoints in the deliverable read those first.

At 24 and 36 months. No dataset found follows a cohort of new YouTube channels for 24 or 36 months. The 360-day crossing rates cannot be chained into a two- or three-year rate: a channel still under 1,000 after one year is a different channel from one starting out, and vidIQ publishes no second-year figure. What can be said at 24 and 36 months comes only from the upload counts above, converted to months at a posting rate (§5), and from creator cases, which are survivors (§9).

03Instagram: the first 12, 24 and 36 months

The Instagram evidence is thinner than YouTube's, and it describes different accounts. No dataset found follows new personal accounts from under 1,000 followers. The four largest sources found are below; they measure different things and do not agree.

Socialinsider analysed 67,541,436 organic posts from about 579,000 Instagram business accounts, which it calls brand accounts, with 1,000 followers or more. Follower growth is defined as "the number of followers a profile gained during the year divided by the number of followers it started the year with", averaged across accounts in each size band. In 2025, average growth was 22% for accounts with 1,000–5,000 followers (131,955 accounts), 20.29% at 5,000–10,000, 17.20% at 10,000–50,000, 13.62% at 50,000–100,000 and 11.25% at 100,000–1,000,000; all five bands grew less than in 2024 (38.00% to 27.00%) (Socialinsider, 2026 Instagram Organic Benchmarks, 20 Feb 2026, updated with Q3 2026 data). The page has internal inconsistencies: its methodology states the period as "January 2024 - December 2026" while the text says the 2026 report holds 2025 values, and one sentence says accounts with more than 100,000 followers were left out while a 100,000–1,000,000 band is reported. Vendor data; brands; a mean of per-account rates, which a few fast-growing accounts can pull up; accounts under 1,000 followers excluded.

Metricool analysed 24,364,803 Instagram posts from 375,118 accounts, comparing January–February 2025 with January–February 2026. Its press release says "only 21% of accounts under 10,000 followers growing in 2026" (Metricool, press release, 16 Jun 2026). The release does not define growing, and Metricool's longer report on the same data gives no follower-growth figure by account size (Metricool, Instagram Trends 2026, 16 Jun 2026). Vendor data; Metricool users, brands and creators mixed; definition unstated.

Buffer's benchmark page reports, across all account sizes, an average monthly follower growth of 7.5% and a median reach of 261 unique accounts per post (Buffer, Instagram benchmarks, last updated 8 Nov 2026). The page gives its sample as 27 million posts from 273,000 accounts in one place and 30 million posts from 250,000 accounts in another, gives no period, and shows its figures by follower band only in charts that could not be read as text. Vendor data; all sizes pooled.

Buffer's posting-frequency study is the only Instagram dataset found that addresses posting rate with a method stronger than comparing different accounts. Its data scientist analysed 2.1 million feed posts (photos, Reels and carousels; Stories excluded) from 102,000 accounts and fitted a fixed-effects model, which compares each account with itself in weeks it posted more against weeks it posted less. Against weeks with no posts, an account's weekly follower growth rate was higher by 0.12% in weeks with 1–2 posts, 0.26% with 3–5, 0.44% with 6–9 and 0.66% with 10 or more (Buffer's figures; its own example converts them at 5,000 followers into about 6, 13, 22 and 33 extra followers a week, so they are points added to the weekly growth rate); median reach per post was about 12% higher at 3–5 posts a week than at 1–2 (Buffer, How often should you post on Instagram, 13 Aug 2025). The article gives no confidence intervals, no counts per band and no study period, and the accounts are Buffer users. Vendor data; within-account association.

A rate means little at a small base. For Mara at 140 followers and three posts a week, 0.26% of weekly growth is about 0.36 followers a week, one follower every three weeks or so (derived: 140 × 0.0026). The same rate at 5,000 followers is 13 a week, which is the example Buffer itself gives.

What these sources do not settle. A 7.5% average monthly growth (Buffer, all sizes), a 22% average yearly growth (Socialinsider, brands at 1,000–5,000) and 21% of small accounts growing at all (Metricool) cannot describe the same population. They come from different users of different tools, measured over different periods, with different definitions, and two of the three are means that a few outliers can dominate. None of them reports an account's followers at 12, 24 or 36 months from a start under 1,000. So this unit gives no Instagram figure for those three points: the expectation sheet marks the rows as unsourced, and PB7 completes them if its research finds a dataset.

Two points follow for someone starting now. First, a YouTube channel that starts in November 2026 or later should plan on the 8,000-hour rule: YouTube says entry thresholds "for new creators" change on 1 February 2027 and does not say whether qualifying or applying decides which rule applies (same source). Mara's day one, 4 January 2027, falls four weeks before the change (Mara, fictional). Second, Instagram's eligibility for its earning tools is not stated as a number on the page Instagram publishes for creators; the Instagram Help Centre pages on Subscriptions and Gifts could not be read as text here, and a vendor guide updated in July 2026 says "Meta's own documentation is not consistent on this" (Inro, Instagram Subscriptions, updated 23 Jul 2026). The professional dashboard in the app is where an account sees what it is eligible for; PB9 takes up the earning lines and their terms. Facebook's own subscriptions page requires 10,000 followers or 250 return viewers, plus 50,000 post engagements or 180,000 watch minutes in the last 60 days, for a Facebook Page (Meta for Creators, Subscriptions); that is a Facebook rule and says nothing about Instagram.

What 8,000 hours means in views. 8,000 hours is 480,000 minutes. vidIQ's guidance band for average percentage viewed on a 5–15 minute video is 40–55%, a band it labels "vidIQ guidance" and publishes without a sample (vidIQ, YouTube Average View Duration, updated 21 Jul 2026). For a 10-minute video (Mara's planned length, Mara, fictional) that is 4 to 5.5 minutes per view, so 8,000 hours needs about 87,300 to 120,000 long-form views in 365 days, or about 3,360 to 4,620 per video at 26 videos a year (derived). The median tenth upload in the first-ten study drew 272 views in 30 days; the line is about 12 to 17 times that (derived). The fan-funding line of 3,000 hours needs about 32,700 to 45,000 views a year on the same assumptions (derived). Vendor heuristic plus platform rule; arithmetic shown.

YouTube. The only route from data to months is the upload counts in §2, converted at a posting rate. These are derived figures: the months at which a channel posting at a steady rate reaches each upload count that the channels which crossed 1,000 had reached.

26 (one every two weeks) 12–51 (8.65%) month 18.5 month 32.3 month 69.2

Sources: vidIQ, 11 Sep 2026 for the bands; vidIQ, 9 Jun 2025 for the upload counts; months derived. Read the table with the two datasets' limits: the first is correlational and unmatched, the second contains only channels that crossed. At 26 videos a year, the 68% of crossers who had 40 or more videos would have reached that count around month 18 or 19, and the 53.5% with 70 or more around month 32. A channel at that rate still under 1,000 at the end of its second or even third year is inside the range these datasets show. Neither dataset says how many channels at that rate never crossed.

Above 1,000 subscribers, vidIQ's frequency study of 10,210,278 channels with at least 1,000 subscribers (uploads counted April 2025 to March 2026, long-form and Shorts together) found median monthly growth of 0.89% in views and 0.10% in subscribers for channels posting 1–3 times a month, against 1.32% and 0.39% at 4–7 a month, 1.70% and 0.63% at 8–11, and 2.18% and 0.91% at 12 or more. The page states this is "correlation, not causation" and that larger channels may simply have the resources to post more (vidIQ, YouTube Upload Frequency Study, 8 Jul 2026). At 0.10% a month, a channel of 1,000 gains about one subscriber a month (derived). Vendor data; correlational.

Metricool's YouTube report, from 799,718 videos published by 71,177 accounts, compares 2025 with 2026 and gives the share of accounts that moved up a size tier "during the period", without defining the window: 9.59% of channels under 2,000 subscribers, 16.01% at 2,000–10,000, 7.01% at 10,000–100,000 and 2.11% at 100,000–1,000,000; 10.08% overall (Metricool, YouTube Trends 2026, 29 Jul 2026). Vendor data; Metricool users.

No dataset found gives the calendar months from a channel's start to 1,000, to 10,000 or to any later milestone. The months above are arithmetic on upload counts, and nothing more.

Instagram. Buffer's fixed-effects estimates in §3 give the association between posting rate and the weekly growth rate. No dataset found gives the months to 1,000 followers, or to any follower count, at any posting rate, and none gives the number of posts accounts had when they reached one. The 1,000-follower Live threshold in §4 therefore has no sourced timeline.

06The plateau, and why most accounts stop there

Where the stall sits. The largest group in the largest YouTube dataset is the channel whose video count did not grow: 27,360,211 of 32,926,088 sub-1,000 channels, 83.1% (derived), showed no net change over 360 days, and 0.20% of them crossed 1,000 (vidIQ, 11 Sep 2026). vidIQ notes that a net change of zero does not prove a channel published nothing, since uploads can be offset by removals. Even so, the most common state of a small YouTube channel in that dataset is one that is not adding videos. Vendor data.

Growth slows with size on both platforms. On YouTube, among the 11,752,034 channels with at least one subscriber and a net increase in video count in the 90 days to 20 July 2026, median monthly subscriber growth falls with size: 1.26% at 1–99 subscribers, 0.59% at 100–499, 0.53% at 500–999, 0.51% at 1,000–9,999, 0.48% at 10,000–99,999, 0.30% at 100,000–999,999 and 0.24% at 1 million or more (vidIQ, 20 Jul 2026). On Instagram, Socialinsider's average yearly growth for brand accounts falls from 22% at 1,000–5,000 followers to 11.25% at 100,000–1,000,000 (Socialinsider, 20 Feb 2026). Across sizes, the percentage rate falls. For a small account the percentages are applied to a small base, so the absolute gain stays small for a long time: at +0.5 subscribers a month, the median 1–99 channel's year looks flat.

Why accounts stop there. No study found records the follower count or the month at which creators stop. That most stop at the plateau is an inference from where stalled channels concentrate. Three kinds of evidence describe the mechanism:

  • Attention drives output. Huberman, Romero and Wu analysed 9,896,816 YouTube videos from 579,471 uploaders, up to 30 April 2008, and found that "a lack of attention leads to a decrease in the number of videos uploaded", which "in many cases asymptotes to no uploads whatsoever" (Huberman, Romero and Wu, arXiv 0809.3030; published as "Crowdsourcing, attention and productivity", Journal of Information Science 35(6), 2009, 758–765, per the library record). Peer-reviewed; the data is from 2008, before most of today's YouTube existed.
  • Creators report burnout and the belief that a break is punished. Billion Dollar Boy, a creator agency, surveyed 1,000 creators in the US and UK: 52% said they had experienced burnout as a direct result of their career and 37% were actively considering leaving; creative fatigue (40%), demanding workloads (31%) and constant screen time (27%) were the leading causes, and among those who had burned out, financial instability was ranked the most severe factor (55%) (Billion Dollar Boy, 7 Jul 2025). Agency survey; self-report. Nguyen and Duffy, in New Media & Society (7 July 2026; 58 creator-made posts, 62 news accounts, 78 interviews), found burnout partly "unspeakable", and recorded the line "If I take a break, I'm going to get punished by the algorithm" (Cornell Chronicle, 8 Jul 2026). Peer-reviewed, reported by the authors' university.
  • Gaps go with fewer views. AIR Media-Tech's study of 18,000 English-language YouTube channels found that channels which "went dark for 14+ days three or more times a year" got fewer views per video than channels that kept their rhythm, and that a predictable schedule went with more views per subscriber than the same number of videos on random days; the page says "This is correlational data, not causal", gives no effect size for the gap finding, and its main analysis covers channels with 100,000 to 1,000,000 monthly views (AIR Media-Tech, 8 Jun 2026). Vendor data; correlational; channels far larger than a starting one. YouTube's performance FAQ says it found "no correlation between break length and changes in views" across "thousands of channels that took a break" (YouTube Help, answer 141805, checked 12 Nov 2026; platform-official, no sample size, period or method given).

Put together, the documented sequence is: per-video numbers far below what was hoped, a subscriber or follower count that looks flat, less output in response, and a channel whose video count stops growing. The stop rule in the deliverable is built so that the first two are expected and recorded, and only the third, the output itself, can end the first year.

07What the evidence says about who continues

No study found follows a cohort of small, part-time creators and reports who is still posting after one, two or three years. What exists is indirect:

  • The 2008 YouTube data shows uploads falling when attention falls (§6). In that model, who continues is partly decided by early response, which the first-ten study shows is low for the median channel and concentrated in a few videos.
  • The early winners are visible early. In a preprint on the same 2008 data, Wu and Huberman define success as a video in the top 1% of views for its upload week and report that about 21% of successful producers succeeded with their first video and 34.1% within their first week; the chance that a later upload succeeds declines with each upload (Wu and Huberman, Persistence and Success in the Attention Economy, arXiv 0904.0489, 2009). Preprint; 2008 data. It cannot measure when producers stop, which the authors say their data does not observe.
  • Hours: in a January 2026 survey of 1,000 US-based creators by The Influencer Marketing Factory, 37% said they spend "five to ten hours per week creating content, on average" (MarketingProfs, 2026). Agency survey, reported by a trade publication; it does not say whether those creators continue longer than others.

What the evidence supports: channels that keep adding videos are the ones that cross milestones (vidIQ, correlational), and attention received is associated with continued output (2008 data). Whether a person with a full-time job and 6 hours a week continues is not measured anywhere found. The checkpoints treat continuing as something Mara decides on evidence at fixed dates, so that a bad week is not the moment the decision is taken.

08The cost: hours and money with a phone

Hours. 6 hours a week is 312 hours a year (derived). No dataset found gives the hours a one-person creator spends per long-form video or per Instagram post; the figures that circulate are individual creators' estimates. The 37% of creators who report 5–10 hours a week (§7) place 6 hours inside the one range that survey reports, and say nothing about output per hour. The only reliable hours figure for any reader is their own: Mara's budget in the deliverable is a plan, logged weekly against what she actually spends, and PB4 times each task.

Money. A phone-only setup has three costs that can be checked and several that cannot:

  • Publishing: none of the platform pages cited in §4 lists a fee to post or to qualify for a programme.
  • Editing: Instagram's Edits app launched on 22 April 2025 as a free download for iOS and Android, exporting "without added watermarks" (PetaPixel, 22 Apr 2025). Secondary report of a platform launch.
  • Audio: RØDE's own store lists its Wireless Micro, a clip-on microphone whose receiver plugs into a USB-C or Lightning phone, at $99.00 USD in every variant (checked 12 November 2026; RØDE brand store). That is one product's list price, given as an example of what a phone microphone costs; PB5 covers whether audio needs one and what not to buy.
  • A tripod or phone clamp, lighting, storage and paid apps: no price was checked for this unit, and any figure Mara uses for them is her own estimate, labelled.

The money question that law decides. If the account earns anything in the first year, from a platform, a brand or a sale, the question to answer is: in the country where you are taxed, does this income have to be declared, and are equipment costs deductible against it? Where to start: in the EU, income tax is national, and the EU's own guidance says tax status "depends on the national laws" of the countries involved (Your Europe, Income taxes abroad FAQ, last checked 18 Jun 2026), so the national tax authority's site is the source; in the UK, HMRC's page on the trading allowance (GOV.UK, Tax-free allowances on property and trading income); in the US, the IRS's guidance on telling a hobby from a business, which says hobby income must still be reported (IRS, FS-2022-38, Oct 2022). This unit is information, not legal or tax advice; PB8 treats the rules in full.

09Survivor bias in creator cases, and how to read around it

Creator cases are told by the creators who succeeded. Two documented first-year cases show the pattern:

  • Sidika Tunc Candogan, an assistant professor, recorded her first YouTube video on 28 July 2023, published 88 long-form videos in her first year, and reports 20,000+ subscribers, 600,000+ views and $300+ revenue at one year; her first video "was viewed only 164 times" (Candogan, First year as a YouTuber, undated, about July 2024). Documented creator case; self-reported.
  • Christian Pascual posted his first video on 9 April 2023, published 36 videos (311 minutes) in his first year alongside his research, and reports 29,385 subscribers at one year (Very Normal, Issue 14, 9 Apr 2024). Documented creator case; self-reported.

Both are true and both are survivors: the posts exist because the year went well, and first years that end with a few dozen subscribers are rarely written up and shared as widely, and no source found counts them. Set against the base rates, 88 videos a year sits in the 52-plus band, where 30.77% crossed 1,000 within 360 days, and 36 videos a year sits in the 12–51 band, where 8.65% did (§2). Both channels are at the far end of distributions whose medians are a few subscribers a month.

How to read any creator case, including those in DD-PB1:

  1. Find the denominator. Ask how many people started the same way in the same year. If the source cannot say, the case shows that an outcome is possible and nothing about how likely it is.
  2. Check the cadence and the hours. Compare the creator's posting rate with yours and find the band it puts them in. A case posting twice your rate belongs to a different row of the table.
  3. Check the start. A first upload date, an existing audience elsewhere, a job that gives material or credibility, and a niche with little competition all change the base rate. Where the source does not state these, say so.
  4. Separate the numbers from the advice. A creator's account of why it worked is anecdote even when the numbers are documented.
  5. Check the date. Platform rules and formats change; the monetisation line in §4 doubles for new creators on 1 February 2027.
  6. Check the sample's exclusions. The same bias sits inside datasets: vidIQ's first-ten study excludes channels that quit before ten videos, and its 2025 upload-count study contains only channels that crossed 1,000 (§2).

10What no dataset measures

None of the sources found for this unit measures:

  • The calendar months from a new account's start to any follower or subscriber milestone, on either platform. The YouTube months in §5 are arithmetic on upload counts.
  • What happens to a YouTube channel over 24 or 36 months from its start; every YouTube rate here covers one year or 90 days.
  • Instagram growth for accounts under 1,000 followers, or for new personal accounts at any size; the Instagram datasets found are brand-heavy, start at 1,000 followers, or pool all sizes.
  • Instagram's follower thresholds for its earning tools; Instagram's creator page publishes none (§4).
  • The share of creators who stop, when they stop, and at what count.
  • Hours spent per video or per post by one-person accounts, and what extra hours buy.

D3 · Mara's stop rule and first-year checkpoints

The stop rule. Mara stops, or rebuilds the system before uploading again, if she publishes fewer than 3 YouTube videos in each of two consecutive quarters. Three a quarter is 12 a year, the floor of the 12–51 band; below it the base rate is the 1–11 band, 1.86% crossing within 360 days (vidIQ, 11 Sep 2026). No subscriber or follower count is grounds to stop in year one: at 26 videos a year, upload 40, which 68% of the channels that crossed 1,000 had reached when they crossed, arrives around month 18.5 (Y5), and no Instagram dataset gives a timeline (I7). The first review at which subscriber count may count towards a stop is at 24 months, read against Y4 and Y5. She may also stop at any checkpoint because she judges the 312 hours worth more elsewhere; that decision is recorded with the numbers of the day.

The verdicts, in order (her rules, with the base rate each rests on):

  1. Stop or rebuild: fewer than 3 YouTube uploads in this quarter and the one before (Y4).
  2. Change one thing: cut the load: over 7 hours in 3 or more weeks of the quarter. The video gets shorter and the Instagram derivatives drop from six a fortnight to four before the YouTube cadence slips; repeated gaps of 14+ days go with fewer views per video (AIR, §6, vendor, correlational), and posting less on Instagram goes with a lower weekly growth rate (I4).
  3. Change the format (same channel, same cadence; structure, length or packaging): from Q2, click-through under 2% or percentage viewed under its band on 3 or more of the quarter's uploads (Y2, Y3).
  4. Continue: none of the above, whatever the subscriber and follower counts.

The seven reads at every checkpoint (PB10 runs them as a 60-minute procedure; Studio and Insights are opened for the review on its date):

  1. YouTube uploads and Instagram posts this quarter against plan (7 or 6 videos; 39 posts).
  2. Hours logged per week against 6, and the number of weeks over 7.
  3. YouTube: median 30-day views across uploads at least 30 days old, and the best upload so far (Y1).
  4. YouTube: click-through rate and percentage viewed for each upload at least 7 days old (Y2, Y3).
  5. YouTube: subscribers, total and net gain per month, against the band median (Y6); watch hours in the last 365 days against 3,000 and 8,000 (Y10, Y11).
  6. Instagram: followers, total and net gain; median reach per post, recorded against 261 as context (I3); what the professional dashboard shows as eligible (I6).
  7. Money spent against the 150 USD cap, and any income.
