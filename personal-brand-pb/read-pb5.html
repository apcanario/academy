01Filming alone with a phone without stopping the work

Every claim carried over from earlier material was re-opened at its source on 12 November 2026, and the platform limits, policies and figures below were checked the same day; each platform rule carries that date unless another is given. The labels are PB1 §1's (platform-official, vendor, correlational, peer-reviewed, anecdote, derived), plus PB4's her rule, which marks a convention Mara sets for herself that no source sets.

The running example is Mara Venn (Mara, fictional): 38, a full-time accountant who restores mid-century chairs, side tables and small cabinets indoors on a table, with hand tools that fit in one box, as One Box Restoration. Of her 12 hours a fortnight, capture has 2.0, the long-form edit 4.0 in one Sunday window, and packaging 1.0 with six Instagram posts 3.0 in a second (PB1, PB3, PB4). Her first piece is a teak side table with white water rings and worn edges (PB4 §6); upload 1 goes public on Tuesday 5 January 2027. Her numbers are illustrations, never evidence.

PB4 fixed the capture layer: the phone on a clamp, a taped mark on the table for stills, nothing staged and nothing filmed twice, a voice note with three prompts at the end of each session. It left framing to this unit. This section adds the frame, the light, the clips and a short shot list.

One frame that serves both shapes

The two platforms want different shapes. YouTube says "The standard aspect ratio for YouTube on a computer is 16:9", and that for other shapes "the player automatically adapts" (YouTube Help, Recommended upload encoding settings, answer 1722171, checked 12 Nov 2026; platform-official). A video "with a square or vertical aspect ratio up to three minutes in length" is categorised as a Short (YouTube Help, Understand three-minute YouTube Shorts, answer 15424877, checked 12 Nov 2026). Meta's Reels guidance says "Vertical content (9:16) helps ensure your reels are immersive" and lists "blurry video (due to low-resolution)" among the things to avoid (Meta Creators, Reels best practices: tips & tricks, 17 Jun 2022; platform-official, written for Reels on Facebook and Instagram).

PB4's rule is that nothing is filmed twice, so one recording has to give both. The arithmetic decides how (derived):

  • A full-height 9:16 crop from a 1920 × 1080 horizontal frame is 607.5 pixels wide (1080 × 9 ÷ 16), about 32% of the frame's width. To fill a 1080 × 1920 vertical frame it must be enlarged about 1.78 times (1080 ÷ 607.5).
  • The same crop from a 3840 × 2160 frame is 1,215 × 2,160 pixels, larger than 1080 × 1920, so it fills the vertical frame with no enlargement.

So the frame is horizontal, recorded at 3840 × 2160 where the phone allows it, with the work kept inside the middle third of the width, the strip a vertical crop keeps. No source measures how much a 1.78-times enlargement costs a Reel in reach or watch time; Meta names low resolution as a fault without a threshold. A reader whose phone records only 1920 × 1080 should not buy a phone on this arithmetic alone (§10).

Frame rate. YouTube: "Content should be encoded and uploaded in the same frame rate it was recorded" (answer 1722171). One rate for every clip of a season means the edit never mixes rates (her rule; the inference is that an edit exports at one rate).

Mara's frame (Mara, fictional): her phone records 3840 × 2160 at 30 frames a second, which she uses for the whole of Season 1. The clamp holds the phone horizontal on the far edge of the table, about 1.0 metre from where she sits, looking slightly down at the work, with the piece in the middle third and her hands and face in frame when she speaks. For a close-up she moves the clamp in, two or three times a session, as PB4's capture table allows.

Light

Phones struggle in low light for a physical reason. Google's engineers write that "For smartphone cameras, which have small lenses and sensors, a major source of noise is the natural variation of the number of photons entering the lens, called shot noise", with a second source in converting each pixel's charge to a number (Levoy and Pritch, Night Sight: Seeing in the Dark on Pixel Phones, Google Research blog, 14 Nov 2018; manufacturer's engineering account). That account is about still photographs, which a phone can build from several exposures; applying it to video, which records a frame at a time, is this unit's inference. The practical consequence is a test before any purchase: record 30 seconds at the time of day the session runs, look at the dark parts of the piece on the phone at full brightness, and change the position, the time or the lamp she already owns before buying light (her rule).

Mara's table stands beside a window, with the window to her left as she faces the phone (Mara, fictional). Her sessions start in the morning; if one runs into dusk, filming stops and the work goes on (her rule).

One clip per step

PB4 first had the phone recording before the first tool comes out of the box. This unit refines that into one clip per step (her rule): she taps record before she picks up the tool for a step and stops when the step ends, so each clip is one step of the restoration and the to-camera lines fall inside the clips they describe. A tap costs seconds and does not stop the work; the gain is footage bounded by construction, which §9 needs, and a phone that is not filling its storage with the minutes between steps. No source measures either effect; the second is checked by a storage test in the deliverable (A, before the season).

02Audio: what the evidence says about bad sound

The common claim, and what holds

A claim repeated in creator advice is that viewers forgive bad video but not bad audio. That is a comparison, and no study found supports it as a comparison. One peer-reviewed study supports a narrower claim: with the content held identical, poor audio lowers how people judge both the speaker and what is said.

Eryn Newman and Norbert Schwarz played listeners the same material at two audio qualities (Newman and Schwarz, "Good Sound, Good Research: How Audio Quality Influences Perceptions of the Research and Researcher", Science Communication 40(2), 2018, 246–257, doi 10.1177/1075547018759345, full text in a copy hosted by Gwern.net; record at ANU Open Research; peer-reviewed):

  • Experiment 1. "Ninety-seven Amazon Mechanical Turk workers" each heard two conference talks from YouTube, in physics and engineering, "trimmed to 2- to 3-minute segments", one with good audio and one through an audio filter called "Large Room", "increasing the echo and decreasing the clarity of the speaker". Good audio was rated higher overall by 0.60 on the five-point scales (95% confidence interval 0.41 to 0.79): 0.93 on how good the talk was, 0.69 on liking the speaker, 0.51 on the importance of the research and 0.33 on the speaker's intelligence.
  • Experiment 2. Ninety-nine workers heard two NPR Science Friday interviews, one made to sound "as if the researcher had called in on a bad phone line". Good audio was rated higher by 0.19 (0.03 to 0.36).
  • Comprehension. Restricted to the 64% of experiment 1's sample who answered correctly about both talks' content, the pattern held.
  • The authors: "Despite identical content, people evaluated the research and researcher less favorably when the audio quality was low."

What it does not show. The listeners were paid online workers rating short scientific talks with audio degraded on purpose; nobody chose to watch and nobody could leave, so the study measures judgement; watch time was not measured. It did not degrade the picture, so it says nothing about which of the two viewers forgive more. The degraded condition in experiment 1 is an echo, which is the fault a phone across a hard-surfaced room is most likely to add; that match is this unit's observation; the study does not make it.

The one direct comparison found points the other way, under its own conditions. A US government laboratory report had viewers rate clips with coded audio and video impairments: differences between the video impairments explained 78% of the variance in overall quality and the audio impairments 10%; for speech-only clips, 90% and 3% (McFarland, Pinson, Ford, Webster, Ingram, Hanes and Anderson, Relating Audio and Video Quality Using CIF Video, NTIA Technical Memorandum TM-10-472, Institute for Telecommunication Sciences, Sep 2010; government laboratory report). The authors say it is "unclear whether this indicates underlying truth, or was merely a consequence of the unbalanced test design", and that "This result will not necessarily be valid for other choices of audiovisual material." Codec damage at low resolution in 2010 is far from a phone in a room.

Where sources disagree. Creator advice: audio is what viewers forgive least. Newman and Schwarz (2018): poor audio lowers judgements of the talk and the speaker with content held identical; picture quality was not tested. McFarland et al. (NTIA, 2010): with both degraded, picture explained far more of the overall rating, possibly because of how the impairments were chosen. This unit: the comparison has no source and is not used; the cost of bad audio is sourced and is the reason for the audio check below.

Two reasons that do not depend on the comparison

  1. Captions are made from the audio. YouTube says automatic captions "might misrepresent the spoken content due to mispronunciations, accents, dialects or background noise", and "You should always review automatic captions and edit any parts that haven't been transcribed properly" (YouTube Help, Use automatic captioning, answer 6373554, checked 12 Nov 2026; platform-official). Instagram's captions are generated by transcribing the audio too: the Captions sticker shows "Transcribing audio" before the words appear, and the words can be edited (RNID, How to use video captions on Instagram, updated 16 Jun 2026; a UK hearing-loss charity's guide; Instagram's own help page was not read). Noisy speech costs correction time on every caption track cut from it (inference).
  2. Distance is free. Audio-Technica states that "the volume of the sound will drop by 6 dB every time the distance is doubled from the source", and that "a microphone placed too far away from the source may pick up too much of the room" (Audio-Technica, Do you have any tips on placing microphones to get the best possible sound?, undated; manufacturer). Sennheiser's manual for two of its clip-on microphones instructs: "Attach the microphone to your clothing, keeping a distance of 25 cm (10") from your mouth" (Sennheiser, Lavalier microphones, instruction manual, 05/2026, ME 2 and ME 4; manufacturer). For Mara (Mara, fictional), the phone's own microphone sits about 1.0 metre from her mouth; a clip-on at 25 cm would be four times closer, about 12 dB more of her voice against the same room (20 × log10 4 = 12.0; derived, using the free-field rule, which a room only approximates). Halving the phone's distance to 0.5 metres gains about 6 dB by the same rule, at no cost.

The audio check (before buying a microphone)

PB1 budgeted a clip-on microphone for Mara only if this unit's audio check fails (PB1, D2). The check uses the platform's own caption machine as the listener, since YouTube says background noise degrades it (answer 6373554). The pass mark is a convention; no source sets one.

  1. At the clamp's working position, record two 30-second lines to camera: one in a quiet moment of the work, one during the noisiest step (for Mara, sanding).
  2. Write down exactly what you said in each.
  3. Upload both to YouTube as unlisted, and when the automatic caption track appears in Studio (YouTube says captions "may not be ready at the time that you upload a video"), count the words it got wrong against what you said.
  4. Pass: no more than 1 word in 20 wrong in either recording (convention). Listen to both on earbuds as well: an echo you can hear fails the check whatever the count (convention).
  5. Fail: first the free fixes, in order: speak during pauses in the noisy step; move the phone closer for to-camera lines; put a folded blanket or rug on hard surfaces near the table (inference from Audio-Technica's "too much of the room"; no source measures it). Re-test. Only a second failure justifies a microphone.
  6. Delete the test uploads.

Mara's result (Mara, fictional): at her first session on Saturday 12 December 2026, the quiet line passes (no errors in 41 words) and the sanding line fails (7 errors in 38 words). She adopts the first fix, speaking in the pauses of a noisy step, and re-tests at the next session on 19 December: no errors in 40 words. She buys no microphone; PB1's microphone line stays unspent, and the check is re-run if a piece needs a noisier tool.

Export. YouTube recommends AAC-LC (or Opus or Eclipsa Audio), stereo, at 48 kHz, 384 kbps for stereo (answer 1722171). Record at 48 kHz wherever the phone lets you set it, so nothing is resampled (her rule). No YouTube page opened for this unit states a loudness target for uploads, so none is given.

03The opening seconds: what the retention figures show

Each platform measures the opening in its own window. The figures below were each checked at source; none shows that a particular kind of opening causes viewers to stay.

YouTube long-form: 30 seconds. YouTube's Intro metric "tells you what percentage of your audience still watched your video after the first 30 seconds"; its advice for a weak intro is to "Modify the first 30 seconds of your video and experiment with different styles" and to "Consider changing your video thumbnail and title to better reflect your video content" (YouTube Help, Measure key moments for audience retention, answer 9314415, checked 12 Nov 2026; platform-official). Its Content tab tips say "A typical intro is about 30 seconds long", that "Videos that have 50% of the audience or more watching after the 30-second mark can be found in the 'above typical intros' list", and "If your top moments happen later in your video, consider introducing that content earlier in the video" (YouTube Help, Content tab analytics tips, answer 12942217, checked 12 Nov 2026; platform-official). The 50% marks where YouTube draws a list; YouTube publishes no target. vidIQ's guidance is to "Confirm the title and thumbnail promise immediately, show the payoff, and remove setup the viewer does not need", and its target band for average percentage viewed on a 5–15 minute video is 40–55%, labelled "vidIQ guidance for target bands" with no sample behind it (vidIQ, YouTube Average View Duration, updated 21 Jul 2026; vendor guidance). PB1's sheet already uses that band (row Y3).

YouTube Shorts: "the initial seconds". YouTube's Shorts metric "Stayed to watch" is "The percentage of times viewers stayed to watch past the initial seconds of a Short"; engaged views count those who "stayed to watch past the initial seconds, not including any loops" (YouTube Help, Understand your YouTube content performance, answer 12220281, checked 12 Nov 2026; platform-official). The page does not say how many seconds, and no YouTube benchmark for it was found.

Instagram Reels: 3 seconds. Instagram announced a skip rate for Reels insights in August 2025: "skip rate is the percentage of views from people who decided to skip your reel during those first 3 seconds", replacing the earlier view rate (Social Media Today, 24 Aug 2025, quoting Instagram; platform statement via the press). Instagram published no benchmark. Socialinsider, from 9,771,822 organic Reels of brand accounts with 1,000 to 1 million followers, January to June 2026, reports an average skip rate of 65.50% for accounts of 1–5K followers, falling to 60.50% at 100K–1M (Socialinsider, Instagram Reels statistics, 24 Jun 2026, updated 6 Nov 2026; vendor data, brand accounts only). Its own tables are loose: the bracket counts sum to 5 more than the stated total, and its smallest bracket is labelled "less than 5000" in the method and "1–5K" in the table. At 140 followers Mara is below the sample. Meta's Reels guidance names what the popular ones share: "a clear beginning, middle and end", or "a strong hook or payoff, like a big reveal, punchline or plot twist" (Meta Creators, 17 Jun 2022; platform-official, no figures).

What the figures support, and what they do not. They support three things: the opening is measured on both platforms, in windows of 30 seconds (long-form) and 3 seconds (Reels); YouTube tells creators to make the opening match the title and thumbnail and to bring the strongest moment forward; and on Reels brand accounts' average skip rate in the first 3 seconds runs from 60.5% to 65.5% across Socialinsider's follower bands (vendor, brand accounts, averages). They do not support any specific formula for the first seconds, any number of seconds for a "hook" other than the platforms' own windows, or the expectation that a given opening will lift a given account's numbers. The rules below are built on the platforms' advice and labelled where they go further.

The opening rules (her rules, each with its basis):

  1. Long-form: the first 30 seconds make the title and thumbnail true. Basis: answer 9314415; vidIQ.
  2. Long-form: a few seconds of the strongest moment come first. For a restoration that is the finished piece, which the video otherwise reaches only at its end. Basis: answer 12942217.
  3. Long-form: the box is opened inside the first 30 seconds. The one box is part of the promise in Mara's positioning sentence ("with tools that fit in one box", PB2 D1), so showing it is part of making the package true. That it raises retention is not shown by any source; the rule follows from rule 1.
  4. Reels: the first 3 seconds show the payoff; any logo or greeting comes after it, if at all. For a restoration Reel the payoff is the finished piece, the "big reveal" in Meta's words. Basis: Instagram's skip-rate window; Meta Creators, 2022.

04The structure of a long-form video

The structure follows from three platform statements. The package and the first 30 seconds make one promise (§3). Chapters must meet YouTube's rules: "Make sure that the first timestamp you list starts with 00:00", "Your video should have at least three timestamps listed in ascending order", and "The minimum length for video chapters is 10 seconds"; chapters "add info and context to each portion of the video and let you easily rewatch different parts of the video" (YouTube Help, Video chapters, answer 9884579, checked 12 Nov 2026; platform-official). And the ending sits where most viewers have already left: "Videos on YouTube generally taper off during the playback period" (answer 9314415).

Chapters follow the promise. PB2's four parts (what was wrong, what I did, what went wrong, what it cost) give the chapters, with the steps as the middle. No source found links chapter spacing to retention, so the lengths below are her rule, set by the material.

End on the last line. Percentage viewed is average view duration over length (vidIQ's formula: "APV (%) = (AVD / total video length) x 100"). A closing minute that most viewers never reach adds to the length more than to the average duration, so the percentage falls (this unit's reasoning from the definition; no source measures an outro's effect). The video ends on the last line of the work, then one sentence pointing to the next piece or the season playlist. End screens "can be added to the last 5–20 seconds of a video", the video "has to be at least 25 seconds long", and viewers on mobile web do not see them, iPad excepted (YouTube Help, Add end screens to videos, answer 6388789, checked 12 Nov 2026; platform-official). Because many viewers will not see the end screen, the spoken bridge does the job on its own (her rule). vidIQ's guidance names both: "Use an end screen and a clear verbal bridge to another relevant video."

The season line. PB4 put an on-screen "piece n of x" line on each video and left its place to this unit. It goes on screen as a lower third (a line of text across the bottom of the frame) for about five seconds inside the first 30, and as the first line of the description; it stays out of the title and the thumbnail, whose words are each a promise the opening must keep (§7; her rule). The last video of a season says it is the last and names the next season's start date (PB4, D1 G).

05Shorts and Reels: length, the hook, captions, the cover

Length. YouTube: "You can now create YouTube Shorts up to three minutes in length", for videos uploaded after 15 October 2024 on standard channels; and "Any Short that is over one minute in duration with an active Content ID claim of any type, including manual claims" "will be blocked globally on YouTube" (answer 15424877, checked 12 Nov 2026; platform-official). Instagram's head, Adam Mosseri, announced in January 2025 that Reels could run up to three minutes, up from 90 seconds (Net Influencer, 20 Jan 2025; platform announcement via the press). A vendor page reports that "a 20-minute recording option has gone out to a limited set of creator and business accounts" and describes it as a recording option (Inro, How long can Instagram Reels be?, updated 23 Jul 2026; vendor, no Instagram source cited); Instagram itself announced 20-minute recording in the Reels camera in November 2025, saying it "allows for more creative freedom and longer storytelling"; Social Media Today reports that Instagram has repeatedly said it "will only recommend Reels that are under 3 minutes in length" (Social Media Today, 23 Nov 2025; platform statement via the press). No Instagram page stating which accounts have it was found, so the length selector in the app is the check.

What length works is a separate question with weaker evidence. Socialinsider's 2026 Reels data (above) gives average reach rates by length of 5.20% under 30 seconds, 5.60% at 30–60 seconds, 5.30% at 60–90 seconds, 4.30% at 90–120 seconds and 3.50% over 120 seconds (Socialinsider, 24 Jun 2026; vendor data, brand accounts, correlational: the Reels that ran longer may differ in other ways). The spread between the three shortest bands is under half a percentage point. Mara's Reels are cut to 30–60 seconds (her rule, inside the band with the highest average and far under every platform limit).

The hook. The first 3 seconds are the window Instagram measures (§3). Mara's reveal Reel opens on the finished piece from the taped mark, then cuts to the same view as found; the text on screen in that first frame names the piece and the fault ("Teak side table, white water rings") (her rule; basis §3, rule 4).

Captions. Instagram can generate captions with the Captions sticker on the "New reel" screen, and the creator can "tap on the words to edit or change them"; viewers can also switch on captions in the app's accessibility settings, which "are not available on computers, only on mobile apps" (RNID, 16 Jun 2026; charity guide). YouTube generates automatic captions for "long-form videos and Shorts" and asks creators to review them (answer 6373554). Captions on a Reel are read on a phone, so Mara burns them in with the sticker, corrects every word against what she said, and keeps them clear of the bottom of the frame, where the app's own caption and buttons sit (her rule; no Instagram page found states the size of that area, and vendor "safe zone" figures were not checked).

The cover. Meta's guidance: "Consider adding a cover photo before you share on Instagram" (Meta Creators, 2022). Mosseri's January 2025 announcement replaced the square profile grid with a tall one (Net Influencer, 20 Jan 2025); a vendor guide states that the grid "now displays images in a 3:4 ratio (specifically 1013x1350 pixels)" and that "your Reels cover photos are still 1080x1920, but they'll appear as 1013x1350 on your grid"; it does not say which part of the cover is kept (Planoly, A complete guide to Instagram's new vertical grid, 28 Jan 2025, updated 31 Oct 2025; vendor guide). Mara's covers are the finished-piece still from the taped mark with the piece's name in the middle of the frame, so both the full cover and the grid preview should carry it, which she checks on her profile after the first post (her rule; the guide does not say where the crop falls).

Originality. Meta: "Don't post Reels that are visibly recycled from other apps (avoid 3rd-party fonts and watermarks)" (Meta Creators, 2022). PB4 §1 sets the same rule from Instagram's original content guidelines: the Instagram versions are exported from her own edit, never downloaded back from another app. Instagram's Edits app is "free for download" and exports "watermark-free" (Instagram, Introducing Edits, 22 Apr 2025; platform-official).

Shorts for Mara. PB3 gave Shorts no hours at 6 hours a week, and PB4 kept that; PB6 may revisit it on its own evidence. The Reel template in the deliverable produces a vertical file under three minutes, which is a Short by YouTube's definition, so a reader with Shorts hours can upload the same file; a Short with music carries the Content ID rule above, which is why Mara's Reels use only her own sound (her rule). YouTube says "Custom thumbnails for Shorts are currently only available to add in YouTube Studio on a computer" (YouTube Help 72431, checked 12 Nov 2026; platform-official), so a reader who works only on a phone, as Mara does, cannot set one.

06Carousels

The limit. Instagram: "You can upload up to 20 photos and videos and share them as a single post in Instagram Feed" (Instagram Help Centre, Share a post with multiple photos or videos on Instagram, read 12 Nov 2026; platform-official). The limit was raised from 10 in a global rollout reported on 9 August 2024 (Metricool, 9 Aug 2024; vendor news report, quoting no Instagram statement).

The shape. Instagram announced on 28 May 2025 that "when you upload a 3:4 image it'll now appear exactly as you shot it", and that "This works with both single-photo posts and carousels" (Social Media Today, 28 May 2025, quoting Instagram's Creators channel; platform statement via the press; no Instagram help page stating feed sizes was found). A vendor guide still advises "Stick with the 4:5 format (1080 x 1350 px) for feed posts" and says the grid shows a 3:4 preview (Metricool, Best Instagram post sizes, 21 Jan 2026; vendor guide). Mara's slides are 1080 × 1350, every slide the same shape, with the words kept in the middle where the grid's 3:4 preview keeps them (her rule).

The first slide. In the feed and on the grid the first slide is the only one a viewer sees before choosing to swipe, so it carries the whole promise of the post (inference from how the format displays). A claim circulates in creator advice that Instagram shows an unengaged carousel again starting from its second slide; no Instagram statement of it was found on 12 November 2026. Two press reports say it does, neither quoting Instagram: Lia Haberman summarises a 2024 Mosseri post as saying the algorithm gives a carousel "a second chance after it's posted, resurfacing it to followers who previously swiped by" (ICYMI, 14 Oct 2025; her summary, not his words), and Uppbeat paraphrases an undated Mosseri interview with Colin and Samir as saying a carousel scrolled past "may come back around with the second slide showing" (Uppbeat, 3 Nov 2026; a paraphrase, not a quotation). No rule here depends on it.

The order. No source found tests the order of slides. Mara's order follows the promise (her rule): the first slide is as found and finished side by side from the taped mark; then what was wrong; then one slide per step; then what went wrong; then the finished piece; then the last slide pointing to the full video through the profile link PB2 set. Eight slides, well under the limit: the 30 minutes PB4 gives each post (PB4 §3) is the binding constraint, and how carousels perform for her is PB7's to test.

07Titles and thumbnails inside YouTube's policy

What YouTube requires of a thumbnail. "For videos, try to upload a thumbnail with an aspect ratio of 16:9", "Have a resolution of 3840 x 2160 pixels for videos" with "a minimum width of 640 pixels", in JPG or PNG, up to 2 MB for a video thumbnail uploaded from a phone and 50 MB from a computer; a creator can "upload your own if your account is verified"; and thumbnails "may be rejected and a strike on your account" when they break the Community Guidelines, with "Repeat offenses" leading to "the removal of your custom thumbnail privileges for 30 days or even account termination" (YouTube Help, Add custom thumbnails on YouTube, answer 72431, checked 12 Nov 2026; platform-official).

YouTube's three statements on misleading packaging. Each describes a title or thumbnail that promises what the video does not deliver; each has a different consequence.

08Captions and contrast for accessibility

Captions. The web accessibility standard requires that "Captions are provided for all prerecorded audio content in synchronized media"; its purpose is to let people who are deaf or hard of hearing follow the media, and captions cover more than dialogue, identifying who is speaking and including "meaningful sound effects" (W3C, Understanding SC 1.2.2: Captions (Prerecorded), read 12 Nov 2026; international standard). WCAG is written for web content; applying it to a creator's videos is this unit's transfer. Both platforms generate captions from the audio and both say, or show, that the creator can correct them (§2, §5). Mara's rule: every caption track is read through and corrected before a post goes public, on YouTube in the cut window's scheduling slot, on Instagram while the Reel is made; a sound that carries meaning (a scraper slipping) gets a bracketed note on YouTube's track.

Contrast. WCAG requires text and "images of text" to have "a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1", and large-scale text at least 3:1, where "18 point text or 14 point bold text is judged to be large enough"; the ratio is "(L1 + 0.05) / (L2 + 0.05)" on relative luminance (W3C, Understanding SC 1.4.3: Contrast (Minimum), read 12 Nov 2026). Thumbnail and slide text are images of text in that sense; applying a web standard to them is again a transfer. 09An editing workflow that fits a fixed time box

The target against the evidence. PB4 gave the 10-minute edit 4.0 hours with a hard stop and asked this unit to show 10 minutes inside 4 hours or say what changes. The only published figure found is a vendor's rule of thumb: "As a rule of thumb, expect 30 minutes to an hour of editing time per minute of finished video", which "includes everything from skimming through the raw footage to rendering the final product" (Tasty Edits, How long does it take to edit a video?, published 19 May 2021, updated 17 Sep 2026; vendor, no method or data). On that figure 10 minutes takes 300 to 600 minutes (derived). Mara has 240. No source found measures how long a one-person edit takes under any workflow, so whether 240 is enough is unsourced; her log is the test.

What the template relies on. Each lever is a mechanism this course can point to; the minutes each saves are unmeasured.

  1. The footage is small by construction. One clip per step, five kinds of shot, stills from one mark (§1). The rule of thumb includes "skimming through the raw footage"; bounded footage shortens that part (inference).
  2. The voice note is the edit list. PB4's three prompts (what I did; what went wrong; what comes next) are recorded at the end of each session; two sessions' notes list the steps and the mistake in order before the edit opens.
  3. The structure is fixed before the edit. The runtime map (§4) is the same for every piece; the edit fills chapters that already exist.
  4. One pass per layer, each time-boxed, with a hard stop. PB4 §5 set out the evidence on switching costs and unfinished tasks and inferred one window ending on a finished thing; the template applies the same inference inside the window: picture, cutaways, sound and text are each done once, in order.

What changes if it does not fit. PB4 §7 already says what happens on the day: an edit not exported at the 4-hour stop is finished at the start of the next cut window, and that fortnight's Instagram drops from six posts to two. Across a quarter, the length changes before the hours, because the hours have no slack (PB3, D2: 24.0 of 24 per four weeks): if three consecutive edits overrun the stop, the next video is planned at about 8 minutes, the length PB3's cut order uses, from the next checkpoint, and the runtime map loses 2:00, 0:40 from each of chapters 3, 4 and 6 (her rule). If three consecutive edits finish with 30 minutes or more to spare, the spare time goes to the Instagram posts and the video stays at 10 minutes (her rule).

Where the edit stops. The 4 hours run from copying the clips to the exported file uploaded as private (her rule for the start; PB4, D1 C sets the export and private upload before the stop). The edit hands the cut window its inputs: the chapter list with timestamps, the Reel's start and end points, two or three candidate thumbnail frames and the stills for the carousels.
