01What a positioning is, and what it is not

Search was off for this unit. It was used on 12 November 2026 to check every source this unit cites, including the platforms' name, handle and text-field rules, and to look for the handles chosen for Mara. Every platform rule below carries that date. The labels for evidence are the ones PB1 §1 defines: platform-official, vendor, correlational, survivor sample, peer-reviewed, anecdote, derived.

The term. Al Ries and Jack Trout made "positioning" common in marketing with Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind (McGraw-Hill, 1981). Graham Mann's notes on the book quote its definition as "Positioning is what you do to the mind of the prospect, not what you do to the product", and summarise its reason as "The mind rejects complexity, so a strong position is usually a single word or idea you can own" (Graham Mann, book notes on Positioning). The book was not opened for this unit; the wording rests on those notes. It is marketing theory written about product advertising, without a dataset, and it is used here as a definition, not as evidence that anything works.

Applied to one person with an account, the definition separates two things that are easy to confuse.

A description of the person lists true attributes. For the running example, Mara Venn (Mara, fictional): 38, a full-time accountant, restores mid-century furniture at weekends, wants to teach restoration to people who have no workshop, 140 Instagram followers, no YouTube subscribers, films on a phone, 6 hours a week. Every item is true and none is ranked. A stranger who reads the list a day later can repeat one or two items at random, and nothing tells them which one the account is about.

A positioning is one sentence that says who the account is for and what it gives them, short and concrete enough that a stranger who has seen the profile or one video can say it to someone else. For Mara (the full statement is in the deliverable): Mara shows people who have no workshop how to restore a mid-century chair, table or small cabinet at home, start to finish, with tools that fit in one box. It names an audience (people with no workshop), what they get (a restoration they can do at home), the subject (mid-century furniture) and the thing that repeats in every video (one piece, start to finish, one box of tools).

Three consequences follow, and the rest of the unit is built on them.

  • The test is what a stranger says, and the author cannot run it on herself. Camerer, Loewenstein and Weber showed in experiments that better-informed people do not manage to set aside what they know when predicting the judgments of less-informed people, and named this the "curse of knowledge"; trading in markets reduced the effect by "approximately 50 percent" without removing it (Camerer, Loewenstein and Weber, Journal of Political Economy 97(5), 1989, 1232–1254). Peer-reviewed; the experiments concern economic judgments, not profiles. The transfer is this unit's: Mara knows what her account is for, so she is the one person who cannot tell whether her sentence carries it. §3 sets out the test with strangers.
  • The sentence has to survive a change of subject. Mara will restore a chair one fortnight and a cabinet the next, and may later move to lamps or upholstery. A sentence built on one object expires with it. §2 asks what evidence exists on which kind of sentence survives that.
  • The sentence is the one input the profile fields carry. The account name, the handle, the Instagram bio and the YouTube description are where a stranger meets it first (§4, §5). PB3 then tests the "who it is for" half against evidence about the audience, and PB4 turns the "what repeats" half into a series.

02The three patterns, and how each survives a change of subject

A personal brand can be built on a topic (what the account is about: mid-century furniture), on a method or repeatable format (what happens in every piece: one item, start to finish, one box of tools), or on the person (who presents it: Mara). Most accounts mix them. The question for a sentence is which of the three carries returning viewers when the subject of the next post changes. The evidence found, by pattern, with its class and grade. No source found compares the three directly, so every grade below is low, and that is the main finding of this section.

The topic

  • Platform-official, YouTube. YouTube's tips for its casual and regular viewer segments (viewers who come back) say: "Try creating content that's more consistent and similar in topic or format, or work with the same host", and "Create a series of videos from topics that have already proven popular with your viewers" (YouTube Help, New, casual, & regular viewers tips). Topic is one of three routes the page names. It gives no effect sizes and ranks none of the three.
  • Platform-official, Instagram. Adam Mosseri, head of Instagram, wrote on 25 August 2021 that Instagram search tries "to match what you type with relevant usernames, bios, captions, hashtags and places", and advised choosing a handle and profile name related to the content, and putting keywords about who you are and what the profile covers in the bio (Instagram, Breaking down how Instagram Search works, 25 Aug 2021). This is evidence that topic words in the name, handle and bio help a stranger find an Instagram account. It says nothing about returning followers or about a change of subject, and it is five years old; PB7 reads Instagram's current statements.
  • Vendor, YouTube. OverseerOS, a vendor of YouTube analytics tools, analysed 1,620 English-language long-form videos with at least 1 million views from 163 channels that had five or more such videos (published 21 August 2026). In 97.5% of the channels every qualifying video had a different topic query; the median channel's most repeated topic entity appeared in 20.0% of its winners. Its conclusion: "Successful channels repeated a recognizable world far more often than they repeated the exact same subject." The article states its own limits: the sample holds only videos past 1 million views, so it cannot compare successful with failed channels, "we cannot calculate the probability that a broad channel succeeds", topics were machine-extracted, and the findings are correlational (OverseerOS, Stick to one niche? 21 Aug 2026). Vendor data; survivor sample; YouTube only.

Grade: weak for survival. A topic is the pattern a stranger finds most easily on Instagram search (platform-official, about discovery). On a change of subject, the only platform statement treats it as one route among three, and the one dataset shows that channels with million-view videos change subject far more often than they repeat it: the winners vary subjects. A topic sentence ("mid-century chair restoration") is a good discovery sentence and the pattern most exposed when the subject moves; no source measures how much is lost when it does.

The method or repeatable format

  • Platform-official, YouTube. The same tips page names "format" alongside topic (YouTube Help, answer 13615784): a viewer can return for a format that does not require the same topic.
  • Platform statement, YouTube, about the mechanism. Todd Beaupré of YouTube's growth team said "For the most part, the algorithm for Discovery is focused more on individual videos", and "We aim to not overemphasize historical data if that data isn't particularly predictive of future video performance" (Search Engine Journal, 4 Mar 2024; a trade publication reporting YouTube's own statements). This unit's inference from those two lines, which no source tests: a change of subject does not by itself cut a new video off from recommendation on YouTube, so the risk of variety falls on returning viewers, the people who came for the last subject. That is the group a fixed format is meant to hold.
  • Peer-reviewed, about products. Aaker and Keller found that attitudes to a brand extension (a known brand on a new product) were higher when there was "a perception of 'fit' between the two product classes" and a perception of high quality for the original brand (Aaker and Keller, "Consumer Evaluations of Brand Extensions", Journal of Marketing 54(1), 1990, 27–41; abstract read on CiteDrive, whose record gives the year as 2018 in error). A secondary analysis of the original study and seven replications found support for the model, with the contribution of each component varying "by brand and culture", and concluded that "it is premature to make firm conclusions about theory on the basis of only one study" (Bottomley and Holden, Journal of Marketing Research 38(4), 2001, 494–500). These studies concern products. No study found tests fit for a creator's account. The transfer is this unit's: if viewers judge a new subject by whether it fits what the account is, an account defined by its method makes each new subject fit by construction.
  • Creator cases, YouTube, survivors. Mike Boyd's channel is a procedure applied to changing subjects: a description of him documents "himself learning a new skill as quickly as possible" (Net Influencer, Learning in public, 4 Mar 2026), and his 2016 round-up, titled "A round up of 2016's 'Learn Quick'", covered the violin, picking a lock, a Rubik's Cube in under two minutes, a reverse-steering bicycle, juggling and a unicycle (Laughing Squid, 30 Dec 2016). vidIQ, a third-party tracker, shows the channel created on 23 October 2008 with 2.93 million subscribers, 197 videos and 477,008,214 views on 11 November 2026 (vidIQ, Mike Boyd). Matt D'Avella's titles carried one formula across subjects: nine 2019 titles contain "30 days", from "I quit sugar for 30 days" (12 February 2019) to "I tried breathwork everyday for 30 days." (10 December 2019) (Rosetta, Matt D'Avella 2019), and "I tried zone 2 training for 30 days (what I learned)" was published on 30 March 2026 (Rosetta); Rosetta is a third-party index of the videos. vidIQ shows his channel created on 25 July 2011 with 4.06 million subscribers and 472 videos on 5 November 2026 (vidIQ, Matt D'Avella). Anecdote: these were chosen because they are known, and the channels that tried the same thing and stalled are in no source.

Grade: weak, and the best supported of the three for returning viewers. One platform statement names format as a route for returning viewers (YouTube, no effect size), one peer-reviewed literature supports fit for products, and two YouTube creators have carried one procedure across many subjects for years (anecdote, survivors). Nothing on Instagram addresses format and change of subject in a source found for this unit; PB7 covers Instagram's own statements on formats.

The person

  • Platform-official, YouTube. The tips page's third route is to "work with the same host" (YouTube Help, answer 13615784): returning viewers can come back for a presenter whatever the subject.
  • Peer-reviewed, about celebrities. Thomson, in three studies of what he calls "human brands", found that people become more strongly attached to one when it "enhances a person's feelings of autonomy and relatedness and does not suppress feelings of competence", and that "strong attachments are predictive of satisfied, trusting, and committed relationships" (Thomson, "Human Brands", Journal of Marketing 70(3), 2006, 104–119). Peer-reviewed; the human brands studied were celebrities, and the study measures attachment, not what happens when the person changes subject.
  • Creator cases, YouTube, survivors. Michelle Khare's channel runs under her own name, and her method has its own name, the "award-winning docuseries Challenge Accepted", in which she "tackled the world's toughest professions and stunts" (her own site, which shows 5.43 million YouTube subscribers, undated: Michelle Khare, About). Matt D'Avella's channel is also under his own name, with the method carried by the titles (above). A related case runs the other way: Mike Boyd and his partner started a second channel, Mike Boyd Climbs, so that he could train in one discipline over a longer period when the quick-learning format felt stale, according to the same profile (4 Mar 2026) (Net Influencer). A long run on one subject went to a new channel, and the method channel kept its format. Anecdote, one creator's choice, and the profile gives no numbers for the second channel.

Grade: weak. The person survives any change of subject by definition, since the name does not mention one. What the person does not do is tell a stranger what the account gives them: a name alone is the weakest sentence on §1's test until the stranger already knows the person. That is why the two own-name cases above carry the method somewhere else, in a series name or in the titles.

What no source shows, and what this unit does with it

No dataset found compares the growth or survival of topic-defined, method-defined and person-defined accounts, on either platform, and no source found shows that a topic account loses its returning viewers when it changes subject. That claim, common in growth advice, is unsourced here. The evidence supports this much: YouTube names topic, format and host as three routes to returning viewers without ranking them (platform-official); Instagram search matches topic words in the name, handle and bio (platform-official, 2021); channels with million-view videos vary their subjects far more than they repeat them (vendor, survivors); product brands extend further when the new product fits (peer-reviewed, products only); and a few method-defined and person-named YouTube channels have carried many subjects for years (anecdote).

The working rule this unit takes from that, and builds the deliverable on, is a combination: topic words for discovery, a repeatable method for returning viewers, the person for trust. Each part does a job the evidence connects it with, and the sentence does not depend on any one subject. It is a rule built from weak evidence, and it is stated as such.

03Testing the sentence: asking strangers to repeat it back

The curse of knowledge in §1 is the reason for the test: the author's own judgment of her sentence is the one judgment that cannot be trusted. The repeat-back test replaces it with what people who do not know the account actually say after meeting it.

What it measures. Whether a stranger, after seeing the profile or one video, can say in their own words who the account is for and what it gives them. It measures comprehension and recall of the positioning. It does not measure whether anyone wants it (PB3 tests the audience against evidence), whether the account will grow (PB6, PB7) or whether the name is legally usable (PB8).

The procedure, in outline (the full script is in the deliverable):

  1. Choose people who do not know the account or its author. Friends and family know the author, and colleagues know her job, so their answers carry what they already know. Where possible, choose people who could be in the audience (for Mara, people who own or like old furniture and have no workshop).
  2. Show one thing, cold. Either the profile screen as a stranger would see it (name, handle, bio or description) or one video, with no introduction.
  3. Wait. Ask after a delay, so the answer is what was kept, not what is still on the screen.
  4. Ask one open question. "In one sentence, what is this account, and who is it for?" No prompts, no multiple choice.
  5. Write the answer down word for word.
  6. Score it against the sentence's parts: who it is for, what it gives them, and what repeats.
  7. Change one field and retest with new people. A person who has taken the test once cannot take it again as a stranger.

What is convention and what is sourced. The reason to use strangers is sourced (the curse of knowledge, peer-reviewed, in economic judgments). The number of people (five), the delay (at least ten minutes, or the next day), the profile-or-video choice and the pass mark (four of five name both who and what) are this course's conventions. No study found validates any of them for testing a creator's positioning. With five people, one answer moves the result by 20 percentage points (derived: 1 of 5), so the test can catch a sentence that fails badly and cannot rank two sentences that both pass. Treat it as a filter, run before launch and again after the first quarter's videos.

Reading the answers. The answers that matter most are the wrong ones, read for which field misled:

  • An answer that gets the subject and misses the audience ("a furniture channel") says the "who it is for" is not in the name, bio or description, or is buried below the first line.
  • An answer that gets the person and nothing else ("some woman who fixes chairs") says the method is not visible.
  • An answer that names a different audience ("for antique dealers") says a word in the profile is pointing elsewhere; find the word.
  • An answer that repeats one striking detail ("she uses one box") and nothing else is a partial pass: the method is visible and the audience and the subject are not.

Asking people. Ask permission first, say what the answers are for, and do not publish anyone's words or face without their agreement. What the rules are on using other people's words and images is PB8's subject.

04Naming: the account name, the handle, and the checks on each

Each platform has two identifiers, and they do different jobs.

  • The account name (YouTube's channel name; Instagram's "name" field) is what a stranger reads first and repeats. It can carry spaces, capitals and topic words, and both platforms let it change.
  • The handle (YouTube's @handle; Instagram's username) is the address: unique on the platform, written without spaces, typed or tapped to find the account. YouTube states that handles "are distinct from channel names" (YouTube Help, Learn about YouTube handles). The handle is the scarce part, because only one account on each platform can hold it.

Three things follow from the table.

  • The YouTube handle rule is the strictest one found. A handle given up is held for 14 days and then anyone can take it (platform-official). A name change costs little; a handle change can lose the old address for good.
  • The Instagram limits rest on third-party pages. Bitly's page, which gives 150 and 30, cites no Instagram source. Keep the bio and username under those figures, and if the app refuses a text, the app is the authority.
  • Keep the name and the handle at 30 characters or fewer on both platforms. 30 is the lowest documented ceiling for the YouTube channel title (Data API) and the third-party figure for the Instagram username; a name that fits both can be used unchanged everywhere.

Checking availability on both platforms

A name is usable when the same handle can be held on both platforms, so a viewer who finds one finds the other. The check, in order:

  1. Write the handle in the form both platforms accept. Lower case, letters and numbers, with at most a period or underscore in the middle. YouTube's rules allow periods, underscores and hyphens not at the start or end (platform-official); a hyphen is not among the characters Instagram's help page suggests, so a hyphen-free handle is the safer form for both.
  2. Try to claim it on both platforms the same day. YouTube only accepts a handle that is not already in use (YouTube Help, answer 11585688); the Instagram username field accepts or refuses the name in the app. A handle that is free on one platform and taken on the other is a reason to choose another, before anything is published.
  3. Search the exact name on both platforms and on the web. An account holding the handle is one problem; an established account with a similar name in the same subject is another, since viewers will confuse the two. A web search for the name also shows what else the words already mean.
  4. Hold the handle as soon as it is chosen. On YouTube, the handle is the one field whose loss cannot be undone (above).

What this unit could check, and what it could not. On 12 November 2026, a web search for each of the three handles in the deliverable returned no profile under that exact handle on Instagram or YouTube. That does not show that a handle is free: a search engine indexes some profiles and not others, and the tools used for this unit cannot open Instagram or YouTube profile pages. Whether any of the five handles in D2 (the three options and the two fallbacks) is in use on either platform was not checked. The deliverable's pick is therefore conditional on step 2, done in the two apps, and names the fallback if it fails.

The trademark question

Whether a name can be used without infringing someone else's trade mark, and whether it could be registered, is a legal question this unit does not answer. PB8 takes it up, with the question to answer and the primary source to open for the EU, the UK and the US. Until then, a name chosen here is a working name, and nothing is spent on it (no logo, no domain, no printed material). This unit is information, not legal advice.

05The fields that carry the positioning

A stranger meets the positioning in three fields before any video: the name, the handle and the text under them. On each platform that text has a different job.

The Instagram bio (150 characters, third-party figure). It sits under the name on the profile screen, and Instagram search matches its words (Mosseri, 2021, above). It holds the sentence compressed: the subject and the audience in the first line, the method in the second, and where the long version lives in the last. It is too short for the person's story, which goes to YouTube.

The Instagram name field. Instagram search matches names and usernames too, and Mosseri's advice is a name related to the content that includes any name people know you by (Instagram, 25 Aug 2021). The name field can therefore carry both the person and the account name ("Mara · One Box Restoration") while the username holds the handle. Instagram's own limit on the name field was not found; keep it at 30 characters or fewer.

The YouTube channel description (1,000 characters in the Data API, shown in the channel header). It holds the full sentence and its expansion: who the channel is for, what each video does, what is left out, how often a video comes, and who presents it. The Help page says the description is "accessible in the channel header", and a reader who opens it reads from the top, so the first line carries the subject and the audience; how many characters show before a "more" link was not checked.

The YouTube channel name and handle. The name is the short form a stranger repeats; the handle is the address printed on Instagram and spoken in videos. The channel keywords field (500 characters, Data API) is a fourth place for topic words; YouTube states no effect of keywords on discovery on any page found, so it is filled and not relied on.

What the fields leave out. A positioning field is not a CV. The person's job, city and personal history are disclosure decisions, and PB8 sets the policy for them. Until PB8 is done, the deliverable keeps them out of the public copy: Mara's job appears in her expansion, which is for her, and not in her bio or description.

D5 · The repeat-back test, as a script

Run 1: the profile, week of 7 December 2026. Run 2: one video, at the Q1 review on Monday 5 April 2027. (Mara, fictional.)

Before.

  1. Find five people who do not know Mara or her work and are not colleagues: for example, friends of friends who own old furniture, or members of a local or online furniture or home group whose rules allow a request like this. Nobody who has taken the test before.
  2. Prepare the thing to show. Run 1: a screenshot of the Instagram profile (name, username, bio) and one of the YouTube channel header (name, handle, the first lines of the description). Run 2: one complete video, the most recent.
  3. Prepare a sheet with five rows and these columns: person, date, what was shown, delay, answer word for word, who (yes/no), what (yes/no), method (yes/no).

The words to use.

"I'm testing how an account comes across to someone who has never seen it. Could you look at this for about a minute? I won't explain anything about it. Later I'll ask you one question. There are no wrong answers, and I won't share what you say with your name on it."

Show the screenshots for about a minute (run 1) or the whole video (run 2). Take them away. Wait at least ten minutes, or until the next day.

"In one sentence, what is that account, and who is it for?"

Write down the answer exactly. Do not prompt, correct or explain. If they ask a question, say: "Whatever you remember is the answer."

"Thank you. Is there anything that confused you?"

Write that answer down too. Then, and only then, tell them what the account is, if they want to know.

Scoring. For each answer:

  • Who: it names people without a workshop, or restoring at home, or beginners at home. Yes or no.
  • What: it names restoring furniture (mid-century or old furniture both count). Yes or no.
  • Method: it mentions one box, a small set of tools, or one piece start to finish. Yes or no.

Pass mark (convention, §3): at least four of five answers have both who and what. The method is recorded and does not decide the pass in run 1.

If it fails. Read the wrong answers against §3's list: subject without audience means the first line of the bio and description is changed to lead with "no workshop"; person without method means the box goes into the first line; a different audience means the misleading word is found and removed. Change one field, then rerun with five new people. Record each run's sheet with the date and keep it with that quarter's review (PB10).
