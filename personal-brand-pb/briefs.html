PERSONAL BRAND · PUBLIC · BRIEFS

Building a Personal Brand on Instagram and YouTube: the briefs

One brief per unit, self-contained: the shared preamble (the reader, the source-material rule, Mara, the neutral-ground rule, the course goal, the sourcing standard, the tone) is baked into every open brief, followed by the unit's Aim, Takes and Gives lines. Written units show their note instead. The course itself is read in Course PB.

PB1

What to expect: odds, timeline, cost

written

Sourced numbers for a one-person account on each platform, the time to each milestone, the plateau where most stop, and what it costs in hours and money.

Aim set a sourced expectation for your first year on each platform and write the rule under which you would stop

Takes A9 as source material; A9 is YouTube-led, so the Instagram benchmarks are new research. Gives the expectation sheet PB6, PB7 and PB10 read against; the hours budget PB3 and PB4 allocate; the checkpoint dates PB10 runs.

Shipsexpectation sheet + hours budget, stop rule and checkpoint dates

Read the unit →
Opus brief
Unit PB1, What to expect: odds, timeline, cost. Search before writing. Cover: what a one-person account can expect on YouTube and on Instagram in its first 12, 24 and 36 months, from the largest datasets available, each with its sample, date and label; the milestones that matter on each platform and what each unlocks, with the monetisation thresholds as the platforms state them today, dated; how long each milestone takes at different posting rates where data exists, and a plain statement where it does not; the plateau and why most accounts stop there; what the evidence says about who continues; the cost in hours and in money of a phone-only setup; survivor bias in every creator case and how to read around it; what no dataset measures. Deliverable: the expectation sheet (one row per benchmark: platform, metric, value, source, sample, date, label), worked for Mara at 6 hours a week; her hours-and-money budget; her stop rule and the quarterly checkpoint dates of her first year with what is read at each (PB10 runs them); then all three as a template.
PB2

Positioning: the sentence a stranger can repeat

written

What a positioning is, the patterns a personal brand is built on with the evidence for each graded, naming and handle checks, and the profile fields that carry it.

Aim state in one sentence who the account is for and what it gives them, and put that sentence into both profiles

Takes A1 as source material; PB1. Gives the positioning PB3 tests against an audience and PB4 turns into a series; the name PB8 checks for trademarks.

Shipspositioning statement + name pick + Instagram bio and YouTube description

Read the unit →
Opus brief
Unit PB2, Positioning: the sentence a stranger can repeat. Cover: what a positioning is and is not (a sentence a stranger can repeat against a description of the person); the patterns a personal brand is built on (a topic, a method or repeatable format, the person) and the evidence on how each survives a change of subject, graded, with the thinness of that evidence said plainly; how to test a sentence by asking strangers to repeat it back; naming: the account name against the handle, each platform's character limits and change rules (dated), checking availability on both platforms, and the trademark question left to PB8; the Instagram bio and the YouTube channel description as the fields that carry the positioning, with their limits. Deliverable: Mara's positioning statement (one sentence and a 60-word expansion), three name options with the checks run on each and one pick, her Instagram bio and YouTube description written in full, and the repeat-back test as a script; then the template.
PB3

Audience and the two platforms

written

Defining an audience from evidence, what Instagram and YouTube each do for a small account, where an email list fits, and how to split the hours.

Aim describe your audience with a basis for every trait and split your hours between Instagram and YouTube

Takes A2 as source material; PB1, PB2. Gives the posting rates PB4 schedules; the platform roles PB6 and PB7 build on; the email-list evidence PB9 prices.

Shipsaudience profile + hours allocation at three budgets

Read the unit →
Opus brief
Unit PB3, Audience and the two platforms. Search before writing. Cover: defining an audience by what its members do and need rather than by demographics alone, and how each trait is evidenced (platform data, large surveys, the public audiences of comparable accounts), each with its label; who uses Instagram and who uses YouTube, from the largest current surveys, dated; what each platform does for a small account (discovery against depth, reach to followers and to non-followers, how long a post keeps working); what is and is not measured about short-form viewers becoming long-form viewers; the email list as the channel you own, with the conversion data that exists; how to split the hours between the platforms at 3, 6 and 10 hours a week, and what is cut first in a bad week. Deliverable: Mara's audience profile (one sentence, then a table of traits with the basis and label for each) and the platform allocation at the three budgets with the cut order; then the template.

written

Capture once, one anchor piece, derivatives from the same material; series and seasons; batching in two windows.

Aim run a publishing system that produces every post of a fortnight from two work sessions

Takes A3 as source material; PB1, PB2, PB3. Gives the calendar PB5 produces for; the cadence PB6, PB7 and PB10 measure.

Shipssystem checklist + 12-week calendar

Read the unit →
Opus brief
Unit PB4, The content system. Cover: the capture layer (what is recorded during the work itself, so nothing is staged or filmed twice); the anchor piece and its cadence; the derivative layer on each platform, with posting rates set from PB3's evidence rather than habit; series and seasons, and why a bounded run with an end is easier to hold than an open one; batching in two windows a fortnight and what the evidence on task-switching does and does not support; the idea backlog; what is dropped first when a week is lost. Deliverable: the system as a reusable checklist, and Mara's 12-week calendar with hours per item, every week summed and inside 6 hours; then the template calendar with the rules for 3 and for 10 hours a week.

Shipsproduction checklist + timed edit template + Reel and carousel templates

Opus brief
Write one unit of a self-study course in the long-form report format of the Learning Project's markdown courses: a long-form report in Markdown. Length is whatever the topic needs (2,000–6,000 words); do not pad and do not cut to a target. Reader: an adult with a real skill or pursuit and a full-time job, who wants to build a personal brand and a following on Instagram and YouTube; has under 1,000 followers on either platform, a phone, no team and 3 to 10 hours a week; is sceptical of growth advice; may live in any country and publishes in English; reads in order (each unit assumes the ones before it), reads on a phone, checks the citations, and wants genuine depth rather than a primer. Address the reader as 'you'. This course is written to be shared with anyone; it is an exception to the Learning Project's single-reader rule (decided 11 Nov 2026). Source material: the Takes line names the units of a private course this unit is adapted from. Take their evidence and their structure where they serve this reader; re-verify every claim you carry over against its source before you use it; carry over nothing about that course's own reader (no name, employer, city, health detail, sport, business, household figure, handle or project of his appears here); and never refer to the private course or its units, which a reader of this course cannot open. Point only to units of this course, by code (PB3, DD-PB1). Running example: Mara Venn (fictional; any resemblance to a real person or account is unintended), 38, a full-time accountant who restores mid-century furniture at weekends and wants to teach restoration to people who have no workshop; she starts with no YouTube subscribers and 140 Instagram followers, films on a phone and has 6 hours a week. Every fact about Mara is invented by the unit that needs it, labelled '(Mara, fictional)' on its first use in the unit, kept consistent with the facts in generator/data/personal-brand/case-facts.md, and never used as evidence for a claim about the world: her numbers are illustrations, and every benchmark she is set against is sourced. Deliverables are built twice: in full for Mara, then as a template the reader fills, each blank saying what goes in it. Neutral ground: no country's tax rates or thresholds are stated as the reader's; an amount appears in its source's currency with its date; where law matters, give the question to answer and the primary source to open for the EU, the UK and the US, and say that the unit is information, not legal or tax advice. Two platforms: a claim about growth, formats or metrics says which platform it holds for. Tone: direct, no flattery, no filler, no motivational language, no 'not X but Y' constructions. Course goal: by the end of the course you can state in one sentence who your account is for and what it gives them; have run a publishing system for twelve weeks inside an hours budget you set yourself; and read your Instagram and YouTube numbers against sourced benchmarks each quarter to decide, with evidence, whether to continue, change format or stop. Sourcing standard: every claim that carries a number, a mechanism or a recommendation is sourced where it is made; acceptable sources are peer-reviewed work, platform-official documentation or statements, large-sample vendor datasets (named, with sample size and date), and documented creator cases with verifiable numbers; creator anecdotes are labelled as such; correlational and vendor data is labelled as such; no uncited figures; no invented handles, names, studies or numbers (Mara's labelled facts apart); if a claim cannot be sourced, say so and leave it out of the deliverable. A unit that reads as advice without evidence is a failed unit. Links are given as links, so the course bibliography can collect them. Figures: where a section's content has a shape, declare it as a fenced viz block of a kind kit/viz.py draws (one to four a unit is a guide; none where nothing has a shape); a figure adds no fact, and its caption says what it shows and where its data comes from. Where the unit depends on current facts (platform rules and thresholds, features, prices, published benchmarks), search before writing and date the facts. End with the unit's deliverable, built out in full, not described.
